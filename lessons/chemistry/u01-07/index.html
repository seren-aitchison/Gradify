Atomic theory

Everything is made of atoms. The modern atomic model grew over two centuries from Dalton's indivisible spheres to a tiny nucleus surrounded by electrons in energy levels. This lesson traces the evidence that changed the model.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Describe Dalton's atomic theory and which parts still hold
  • Explain how Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr changed the model, and the evidence for each
  • Describe the modern nuclear atom

Before you start

  • Elements and compounds

Dalton (1808)

John Dalton proposed that elements are made of tiny indivisible atoms, that atoms of one element are identical, and that compounds form when atoms combine in whole-number ratios. This explained the laws of definite and multiple proportions. We now know atoms are divisible and that isotopes of an element differ in mass, but the idea of atoms combining in fixed ratios remains central.

Inside the atom

  • Thomson (1897) discovered the electron from cathode rays and proposed the "plum pudding" model: electrons in a sphere of positive charge.
  • Rutherford (1911), from the gold-foil experiment of Geiger and Marsden: most alpha particles passed straight through, but a few bounced back. The atom must be mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bohr (1913): electrons occupy fixed energy levels (shells); atoms emit light of specific wavelengths when electrons drop between levels.
  • Chadwick (1932) discovered the neutron, explaining why atoms are heavier than their protons alone.

The modern atom

The nucleus, about 10⁻¹⁵ m across, contains protons and neutrons and almost all the mass; the atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Electrons are described by quantum mechanics as occupying orbitals, regions of probability, within energy levels.

The history shows how science works: each model explained the evidence of its time, and was replaced when new experiments (cathode rays, alpha scattering, line spectra) produced results it couldn't explain. Models are judged by how well they explain and predict, not by whether they are final.

Key idea

The model of the atom changed as new evidence appeared: indivisible atoms, then electrons, then the nucleus, then energy levels and orbitals.

Common mistake
Picturing electrons orbiting like planets. Bohr's orbits were a step; electrons are described by orbitals (probability clouds), not fixed paths.

Summary

  • Dalton → Thomson (electrons) → Rutherford (nucleus) → Bohr (energy levels) → Chadwick (neutron) → quantum orbitals.
  • The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom.

Key terms

Nucleus
The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons
Energy level
A fixed energy that electrons in an atom can have; also called a shell

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

What did the gold-foil experiment show?

Most of the atom is empty space and the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

Which part of Dalton's theory is no longer accepted, and why?

That atoms are indivisible (they contain electrons, protons and neutrons) and that all atoms of an element are identical (isotopes differ in mass).

Check your understanding

  1. Which particle did Thomson discover?
    • The electron
    • The neutron
    • The nucleus
    • The proton
    Show the answer

    The electron. Thomson identified electrons as negatively charged particles in cathode rays in 1897.

  2. In the gold-foil experiment, why did a few alpha particles bounce back?
    • Alpha particles are negative
    • The foil was too thick
    • They hit electrons
    • They hit or came very close to a small, dense, positively charged nucleus and were repelled
    Show the answer

    They hit or came very close to a small, dense, positively charged nucleus and were repelled. Only a concentrated positive charge could deflect fast, positive alpha particles through large angles.

  3. Why couldn't the plum-pudding model explain the gold-foil results?
    • It had no electrons
    • It predicted that all particles would bounce back
    • It said atoms were indivisible
    • Its positive charge was spread out, which would deflect alpha particles only slightly, never back towards the source
    Show the answer

    Its positive charge was spread out, which would deflect alpha particles only slightly, never back towards the source. Spread-out charge produces weak fields; large-angle scattering needs the charge concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
