Balancing equations

Balancing makes the number of atoms of each element the same on both sides of an equation, reflecting the conservation of mass. This lesson gives a systematic method and practice with harder cases.

Before you start

  • Chemical equations

Conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants (Lavoisier, 1789). In an open container, mass can seem to change when a gas escapes or is absorbed.

A method

  • Write the correct formulae; never change them while balancing.
  • Count the atoms of each element on each side.
  • Balance elements that appear in only one substance on each side first; leave hydrogen and oxygen, and elements that appear as free elements, until last.
  • Treat a polyatomic ion that appears unchanged on both sides as a single unit.
  • Check every element at the end, and use the smallest whole-number coefficients.
Worked example

Balance the combustion of propane: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

  1. Carbon: 3 on the left, so 3CO₂.
  2. Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so 4H₂O.
  3. Oxygen: right side has 3 × 2 + 4 × 1 = 10 atoms, so 5O₂.
  4. Balanced: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.
Worked example

Balance Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃.

  1. Oxygen is 2 on the left and 3 on the right; the lowest common multiple is 6: 3O₂ and 2Al₂O₃.
  2. Then aluminium: 4 on the right, so 4Al.
  3. Balanced: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃.

Balance by adjusting coefficients only, leaving free elements to last and using the smallest whole numbers.

Common mistakeStopping before checking every element. Changing one coefficient often unbalances another element.

Summary

  • Atoms are conserved, so equations must balance.
  • Balance elements in single compounds first, free elements last, and check everything.

Key terms

Conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants
Balanced equation
An equation with the same number of atoms of each element on both sides

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Why must chemical equations be balanced?

Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in reactions, so each element must have the same number of atoms on both sides.

Balance: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂.

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.

Check your understanding

  1. Which equation is balanced?
    • 2H₂ + 2O₂ → 2H₂O
    • 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
    • H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
    • H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
    Show the answer

    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. This one has 4 H and 2 O atoms on each side, so it is balanced.

  2. Balance Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂. What are the coefficients?
    • 1, 1, 2, 1
    • 1, 2, 2, 2
    • 1, 3, 2, 3
    • 2, 3, 4, 3
    Show the answer

    1, 3, 2, 3. Fe₂O₃ gives 2 Fe; its 3 O atoms go to 3 CO₂, needing 3 CO: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.

  3. Magnesium ribbon is heated in an open crucible and its mass increases. Why doesn't this break the law of conservation of mass?
    • Magnesium expands when heated
    • Mass is created when metals are heated
    • Oxygen from the air combines with the magnesium; including its mass, total mass is conserved
    • The crucible gained mass
    Show the answer

    Oxygen from the air combines with the magnesium; including its mass, total mass is conserved. The product MgO includes oxygen atoms that came from the air; the system wasn't closed.
