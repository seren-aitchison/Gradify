Physical and chemical changes

In a physical change the same substance remains, perhaps in a different state or shape; in a chemical change new substances form. This lesson sets out the evidence for each and why the distinction matters.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Distinguish physical and chemical changes and give examples
  • List evidence that a chemical reaction has taken place
  • Explain both kinds of change at the particle level

Before you start

  • Matter and its classification

Physical changes

Changes of state (melting, boiling, condensing), dissolving and changes of shape are physical changes. The particles are the same before and after; only their arrangement, spacing or movement changes. Physical changes are usually easy to reverse.

Chemical changes

In a chemical change, bonds are broken and new bonds are made, so atoms are rearranged into new substances. Burning, rusting, cooking and digestion are chemical changes. They are often hard to reverse.

Evidence of a reaction

  • A gas is given off (bubbles not caused by boiling).
  • A colour change that isn't just mixing or dilution.
  • A precipitate (an insoluble solid) forms from solutions.
  • An energy change: heat released or absorbed, light or sound.

None of these is proof on its own: boiling releases bubbles and dissolving some salts makes the solution colder, both physical changes. The best evidence is that a new substance with different properties has formed.

Worked example

Classify (a) ice melting, (b) magnesium burning, (c) sugar dissolving in tea, (d) iron rusting.

  1. (a) Physical: H₂O remains H₂O.
  2. (b) Chemical: magnesium oxide, a new white solid, forms with a bright flame.
  3. (c) Physical: sugar molecules spread among water molecules; evaporating the water recovers the sugar.
  4. (d) Chemical: iron reacts with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron(III) oxide.

A chemical change makes new substances by breaking and forming bonds; a physical change doesn't.

Common mistakeCalling boiling water a chemical change because bubbles form. The bubbles are water vapour, the same substance.

Summary

  • Physical changes keep the same substances; chemical changes form new ones.
  • Gas, colour change, precipitate and energy change are evidence of a reaction, not proof.

Key terms

Physical change
A change in which no new substance is formed
Chemical change
A change in which one or more new substances are formed
Precipitate
An insoluble solid formed when solutions react

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Give four observations that suggest a chemical reaction has happened.

Gas produced, colour change, precipitate formed, temperature change (or light given out).

Why is dissolving salt in water a physical change?

No new substance forms: evaporating the water gives back the salt.

Check your understanding

  1. Which is a chemical change?
    • Alcohol evaporating
    • Glass breaking
    • Ice melting
    • Wood burning
    Show the answer

    Wood burning. Burning makes new substances (CO₂, water, ash); the others change only shape or state.

  2. Mixing two colourless solutions forms a yellow solid. What has most likely happened?
    • A chemical reaction producing an insoluble precipitate
    • A physical change in which one of the solutions froze solid
    • A physical change: the solid was already dissolved before
    • Water evaporated, leaving behind the yellow solute as a solid
    Show the answer

    A chemical reaction producing an insoluble precipitate. A new solid substance appearing from two solutions is a precipitation reaction.

  3. When ammonium nitrate dissolves in water the temperature falls sharply. Is this evidence of a chemical reaction?
    • No: reactions always give out heat, so a fall in temperature proves that only a physical change has happened
    • Not by itself: dissolving is usually classed as physical, and an energy change alone doesn't prove a new substance has formed
    • Yes: a sharp temperature fall can only come from bonds forming in a reaction, so a new substance must have been made
    • Yes: ammonium nitrate is a compound, so whenever it is mixed with water a chemical reaction must take place
    Show the answer

    Not by itself: dissolving is usually classed as physical, and an energy change alone doesn't prove a new substance has formed. Energy changes accompany both physical and chemical processes; evidence of a new substance is needed.
