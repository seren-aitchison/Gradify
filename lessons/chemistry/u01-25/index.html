Units and measurements

Every measurement in chemistry needs a number, a unit and an uncertainty. This lesson covers the SI units used in chemistry, common non-SI units, choosing apparatus and estimating measurement uncertainty.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Use SI and common chemistry units for mass, volume, amount, temperature, pressure and energy
  • Choose apparatus for the precision required
  • Estimate the uncertainty of a measurement and combine percentage uncertainties

Before you start

  • Significant figures

Units in chemistry

  • Mass: kg (SI), usually g. Volume: m³ (SI), usually dm³ and cm³ (1 dm³ = 1 L = 1,000 cm³).
  • Amount: mol. Concentration: mol dm⁻³.
  • Temperature: kelvin, K = °C + 273.15. Gas laws need kelvin.
  • Pressure: Pa (N m⁻²); 1 atm = 101,325 Pa; 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa (used for standard conditions).
  • Energy: J, usually kJ mol⁻¹ for enthalpy changes.

Choosing apparatus

Burettes (±0.05 cm³ per reading) and pipettes (±0.06 cm³ for 25 cm³) are far more precise than measuring cylinders (±0.5 cm³ for 50 cm³), and a two-decimal-place balance reads to ±0.005 g. Choose apparatus so the percentage uncertainty is small compared with the effect you are measuring.

Uncertainty

percentage uncertainty = uncertainty / measured value × 100

For a burette titre, two readings are taken, so the uncertainty is 2 × 0.05 = ±0.10 cm³. When values are multiplied or divided, their percentage uncertainties add.

Worked example

A titre is 24.50 cm³ (burette uncertainty ±0.05 cm³ per reading). Find its percentage uncertainty.

  1. Absolute uncertainty = 2 × 0.05 = 0.10 cm³.
  2. Percentage uncertainty = 0.10 ÷ 24.50 × 100 = 0.41%.

Report a value with its unit and uncertainty, convert to kelvin for gas laws, and choose apparatus to keep percentage uncertainty small.

Common mistakeUsing °C in gas-law calculations. Temperatures must be in kelvin.

Summary

  • Kelvin for temperature in calculations; dm³ and cm³ for volume; mol for amount.
  • Percentage uncertainties add when values are multiplied or divided.

Key terms

Kelvin
The SI unit of thermodynamic temperature; 0 K is absolute zero and T/K = θ/°C + 273.15
Absolute uncertainty
The uncertainty of a measurement in the same units as the measurement
Percentage uncertainty
The absolute uncertainty as a percentage of the measured value

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Convert 25 °C to kelvin.

298 K (298.15 K).

Why is the uncertainty of a burette titre twice the reading uncertainty?

The titre is the difference between two readings, each with its own uncertainty.

Check your understanding

  1. What is 1 dm³ in cm³?
    • 1,000 cm³
    • 1,000,000 cm³
    • 10 cm³
    • 100 cm³
    Show the answer

    1,000 cm³. 1 dm = 10 cm, so 1 dm³ = 10³ cm³ = 1,000 cm³.

  2. Which apparatus gives the most precise measurement of 25.0 cm³ of solution?
    • A 100 cm³ beaker
    • A 25 cm³ conical flask
    • A 25 cm³ volumetric pipette
    • A 50 cm³ measuring cylinder
    Show the answer

    A 25 cm³ volumetric pipette. A class A pipette is accurate to about ±0.06 cm³; the others are much less precise.

  3. A concentration is calculated from a mass (±1.0%) and a volume (±0.2%). What is the approximate percentage uncertainty of the concentration?
    • 0.2%
    • 0.8%
    • 1.0%
    • 1.2%
    Show the answer

    1.2%. When quantities are divided, percentage uncertainties add: 1.0 + 0.2 = 1.2%.
