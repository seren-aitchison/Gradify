Chemistry · School 1: Foundations of Chemistry · Matter

States of matter

Substances exist as solids, liquids or gases depending on the balance between the energy of their particles and the forces between them. The particle model explains each state's properties and the energy changes when state changes.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Describe the particle arrangement and motion in solids, liquids and gases
  • Explain changes of state in terms of energy and intermolecular forces
  • Interpret a heating curve

Before you start

  • Matter and its classification

The particle model

  • Solid: particles closely packed in a regular arrangement, vibrating about fixed positions; fixed shape and volume.
  • Liquid: particles close together but randomly arranged and moving past each other; fixed volume, takes the shape of its container.
  • Gas: particles far apart, moving fast and randomly in all directions; fills its container and is easily compressed.

Changing state

Melting, boiling and sublimation need energy to overcome the forces between particles. Freezing and condensing release energy as forces form. During a change of state the temperature stays constant: the energy goes into separating (or is released by bringing together) particles, not into making them move faster.

Heating curves

A heating curve (temperature against time with steady heating) rises in each state and has flat sections at the melting and boiling points. The boiling plateau is longer than the melting one for water, because boiling separates the particles completely and needs more energy (enthalpy of vaporisation 40.7 kJ/mol versus enthalpy of fusion 6.01 kJ/mol).

Worked example

Explain why sweating cools you.

  1. Water molecules with the most kinetic energy escape from the liquid on your skin (evaporation).
  2. The remaining molecules have a lower average kinetic energy, so the temperature of the sweat falls.
  3. Heat then flows from your skin to the cooler sweat.

State depends on particle energy versus the strength of forces between particles; temperature is constant during a change of state.

Common mistakeThinking particles in a solid don't move. They vibrate constantly about fixed positions.

Summary

  • Solids vibrate in place, liquids slide past each other, gases move freely.
  • Energy is absorbed in melting and boiling and released in freezing and condensing, at constant temperature.

Key terms

Sublimation
A change directly from solid to gas without melting
Enthalpy of vaporisation
The energy needed to turn one mole of a liquid into gas at its boiling point
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion; the average kinetic energy of particles rises with temperature

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Why does temperature stay constant while ice melts?

The energy supplied is used to overcome the forces holding particles in the solid, not to increase their kinetic energy.

Why can gases be compressed but liquids hardly at all?

Gas particles are far apart with empty space between them; liquid particles are already touching.

Check your understanding

  1. In which state do particles move fastest at the same temperature?
    • Gas
    • Liquid
    • Solid
    • They have the same average kinetic energy
    Show the answer

    They have the same average kinetic energy. Temperature measures average kinetic energy; at the same temperature it is the same, though gas particles move freely over larger distances.

  2. Why is the boiling plateau on water's heating curve longer than the melting plateau?
    • Boiling separates the particles completely, needing much more energy than melting, which only loosens the arrangement
    • Steam is heavier than ice
    • The thermometer is slower at high temperature
    • Water boils at a higher temperature
    Show the answer

    Boiling separates the particles completely, needing much more energy than melting, which only loosens the arrangement. The enthalpy of vaporisation (40.7 kJ/mol) is much larger than the enthalpy of fusion (6.01 kJ/mol).

  3. Iodine crystals turn straight into a purple vapour when warmed. What does this suggest about the forces between iodine molecules?
    • Iodine is an ionic compound
    • Iodine molecules split into atoms
    • They are very strong covalent bonds
    • They are weak enough to be overcome at low temperature
    Show the answer

    They are weak enough to be overcome at low temperature. Sublimation of a molecular solid shows weak intermolecular forces; the covalent I–I bonds stay intact.
