Biology · School 2: Cell Biology · Specialisation and communication

Cellular organisation

Multicellular organisms are organised in levels: specialised cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work together in organ systems. This lesson connects cell biology to the body as a whole.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Define tissue, organ and organ system with examples
  • Name the four main types of animal tissue and the main plant tissues
  • Explain why multicellular organisms need organisation

Before you start

  • Cell specialisation

Cells to systems

  • A tissue is a group of similar cells working together, with the material between them: for example, cardiac muscle.
  • An organ is a structure made of several tissues working together: the heart contains muscle, connective tissue, nervous tissue and epithelium.
  • An organ system is a group of organs working together: the heart and blood vessels form the circulatory system.

Types of tissue

Animals have four basic tissue types: epithelial (covering surfaces and lining tubes), connective (supporting and binding, including bone, cartilage, blood and fat), muscle (skeletal, cardiac and smooth), and nervous. Plants have dermal tissue (epidermis), vascular tissue (xylem and phloem) and ground tissue (such as mesophyll), produced by dividing regions called meristems.

Why organisation is needed

A large organism's cells are far from the outside, and its small surface area to volume ratio means diffusion across the body surface is far too slow. Specialised organ systems solve this: lungs and gut provide large exchange surfaces, the circulation carries materials to every cell, and nervous and endocrine systems coordinate everything. Each cell still depends on its own membrane transport and metabolism, so organisation builds on, and depends on, the cell biology of this school.

Organisation also brings emergent properties: a heart pumps, though no single muscle cell does; a brain thinks, though no neuron does.

Cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs form systems; this organisation overcomes the limits of size and lets specialised cells cooperate.

Common mistakeCalling blood "not a tissue" because it is liquid. Blood is a connective tissue: cells in a fluid matrix, plasma.

Summary

  • Organisation runs from cells to tissues, organs and organ systems.
  • Animals have epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue; plants have dermal, vascular and ground tissue.
  • Organ systems overcome the limits of diffusion in large organisms.

Key terms

Tissue
A group of similar cells, with the material between them, that work together to carry out a function
Organ
A structure made of several tissues working together to perform a function
Organ system
A group of organs working together to carry out a major function

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Name the four main types of animal tissue.

Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue.

Why does the stomach count as an organ?

It is made of several tissues (epithelium, muscle, connective and nervous tissue) working together to digest food.

Check your understanding

  1. Which is a tissue?
    • A red blood cell
    • Cardiac muscle
    • The circulatory system
    • The heart
    Show the answer

    Cardiac muscle. Cardiac muscle is a group of similar cells; the heart is an organ and the circulatory system an organ system.

  2. Xylem, phloem and mesophyll are found together in a leaf. What is the leaf?
    • A single specialised cell
    • A single tissue
    • An organ system
    • An organ, made of several tissues
    Show the answer

    An organ, made of several tissues. A leaf combines dermal, vascular and ground tissues working together, which makes it an organ.

  3. Why can't a large animal rely on diffusion across its skin for its oxygen supply, as a flatworm can?
    • Its skin has no cell membranes
    • Its surface area to volume ratio is small and most cells are far from the surface, so it needs lungs and a circulation
    • Large animals need no oxygen
    • Oxygen can't diffuse through tissues
    Show the answer

    Its surface area to volume ratio is small and most cells are far from the surface, so it needs lungs and a circulation. Diffusion is only fast over short distances; organ systems provide exchange surfaces and transport to every cell.
