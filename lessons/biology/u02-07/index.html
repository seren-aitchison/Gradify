Bacterial cells

Bacteria are the most familiar prokaryotes. Their cell walls, built from peptidoglycan, come in two main designs that the Gram stain tells apart, and these differences matter for how we treat bacterial infections.

Before you start

  • Prokaryotic cells

The peptidoglycan wall

The bacterial wall is made of peptidoglycan: long sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides into a single mesh around the cell. It holds the cell's shape (rods, spheres, spirals) and stops it bursting from osmotic water uptake.

Gram-positive and Gram-negative

  • Gram-positive bacteria (such as Staphylococcus) have a thick peptidoglycan layer that keeps the crystal violet stain and appears purple.
  • Gram-negative bacteria (such as E. coli) have a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide; they lose the stain and are counterstained pink.

The outer membrane makes Gram-negative bacteria harder to kill with some antibiotics, because it blocks many molecules from reaching the wall.

Why antibiotics can be selective

Human cells have no peptidoglycan. Penicillins block the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan, so growing bacteria build weak walls and burst. Other antibiotics target bacterial 70S ribosomes, which differ from our cytoplasmic 80S ribosomes. Targeting structures that only bacteria have is what makes these drugs selectively toxic.

Key idea

Bacterial walls are made of peptidoglycan; its thickness and the presence of an outer membrane divide bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative.

Common mistake
Thinking antibiotics work against viruses. Viruses have no wall or ribosomes of their own, so antibiotics have nothing to act on.

Summary

  • Peptidoglycan walls give bacteria their shape and protect them from bursting.
  • Gram-positive bacteria have thick walls; Gram-negative have thin walls plus an outer membrane.
  • Antibiotics are selective because they target bacterial walls and ribosomes.

Key terms

Peptidoglycan
The mesh of sugar chains cross-linked by peptides that forms the bacterial cell wall
Gram stain
A staining method that separates bacteria into Gram-positive (purple) and Gram-negative (pink) by their wall structure
Selective toxicity
Harming a pathogen while sparing the host, by targeting structures the host lacks

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Why do Gram-positive bacteria stain purple?

Their thick peptidoglycan layer keeps the crystal violet stain after the decolourising step.

Why doesn't penicillin harm human cells?

It blocks the cross-linking of peptidoglycan, which human cells don't have.

Check your understanding

  1. What is the bacterial cell wall made of?
    • Cellulose
    • Chitin
    • Peptidoglycan
    • Phospholipids
    Show the answer

    Peptidoglycan. Bacterial walls are peptidoglycan; cellulose is the plant wall polymer and chitin the fungal one.

  2. A bacterium stains pink after a Gram stain. What does this tell you about its structure?
    • It has a thick peptidoglycan layer
    • It has a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane
    • It has no cell wall at all
    • It is a fungus
    Show the answer

    It has a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane. Pink after the counterstain means Gram-negative: a thin wall with an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane.

  3. Penicillin kills actively dividing bacteria but has little effect on bacteria that are not growing. Why?
    • It only enters dividing cells' nuclei
    • It stops new peptidoglycan cross-links forming, which only matters while cells are building new wall
    • It works by blocking photosynthesis
    • Non-growing bacteria have no wall
    Show the answer

    It stops new peptidoglycan cross-links forming, which only matters while cells are building new wall. The drug weakens newly made wall; growing cells expanding with defective walls burst, while dormant cells aren't building wall.
