Membrane structure

Membranes are built from a double layer of phospholipids with proteins floating in it, a structure described by the fluid mosaic model. This lesson explains how the parts fit together and why the structure behaves as it does.

Before you start

  • Cell membranes

The phospholipid bilayer

A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head (a phosphate group) and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. In water, phospholipids arrange themselves into a bilayer with heads facing the water on both sides and tails hidden in the middle. The hydrophobic core, about 3–4 nm thick within a membrane of roughly 5–10 nm, is what blocks ions and polar molecules.

The fluid mosaic model

In the fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972), the bilayer is fluid: phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within it. Proteins are scattered through it like tiles in a mosaic.

  • Integral (transmembrane) proteins span the bilayer: channels, carriers, pumps and many receptors.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on one surface, often attached to integral proteins.

Cholesterol and carbohydrates

Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids in animal membranes. It restrains their movement at body temperature, making the membrane less fluid and less leaky, but stops them packing too tightly when cold. Glycoproteins and glycolipids carry short carbohydrate chains on the outer surface, forming the glycocalyx, which is used for cell recognition (such as ABO blood groups) and adhesion.

Membrane fluidity depends on temperature and on the fatty acids: unsaturated tails, with kinks from their double bonds, pack loosely and keep a membrane fluid. Organisms that live in the cold have more unsaturated fatty acids in their membranes.

A membrane is a fluid bilayer of phospholipids with proteins embedded in it; cholesterol tunes its fluidity and carbohydrates on the outside identify the cell.

Common mistakeDrawing membrane proteins in fixed positions. Many move sideways freely, which is why the model is called fluid.

Summary

  • Phospholipids form a bilayer because their tails are hydrophobic and their heads hydrophilic.
  • The fluid mosaic model describes proteins moving in a fluid bilayer.
  • Cholesterol regulates fluidity; glycoproteins and glycolipids act in recognition.

Key terms

Phospholipid bilayer
A double layer of phospholipids with hydrophilic heads outwards and hydrophobic tails inwards
Fluid mosaic model
The model of membranes as a fluid lipid bilayer with a mosaic of proteins moving within it
Glycoprotein
A protein with carbohydrate chains attached, important in cell recognition

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Why do phospholipids form a bilayer in water?

Their hydrophobic tails avoid water by facing each other in the middle, while their hydrophilic heads face the water on both sides.

What does cholesterol do in animal membranes?

It regulates fluidity: it reduces fluidity and permeability at body temperature and prevents tight packing when cold.

Check your understanding

  1. Which part of a phospholipid is hydrophobic?
    • The fatty acid tails
    • The glycerol only
    • The phosphate head
    • The whole molecule
    Show the answer

    The fatty acid tails. The two fatty acid tails are non-polar; the phosphate head is polar and hydrophilic.

  2. In a famous experiment, a mouse cell and a human cell were fused, with their membrane proteins labelled in different colours. Within 40 minutes the colours were mixed over the whole surface. What does this show?
    • Membrane proteins move sideways within a fluid bilayer
    • Proteins are fixed in place
    • The bilayer is made of protein
    • The cells exchanged DNA
    Show the answer

    Membrane proteins move sideways within a fluid bilayer. Frye and Edidin's 1970 experiment showed lateral diffusion of proteins, key evidence for the fluid mosaic model.

  3. Bacteria grown at low temperature make membranes with more unsaturated fatty acids. Why does this help?
    • Kinked unsaturated tails pack less tightly, keeping the membrane fluid enough to work in the cold
    • They add cholesterol to the membrane
    • They make the membrane impermeable to water
    • Unsaturated fatty acids are more hydrophilic
    Show the answer

    Kinked unsaturated tails pack less tightly, keeping the membrane fluid enough to work in the cold. Membranes become rigid when cold; double bonds put kinks in the tails that stop them packing closely.
