Biology · School 2: Cell Biology · Membranes and transport

Membrane transport

Substances cross membranes in several ways, which differ in whether they need energy and whether they need a protein. This lesson gives an overview that the following lessons explore one by one.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Distinguish passive from active transport
  • Choose the transport mechanism a given substance is likely to use
  • Explain how the size, charge and polarity of a molecule affect how it crosses a membrane

Before you start

  • Membrane structure

Passive and active

  • Passive transport needs no energy from the cell: substances move down their concentration gradient. It includes simple diffusion through the lipid, facilitated diffusion through channel or carrier proteins, and osmosis (water).
  • Active transport uses energy, usually from ATP, to move substances against their gradient through protein pumps.
  • Bulk transport, endocytosis and exocytosis, moves large particles or volumes in vesicles and also uses energy.

What decides the route

Small, non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones) diffuse straight through the lipid. Ions are charged and surrounded by water, so they can't enter the hydrophobic core and need channels or pumps. Large polar molecules such as glucose and amino acids need carrier proteins. Macromolecules and particles such as bacteria need vesicles.

Gradients

A concentration gradient is a difference in concentration between two regions. For ions, the electrical difference across the membrane matters too, so ions move down an electrochemical gradient. Cells spend a large share of their ATP maintaining gradients, especially of sodium and potassium ions, which then power other transport.

Rates matter as much as routes: the rate of passive transport rises with a steeper gradient, a larger surface area and a thinner barrier, which is why exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi are large and thin.

Passive transport goes down gradients without energy; active and bulk transport use energy. The route depends on a molecule's size, charge and polarity.

Common mistakeSaying diffusion stops when molecules are evenly spread. Molecules keep moving; there is simply no net movement once concentrations are equal.

Summary

  • Passive transport (diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis) needs no energy.
  • Active and bulk transport use ATP.
  • Size, charge and polarity decide which route a substance takes.

Key terms

Passive transport
Movement across a membrane down a concentration gradient, without using energy from the cell
Active transport
Movement across a membrane against a concentration gradient, using energy from ATP
Electrochemical gradient
The combined concentration and electrical difference that drives ions across a membrane

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Which transport processes are passive?

Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis.

Why can't ions diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer?

They are charged and surrounded by a shell of water, so they can't dissolve in the hydrophobic core.

Check your understanding

  1. Which process needs ATP?
    • Active transport
    • Facilitated diffusion
    • Osmosis
    • Simple diffusion
    Show the answer

    Active transport. Only active transport (and bulk transport) uses the cell's energy; the others are driven by gradients.

  2. A substance moves into a cell against its concentration gradient, and the movement stops when a respiratory poison is added. What is the mechanism?
    • Active transport
    • Facilitated diffusion
    • Osmosis
    • Simple diffusion
    Show the answer

    Active transport. Moving against a gradient needs energy; stopping respiration removes the ATP supply.

  3. Steroid hormones such as testosterone act on receptors inside cells, while insulin acts on receptors in the plasma membrane. What explains the difference?
    • Insulin is too small to be detected inside
    • Steroids are proteins
    • Steroids are small and lipid-soluble so they cross the bilayer; insulin is a large polar protein that can't
    • The bilayer is permeable to all hormones
    Show the answer

    Steroids are small and lipid-soluble so they cross the bilayer; insulin is a large polar protein that can't. Lipid-soluble molecules diffuse through the membrane to internal receptors; large polar molecules must signal from outside.
