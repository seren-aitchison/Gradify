Stem cells

Stem cells are unspecialised cells that can divide to renew themselves and can also give rise to specialised cells. They build the body in development, repair it in adult life, and hold great promise, and some controversy, in medicine.

Before you start

  • Cell specialisation

Two properties

A stem cell can self-renew (divide to make more stem cells) and differentiate into specialised cell types. Often one division produces one of each, keeping the stem cell population steady while supplying new specialised cells.

How much potential

  • Totipotent: can form every cell type, including the placenta. The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions are totipotent.
  • Pluripotent: can form all the body's cell types, but not the placenta: the inner cell mass of the blastocyst, the source of embryonic stem cells.
  • Multipotent: can form several related types: haematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow make all the blood cells.
  • Unipotent: can form one type, such as the cells in skin that make keratinocytes.

Medicine and ethics

Bone marrow (haematopoietic stem cell) transplants have been used for decades to treat leukaemia and some inherited blood diseases. Embryonic stem cells could in principle replace any tissue, but obtaining them destroys an early embryo, which many people object to. In 2006 Takahashi and Yamanaka showed that adult mouse cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) by switching on four genes, avoiding embryos and allowing cells matched to the patient.

Risks remain: pluripotent cells can form tumours if any stay undifferentiated, and many treatments sold commercially as "stem cell therapy" are unproven. In the UK, uses of human embryos are regulated by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority.

Key idea

Stem cells self-renew and differentiate; their potential ranges from totipotent to unipotent, and iPSCs offer pluripotency without embryos.

Common mistake
Thinking all stem cells can become any cell. Most adult stem cells are multipotent or unipotent, limited to their own tissue.

Summary

  • Stem cells self-renew and differentiate.
  • Potency ranges from totipotent (zygote) to pluripotent, multipotent and unipotent.
  • Bone marrow transplants are established; embryonic and iPSC therapies are developing, with ethical and safety questions.

Key terms

Stem cell
An unspecialised cell that can self-renew and differentiate into specialised cells
Pluripotent
Able to give rise to all the cell types of the body, but not the placenta
Induced pluripotent stem cell
An adult cell reprogrammed to a pluripotent state by switching on particular genes

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

What are the two defining properties of stem cells?

Self-renewal (dividing to make more stem cells) and differentiation into specialised cells.

What is the difference between totipotent and pluripotent?

Totipotent cells can form every cell type including the placenta; pluripotent cells can form every body cell type but not the placenta.

Check your understanding

  1. Which cells are pluripotent?
    • Cells of the inner cell mass of a blastocyst
    • Haematopoietic stem cells
    • Mature neurons
    • Red blood cells
    Show the answer

    Cells of the inner cell mass of a blastocyst. The inner cell mass gives rise to every tissue of the body, so it is pluripotent; blood stem cells are multipotent.

  2. Why does a bone marrow transplant restore all types of blood cell?
    • It contains mature red blood cells only
    • It contains totipotent cells
    • It provides multipotent haematopoietic stem cells, which make every kind of blood cell
    • It replaces the patient's DNA everywhere
    Show the answer

    It provides multipotent haematopoietic stem cells, which make every kind of blood cell. Blood stem cells settle in the marrow and produce red cells, white cells and platelets.

  3. What is the main advantage of iPSCs over embryonic stem cells for treating a patient?
    • They are easier to obtain from bone marrow
    • They are unipotent and so safer
    • They can be made from the patient's own cells, avoiding rejection and the destruction of embryos
    • They can't form tumours
    Show the answer

    They can be made from the patient's own cells, avoiding rejection and the destruction of embryos. Reprogramming the patient's own cells gives genetically matched pluripotent cells without using embryos, though tumour risk remains.
