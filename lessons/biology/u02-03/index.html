Eukaryotic cells

Eukaryotic cells, found in animals, plants, fungi and protists, keep their DNA in a nucleus and divide their interior into membrane-bound compartments. This compartmentalisation lets them grow larger and do many incompatible jobs at once.

Before you start

  • Prokaryotic cells

What makes a cell eukaryotic

  • A nucleus surrounded by a double-membrane nuclear envelope.
  • Membrane-bound organelles: endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, mitochondria (and chloroplasts in plants and algae).
  • Linear chromosomes with DNA wound around histone proteins.
  • A cytoskeleton that shapes the cell and moves things within it.
  • 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

Eukaryotic cells are usually 10–100 µm across, roughly ten times the length and a thousand times the volume of a typical bacterium.

Why compartments matter

Membranes create separate spaces with different conditions. Lysosomes keep an acidic pH (about 4.5–5) for digestive enzymes that would damage the rest of the cell; mitochondria build up proton gradients across their inner membrane. Compartments also concentrate the enzymes of one pathway together.

Where eukaryotic cells came from

The endosymbiotic theory, developed by Lynn Margulis (1967), holds that mitochondria descend from aerobic bacteria and chloroplasts from cyanobacteria that were engulfed by a host cell and kept. The evidence: both organelles have double membranes, their own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, and divide by fission; their genes are most similar to those of free-living bacteria.

Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound compartments, which let them be larger and more specialised than prokaryotes.

Common mistakeThinking "eukaryotic" means "multicellular". Many eukaryotes, such as yeast and amoebae, are single cells.

Summary

  • Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, linear chromosomes and a cytoskeleton.
  • Compartments keep incompatible processes apart.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria.

Key terms

Eukaryote
An organism whose cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles
Organelle
A specialised structure inside a cell with a particular job
Endosymbiotic theory
The theory that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from bacteria living inside an ancestral host cell

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Give three differences between eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells.

Eukaryotes have a nucleus, membrane-bound organelles and linear chromosomes with histones (and 80S ribosomes); prokaryotes have a nucleoid, no membrane-bound organelles and usually a circular chromosome (70S ribosomes).

Give two pieces of evidence that mitochondria were once bacteria.

They have their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, a double membrane, and divide by fission.

Check your understanding

  1. Which feature is found in eukaryotic but not prokaryotic cells?
    • A nuclear envelope
    • A plasma membrane
    • DNA
    • Ribosomes
    Show the answer

    A nuclear envelope. All cells have ribosomes, a membrane and DNA; only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nuclear envelope.

  2. An organism is single-celled, has a nucleus and mitochondria, and no cell wall. What is it?
    • A bacterium
    • A eukaryote such as an amoeba
    • A virus
    • An archaeon
    Show the answer

    A eukaryote such as an amoeba. A nucleus and mitochondria make it eukaryotic; being single-celled doesn't make it prokaryotic.

  3. Why do mitochondria have ribosomes that resemble bacterial ribosomes?
    • Bacteria constantly invade mitochondria
    • Eukaryotic ribosomes are too large to enter the nucleus
    • They are descended from bacteria and kept part of their own protein-making system
    • They make all of the cell's proteins
    Show the answer

    They are descended from bacteria and kept part of their own protein-making system. Mitochondrial 70S-type ribosomes are a relic of their bacterial ancestry, part of the evidence for endosymbiosis.
