Biology · School 2: Cell Biology · Membranes and transport

Active transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient using energy, usually from ATP. It lets cells take up scarce nutrients, keep ion gradients that power nerves and other transport, and excrete wastes.

Pumps

In active transport, a carrier protein binds the substance, and energy from ATP hydrolysis changes the protein's shape so the substance is released on the other side, against its gradient. Proteins that do this are called pumps. Cells doing a lot of active transport, such as root hair cells and kidney tubule cells, have many mitochondria.

The sodium–potassium pump

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, discovered by Jens Skou (1957), pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in for each ATP used. It keeps sodium low and potassium high inside cells, makes the inside slightly negative, and in neurons maintains the resting potential needed for nerve impulses. It uses a large share of the body's ATP at rest, by some estimates around a fifth.

Co-transport

Some transporters use the energy stored in one gradient to move another substance. In the small intestine, the sodium–glucose co-transporter (SGLT1) lets Na⁺ flow into the cell down its gradient and carries glucose in with it, against the glucose gradient. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump on the other side of the cell keeps the sodium gradient going. This is secondary active transport: ATP is used, but indirectly.

Oral rehydration solution, which has saved millions of lives in cholera and other diarrhoeal diseases, contains both glucose and salt because co-transport of glucose pulls sodium, and water with it, into the body.

Active transport uses energy to move substances against their gradient; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the gradients that co-transporters then use.

Common mistakeSaying active transport moves substances "from low to high concentration because it uses energy" without the mechanism. ATP changes the shape of a specific carrier protein; without the protein, energy alone moves nothing.

Summary

  • Active transport uses ATP to change carrier shape and move substances against gradients.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP.
  • Co-transporters use ion gradients made by pumps to move nutrients such as glucose.

Key terms

Active transport
Movement against a concentration gradient using energy, through carrier proteins called pumps
Sodium–potassium pump
A membrane protein that uses ATP to pump 3 Na⁺ out of and 2 K⁺ into a cell
Co-transport
Movement of one substance against its gradient coupled to movement of another down its gradient

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

How many sodium and potassium ions does the sodium–potassium pump move per ATP?

3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.

Why is glucose absorption in the intestine called secondary active transport?

The co-transporter uses the sodium gradient, which is made by the ATP-driven Na⁺/K⁺ pump, rather than ATP directly.

Check your understanding

  1. What does the sodium–potassium pump use as its energy source?
    • ATP
    • Light
    • Osmosis
    • The glucose gradient
    Show the answer

    ATP. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase hydrolyses ATP to change shape and move ions against their gradients.

  2. Root hair cells absorb nitrate ions from soil where nitrate is less concentrated than inside the cells. Which feature would you expect these cells to have?
    • Chloroplasts to make nitrate
    • Many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport
    • No cell membrane
    • Very few carrier proteins
    Show the answer

    Many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. Uptake against the gradient is active and needs ATP, so the cells are rich in mitochondria.

  3. Ouabain blocks the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Why does it also stop glucose absorption by intestinal cells?
    • Glucose uses the Na⁺/K⁺ pump directly
    • Ouabain blocks osmosis
    • Ouabain destroys glucose
    • Without the pump, the sodium gradient that drives the glucose co-transporter runs down
    Show the answer

    Without the pump, the sodium gradient that drives the glucose co-transporter runs down. Co-transport depends on the sodium gradient, which only the pump can maintain.
