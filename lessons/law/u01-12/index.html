Statutory interpretation

Statutory interpretation is how courts work out what an Act means when it is applied to facts. The modern approach is contextual and purposive, informed by older rules, presumptions and permitted aids.

  • Describe the traditional rules and the modern purposive approach
  • Apply aids, presumptions and section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998

The traditional rules

  • Literal rule: give words their ordinary meaning even if the result is harsh (Whiteley v Chappell (1868)).
  • Golden rule: depart from the literal meaning to avoid an absurd or repugnant result (Re Sigsworth [1935]; Adler v George [1964]).
  • Mischief rule (Heydon's Case (1584)): find the defect the Act was meant to remedy and interpret to suppress it (Smith v Hughes [1960]).
  • Purposive approach: read the words in context to give effect to Parliament's purpose. Now the dominant approach: R (Quintavalle) v Secretary of State for Health [2003] UKHL 13; R (O) v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2022] UKSC 3.

Aids

  • Internal: the long title, headings, the rest of the Act, the explanatory notes in context.
  • External: dictionaries, earlier Acts, Law Commission reports, and Hansard where the Act is ambiguous, the statement is by the minister or promoter and it is clear (Pepper v Hart [1993]).
  • Language rules: ejusdem generis (general words take colour from a list of specific ones; Powell v Kempton Park (1899)), noscitur a sociis, expressio unius est exclusio alterius.

Presumptions

  • Against changing the common law; requiring mens rea for crimes (Sweet v Parsley [1970]); against retrospective effect; against binding the Crown; against ousting the courts' jurisdiction.

Human rights

Under the Human Rights Act 1998, s 3, legislation must be read and given effect compatibly with Convention rights "so far as it is possible to do so" (Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza [2004]). If it is not possible, a higher court may make a declaration of incompatibility (s 4).

Key idea

Start from the words, read in their context and with their purpose; use the older rules as labels for reasoning, not as a menu.

Summary

  • Courts use literal, golden and mischief rules within a contextual, purposive approach.
  • Section 3 HRA requires Convention-compatible interpretation where possible.

Case and statute cards

Case

Pepper v Hart [1993] AC 593

Facts
Teachers at a fee-paying school challenged the tax charged on concessionary places for their children; the statute was ambiguous.
Held
The court could refer to Hansard, where the minister's statement resolved the point in the teachers' favour.
Principle
Courts may refer to clear ministerial statements in Hansard where legislation is ambiguous, obscure or leads to absurdity.

Questions this page answers

What happened in Pepper v Hart?

Teachers at a fee-paying school challenged the tax charged on concessionary places for their children; the statute was ambiguous. Held: The court could refer to Hansard, where the minister's statement resolved the point in the teachers' favour.

What principle does Pepper v Hart establish?

Courts may refer to clear ministerial statements in Hansard where legislation is ambiguous, obscure or leads to absurdity.

What does “purposive approach” mean?

Interpreting legislation to give effect to its purpose

What does the golden rule allow?

A court to depart from the literal meaning to avoid an absurd or repugnant result.

When may Hansard be used?

Under Pepper v Hart: where the legislation is ambiguous or obscure or leads to absurdity, the statement is by a minister or promoter, and the statement is clear.

Define the purposive approach.

Interpreting legislation in context to give effect to Parliament's purpose.

What does the literal rule require?

Giving words their ordinary meaning even if the result is harsh.

Which case illustrates the literal rule?

Whiteley v Chappell (1868).

What does the mischief rule require?

Finding the defect the Act was meant to remedy and interpreting it to suppress that mischief.

Which case established the mischief rule?

Heydon's Case (1584).

Name two cases showing the purposive approach is now dominant.

R (Quintavalle) v Secretary of State for Health [2003] and R (O) v Secretary of State for the Home Department [2022].

Name three internal aids to statutory interpretation.

Any three of: the long title, headings, the rest of the Act, the explanatory notes in context.

What does the ejusdem generis rule say?

General words take their meaning from a list of specific words they follow.

Name four presumptions of statutory interpretation.

Any four of: against changing the common law, requiring mens rea, against retrospective effect, against binding the Crown, against ousting the courts' jurisdiction.

What does s 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 require?

Legislation must be read and given effect compatibly with Convention rights so far as it is possible to do so.

Check your understanding

  1. Which rule asks what problem the Act was passed to fix?
    • Ejusdem generis
    • Expressio unius
    • The golden rule
    • The literal rule
    • The mischief rule
    Show the answer

    The mischief rule. The mischief rule, from Heydon's Case, looks to the defect in the earlier law that the Act remedies.

  2. A statute bans "dogs, cats, rabbits and other animals" from a park. A visitor brings a lion. Which approach most directly suggests the lion is not covered?
    • Ejusdem generis: "other animals" is read as animals like those listed
    • Expressio unius: listing pets excludes all other animals
    • Pepper v Hart: Hansard shows what the minister meant
    • The golden rule: a lion is too absurd to consider
    • The literal rule: "animals" plainly includes every animal
    Show the answer

    Ejusdem generis: "other animals" is read as animals like those listed. General words following a list of specific items are limited to things of the same kind. (A purposive reading might reach the opposite conclusion, which is why the rules guide rather than decide.)

  3. Section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998 cannot be used to give a statute a Convention-compatible reading. What can a higher court do?
    • Declare the statute void from the date of the judgment
    • Disapply the incompatible section in this case only
    • Make a declaration of incompatibility, leaving the statute in force
    • Order the minister to amend the statute by remedial order
    • Read words into the statute regardless of its meaning
    Show the answer

    Make a declaration of incompatibility, leaving the statute in force. Section 4 allows a declaration of incompatibility, which does not affect the statute's validity.

  4. A statute makes it an offence to "sell or offer for sale" an offensive weapon. A shopkeeper displays one in his window with a price tag. Which result is most likely under the traditional approach, and why?
    • An offence under the purposive approach in every case
    • An offence: Pepper v Hart shows Parliament meant displays
    • An offence: the mischief rule extends "sell" to displays
    • No offence, because the golden rule always favours defendants
    • No offence: a display is an invitation to treat, not a sale or offer for sale (Fisher v Bell)
    Show the answer

    No offence: a display is an invitation to treat, not a sale or offer for sale (Fisher v Bell). Fisher v Bell applied the contract law meaning of offer; Parliament then amended the Act in 1961 to cover exposing for sale.
