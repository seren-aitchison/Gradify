Legal writing

Legal writing must be accurate, clear and organised for its reader, whether that is an examiner, a judge or a client. Good legal writing makes the answer and its authority easy to find.

Study material, not legal advice. The law is stated for England and Wales unless a lesson says otherwise; Scotland and Northern Ireland differ (School 17). Completing a LawGradify course is not a legal qualification and does not authorise you to practise. Law changes: check current sources before you rely on anything here.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Apply the principles of clear, accurate legal writing
  • Identify and correct common faults

Principles

  • Know the reader and purpose: an exam answer, a client letter and a skeleton argument need different things.
  • Answer first: lead with the conclusion, then support it.
  • Structure: headings, numbered paragraphs, one issue per section.
  • Plain English: short sentences, active voice, everyday words; define technical terms for lay readers.
  • Precision: use terms of art correctly ("liable" not "guilty" in civil law; "may" versus "must").
  • Authority: support each legal proposition with a statute or case; don't over-cite.
  • Accuracy: check facts, names, dates and figures.

Common faults

  • Narrating the facts at length without analysis.
  • Stating the law without applying it.
  • Hedging every sentence until there is no advice.
  • Legalese ("hereinafter", "the said").
Worked example

Rewrite: "It is the opinion of the writer that it is possible that the client may potentially have a claim."

  1. Remove hedging and passive voice.
  2. "Mrs Patel has a strong claim in negligence against the council, for these reasons."

Courts expect the same discipline: CPR Part 16 and its Practice Direction set out what a statement of case must contain, and judges regularly criticise documents that are long, unfocused or unclear.

Every legal paragraph should do one job: state a rule, apply it, or conclude. Readers should be able to tell which.

Summary

  • Legal writing must be accurate, clear and organised for its reader.
  • Avoid unsupported assertions, waffle and missing authority.

Key terms

IRAC
Issue, rule, application, conclusion: a structure for legal problem answers
Plain English
Clear, direct language suited to the reader

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

What is the "answer first" principle?

Put the conclusion at the start, then give the reasons and authority.

Name two common faults in legal writing.

Any two of: narrating facts without analysis; stating law without applying it; excessive hedging; legalese; inaccuracy.

What does IRAC stand for?

Issue, rule, application, conclusion.

Define plain English.

Clear, direct language suited to the reader.

Name three readers whose needs differ in legal writing.

An examiner, a judge and a client.

What does good structure in legal writing involve?

Headings, numbered paragraphs and one issue per section.

Check your understanding

  1. Which is the clearest opening for advice to a client?
    • "Hereinafter, the deposit shall be referred to as the sum."
    • "It is respectfully submitted that there may be a possibility of recovery."
    • "The law of contract is very old and complex."
    • "We have read the documents."
    • "You are likely to succeed in recovering your deposit, for three reasons."
    Show the answer

    "You are likely to succeed in recovering your deposit, for three reasons.". Plain, direct, conclusion-first writing serves a lay client best.

  2. An exam answer sets out the law on duty of care accurately but never mentions the facts given. What is the main weakness?
    • It cites too many cases
    • It doesn't give a conclusion first
    • It fails to apply the law to the facts
    • It is too short
    • It uses plain English
    Show the answer

    It fails to apply the law to the facts. Problem questions reward application: the law must be used on the facts.
