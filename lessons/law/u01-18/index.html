Delegated legislation

Delegated (secondary) legislation is law made by a person or body other than Parliament, under powers given by an Act (the parent or enabling Act). It is now far more voluminous than primary legislation.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Describe the forms of delegated legislation and why Parliament delegates
  • Explain parliamentary and judicial controls

Forms

  • Statutory instruments: made by ministers; the commonest form.
  • Orders in Council: made by the King on the advice of the Privy Council, often in emergencies or to transfer functions.
  • Byelaws: made by local authorities and public bodies for their areas.
  • Procedure rules: such as the Civil Procedure Rules.

Why Parliament delegates

  • Saves parliamentary time; allows technical detail; lets law change quickly; uses expertise and local knowledge.

Controls

  • Parliamentary: the negative procedure (law unless annulled within 40 days) or the affirmative procedure (needs approval); the Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments; the Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee in the Lords.
  • Judicial: judicial review for being ultra vires. Substantive ultra vires: beyond the powers granted (R (Public Law Project) v Lord Chancellor [2016] UKSC 39: a legal aid residence test made by statutory instrument was outside the parent Act's powers; R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017]). Procedural ultra vires: required procedure ignored, such as consultation (Agricultural Training Board v Aylesbury Mushrooms [1972]). Also unreasonableness (Strickland v Hayes BC [1896]).

Concerns

Wide "Henry VIII powers" let ministers amend or repeal Acts by statutory instrument, and "skeleton bills" leave most of the substance to regulations. Both reduce parliamentary scrutiny and have been criticised by parliamentary committees.

Key idea

Delegated legislation is valid only if made within the parent Act's powers by the right procedure. That's what makes it reviewable when Acts are not.

Summary

  • Delegated legislation is made under powers in a parent Act.
  • It is controlled by affirmative and negative procedures and judicial review.

Key terms

Statutory instrument
The most common form of delegated legislation
Ultra vires
Beyond the powers granted by the parent Act

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

What is a Henry VIII power?

A power in an Act allowing ministers to amend or repeal primary legislation by delegated legislation.

What is the negative resolution procedure?

The instrument becomes or stays law unless either House annuls it within a set period (usually 40 days).

Define delegated legislation.

Law made by a person or body other than Parliament under powers given by a parent or enabling Act.

Define ultra vires.

Beyond the powers granted by the parent Act.

What are Orders in Council?

Delegated legislation made by the King on the advice of the Privy Council, often in emergencies or to transfer functions.

Who makes byelaws?

Local authorities and public bodies, for their areas.

Check your understanding

  1. Which body makes byelaws?
    • Government ministers, as statutory instruments
    • Local authorities and certain public bodies
    • Magistrates' courts, for their local area
    • The Law Commission, after consultation
    • The Privy Council, as Orders in Council
    Show the answer

    Local authorities and certain public bodies. Byelaws are made by local and public bodies for their areas.

  2. A minister makes regulations without the consultation the parent Act requires. What is the likely ground of challenge?
    • Breach of the Sewel convention
    • Implied repeal
    • Parliamentary privilege
    • Procedural ultra vires
    • Substantive ultra vires
    Show the answer

    Procedural ultra vires. Failing to follow a mandatory procedure in the parent Act is procedural ultra vires (Aylesbury Mushrooms).

  3. A minister makes regulations under an Act that allows rules "for the efficient running of tribunals". The regulations impose fees so high that most claimants can't bring claims. Which challenge is strongest?
    • The fees breach the Human Rights Act, so Parliament must repeal them
    • The minister acted irrationally, which can't be reviewed for statutory instruments
    • The regulations are ultra vires, because the Act doesn't authorise preventing access to justice (UNISON)
    • The regulations are valid because Parliament delegated the power
    • The regulations are void because they weren't debated in Parliament
    Show the answer

    The regulations are ultra vires, because the Act doesn't authorise preventing access to justice (UNISON). General words don't authorise interference with the constitutional right of access to the courts.
