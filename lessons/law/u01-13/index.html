Parliamentary sovereignty

Parliamentary sovereignty means the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court or other body can set aside an Act of Parliament. It is the central principle of the UK constitution.

  • Explain Dicey's account of parliamentary sovereignty
  • Evaluate challenges and qualifications to sovereignty

Dicey's three limbs

  • Parliament can make or unmake any law whatever.
  • No Parliament can bind its successors.
  • No court or person can override or set aside an Act of Parliament.

Illustrations

  • Courts apply Acts as enacted, without inquiring into how they were passed: the "enrolled Act rule" (British Railways Board v Pickin [1974]).
  • Implied repeal: a later inconsistent Act repeals an earlier one to the extent of the inconsistency (Ellen Street Estates v Minister of Health [1934]).
  • Constitutional statutes are not impliedly repealed; only express words or necessary implication will do (Thoburn v Sunderland City Council [2002]).

Challenges and qualifications

  • EU membership: courts disapplied Acts that conflicted with EU law (R v Secretary of State for Transport, ex p Factortame (No 2) [1991]), on the basis of the European Communities Act 1972. This ended with Brexit: s 5 of the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 removed EU supremacy for post-exit legislation, and the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 abolished the principle of supremacy in domestic law from the start of 2024.
  • Human Rights Act 1998: courts interpret compatibly or declare incompatibility, but cannot strike down Acts.
  • Devolution: Westminster keeps the power to legislate on devolved matters; the Sewel convention says it will not normally do so without consent, but this is not legally enforceable (R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU [2017] UKSC 5).
  • The rule of law: some judges have suggested, obiter, limits on sovereignty (Jackson v Attorney General [2005]).

Sovereignty is a legal principle. Political, conventional and practical limits exist, but no court will hold an Act invalid.

Summary

  • No court can set aside an Act of Parliament.
  • EU membership, devolution and the HRA raised qualifications.

Case and statute cards

Case

Thoburn v Sunderland City Council [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin)

Facts
Market traders ('metric martyrs') were convicted for using only imperial measures, against regulations made under the European Communities Act 1972.
Held
The 1972 Act was a constitutional statute not impliedly repealed by a later Act.
Principle
Constitutional statutes can't be impliedly repealed; only express words or necessary implication will do.

What are Dicey's three limbs of parliamentary sovereignty?

Parliament can make or unmake any law; no Parliament can bind its successors; no one can override or set aside an Act.

What is the enrolled Act rule?

Courts will not inquire into the internal procedure by which an Act was passed (Pickin).

Define parliamentary sovereignty.

The principle that the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law and no court can set aside an Act.

Define implied repeal.

A later inconsistent Act repeals an earlier one to the extent of the inconsistency.

Check your understanding

  1. An Act passed in 2026 conflicts with an ordinary Act of 1990. What normally happens?
    • Both remain fully in force
    • The 1990 Act prevails as the older law
    • The 2026 Act impliedly repeals the 1990 Act to the extent of the conflict
    • The courts refer the conflict to the Supreme Court
    • The courts strike down the 2026 Act
    Show the answer

    The 2026 Act impliedly repeals the 1990 Act to the extent of the conflict. Under implied repeal the later Act prevails (Ellen Street Estates), unless the earlier Act is a constitutional statute.

  2. Parliament passes an Act on a devolved matter without the Scottish Parliament's consent. A client asks a court to declare it invalid. What is the likely outcome?
    • The Act applies only in England
    • The Act is valid: the Sewel convention is not legally enforceable
    • The Act is void for breach of the Sewel convention
    • The Act needs a referendum
    • The Supreme Court will strike it down
    Show the answer

    The Act is valid: the Sewel convention is not legally enforceable. Miller (2017) held that the Sewel convention is a political, not legal, constraint.

  3. A 2025 Act conflicts with the Human Rights Act 1998, and the conflict can't be resolved by interpretation. A party asks the High Court to apply the 1998 Act instead. What is the likely outcome?
    • The 1998 Act prevails as a constitutional statute
    • The 2025 Act applies; a higher court may make a declaration of incompatibility
    • The 2025 Act is impliedly repealed by the 1998 Act
    • The court strikes down the 2025 Act as unconstitutional
    • The High Court disapplies the 2025 Act as in Factortame
    Show the answer

    The 2025 Act applies; a higher court may make a declaration of incompatibility. A later Act prevails; constitutional status protects the HRA only from implied repeal, and courts can't disapply primary legislation for Convention incompatibility (s 4).
