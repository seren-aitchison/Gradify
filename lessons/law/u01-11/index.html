Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta

The ratio decidendi ("reason for deciding") is the legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts, and it is the binding part of a judgment. Obiter dicta ("things said by the way") are everything else, and they persuade but don't bind.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Identify the ratio decidendi of a case
  • Distinguish obiter dicta and explain their persuasive weight

Finding the ratio

  • Identify the material facts the court relied on.
  • Identify the legal question and the answer the court gave.
  • Ask which reasoning was necessary to reach that answer. The Wambaugh test: invert the proposition; if the decision would have been different, it was part of the ratio.
  • With several judgments, look for the principle a majority agreed on. There may be no single ratio.

Obiter dicta

  • Statements about hypothetical facts, wider statements than needed, and reasoning in dissents.
  • Can be highly persuasive, especially from the Supreme Court. In R v Howe [1987] the House of Lords said obiter that duress is no defence to attempted murder; the Court of Appeal followed it in R v Gotts [1992].
Worked example

In Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], what is the ratio, and what is obiter?

  1. Material facts: a manufacturer's product reached a consumer with no chance of intermediate examination, causing injury.
  2. Ratio (narrow): a manufacturer owes a duty of reasonable care to the ultimate consumer of its products in those circumstances.
  3. Lord Atkin's wider "neighbour principle" was, strictly, wider than needed: obiter, though later adopted as a general approach.
Key idea

The ratio is never just the outcome ("the claimant won"). It is the legal proposition the outcome depended on.

Summary

  • The ratio binds; obiter dicta can persuade.
  • Finding the ratio means identifying the material facts and the reasoning.

Key terms

Ratio decidendi
The legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts
Obiter dictum
A statement in a judgment that isn't necessary for the decision

Case and statute cards

Case

R v Howe [1987] AC 417

Facts
The House of Lords held duress no defence to murder and said, in passing, that it should not be available for attempted murder either.
Held
The remark on attempted murder was obiter, later adopted in R v Gotts [1992].
Principle
Statements not necessary to the decision are obiter dicta, persuasive but not binding.

R v Howe [1987] AC 417

What is the ratio decidendi?

The legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts; the binding part of a judgment.

Is a dissenting judgment binding?

No. Its reasoning is obiter and at most persuasive.

Define obiter dictum.

A statement in a judgment that is not necessary for the decision; persuasive but not binding.

State the Wambaugh test for finding the ratio.

Invert the proposition; if the decision would then have been different, the proposition was part of the ratio.

Check your understanding

  1. Which part of a Supreme Court judgment binds a later Court of Appeal?
    • Dissenting judgments
    • Every statement in the leading judgment
    • Obiter dicta
    • The ratio decidendi
    • The summary of counsel's arguments
    Show the answer

    The ratio decidendi. Only the ratio binds; obiter dicta and dissents persuade.

  2. A judge remarks that "had the defendant been a professional, the result would have been different", but the defendant was not a professional. How should a later court treat this remark?
    • As a binding rule for all professionals
    • As irrelevant and inadmissible
    • As obiter: persuasive, not binding
    • As overruling earlier cases
    • As part of the ratio, binding on lower courts
    Show the answer

    As obiter: persuasive, not binding. Statements about hypothetical facts that did not arise are obiter dicta.

  3. A Supreme Court judgment states a wide principle, but the case could have been decided on a narrower ground that all five justices agreed on. A later court faces facts outside the narrow ground. What is the best approach?
    • Follow the dissent if it addressed the new facts
    • Ignore the wide principle because obiter has no value
    • Refer the question back to the Supreme Court
    • Treat the narrow agreed ground as the ratio and the wide principle as highly persuasive obiter
    • Treat the wide principle as binding because the Supreme Court stated it
    Show the answer

    Treat the narrow agreed ground as the ratio and the wide principle as highly persuasive obiter. The ratio is the reasoning necessary to the decision; wider statements are obiter, though Supreme Court obiter carries great weight (compare R v Gotts following R v Howe).
