Civil and criminal law

Criminal law deals with wrongs against society, prosecuted by the state and punished; civil law deals with disputes between private parties, usually ended with compensation or another remedy. The same facts can give rise to both.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Distinguish civil and criminal law by parties, purpose, standard of proof and outcome
  • Apply both to a single set of facts

The differences

  • Purpose: criminal law punishes and protects the public; civil law compensates and puts matters right.
  • Parties: R (the Crown) v Defendant; Claimant v Defendant.
  • Who starts it: usually the Crown Prosecution Service after a police investigation; in civil cases, the person wronged.
  • Standard of proof: criminal: beyond reasonable doubt ("so that you are sure"); civil: on the balance of probabilities.
  • Outcome: guilty or not guilty, then sentence; liable or not liable, then remedy (damages, injunction).
  • Courts: magistrates' courts and the Crown Court; county court and High Court.
Criminal: sure (beyond reasonable doubt)the prosecution bears the burden
Civil: more likely than not (balance of probabilities)the claimant usually bears the burden

One set of facts, two cases

Worked example

A driver runs a red light and injures a cyclist.

  1. Criminal: the CPS may prosecute for a driving offence; the result is a fine, disqualification or prison.
  2. Civil: the cyclist can sue in negligence for damages for injury and losses.
  3. A conviction can be used as evidence in the civil claim (Civil Evidence Act 1968, s 11).

An acquittal does not prevent a civil claim, because the civil standard of proof is lower.

Common mistakeDon't say a defendant is "guilty" in a civil case, or that a criminal court awards "damages" as its main outcome. Use "liable" for civil and "guilty" for criminal. (Criminal courts can make compensation orders, but that is ancillary.)

Summary

  • Criminal law punishes wrongs against society; civil law resolves private disputes.
  • One set of facts can lead to both a prosecution and a civil claim.

Key terms

Burden of proof
The obligation to prove a case
Standard of proof
Beyond reasonable doubt in criminal cases; balance of probabilities in civil cases

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

What is the standard of proof in criminal cases?

Beyond reasonable doubt: the jury or magistrates must be sure of guilt.

How is a criminal case named?

R v [Defendant], because the Crown prosecutes.

State the purpose of criminal law compared with civil law.

Criminal law punishes and protects the public; civil law compensates and puts matters right.

Who usually starts a criminal case?

The Crown Prosecution Service, after a police investigation.

What is the standard of proof in civil cases?

The balance of probabilities: more likely than not.

Who usually bears the burden of proof in a civil case?

The claimant.

Check your understanding

  1. Who must prove the case in a typical civil claim, and to what standard?
    • The claimant, beyond reasonable doubt
    • The claimant, on the balance of probabilities
    • The court, on its own initiative
    • The defendant, beyond reasonable doubt
    • The state, on the balance of probabilities
    Show the answer

    The claimant, on the balance of probabilities. In civil cases the claimant usually bears the burden, on the balance of probabilities.

  2. A client was acquitted of assault and asks whether the victim can still sue them. What is the best advice?
    • No: an acquittal bars any later claim
    • No: civil claims must be brought before criminal trials
    • Only the CPS can bring the civil claim
    • Yes, but only if new evidence emerges
    • Yes: a civil claim can succeed on the lower civil standard despite the acquittal
    Show the answer

    Yes: a civil claim can succeed on the lower civil standard despite the acquittal. The civil standard is lower, so a civil court can find liability where a criminal court was not sure of guilt.
