The doctrine of precedent

Under the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis, "to stand by things decided"), courts follow the legal principles in earlier decisions of higher courts so that like cases are treated alike. It gives the common law its consistency and its capacity to develop.

Kinds of precedent

  • Binding: the ratio of a higher court (or, sometimes, the same court) must be followed.
  • Persuasive: obiter dicta, dissenting judgments, decisions of lower courts, the Privy Council and other common law jurisdictions, which a court may follow.
  • Original: the first decision on a point (Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] on manufacturers' duty to consumers).

How courts avoid a precedent

  • Distinguishing: the material facts are different enough that the earlier ratio does not apply (Balfour v Balfour and Merritt v Merritt).
  • Overruling: a court in a later case decides an earlier decision's law was wrong, as the House of Lords did using the 1966 Practice Statement in R v Shivpuri [1987] (overruling Anderton v Ryan [1985] on impossible attempts).
  • Reversing: a higher court overturns the decision in the same case on appeal.
  • Departing: a court declines to follow its own earlier decision, where allowed.

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Strengths: certainty, consistency, fairness, efficiency, flexibility through distinguishing.
  • Weaknesses: rigidity, complexity and volume of case law, slow development, and fine distinctions that can look arbitrary.

Investigate with the lab. Decide whether each earlier decision binds, persuades or must be taken into account, given the courts involved.

Key idea

Precedent binds the ratio, not the result. You follow the principle the earlier court used to decide its material facts.

Common mistake
Don't confuse overruling (a different, later case) with reversing (the same case on appeal).

Summary

  • Precedent brings consistency and predictability.
  • Courts may distinguish, overrule or reverse precedents.

Questions this page answers

Use them as flashcards: cover the answer, say it out loud, then check.

What happened in R v Shivpuri?

Within a year of deciding Anderton v Ryan, the House of Lords considered the same question on attempting the impossible. Held: It used the 1966 Practice Statement to overrule Anderton v Ryan.

What principle does R v Shivpuri establish?

The House of Lords (now the Supreme Court) may depart from its own decisions when it appears right to do so.

What does “stare decisis” mean?

Standing by things decided: following earlier decisions

What does “distinguishing” mean?

Avoiding a precedent because the material facts differ

What does stare decisis mean?

"To stand by things decided": courts follow the principles of earlier decisions.

What is distinguishing?

Declining to follow an earlier decision because its material facts differ significantly from the present case.

What is persuasive precedent?

Material a court may follow but need not, such as obiter dicta, dissents, lower court decisions, the Privy Council and other common law jurisdictions.

What is original precedent?

The first decision on a point of law.

Give the lesson's example of original precedent.

Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], on manufacturers' duty to consumers.

Define overruling.

A court in a later case decides that an earlier decision's law was wrong.

Define reversing.

A higher court overturns the decision in the same case on appeal.

What did R v Shivpuri [1987] decide?

The House of Lords used the 1966 Practice Statement to overrule Anderton v Ryan on attempting the impossible.

Which pair of cases illustrates distinguishing?

Balfour v Balfour and Merritt v Merritt.

Name four strengths of the doctrine of precedent.

Any four of: certainty, consistency, fairness, efficiency, flexibility through distinguishing.

Name three weaknesses of the doctrine of precedent.

Any three of: rigidity, complexity and volume of case law, slow development, fine distinctions that can look arbitrary.

Which part of an earlier decision binds under precedent?

The ratio, not the result: the principle the earlier court used to decide its material facts.

Check your understanding

  1. The Court of Appeal decides a case; the Supreme Court later hears a different case on the same point and says the Court of Appeal was wrong. What has the Supreme Court done?
    • Departed from its own decision
    • Distinguished the earlier decision
    • Followed the earlier decision
    • Overruled the earlier decision
    • Reversed the earlier decision
    Show the answer

    Overruled the earlier decision. Overruling happens in a later, different case; reversing happens on appeal in the same case.

  2. Counsel argues that a binding precedent should not apply because, in that case, the parties were living together amicably, whereas here they had separated. What technique is counsel using?
    • Applying the Practice Statement
    • Distinguishing
    • Overruling
    • Relying on obiter dicta
    • Reversing
    Show the answer

    Distinguishing. Showing a materially different fact to avoid an otherwise binding ratio is distinguishing (compare Balfour v Balfour with Merritt v Merritt).

  3. Is the Court of Appeal bound by Supreme Court decisions?
    • No: it can ignore them freely
    • Only if the decision is recent
    • Only in criminal cases
    • Yes: lower courts are bound by higher courts' decisions
    Show the answer

    Yes: lower courts are bound by higher courts' decisions. Under stare decisis, decisions of higher courts bind lower courts.
