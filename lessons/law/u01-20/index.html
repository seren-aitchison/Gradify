Tribunals

Tribunals decide disputes in specialist areas, mostly between individuals and the state (benefits, immigration, tax) and some between private parties (employment).

  • Describe the structure of the tribunal system
  • Evaluate the advantages and drawbacks of tribunals

The structure

  • The Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 created a unified system: the First-tier Tribunal (organised into chambers, such as Social Entitlement, Immigration and Asylum, Tax, Property) and the Upper Tribunal, which hears appeals on points of law and some judicial review.
  • Employment tribunals and the Employment Appeal Tribunal sit outside the two-tier structure but within the same leadership.
  • Appeals on law go from the Upper Tribunal to the Court of Appeal, with permission.

Features

  • Specialist panels, often a judge plus expert members (doctors, people with workplace experience).
  • Less formal procedure, generally no costs in many jurisdictions, and many parties represent themselves.
  • Judicial independence: tribunal judges are part of the judiciary; the Senior President of Tribunals leads them.

Advantages and drawbacks

  • For: expertise, speed, lower cost, informality.
  • Against: limited legal aid, inequality of arms when the state or an employer is represented and the individual isn't, and delays in some chambers.
Key idea

Upper Tribunal decisions on unappealable matters remain subject to judicial review only in limited circumstances (R (Cart) v Upper Tribunal [2011]), and Parliament later narrowed even that (Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022, s 2).

Common mistake
Employment tribunals have their own fee and costs history: fees were abolished after R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017]. Check the current position before stating whether fees apply.

Summary

  • Tribunals decide specialist disputes, mostly between individuals and the state.
  • They are cheaper and more expert but can be hard to navigate without help.

Key terms

First-tier Tribunal
The tribunal that hears most first-instance tribunal cases
Upper Tribunal
The tribunal that hears appeals from the First-tier Tribunal

Case and statute cards

Statute

Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007, s 3

Creates the First-tier Tribunal and the Upper Tribunal; the Upper Tribunal is a superior court of record.

Flashcards

Statute card: what does Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007, s 3 provide?

Creates the First-tier Tribunal and the Upper Tribunal; the Upper Tribunal is a superior court of record.

Which Act created the First-tier and Upper Tribunals?

The Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007.

Where does an appeal on a point of law from the Upper Tribunal go?

To the Court of Appeal, with permission.

Define the First-tier Tribunal.

The tribunal that hears most first-instance tribunal cases, organised into chambers.

Name four chambers of the First-tier Tribunal.

Social Entitlement, Immigration and Asylum, Tax, and Property.

What does the Upper Tribunal hear besides appeals on points of law?

Some judicial review.

Check your understanding

  1. Which body hears appeals from the First-tier Tribunal on points of law?
    • The County Court
    • The Crown Court
    • The Privy Council
    • The Supreme Court directly
    • The Upper Tribunal
    Show the answer

    The Upper Tribunal. The Upper Tribunal is the appellate tier of the unified tribunal system.

  2. A client refused a disability benefit wants to challenge the decision. Where would the appeal normally be heard first?
    • The County Court, as a claim against the department
    • The First-tier Tribunal, after mandatory reconsideration
    • The High Court, by judicial review of the decision
    • The Social Security Ombudsman, after a complaint
    • The Upper Tribunal, as the specialist benefits court
    Show the answer

    The First-tier Tribunal, after mandatory reconsideration. Benefit appeals go to the First-tier Tribunal; judicial review is a last resort where no appeal route exists.
