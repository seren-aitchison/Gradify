Legal institutions and regulators

A web of institutions supports the legal system: government departments, prosecutors, law reform bodies and professional regulators. Knowing who does what is part of commercial awareness and is tested on SQE1 under Legal Services.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Identify the main government and prosecution bodies
  • Describe the bodies responsible for law reform and regulating legal services

Government and prosecution

  • Ministry of Justice: courts, prisons, probation and legal aid policy; led by the Lord Chancellor and Secretary of State for Justice.
  • HM Courts and Tribunals Service (HMCTS): runs courts and tribunals.
  • Crown Prosecution Service: prosecutes most criminal cases, led by the Director of Public Prosecutions, superintended by the Attorney General.
  • Legal Aid Agency: administers legal aid.

Law reform

  • The Law Commission (Law Commissions Act 1965): independent body that reviews and recommends reform of the law of England and Wales. Scotland has the Scottish Law Commission.

Regulating legal services

  • Legal Services Act 2007: the Legal Services Board oversees "approved regulators" and sets regulatory objectives (including protecting the public interest, supporting the rule of law, improving access to justice and protecting consumers).
  • Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA): regulates solicitors and firms (the regulatory arm of the Law Society).
  • Bar Standards Board (BSB): regulates barristers (independent arm of the Bar Council).
  • Other approved regulators cover chartered legal executives, licensed conveyancers (the Council for Licensed Conveyancers), costs lawyers, notaries, and patent and trade mark attorneys.
  • Reserved legal activities (s 12): rights of audience, conduct of litigation, reserved instrument activities, probate activities, notarial activities, administration of oaths. Only authorised persons may carry them on.
  • Legal Ombudsman: handles complaints about poor service from lawyers.
  • Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal: hears serious disciplinary cases against solicitors.
Key idea

Regulators deal with conduct and standards; the Legal Ombudsman deals with service complaints and can order redress. They are different routes.

Common mistake
Regulatory arrangements change (the regulation of chartered legal executives has been under review, for example). Check the current regulator before naming it in an answer.

Summary

  • The Ministry of Justice and CPS have key roles in the justice system.
  • The Law Commission proposes reform; the LSB oversees front-line regulators.

Key terms

Crown Prosecution Service
The main public prosecutor in England and Wales
Legal Services Board
The oversight regulator for legal services in England and Wales

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Name the six reserved legal activities.

Rights of audience, conduct of litigation, reserved instrument activities, probate activities, notarial activities and the administration of oaths (Legal Services Act 2007, s 12).

Who regulates solicitors?

The Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA).

Define the Legal Services Board.

The oversight regulator for legal services in England and Wales, overseeing approved regulators.

What does the Ministry of Justice cover?

Courts, prisons, probation and legal aid policy.

Who leads the Crown Prosecution Service, and who superintends it?

The Director of Public Prosecutions leads it; the Attorney General superintends it.

Which body administers legal aid?

The Legal Aid Agency.

Check your understanding

  1. Which body handles complaints about poor service from a solicitor?
    • The Crown Prosecution Service
    • The Judicial Appointments Commission
    • The Law Commission
    • The Legal Ombudsman
    • The Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal
    Show the answer

    The Legal Ombudsman. Service complaints go to the Legal Ombudsman after the firm's own complaints procedure.

  2. An unqualified adviser offers to issue court proceedings for clients for a fee. Which concept is engaged?
    • Anyone may conduct litigation if they don't call themselves a solicitor
    • Conduct of litigation is reserved to authorised persons
    • It is lawful if the adviser has a law degree
    • It is lawful if the client signs a written waiver first
    • Only the issuing of claims over £10,000 is reserved
    Show the answer

    Conduct of litigation is reserved to authorised persons. Carrying on a reserved legal activity without authorisation is an offence under the Legal Services Act 2007, s 14.
