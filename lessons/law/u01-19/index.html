The judiciary

The judiciary is the body of judges and magistrates who decide cases. Its structure, appointment and diversity affect public confidence in the justice system.

Who the judges are

  • Superior judges: Justices of the Supreme Court, Lords and Lady Justices of Appeal, High Court judges.
  • Inferior judges: circuit judges (Crown Court and County Court), recorders (part-time), district judges, district judges (magistrates' courts), tribunal judges.
  • Magistrates (justices of the peace): unpaid lay volunteers who sit in benches of two or three with a legally qualified legal adviser.
  • Heads: the Lord Chief Justice is head of the judiciary of England and Wales; the Master of the Rolls heads civil justice; the President of the Supreme Court leads that court.

Appointment

  • Through the Judicial Appointments Commission, on merit, after fair and open competition (Constitutional Reform Act 2005). Supreme Court justices are chosen by an ad hoc selection commission.
  • Eligibility depends on years of post-qualification legal experience (Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007): for example, seven years for High Court appointments.
  • Mandatory retirement age: 75 since 2022 (Public Service Pensions and Judicial Offices Act 2022).

Diversity

The senior judiciary remains less diverse than the population by sex, ethnicity and background, though it is improving. The JAC may use the "equal merit" provision to prefer a candidate from an under-represented group where candidates are of equal merit.

The judicial role

  • Deciding facts and law at trial; managing cases; sentencing; developing the common law; interpreting statutes; reviewing executive action.

Judges declare and develop the law, but the democratic legitimacy to make major policy choices lies with Parliament. That tension runs through debates on judicial law-making.

Summary

  • Judges are appointed on merit through the Judicial Appointments Commission.
  • Diversity remains a concern for public confidence.

Key terms

Judicial Appointments Commission
The body that selects candidates for judicial office in England and Wales
Lord Chief Justice
The head of the judiciary of England and Wales

Flashcards

Answer from memory, then open the card to check.

Who is the head of the judiciary of England and Wales?

The Lord Chief Justice.

What is the mandatory retirement age for judges?

75, since the Public Service Pensions and Judicial Offices Act 2022.

Name the three ranks of superior judges.

Justices of the Supreme Court, Lords and Lady Justices of Appeal, and High Court judges.

Where do circuit judges sit?

In the Crown Court and County Court.

What is a recorder?

A part-time judge.

How do magistrates sit?

As unpaid lay volunteers in benches of two or three with a legally qualified legal adviser.

Check your understanding

  1. Who appoints most judges in England and Wales?
    • Parliament elects judges by a free vote
    • The Judicial Appointments Commission selects candidates on merit
    • The Law Society and Bar Council nominate them
    • The Lord Chancellor chooses without a selection body
    • The Prime Minister chooses, advised by the Cabinet
    Show the answer

    The Judicial Appointments Commission selects candidates on merit. The JAC runs selection on merit; formal appointment follows.

  2. A bench in the magistrates' court is made up of three lay justices. Who advises them on the law?
    • A Crown Court judge, by telephone if needed
    • A district judge from the same courthouse
    • A legally qualified legal adviser who sits with the bench
    • The defence solicitor, if the defendant agrees
    • The prosecutor, as an officer of the court
    Show the answer

    A legally qualified legal adviser who sits with the bench. Lay magistrates are advised on law and procedure by a qualified legal adviser, but decide the case themselves.
