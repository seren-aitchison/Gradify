H: The idea
P: **Enzymes** are biological catalysts, almost all globular proteins, that speed up reactions by lowering their activation energy. They are unchanged at the end of each reaction, so a single enzyme molecule can catalyse thousands of reactions a second.
P: The **active site** is a small cleft or pocket on the enzyme, formed by a few amino acids whose side chains bind the substrate and catalyse its reaction. Its shape and chemistry are set by the protein's tertiary structure.
P: **Activation energy** is the energy reacting molecules need before they can react. An enzyme provides a different route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules react each second at body temperature.
K: **Enzyme**: a biological catalyst, usually a globular protein, that lowers the activation energy of a reaction.
H: Enzymes are specific
P: **Enzyme specificity** comes from the active site's shape and from the chemical interactions its amino acids make with the substrate: hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions and hydrophobic contacts. Only a substrate that matches both binds well enough to react, so enzymes can even tell apart mirror-image forms of a molecule.

H: Denaturation
P: Heat and extreme pH break the hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds that hold an enzyme's tertiary structure, so the active site loses its shape and the substrate can no longer bind: the enzyme is **denatured**.
H: How an enzyme works: the lock and key model
P: In the lock and key model, the formation of an enzyme-substrate complex needs no change in shape: the active site is treated as rigid and already complementary to the substrate. The model explains specificity, but not how binding itself helps the reaction along, or why some enzymes bind a range of related substrates.
- The substrate collides with the enzyme's active site.
- Because their shapes are complementary, the substrate fits into the active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex.
- The reaction happens while the substrate is held in the active site: it is broken down or joined to another molecule.
- The products are a different shape, so they leave the active site.
- The enzyme is unchanged and its active site is free to bind another substrate molecule.
H: The induced fit model
P: The induced fit model, now preferred, says the active site is flexible.
- The substrate binds to an active site that is close to, but not exactly, complementary to it.
- Binding causes the active site to change shape slightly, moulding around the substrate to form the enzyme-substrate complex.
- The closer fit puts strain on bonds in the substrate and brings reacting groups together, lowering the activation energy.
- The products are released and the active site returns to its original shape.
!: "An enzyme is used up in the reaction it catalyses." An enzyme is unchanged at the end of the reaction, so it can catalyse the same reaction again and again.
!: "In induced fit, the enzyme changes shape permanently." The active site changes shape only while the substrate is bound; it returns to its original shape when the products are released.
