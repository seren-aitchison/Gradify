Osmosis

Osmosis is the overall movement of water across a partially permeable membrane, out of a dilute solution and into a more concentrated one.

GCSE

Learning objectives

  • Define osmosis and use the term correctly
  • Explain why water moves overall into the concentrated solution
  • Predict and explain what osmosis does to animal and plant cells

The idea

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. It happens because particles move about randomly: more of them leave a crowded region than arrive in it, so they spread out until they are evenly mixed.

A solution is a solute (such as sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent; in living things the solvent is water. A dilute solution contains little solute, so most of its molecules are water. A concentrated solution contains a lot of solute, so a smaller share of its molecules are water.

A partially permeable membrane lets some molecules cross but not others. A cell membrane lets small molecules such as water pass through, but holds back larger solute molecules such as sucrose.

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a partially permeable membrane, from a dilute solution into a more concentrated one. It is diffusion of water: water moves from where its molecules are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated.

Osmosis: the diffusion of water: its overall movement across a partially permeable membrane, out of a dilute solution and into a more concentrated one.

Osmosis is passive

Osmosis is a passive process: the cell uses no energy from respiration to move water this way. Water simply moves down its own concentration gradient.

When there is no net movement

If both sides have the same concentration of water, water molecules still cross in both directions, but equally often. There is no net movement of water.

Why water moves overall into the concentrated solution

A particle model explains the direction.

  • Water molecules move about randomly, and some pass through the membrane in each direction.
  • In the dilute solution a bigger share of the molecules are water, so more water molecules reach the membrane from that side each second.
  • So more water crosses from the dilute side to the concentrated side than crosses back.
  • The overall (net) movement of water is into the concentrated solution.
  • The solute molecules are too large to pass through the membrane, so they stay on their own side.
Common mistake"In osmosis, the sugar or salt moves across the membrane." In osmosis it is water that moves overall; the partially permeable membrane holds the solute molecules back.
Common mistake"Water moves from the concentrated solution to the dilute solution." Water moves overall from the dilute solution, which has more water, to the concentrated solution, which has less.
Common mistake"Water moves from a high concentration to a low concentration." Say which concentration you mean: water moves from a high concentration of water (a dilute solution) to a low concentration of water (a concentrated solution). The two are opposites, so leaving it out makes the answer ambiguous.
Common mistake"Osmosis needs energy from respiration." Osmosis is passive: water moves down its own gradient, and the cell spends no energy on it. Moving substances against a gradient (active transport) is what needs energy.
Common mistake"When both solutions have the same concentration, water stops moving." Water molecules keep crossing in both directions; they just cross equally often, so there is no net movement.
Common mistake"A plant cell in pure water swells until it bursts, like a red blood cell." The cell wall pushes back and stops the plant cell bursting; it becomes turgid instead.

What osmosis does to animal and plant cells

Whether water enters or leaves a cell depends on the solution around it. Plant cells have a cell wall; animal cells do not, and that changes what happens.

  • A red blood cell is placed in pure water: water enters by osmosis, and the cell swells and bursts. The cell's contents are more concentrated than pure water, and an animal cell has no wall to stop it expanding.
  • A red blood cell is placed in a concentrated salt solution: water leaves by osmosis, and the cell shrinks. The solution has a lower concentration of water than the cell's contents.
  • A plant cell is placed in pure water: water enters by osmosis, and the cell becomes turgid without bursting. The cell wall pushes back on the swelling cell and stops it bursting; a firm, turgid cell helps hold the plant up.
  • A plant cell is placed in a concentrated sugar solution: water leaves by osmosis, and the cell becomes flaccid, then plasmolysed. As the cell loses water its contents shrink and the membrane pulls away from the cell wall.
  • A cell is placed in a solution with the same concentration of water as its contents: there is no net movement of water, and the cell stays the same. Water crosses both ways equally often.

Key terms

Diffusion
The net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random movement
Concentration gradient
A difference in the concentration of a substance between two regions
Solute
A substance dissolved in a solvent, such as sugar or salt in water
Dilute solution
A solution with little solute, so it has a high concentration of water
Concentrated solution
A solution with a lot of solute, so it has a lower concentration of water
Partially permeable membrane
A membrane that lets some molecules, such as water, cross but stops others, such as larger solute molecules
Osmosis
The diffusion of water: its overall movement across a partially permeable membrane, out of a dilute solution and into a more concentrated one
Passive transport
Movement of substances across a membrane down a gradient, using no energy from the cell
Turgid
Describes a plant cell that has taken in water and is firm, its contents pressing on the cell wall
Flaccid
Describes a plant cell that has lost water and is soft, no longer pressing on its wall
Plasmolysis
The shrinking of a plant cell's contents so the membrane pulls away from the cell wall, when the cell loses a lot of water
Cell wall
A layer outside the cell membrane of plant cells (and bacteria) that strengthens the cell and stops it bursting

Summary

  • Osmosis is the overall movement of water across a partially permeable membrane, out of a dilute solution and into a more concentrated one.
  • Osmosis is passive: it uses no energy from respiration.
  • Water crosses the membrane both ways, but more crosses from the dilute side, so the net movement is into the concentrated solution; the solute can't cross.
  • In pure water an animal cell swells and may burst, while a plant cell becomes turgid because its wall resists; in a concentrated solution both lose water, and a plant cell can become plasmolysed.
