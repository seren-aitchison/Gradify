Osmosis

This practical measures how the mass of plant tissue changes in sugar (or salt) solutions of different concentrations, and uses the results to estimate the concentration of the tissue's own cells.

GCSE

Learning objectives

  • Plan and carry out investigating osmosis in plant tissue, and analyse and evaluate the results
  • Calculate percentage change in mass
  • Interpret potato cylinders in sugar solutions

What you need to know first

  • Osmosis
  • Diffusion
  • Solutes, solvents and solutions
  • Partially permeable membranes

Aim

To investigate how the concentration of a sugar solution affects the change in mass of potato tissue, and to estimate the concentration at which the tissue neither gains nor loses water.

Prediction: Potato cylinders will gain mass in dilute solutions and lose mass in concentrated ones; somewhere in between there will be no change.

Equipment

  • A potato
  • A cork borer and a ruler (or a chip cutter)
  • A scalpel and a white tile
  • Boiling tubes (or test tubes) in a rack, labelled
  • Sucrose solutions: 0 (distilled water), 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol/dm³
  • Sugar (sucrose) or salt solutions: 0 (distilled water), 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol/dm³
  • A top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g
  • Paper towels
  • A timer

Variables

  • Independent variable (what you change): The concentration of the sugar solution
  • Dependent variable (what you measure): The percentage change in mass of the potato cylinder
  • Control variables (kept the same): The volume of solution in each tube; The time the cylinders are left in the solutions; The size and surface area of each cylinder; The temperature; Using cylinders from the same potato

Method

  • 1. Use the cork borer to cut cylinders from the same potato, and trim them with the scalpel on the tile to the same length (for example 3 cm), removing any skin.
  • 2. Blot each cylinder gently with a paper towel and record its starting mass.
  • 3. Put the same volume (for example 10 cm³) of each concentration into a labelled tube.
  • 4. Put one cylinder in each tube and start the timer; leave them for the same time (for example 30 minutes, or overnight), at room temperature.
  • 5. Remove each cylinder, blot it gently in the same way, and record its final mass.
  • 6. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each cylinder.
  • 7. Repeat with more cylinders at each concentration and calculate a mean for each.

Why some steps matter:

  • Blot each cylinder before weighing it: Liquid on the surface would add to the mass without having entered the cells
  • Use percentage change, not change in mass: The cylinders start at slightly different masses, and percentage change makes them comparable
  • Cut all the cylinders from the same potato: Different potatoes can have cells of different concentrations
  • Leave every tube for the same time: The longer a cylinder is left, the more water can move, up to equilibrium

Safety

Competence and safetyCut away from your fingers on a white tile, and carry the scalpel and cork borer point down.
Competence and safetyWipe up spills straight away: sugar solutions make the floor slippery.
Competence and safetyDon't eat any of the potato or drink the solutions in the laboratory.

Recording results

Record your results in a table with a row for each concentration and its repeats:

  • Concentration of sugar solution (mol/dm³) · Starting mass (g) · Final mass (g) · Change in mass (g) · Percentage change in mass (%)

Analysing results

  • Work out the percentage change in mass for each cylinder, then the mean for each concentration.
  • Plot the mean percentage change (y-axis) against concentration (x-axis), with zero in the middle of the y-axis, and draw a line of best fit.
  • Read off where the line crosses zero: at that concentration the solution has the same concentration of water as the potato cells, so there is no net movement.

To compare pieces of tissue that started at different masses, work out the percentage change in mass. A gain means water entered by osmosis; a loss means water left.

percentage change in mass = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100
Worked example

A cylinder of potato has a mass of 2.50 g. After 30 minutes in a sugar solution its mass is 2.80 g. Find the percentage change in mass, and say what it shows.

  1. Change in mass = 2.80 − 2.50 = +0.30 g.
  2. Percentage change = 0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +12%.
  3. The potato gained mass, so water entered its cells by osmosis: the solution had a higher concentration of water than the potato cells.

Evaluating the investigation

  • Blotting is a source of error: blotting harder or more lightly changes the measured mass. Blot every cylinder the same way.
  • Water can evaporate from the tubes if they are left a long time: cover them.
  • Using more concentrations near where the line crosses zero gives a more precise estimate.
  • Repeating and finding a mean makes anomalies easier to spot; an anomalous result should be repeated, not just ignored.
Common mistake"Water moves from a high concentration to a low concentration." Say which concentration you mean: water moves from a high concentration of water (a dilute solution) to a low concentration of water (a concentrated solution). The two are opposites, so leaving it out makes the answer ambiguous.
Common mistake"You can compare the cylinders by their change in mass in grams." Cylinders start at different masses, so compare the percentage change in mass: the same change in grams is a bigger share of a lighter cylinder.
