## Osmosis
S: Osmosis is the net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

PRE: Diffusion
H: The idea
P: **Water potential** (ψ, the Greek letter psi, measured in kPa) describes how strongly water tends to leave a solution. Pure water at atmospheric pressure has the highest water potential, defined as 0 kPa. Dissolving solutes lowers it, so every solution has a negative water potential, and the more concentrated the solution, the more negative it is.
P: Solutions are described relative to a cell. A **hypotonic** solution has a higher water potential than the cell, so water enters it; an **isotonic** solution has the same water potential, so there is no net movement; a **hypertonic** solution has a lower water potential, so water leaves the cell.
P: **Eukaryotic cells** (animals, plants, fungi and protists) have a nucleus bounded by a double membrane, and other membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus. Their ribosomes are 80S, and most are 10–100 µm across.
P: A **partially permeable membrane** lets some molecules cross but not others. A cell membrane lets small molecules such as water pass through, but holds back larger solute molecules such as sucrose.
P: **Osmosis** is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher (less negative) water potential to a region of lower (more negative) water potential.
K: **Osmosis**: the net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.
H: Osmosis is passive
P: Osmosis is a passive process: the cell uses no energy from respiration to move water this way. Water simply moves down its own concentration gradient.
D: bio-osmosis-cells
H: Aquaporins
P: Some water diffuses slowly through the lipid bilayer itself, but most crosses through **aquaporins**: channel proteins that let water molecules through in single file while keeping out ions. Cells that move a lot of water, such as red blood cells and the cells lining kidney collecting ducts, have many of them, and the number of aquaporins in a membrane controls how quickly water crosses it.
H: Calculations
P: A plant cell's water potential is the sum of its solute potential, which is negative, and its pressure potential, which is positive when the cell wall pushes back on a full cell.
E: ψ = ψs + ψp :: water potential = solute potential + pressure potential, all in kPa
X: A plant cell has a solute potential of −750 kPa and a pressure potential of +250 kPa. It is placed in a solution with a water potential of −600 kPa. Which way does water move?
> Cell: ψ = ψs + ψp = −750 + 250 = −500 kPa.
> −500 kPa is higher (less negative) than −600 kPa.
> Water moves from the higher to the lower water potential: out of the cell into the solution.
!: "A more concentrated solution has a higher water potential." Solutes lower water potential: the more concentrated a solution, the more negative its ψ. Pure water, at 0 kPa, is the highest.
!: "Osmosis needs energy from respiration." Osmosis is passive: water moves down its own gradient, and the cell spends no energy on it. Moving substances against a gradient (active transport) is what needs energy.
!: "When both solutions have the same concentration, water stops moving." Water molecules keep crossing in both directions; they just cross equally often, so there is no net movement.
H: What osmosis does to animal and plant cells
P: Whether water enters or leaves a cell depends on the water potential of the solution around it compared with the cell's. Plant cells have a cell wall, which adds a pressure potential; animal cells do not.
- **A red blood cell is placed in pure water:** water enters by osmosis and the cell swells and bursts (haemolysis). Pure water (0 kPa) has a higher water potential than the cytoplasm, and with no wall nothing opposes the swelling.
- **A red blood cell is placed in a concentrated salt solution:** water leaves by osmosis and the cell shrinks and crenates. The hypertonic solution has a lower water potential than the cell's contents.
- **A plant cell is placed in pure water:** water enters by osmosis until the cell is fully turgid. As the cell fills, the wall pushes back: the pressure potential rises until the cell's water potential reaches that of the water outside.
- **A plant cell is placed in a concentrated sugar solution:** water leaves by osmosis and the cell becomes flaccid, then plasmolysed. Once the pressure potential falls to zero, further loss of water pulls the membrane away from the wall.
- **A cell is placed in an isotonic solution:** there is no net movement of water and the cell stays the same. The water potentials inside and outside are equal.
T: Water potential | The tendency of water to move out of a solution, ψ in kPa; pure water at atmospheric pressure has the highest value, 0 kPa
T: Solute potential | The part of water potential due to dissolved solutes, ψs: zero for pure water, negative for any solution
T: Pressure potential | The part of water potential due to pressure, ψp: in a turgid plant cell, the positive pressure of the wall on the contents
T: Hypotonic | A solution with a higher water potential than a cell's contents, so water enters the cell
T: Isotonic | A solution with the same water potential as a cell's contents: no net movement of water
T: Hypertonic | A solution with a lower water potential than a cell's contents, so water leaves the cell
T: Eukaryotic cell | A cell with a membrane-bound nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles
T: Partially permeable membrane | A membrane that lets some molecules, such as water, cross but stops others, such as larger solute molecules
T: Osmosis | The net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane
T: Passive transport | Movement of substances across a membrane down a gradient, using no energy from the cell
T: Aquaporin | A channel protein in cell membranes that lets water molecules cross quickly while excluding ions
T: Turgid | Describes a plant cell that has taken in water and is firm, its contents pressing on the cell wall
T: Flaccid | Describes a plant cell that has lost water and is soft, no longer pressing on its wall
T: Plasmolysis | The shrinking of a plant cell's contents so the membrane pulls away from the cell wall, when the cell loses a lot of water
T: Cell wall | A layer outside the cell membrane of plant cells (and bacteria) that strengthens the cell and stops it bursting

SUM: Hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic compare a solution's water potential with a cell's.
