Osmosis

Osmosis is the net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

Undergraduate

Learning objectives

  • Define osmosis and use the term correctly
  • Explain what water potential is
  • Use ψ = ψs + ψp to work out water potential of a cell
  • Predict and explain what osmosis does to animal and plant cells

What you need to know first

  • Diffusion
  • Solutes, solvents and solutions
  • Partially permeable membranes

The idea

Water potential (ψ, the Greek letter psi, measured in kPa) describes how strongly water tends to leave a solution. Pure water at atmospheric pressure has the highest water potential, defined as 0 kPa. Dissolving solutes lowers it, so every solution has a negative water potential, and the more concentrated the solution, the more negative it is.

Solutions are described relative to a cell. A hypotonic solution has a higher water potential than the cell, so water enters it; an isotonic solution has the same water potential, so there is no net movement; a hypertonic solution has a lower water potential, so water leaves the cell.

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher (less negative) water potential to a region of lower (more negative) water potential.

Osmosis is a passive process: the cell uses no energy from respiration to move water this way. Water simply moves down its own concentration gradient.

Some water diffuses slowly through the lipid bilayer itself, but most crosses through aquaporins: channel proteins that let water molecules through in single file while keeping out ions. Cells that move a lot of water, such as red blood cells and the cells lining kidney collecting ducts, have many of them, and the number of aquaporins in a membrane controls how quickly water crosses it.

Calculations

A plant cell's water potential is the sum of its solute potential, which is negative, and its pressure potential, which is positive when the cell wall pushes back on a full cell.

ψ = ψs + ψpwater potential = solute potential + pressure potential, all in kPa
Worked example

A plant cell has a solute potential of −750 kPa and a pressure potential of +250 kPa. It is placed in a solution with a water potential of −600 kPa. Which way does water move?

  1. Cell: ψ = ψs + ψp = −750 + 250 = −500 kPa.
  2. −500 kPa is higher (less negative) than −600 kPa.
  3. Water moves from the higher to the lower water potential: out of the cell into the solution.

What osmosis does to animal and plant cells

Whether water enters or leaves a cell depends on the solution around it. Plant cells have a cell wall; animal cells do not, and that changes what happens.

  • A red blood cell is placed in pure water: water enters by osmosis, and the cell swells and bursts. The cell's contents are more concentrated than pure water, and an animal cell has no wall to stop it expanding.
  • A red blood cell is placed in a concentrated salt solution: water leaves by osmosis, and the cell shrinks. The solution has a lower concentration of water than the cell's contents.
  • A plant cell is placed in pure water: water enters by osmosis, and the cell becomes turgid without bursting. The cell wall pushes back on the swelling cell and stops it bursting; a firm, turgid cell helps hold the plant up.
  • A plant cell is placed in a concentrated sugar solution: water leaves by osmosis, and the cell becomes flaccid, then plasmolysed. As the cell loses water its contents shrink and the membrane pulls away from the cell wall.
  • A cell is placed in a solution with the same concentration of water as its contents: there is no net movement of water, and the cell stays the same. Water crosses both ways equally often.
Common mistake"Osmosis needs energy from respiration." Osmosis is passive: water moves down its own gradient, and the cell spends no energy on it. Moving substances against a gradient (active transport) is what needs energy.
Common mistake"When both solutions have the same concentration, water stops moving." Water molecules keep crossing in both directions; they just cross equally often, so there is no net movement.

Key terms

Water potential
The tendency of water to move out of a solution, ψ in kPa; pure water at atmospheric pressure has the highest value, 0 kPa
Solute potential
The part of water potential due to dissolved solutes, ψs: zero for pure water, negative for any solution
Pressure potential
The part of water potential due to pressure, ψp: in a turgid plant cell, the positive pressure of the wall on the contents
Hypotonic
A solution with a higher water potential than a cell's contents, so water enters the cell
Isotonic
A solution with the same water potential as a cell's contents: no net movement of water
Hypertonic
A solution with a lower water potential than a cell's contents, so water leaves the cell
Osmosis
The net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane
Passive transport
Movement of substances across a membrane down a gradient, using no energy from the cell
Aquaporin
A channel protein in cell membranes that lets water molecules cross quickly while excluding ions
Turgid
Describes a plant cell that has taken in water and is firm, its contents pressing on the cell wall
Flaccid
Describes a plant cell that has lost water and is soft, no longer pressing on its wall
Plasmolysis
The shrinking of a plant cell's contents so the membrane pulls away from the cell wall, when the cell loses a lot of water

Summary

  • Osmosis is the net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.
  • Pure water has the highest water potential, 0 kPa; solutes lower it, so solutions have negative water potentials.
  • Hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic compare a solution's water potential with a cell's.
  • Osmosis is passive: it uses no energy from respiration.
  • Most water crosses membranes through aquaporins, and their number sets how fast it crosses.
