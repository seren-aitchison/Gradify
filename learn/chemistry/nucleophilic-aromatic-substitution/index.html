Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution

Aryl halides resist SN1 and SN2, but electron-withdrawing groups at the 2- and 4-positions allow SNAr: the nucleophile adds to form a stabilised Meisenheimer complex and the leaving group is then eliminated.

Chemistry · Organic Reaction Mechanisms · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why simple aryl halides resist nucleophilic substitution
  • Describe the addition-elimination (SNAr) mechanism
  • Explain how electron-withdrawing groups activate the ring

Why it is unusual

  • Aryl halides do not undergo SN1 or SN2 easily.
  • Backside attack is blocked by the ring.
  • Aryl cations are very unstable.
  • The C–X bond is strengthened by overlap with the ring.

SNAr mechanism

  • Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂) at the 2- or 4-position activate the ring.
  • The nucleophile adds to the carbon bearing the leaving group.
  • A negatively charged Meisenheimer complex forms, stabilised by the nitro group.
  • The leaving group is eliminated, restoring aromaticity.

1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene reacts readily with hydroxide, while chlorobenzene needs extreme conditions. Sanger used 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene to label amino acids at the start of protein chains, part of his work sequencing insulin. In SNAr, fluoride is often the best leaving group because the addition step is rate-determining.

Worked example

Sanger's reagent.

  1. The amino group of a protein attacks the C–F carbon.
  2. A Meisenheimer complex forms, stabilised by two nitro groups.
  3. Fluoride leaves, restoring the aromatic ring.
  4. The N-terminal amino acid is tagged with a yellow label.
Common mistakeApplying SN2 to aryl halides. The ring blocks backside attack, so a different mechanism is needed.

In 3 lines: Aryl halides resist SN1/SN2. Addition then elimination. Nitro groups activate.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which compound undergoes SNAr most readily?
    • 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene
    • Chlorobenzene
    • Methylbenzene
    • Benzene
    Show the answer

    Answer: 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene. Two nitro groups strongly stabilise the negative intermediate.

  2. Why can aryl halides not react by SN2?
    • Aryl halides have no leaving group
    • The ring blocks backside attack on the carbon
    • They form very stable aryl cations
    • The C–X bond is extremely weak
    Show the answer

    Answer: The ring blocks backside attack on the carbon. The nucleophile cannot approach from behind the C–X bond.

  3. Why is fluoride often a good leaving group in SNAr but poor in SN2?
    • Fluoride is the weakest base of all
    • C–F bonds are the weakest bonds
    • In SNAr the addition step is rate-determining, and F speeds it by withdrawing electrons
    • SNAr does not involve a leaving group
    Show the answer

    Answer: In SNAr the addition step is rate-determining, and F speeds it by withdrawing electrons. Bond breaking is not rate-determining in SNAr.

Key terms

SNAr
Nucleophilic aromatic substitution by addition-elimination
Meisenheimer complex
The negatively charged intermediate in SNAr
Activating group
A group that makes a ring more reactive to a given reagent
