Rate-determining Steps

The rate-determining step is the slowest step and controls the overall rate; the rate equation includes species in or before it, so rate equations distinguish mechanisms such as SN1 (rate = k[RX]) and SN2 (rate = k[RX][OH⁻]).

Chemistry · Chemical Kinetics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define the rate-determining step
  • Deduce a rate equation from a mechanism
  • Propose a mechanism consistent with a rate equation

The slow step

  • The rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest step in a mechanism.
  • The overall rate cannot be faster than this step.
  • It is like the narrowest point of a bottleneck.
  • It usually has the highest activation energy.

Using the RDS

  • The rate equation contains species in the RDS and any steps before it.
  • Species only involved after the RDS are zero order.
  • If an intermediate appears in the RDS, its formation from earlier species is included.
  • Example: SN1 hydrolysis of tertiary halogenoalkanes has rate = k[RX].

In SN1 reactions, the slow step is the C–X bond breaking to form a carbocation, so hydroxide ions do not appear in the rate equation. In SN2 reactions, both RX and OH⁻ are in the single step, so rate = k[RX][OH⁻]. The rate equation therefore reveals mechanism.

Worked example

SN1 versus SN2.

  1. 2-bromo-2-methylpropane + OH⁻: rate = k[RBr].
  2. So the slow step involves only RBr, forming a carbocation.
  3. Bromoethane + OH⁻: rate = k[RBr][OH⁻].
  4. So the single step involves both species colliding.
Common mistakeAssuming the first step is always the slowest. The rate-determining step can be any step in the sequence.

In 3 lines: Slowest step controls rate. Species up to the RDS. Rate equations reveal mechanisms.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which step usually has the highest activation energy?
    • The fastest step
    • The final step always
    • The rate-determining step
    • The first step always
    Show the answer

    Answer: The rate-determining step. The slowest step has the biggest barrier.

  2. Why does OH⁻ not appear in the SN1 rate equation?
    • It is a catalyst that is regenerated
    • It is not a reactant
    • It is in the slow step
    • It reacts only after the slow carbocation-forming step
    Show the answer

    Answer: It reacts only after the slow carbocation-forming step. Species after the RDS do not affect rate.

  3. A reaction has rate = k[A]. Which mechanism fits?
    • A slow step involving only A, then fast steps with B
    • A single step where A and B collide
    • A slow step involving two B molecules
    • A fast step with A followed by slow B
    Show the answer

    Answer: A slow step involving only A, then fast steps with B. Only A appears in the slow step, so only A is in the rate equation.
