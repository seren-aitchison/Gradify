Oxidation States

First-row transition metals show variable oxidation states rising to +7 at manganese; high states are stabilised by oxide and fluoride ligands, low states by π-acceptors, and stability also depends on pH and d-electron configuration.

Chemistry · Inorganic Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the range of oxidation states of first-row transition metals
  • Explain which oxidation states are stable and why
  • Explain how ligands and pH stabilise oxidation states

Range of states

  • States increase to a maximum at manganese (+7) across the first row.
  • Beyond manganese, high states become less stable.
  • +2 is common for later metals; higher states are common for early metals.
  • Maximum states often equal the number of 4s plus 3d electrons up to Mn.

Stabilising factors

  • Oxide and fluoride ligands stabilise high states, as in MnO₄⁻ and CrO₄²⁻.
  • π-acceptor ligands such as CO stabilise low states, even 0.
  • pH: high states are often more oxidising in acid.
  • Half-filled d⁵ (Mn²⁺, Fe³⁺) has extra stability.

Vanadium shows four colours as it is reduced by zinc in acid: yellow +5, blue +4, green +3 and violet +2. Manganese(VII) in manganate is a powerful oxidising agent. Copper(I) disproportionates in water into copper metal and copper(II).

Worked example

Vanadium colour sequence.

  1. VO₂⁺ (+5) is yellow.
  2. VO²⁺ (+4) is blue.
  3. V³⁺ (+3) is green.
  4. V²⁺ (+2) is violet.
Common mistakeThinking each transition metal has one fixed oxidation state. Most show several, often with different colours.

In 3 lines: Maximum at manganese. Ligands stabilise states. Vanadium colours.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which ligands stabilise high oxidation states?
    • Carbon monoxide and other π-acceptors
    • Neutral hydrocarbons such as methane
    • Oxide and fluoride ions, as in MnO₄⁻
    • Noble gas atoms such as argon
    Show the answer

    Answer: Oxide and fluoride ions, as in MnO₄⁻. Small, electronegative, highly charged ligands donate strongly.

  2. Why is iron(III) relatively stable?
    • Fe³⁺ has a completely filled d shell
    • Fe³⁺ has no d electrons at all
    • Fe³⁺ is a noble gas configuration
    • Fe³⁺ has a half-filled d⁵ configuration
    Show the answer

    Answer: Fe³⁺ has a half-filled d⁵ configuration. Half-filled d subshells have extra exchange stability.

  3. Why does copper(I) disproportionate in water?
    • Cu²⁺ is strongly hydrated, making Cu(0) and Cu(II) more stable together
    • Copper(I) has no electrons
    • Water oxidises all copper to +3
    • Copper(I) is a noble gas
    Show the answer

    Answer: Cu²⁺ is strongly hydrated, making Cu(0) and Cu(II) more stable together. Favourable hydration of Cu²⁺ drives 2Cu⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺.

Key terms

Oxidation state
The charge an atom would have if all bonds were ionic
Disproportionation
Simultaneous oxidation and reduction of one species
Oxoanion
A negative ion containing oxygen, such as MnO₄⁻
