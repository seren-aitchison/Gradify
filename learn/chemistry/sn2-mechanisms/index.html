SN2 Mechanisms

SN2 is a concerted backside attack with rate = k[R–X][Nu], giving inversion of configuration; it is fastest for methyl and primary substrates with strong nucleophiles in polar aprotic solvents and is blocked at tertiary centres by steric hindrance.

Chemistry · Organic Reaction Mechanisms · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the one-step SN2 mechanism
  • Explain second-order kinetics in SN2
  • Explain inversion of configuration and steric effects

Mechanism

  • The nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group (backside attack).
  • Bond forming and bond breaking happen together.
  • The transition state has five groups around carbon, partly bonded.
  • Rate = k[R–X][Nu]: second order, bimolecular.

Features

  • Inversion of configuration, like an umbrella turning inside out (Walden inversion).
  • Favoured by methyl and primary substrates, which are uncrowded.
  • Favoured by strong nucleophiles and polar aprotic solvents.
  • Tertiary substrates are too crowded for SN2.

(R)-2-bromobutane reacting with hydroxide by SN2 gives (S)-butan-2-ol, showing inversion. SN2 reactions are important in biology, such as methylation by S-adenosylmethionine. Steric hindrance explains the order methyl > primary > secondary for SN2 rates.

Worked example

Inversion in practice.

  1. OH⁻ approaches C2 of (R)-2-bromobutane from behind.
  2. The other three groups flatten in the transition state.
  3. Br⁻ leaves as the groups flip over.
  4. The product is (S)-butan-2-ol.
Common mistakeThinking SN2 always changes R to S. Inversion is geometric; the label may stay the same if priorities change.

In 3 lines: One concerted step. Rate = k[R–X][Nu]. Inversion.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which substrate reacts fastest by SN2?
    • Bromomethane, an uncrowded methyl halide
    • 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, a tertiary halide
    • 2-bromopropane, a secondary halide
    • 2-bromobutane, a secondary halide
    Show the answer

    Answer: Bromomethane, an uncrowded methyl halide. Least steric hindrance allows easiest backside attack.

  2. Why do tertiary haloalkanes not react by SN2?
    • Tertiary carbons have no leaving group
    • Three bulky groups block backside attack by the nucleophile
    • The nucleophile is too small to react
    • The C–Br bond in a tertiary haloalkane is too strong to break
    Show the answer

    Answer: Three bulky groups block backside attack by the nucleophile. Steric hindrance prevents the nucleophile reaching carbon.

  3. Why do polar aprotic solvents speed up SN2?
    • They stabilise carbocation intermediates strongly
    • They donate protons directly to the nucleophile
    • They do not solvate anionic nucleophiles strongly, leaving them reactive
    • They slow every reaction step down to a crawl
    Show the answer

    Answer: They do not solvate anionic nucleophiles strongly, leaving them reactive. Free, poorly solvated nucleophiles attack the carbon much faster.
