Adsorption

Physisorption is weak and reversible with multilayers, while chemisorption forms bonds in a monolayer; the Langmuir isotherm θ = KP/(1 + KP) describes monolayer coverage and catalysts need intermediate binding strength (Sabatier principle).

Chemistry · Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish physisorption and chemisorption
  • Describe the Langmuir isotherm
  • Explain the role of adsorption in catalysis

Types

  • Physisorption: weak London forces, low enthalpy (about −20 kJ mol⁻¹), multilayers possible, reversible.
  • Chemisorption: chemical bonds form, high enthalpy (about −200 kJ mol⁻¹), monolayer only.
  • Adsorption is usually exothermic.
  • Adsorption (on a surface) differs from absorption (into a bulk).

Langmuir isotherm

  • Assumes a monolayer with equivalent, independent sites.
  • θ = KP / (1 + KP), where θ is the fraction of sites covered.
  • At low pressure, θ ∝ P; at high pressure, θ → 1.
  • Real surfaces may deviate from these assumptions.

In heterogeneous catalysis, reactants chemisorb on the surface, weakening bonds; products then desorb. The Sabatier principle says binding should be neither too weak nor too strong. Activated carbon removes pollutants by physisorption on its huge surface area.

Worked example

Langmuir coverage.

  1. K = 0.5 kPa⁻¹, P = 2 kPa.
  2. KP = 1.0.
  3. θ = 1.0 / (1 + 1.0) = 0.5.
  4. Half the surface sites are covered.
Common mistakeConfusing adsorption with absorption. Adsorption occurs only on the surface.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which type of adsorption can form multilayers?
    • Chemisorption
    • Physisorption
    • Absorption into the bulk
    • Neither type
    Show the answer

    Answer: Physisorption. Weak forces allow molecules to stack in layers.

  2. Why should a catalyst bind reactants neither too weakly nor too strongly?
    • Strong binding always speeds reactions
    • Weak binding always gives the best rates
    • Too weak gives no activation; too strong blocks the surface
    • Binding strength has no effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Too weak gives no activation; too strong blocks the surface. The Sabatier principle balances activation and release.

  3. Why is activated carbon an effective adsorbent?
    • It chemically destroys all pollutants
    • It has very little surface area
    • It absorbs pollutants into its bulk
    • Its enormous surface area provides many physisorption sites
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its enormous surface area provides many physisorption sites. Pores give hundreds of square metres per gram.

Key terms

Physisorption
Weak adsorption by intermolecular forces
Chemisorption
Adsorption by forming chemical bonds
Langmuir isotherm
θ = KP/(1 + KP) for monolayer adsorption
