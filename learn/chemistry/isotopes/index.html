Isotopes

Isotopes have the same number of protons and electrons but different numbers of neutrons, so they react alike but differ in mass.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define isotopes and write isotope symbols
  • Explain why isotopes have the same chemical properties
  • Calculate relative atomic mass from isotopic abundances

Same element, different mass

All atoms of an element have the same number of protons, but the number of neutrons can vary. Chlorine has two stable isotopes: chlorine-35 (17 protons, 18 neutrons) and chlorine-37 (17 protons, 20 neutrons). Their symbols are ³⁵₁₇Cl and ³⁷₁₇Cl, with the mass number at top left and the atomic number at bottom left.

Same chemistry

Chemical reactions involve electrons, and isotopes of an element have the same number and arrangement of electrons, so they react in the same way. Their physical properties that depend on mass differ slightly: heavy water, D₂O, is about 11% denser than H₂O and boils at 101.4 °C.

Relative atomic mass

An element's relative atomic mass Ar is the weighted mean mass of its atoms, relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Mass spectrometry measures the abundance of each isotope.

Worked example

Chlorine is 75.8% chlorine-35 and 24.2% chlorine-37. Calculate Ar (use 35.0 and 37.0 as isotope masses).

  1. Ar = (75.8 × 35.0 + 24.2 × 37.0) ÷ 100.
  2. = (2653 + 895.4) ÷ 100 = 35.5.

Some isotopes have unstable nuclei and are radioactive. Carbon-14, made in the atmosphere, decays with a half-life of about 5730 years, which allows radiocarbon dating; technetium-99m is used in medical imaging.

Investigate with the lab. Build carbon-12, then carbon-13 and carbon-14 by adding neutrons, and check whether the element changes.

Common mistakeSaying isotopes are different elements. They are the same element, because the number of protons is the same.

In 3 lines: Isotopes differ only in neutron number. They have the same chemistry because their electrons are the same. Ar is the abundance-weighted mean of isotope masses.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What do isotopes of the same element have in common?
    • The same number of protons
    • The same number of neutrons
    • The same mass number
    • The same mass
    Show the answer

    Answer: The same number of protons. Isotopes share the atomic number (protons) but differ in neutrons, and so in mass.

  2. A sample of copper's relative atomic mass is 63.5, and its isotopes are copper-63 and copper-65. Which is more abundant?
    • Copper-65
    • Copper-63, because 63.5 is closer to 63 than to 65
    • They are equally abundant
    • It can't be told without more data
    Show the answer

    Answer: Copper-63, because 63.5 is closer to 63 than to 65. A weighted mean lies nearer the more abundant isotope; 63.5 implies about 75% copper-63.

  3. Heavy water (D₂O) can be separated from ordinary water by repeated electrolysis, but no ordinary chemical reaction separates them cleanly. Why?
    • D₂O is a different compound with different bonds
    • Deuterium has a different number of protons
    • Their chemistry is the same, but the small mass difference slightly changes rates, which repeated processing exploits
    • D₂O doesn't react at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: Their chemistry is the same, but the small mass difference slightly changes rates, which repeated processing exploits. Isotopes react the same way, but mass affects reaction rates a little (the kinetic isotope effect), enough for gradual enrichment.

  4. How do isotopes of an element differ?
    • In their number of protons
    • In their number of electrons
    • In their chemical reactions
    • In their number of neutrons
    Show the answer

    Answer: In their number of neutrons. Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers.

Key terms

Isotope
An atom of an element with a different number of neutrons from other atoms of that element
Relative atomic mass
The weighted mean mass of an element's atoms compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom
Half-life
The time for half the nuclei of a radioactive isotope in a sample to decay
