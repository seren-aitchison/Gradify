Electron Affinity

First electron affinities are usually exothermic and most so for the halogens; second electron affinities are endothermic because of repulsion.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define first and second electron affinity and write equations for them
  • Explain the trends in first electron affinity
  • Explain why second electron affinities are endothermic

Definitions

The first electron affinity is the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms each gain an electron to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g), ΔH = −349 kJ mol⁻¹. It is usually exothermic, because the added electron is attracted by the nucleus. (Some textbooks quote the energy released as a positive number, so check the sign convention.)

Trends

The halogens have the most exothermic first electron affinities: an extra electron completes their outer p subshell and is strongly attracted. Electron affinity generally becomes less exothermic down a group, as the added electron goes into a shell further from the nucleus. Fluorine is an exception: its value (−328 kJ mol⁻¹) is less exothermic than chlorine's, because its small 2p subshell is crowded and the incoming electron is repelled by the electrons already there.

Elements whose extra electron would have to enter a new or half-filled subshell have small or unfavourable values: noble gases, and atoms such as nitrogen (2p³), whose value is close to zero.

Second electron affinity

Adding a second electron to an ion that is already negative means forcing it against repulsion, so the second electron affinity is endothermic. For oxygen, O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g) is −141 kJ mol⁻¹, but O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) is about +800 kJ mol⁻¹. Oxide ions still exist in solids such as MgO because the lattice energy released when the ions pack together more than repays this cost.

Common mistakeConfusing electron affinity with electronegativity. Electron affinity is a measured energy change for isolated gaseous atoms; electronegativity describes the pull of an atom on shared electrons in a bond.

In 3 lines: First electron affinity: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g), usually exothermic. Halogens have the most exothermic values; chlorine more than fluorine. Second electron affinities are endothermic because an electron is added to a negative ion.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which group has the most exothermic first electron affinities?
    • Group 1
    • Group 17, the halogens
    • Group 2
    • Group 18
    Show the answer

    Answer: Group 17, the halogens. Gaining one electron completes the halogens' outer subshell, releasing the most energy.

  2. Which equation represents the second electron affinity of sulfur?
    • S(g) + 2e⁻ → S²⁻(g)
    • S(g) → S⁺(g) + e⁻
    • S⁻(g) + e⁻ → S²⁻(g)
    • S²⁻(s) → S(s) + 2e⁻
    Show the answer

    Answer: S⁻(g) + e⁻ → S²⁻(g). The second electron affinity adds one electron to the gaseous 1− ion.

  3. Why is fluorine's first electron affinity less exothermic than chlorine's, though fluorine is higher in the group?
    • Fluorine has fewer protons than chlorine
    • Fluorine's extra electron goes into the third shell
    • Fluorine is a noble gas
    • The added electron enters fluorine's small, crowded 2p subshell and is repelled by the electrons already there
    Show the answer

    Answer: The added electron enters fluorine's small, crowded 2p subshell and is repelled by the electrons already there. Electron–electron repulsion in the compact second shell partly offsets the strong nuclear attraction.

Key terms

First electron affinity
The enthalpy change when each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms gains an electron to form 1− ions
Second electron affinity
The enthalpy change when each ion in one mole of gaseous 1− ions gains an electron to form 2− ions
Exothermic
Releasing energy to the surroundings (negative ΔH)
