Quantum Mechanical Model

In the quantum mechanical model the electron is a wave; the Schrödinger equation gives its allowed energies and orbitals, and ψ² gives the probability of finding it.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why the Bohr model was replaced
  • Describe what the Schrödinger equation and its wavefunctions tell us
  • Explain the uncertainty principle and its consequence for electrons in atoms

Beyond Bohr

Bohr's model (1913) gave hydrogen's energy levels correctly, but it failed for atoms with more than one electron and couldn't explain the relative brightness of spectral lines or why only certain orbits were allowed. Treating the electron as a wave solved these problems.

The Schrödinger equation

In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger wrote an equation for the electron's wave. Its solutions, wavefunctions (ψ), exist only for particular energies, so quantisation follows naturally, just as a guitar string can vibrate only at certain frequencies. The square of the wavefunction, ψ², gives the probability density: how likely the electron is to be found in each small region. An orbital is a wavefunction, and its familiar shape is a picture of where ψ² is large.

Uncertainty

Werner Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927) states that the position and momentum of a particle can't both be known exactly at once: Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π. For an electron confined to an atom, the uncertainty in momentum is so large that a definite path is meaningless. That is why the model gives probabilities, not orbits.

For hydrogen, the solutions give energies Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J ÷ n², exactly matching the spectrum, and for other atoms, approximate methods based on the same equation predict energies and shapes that agree with experiment.

Investigate with the lab. Look at the 3D probability clouds and the radial distributions: they show where an electron is likely to be, not a fixed path.

Common mistakeThinking an orbital's boundary is where the electron stays. It is only a surface enclosing most (often 90%) of the probability; there is a small chance of finding the electron further out.

In 3 lines: Bohr's orbits failed for many-electron atoms. The Schrödinger equation's wavefunctions give quantised energies; ψ² gives probability. The uncertainty principle rules out definite electron paths.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does an orbital represent in the quantum mechanical model?
    • A fixed circular path
    • A region where there is a high probability of finding the electron
    • The nucleus
    • A group of atoms
    Show the answer

    Answer: A region where there is a high probability of finding the electron. Orbitals are probability distributions given by ψ².

  2. Why can't we say exactly where an electron in an atom is and how it is moving?
    • Electrons move too fast for cameras
    • Electrons have no mass
    • The uncertainty principle forbids knowing position and momentum precisely at the same time
    • Microscopes aren't good enough yet
    Show the answer

    Answer: The uncertainty principle forbids knowing position and momentum precisely at the same time. This is a fundamental limit, not a limit of technology.

  3. How does the quantum mechanical model explain why electron energies are quantised?
    • Electrons are stopped by walls in the atom
    • The nucleus emits energy in packets
    • Electrons are particles that can't move
    • Only certain wave patterns satisfy the Schrödinger equation for an electron bound to a nucleus, as only certain notes fit on a string
    Show the answer

    Answer: Only certain wave patterns satisfy the Schrödinger equation for an electron bound to a nucleus, as only certain notes fit on a string. Boundary conditions on a confined wave allow only particular solutions, each with its own energy.

  4. How does the quantum mechanical model describe an electron's location?
    • As a probability distribution around the nucleus
    • As a fixed circular orbit at set distances
    • As a point at the centre of the nucleus
    • As moving in a straight line through the atom
    Show the answer

    Answer: As a probability distribution around the nucleus. Orbitals are regions where an electron is likely to be found.

Key terms

Wavefunction
A mathematical function (ψ) describing an electron's wave in an atom; ψ² gives its probability density
Schrödinger equation
The equation whose solutions give the allowed energies and wavefunctions of electrons
Uncertainty principle
Position and momentum can't both be known exactly: Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π
