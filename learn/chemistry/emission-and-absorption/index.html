Emission and Absorption

Emission spectra are bright lines from falling electrons; absorption spectra are dark lines at the same wavelengths from rising electrons.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish emission and absorption spectra
  • Explain flame test colours in terms of electron transitions
  • Relate the convergence limit of a spectrum to ionisation energy

Two kinds of spectrum

An emission spectrum comes from excited atoms, in a flame or a discharge tube, and appears as bright lines on a dark background. An absorption spectrum appears when white light passes through cooler atoms: they absorb exactly the wavelengths that match their energy gaps, leaving dark lines in a continuous spectrum. For the same element the lines fall at the same wavelengths, because the same energy gaps are involved.

Flame tests

Heating a metal compound in a flame excites its electrons; as they fall back, the light emitted has a characteristic colour.

  • Lithium: crimson red
  • Sodium: yellow-orange (the strong double line near 589 nm)
  • Potassium: lilac
  • Calcium: orange-red (brick red)
  • Copper: blue-green

Atomic absorption and emission spectrometers use the same principle to measure metal concentrations in water, blood and food very precisely.

Spectra and ionisation energy

As n increases the levels converge on the ionisation limit, where the electron is free. The convergence limit of the Lyman series, at 91.2 nm, corresponds to removing the electron from the n = 1 level, so it gives hydrogen's ionisation energy.

Worked example

The Lyman series of hydrogen converges at 91.2 nm. Calculate hydrogen's first ionisation energy in kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. E per atom = hc/λ = 1.987 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 9.12 × 10⁻⁸ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  2. Per mole: 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹ (the accepted value is 1312).

Dark absorption lines in sunlight, first catalogued by Fraunhofer, revealed the Sun's elements; helium was discovered in the solar spectrum in 1868, before it was found on Earth.

Common mistakeAttributing a flame colour to the whole compound. The colour comes from the metal ion's electron transitions; sodium chloride and sodium sulfate give the same yellow.

In 3 lines: Emission gives bright lines; absorption gives dark lines at the same wavelengths. Flame colours identify metal ions. The convergence limit gives the ionisation energy.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which metal ion gives a lilac flame?
    • Sodium
    • Lithium
    • Copper
    • Potassium
    Show the answer

    Answer: Potassium. Potassium's flame is lilac; sodium is yellow-orange, lithium crimson and copper blue-green.

  2. Light from a star shows dark lines at the wavelengths of hydrogen's Balmer series. What does this tell you?
    • Hydrogen atoms in the star's outer layers absorb those wavelengths
    • The star contains no hydrogen
    • Hydrogen is emitting light at those wavelengths
    • The star is moving away very fast
    Show the answer

    Answer: Hydrogen atoms in the star's outer layers absorb those wavelengths. Dark lines are absorption by cooler gas between the hot interior and us.

  3. Why do the emission and absorption lines of an element occur at exactly the same wavelengths?
    • Absorption is just emission seen backwards in time
    • Both involve electrons moving between the same pairs of energy levels, in opposite directions
    • All elements absorb the same wavelengths
    • The light changes wavelength when absorbed
    Show the answer

    Answer: Both involve electrons moving between the same pairs of energy levels, in opposite directions. The energy gap between two levels is the same whether the electron moves up or down.

Key terms

Emission spectrum
Bright lines at the wavelengths emitted by excited atoms
Absorption spectrum
Dark lines in a continuous spectrum, where atoms have absorbed particular wavelengths
Convergence limit
The wavelength at which the lines of a spectral series merge, corresponding to ionisation
