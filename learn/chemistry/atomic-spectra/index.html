Atomic Spectra

Line spectra arise because electrons have only certain energies; each emitted photon's energy equals the gap between two levels.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why atoms produce line spectra rather than continuous spectra
  • Use E = hf and c = fλ to relate energy, frequency and wavelength
  • Describe the series of lines in the hydrogen spectrum

Light and energy

Light behaves as a stream of photons, each with energy E = hf, where h is the Planck constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and f the frequency. Since c = fλ (c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹), E = hc/λ: shorter wavelength means more energetic photons.

E = hf = hc / λ
Worked example

Calculate the energy of a photon of red light with wavelength 656 nm.

  1. E = hc / λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸) ÷ (656 × 10⁻⁹).
  2. E = 1.987 × 10⁻²⁵ ÷ 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Why lines

Electrons in an atom can only have certain energies. When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, it emits a photon whose energy exactly equals the difference, ΔE = hf. Because only certain differences are possible, only certain wavelengths appear: a line spectrum. Each element has its own pattern of levels, so its spectrum is a fingerprint.

The hydrogen spectrum

  • Lyman series: transitions down to n = 1, in the ultraviolet.
  • Balmer series: transitions down to n = 2, in the visible: 656 nm (red, from n = 3), 486 nm (blue-green, n = 4), 434 nm and 410 nm (violet).
  • Paschen series: transitions down to n = 3, in the infrared.

In each series the lines get closer together at higher energy and converge to a limit, because the energy levels themselves crowd together as n increases. Niels Bohr's 1913 model of hydrogen reproduced these wavelengths, the first success of quantum ideas in chemistry.

Common mistakeThinking the lines come from electrons moving up. Emission lines come from electrons falling to lower levels; absorption lines from electrons moving up.

In 3 lines: E = hf = hc/λ for each photon. Line spectra show electrons occupy fixed energy levels. Hydrogen's Lyman, Balmer and Paschen series end on n = 1, 2 and 3.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What happens when an electron moves from a higher to a lower energy level?
    • A photon is emitted with energy equal to the difference between the levels
    • A photon is absorbed
    • The atom gains a proton
    • The electron leaves the atom
    Show the answer

    Answer: A photon is emitted with energy equal to the difference between the levels. Dropping to a lower level releases the energy difference as one photon.

  2. Which transition in hydrogen produces the red 656 nm Balmer line?
    • n = 2 to n = 1
    • n = 3 to n = 2
    • n = 4 to n = 3
    • n = 1 to n = 2
    Show the answer

    Answer: n = 3 to n = 2. Balmer lines end on n = 2; the lowest-energy, longest-wavelength one starts from n = 3.

  3. Why do the lines in each hydrogen series get closer together towards the short-wavelength end?
    • Electrons move faster in higher levels
    • The nucleus emits the extra light
    • The energy levels get closer together as n increases, so the differences between them converge
    • Higher levels hold more electrons
    Show the answer

    Answer: The energy levels get closer together as n increases, so the differences between them converge. Because the energy levels converge towards the ionisation limit, transitions from successive high levels differ less and less in energy.

Key terms

Photon
A packet of light energy, E = hf
Line spectrum
A spectrum of separate lines at particular wavelengths, characteristic of an element
Planck constant
h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, relating a photon's energy to its frequency
