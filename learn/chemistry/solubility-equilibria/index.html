Solubility Equilibria

Sparingly soluble salts form equilibria between solid and ions in saturated solutions; solubility rules predict precipitates, which form when ion concentrations exceed solubility, with uses in analysis and water treatment.

Chemistry · Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Write equilibrium equations for dissolving sparingly soluble salts
  • Describe general solubility rules for ionic compounds
  • Explain precipitation reactions

Equilibria

  • A saturated solution of a sparingly soluble salt is in equilibrium: AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
  • Ions dissolve and precipitate at equal rates.
  • The solid is in contact with the solution.
  • Solubility is often very small, such as 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

Solubility rules and precipitation

  • Nitrates and most sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble.
  • Most chlorides are soluble, except silver and lead.
  • Most carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble, except group 1.
  • Mixing solutions forms a precipitate if ion concentrations exceed solubility.

Precipitation reactions identify ions and remove pollutants from water. Kidney stones form when calcium oxalate precipitates. Solubility also controls the formation of limestone caves and stalactites.

Worked example

Forming a precipitate.

  1. Silver nitrate solution is added to sodium chloride solution.
  2. Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ exceed AgCl's solubility.
  3. A white precipitate forms: Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s).
  4. The solution reaches equilibrium with the solid.
Common mistakeThinking insoluble salts do not dissolve at all. They dissolve very slightly, establishing an equilibrium.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which salt is insoluble in water?
    • Silver chloride
    • Sodium nitrate
    • Potassium chloride
    • Ammonium sulfate
    Show the answer

    Answer: Silver chloride. Silver chloride is a white insoluble precipitate.

  2. When does a precipitate form on mixing two solutions?
    • Whenever any two solutions mix
    • When ion concentrations exceed the salt's solubility
    • Only when heated
    • Only with group 1 salts
    Show the answer

    Answer: When ion concentrations exceed the salt's solubility. Excess ions combine into solid until equilibrium.

  3. Why is a saturated solution of AgCl described as dynamic?
    • Nothing happens once saturated
    • AgCl stops dissolving forever
    • Ions dissolve and precipitate at equal rates
    • The solid disappears
    Show the answer

    Answer: Ions dissolve and precipitate at equal rates. Equilibrium involves continuous exchange.

Key terms

Sparingly soluble
Dissolving only to a very small extent
Precipitate
An insoluble solid formed from solution
Saturated solution
A solution in equilibrium with undissolved solute
