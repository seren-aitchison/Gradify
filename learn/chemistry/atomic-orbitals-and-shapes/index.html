Atomic Orbitals and Shapes

s orbitals are spherical, p orbitals two-lobed along an axis, and most d orbitals four-lobed; an orbital has l angular and n − l − 1 radial nodes.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the shapes of s, p and d orbitals
  • Explain what nodes are and count them for a given orbital
  • Relate orbital shape and size to n and l

The shapes

  • s orbitals are spherical. A 2s orbital is larger than a 1s, and a 3s larger again.
  • p orbitals have two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, with a plane of zero probability (a nodal plane) between them. The three p orbitals point along the x, y and z axes: pₓ, p_y and p_z.
  • d orbitals: four of the five have four lobes in a cloverleaf pattern; the fifth, d_z², has two lobes along the z axis and a ring around the middle.

Nodes

A node is a region where the probability of finding the electron is zero. An orbital has n − 1 nodes in total: l angular nodes (planes or cones through the nucleus) and n − l − 1 radial nodes (spheres). 1s has none; 2s has one radial node; 2p has one angular node (the nodal plane); 3p has one of each.

Worked example

How many radial and angular nodes does a 3d orbital have?

  1. Angular nodes = l = 2. Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = 3 − 2 − 1 = 0.
  2. Total = 2 = n − 1, as expected.

Why shape matters

Bonds form where orbitals on neighbouring atoms overlap. The directional lobes of p orbitals explain why many molecules have definite shapes and angles, and the lobes of d orbitals explain the colours and magnetism of transition metal complexes, because the lobes point either towards or between surrounding ligands.

Orbitals with larger n are bigger and their electrons are, on average, further from the nucleus, which is why atomic radius increases down a group.

Common mistakeThinking a p orbital's two lobes hold one electron each. The orbital is one wavefunction: one or two electrons occupy the whole two-lobed shape.

In 3 lines: s is spherical; p has two lobes along x, y or z; d mostly four lobes. Angular nodes = l; radial nodes = n − l − 1; total n − 1. Orbital shapes direct bonding and molecular shape.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How many lobes does a p orbital have?
    • Two
    • One
    • Four
    • Six
    Show the answer

    Answer: Two. A p orbital has two lobes on either side of the nucleus, separated by a nodal plane.

  2. How many angular nodes does any p orbital have?
    • Zero
    • One
    • Two
    • Three
    Show the answer

    Answer: One. The number of angular nodes equals l, which is 1 for p orbitals.

  3. Why is there zero probability of finding a 2p electron at the nucleus, while a 1s electron's probability density is highest there?
    • 2p electrons move too fast
    • The nucleus repels p electrons
    • The 2p orbital has a nodal plane through the nucleus; s orbitals have no angular node
    • 1s electrons are inside the nucleus
    Show the answer

    Answer: The 2p orbital has a nodal plane through the nucleus; s orbitals have no angular node. The angular node of a p orbital passes through the nucleus, while spherical s orbitals have their maximum density at the centre.

Key terms

Node
A region in an orbital where the probability of finding the electron is zero
Nodal plane
A flat angular node through the nucleus, as in a p orbital
Radial node
A spherical node at a fixed distance from the nucleus
