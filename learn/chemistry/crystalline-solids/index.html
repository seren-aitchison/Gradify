Crystalline Solids

Crystalline solids have long-range order, sharp melting points and characteristic faces; they can be anisotropic and cleave along planes, and defects such as vacancies and dislocations strongly affect their properties.

Chemistry · States of Matter and Physical Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the features of crystalline solids
  • Explain anisotropy and cleavage in crystals
  • Describe crystal defects and their effects

Features

  • Particles arranged in a regular, repeating lattice with long-range order.
  • Sharp melting points, because all bonds are equivalent.
  • Flat faces and characteristic angles.
  • Can be ionic, molecular, covalent network or metallic.

Directions and defects

  • Anisotropy: some properties vary with direction, such as graphite conducting along layers.
  • Cleavage: crystals split along planes of weakness.
  • Point defects: vacancies or extra atoms.
  • Dislocations: misaligned rows that let metals deform.

Defects matter: doping silicon introduces controlled point defects, and dislocations explain why metals bend rather than shatter. Large single crystals of silicon are grown for computer chips. Gemstones are prized for their crystal faces and optical properties.

Worked example

Cleaving calcite.

  1. A calcite crystal is tapped along a particular direction.
  2. It splits cleanly into smaller rhombohedral pieces.
  3. The cleavage follows planes of weaker bonding in the lattice.
  4. The angles of the faces stay the same.
Common mistakeThinking crystals are always perfect. Real crystals contain defects that affect their properties.

In 3 lines: Long-range order. Anisotropy and cleavage. Defects matter.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is an example of anisotropy?
    • Glass softening over a range
    • Water flowing in any direction
    • Graphite conducting better along its layers
    • Salt dissolving in water
    Show the answer

    Answer: Graphite conducting better along its layers. Graphite's properties differ along and across layers.

  2. Why do metals bend instead of shattering?
    • Metals contain no defects at all
    • Metal bonds are directional
    • Metals are amorphous
    • Dislocations let rows of atoms slip
    Show the answer

    Answer: Dislocations let rows of atoms slip. Moving dislocations allow plastic deformation.

  3. Why are controlled point defects useful in silicon?
    • Doping atoms change its electrical conductivity
    • They make the silicon melt at lower temperatures
    • They remove all electrons
    • They make silicon amorphous
    Show the answer

    Answer: Doping atoms change its electrical conductivity. Dopants create the charge carriers used in chips.

Key terms

Anisotropy
Variation of properties with direction
Cleavage
Splitting along planes of weakness
Dislocation
A line defect in a crystal
