Carbonyl Chemistry

Carbonyl compounds react with nucleophiles at the δ+ carbon; aldehydes and ketones undergo addition, while acid derivatives undergo addition-elimination through a tetrahedral intermediate, with reactivity acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide > carboxylate.

Chemistry · Functional Group Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Compare reactivity of carbonyl compounds
  • Distinguish nucleophilic addition from addition-elimination
  • Explain how substituents affect carbonyl reactivity

Two pathways

  • Aldehydes and ketones: nucleophilic addition (no leaving group).
  • Acid derivatives: nucleophilic addition-elimination (a leaving group departs).
  • Both start with attack on the δ+ carbonyl carbon.
  • A tetrahedral intermediate forms in both.

Reactivity order

  • Acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide > carboxylate.
  • Better leaving groups and less electron donation increase reactivity.
  • Derivatives can be converted down the order easily, not up.
  • Amides and carboxylates are stabilised by delocalisation.

This reactivity order explains why acyl chlorides make esters and amides readily, while amides need harsh conditions to hydrolyse. Biological acyl transfer uses thioesters, which are more reactive than esters, as in acetyl-CoA.

Worked example

Converting down the ladder.

  1. Ethanoyl chloride + ethanol → ethyl ethanoate (easy).
  2. Ethyl ethanoate + ammonia → ethanamide (possible).
  3. Ethanamide → ethanoyl chloride directly (not feasible).
  4. Going up the order needs a more reactive reagent.
Common mistakeTreating all carbonyl compounds as reacting the same way. Only acid derivatives have leaving groups.

In 3 lines: Addition or addition-elimination. Reactivity order. Convert downhill.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which carbonyl compound is least reactive towards nucleophiles?
    • An acyl chloride
    • An amide
    • An acid anhydride
    • An ester
    Show the answer

    Answer: An amide. Nitrogen lone pair donation stabilises the amide carbonyl.

  2. Why do ketones undergo addition rather than addition-elimination?
    • They have a chloride leaving group
    • Ketones never react with nucleophiles
    • They have no good leaving group on the carbonyl carbon
    • Ketones always lose an alkyl group
    Show the answer

    Answer: They have no good leaving group on the carbonyl carbon. Alkyl groups are not able to leave as anions.

  3. Why does biology use thioesters such as acetyl-CoA for acyl transfer?
    • Thioesters are completely unreactive
    • Thioesters contain no carbonyl group
    • Esters are more reactive than thioesters
    • Thioesters are more reactive than esters, so transfer is easier
    Show the answer

    Answer: Thioesters are more reactive than esters, so transfer is easier. Poorer orbital overlap with sulfur leaves the carbonyl more electrophilic.

Key terms

Tetrahedral intermediate
The sp³ intermediate after nucleophilic attack on C=O
Acyl transfer
Moving an acyl group from one nucleophile to another
Thioester
An ester of a thiol, RCOSR′
