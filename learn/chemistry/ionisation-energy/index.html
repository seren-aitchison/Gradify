Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy depends on nuclear charge, distance and shielding; jumps in successive ionisation energies reveal how many electrons are in each shell.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define first and successive ionisation energies and write equations for them
  • Explain the factors that affect ionisation energy
  • Use successive ionisation energies to deduce an element's group

Definitions

The first ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions. For sodium: Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻, 496 kJ mol⁻¹. The second ionisation energy removes an electron from each 1+ ion: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Ionisation is always endothermic.

What affects it

  • Nuclear charge: more protons attract the electron more strongly, raising ionisation energy.
  • Distance from the nucleus: an electron in a higher shell is further away and held less strongly.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, reducing the attraction it feels.
  • Subshell and pairing: p electrons are a little easier to remove than s electrons of the same shell; a paired electron is easier to remove than an unpaired one.

Successive ionisation energies

Each successive ionisation energy is larger, because the electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. A very large jump shows that the next electron comes from a new shell, closer to the nucleus. Magnesium's first three are 738, 1451 and 7733 kJ mol⁻¹: the jump after the second means magnesium has two outer electrons and is in group 2.

Worked example

An element's first four ionisation energies are 578, 1817, 2745 and 11 577 kJ mol⁻¹. Which group is it in?

  1. The big jump is between the third (2745) and fourth (11 577).
  2. So it has three outer electrons: group 13 (it is aluminium).

Trends

First ionisation energy generally rises across a period (more protons, similar shielding) and falls down a group (more shells, more shielding). Small dips occur where a new subshell starts (boron 801 below beryllium 899, because a 2p electron is higher in energy than a 2s) and where pairing begins in a p subshell (oxygen 1314 below nitrogen 1402).

Common mistakeWriting ionisation equations without state symbols, or for solids. Ionisation energies are defined for gaseous atoms or ions: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.

In 3 lines: Ionisation energies are endothermic and defined for gaseous species. Nuclear charge raises them; distance and shielding lower them. Big jumps in successive ionisation energies mark a new shell and give the group.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which element has the highest first ionisation energy?
    • Hydrogen
    • Lithium
    • Helium
    • Sodium
    Show the answer

    Answer: Helium. Helium (2372 kJ mol⁻¹) has two protons and its electrons are in the first shell with no shielding.

  2. An element has first ionisation energies of 496 and 4562 kJ mol⁻¹. Which group is it in?
    • Group 2
    • Group 13
    • Group 17
    • Group 1
    Show the answer

    Answer: Group 1. The huge jump after the first means it has only one outer electron.

  3. Why is boron's first ionisation energy (801 kJ mol⁻¹) lower than beryllium's (899 kJ mol⁻¹), though boron has more protons?
    • Boron's outer electron is in a 2p orbital, higher in energy and more shielded than beryllium's 2s electrons
    • Boron's electron is in the third shell
    • Beryllium has a larger nuclear charge
    • Boron's electrons are all paired
    Show the answer

    Answer: Boron's outer electron is in a 2p orbital, higher in energy and more shielded than beryllium's 2s electrons. The 2p electron is slightly further out and shielded by the 2s electrons, so it is easier to remove.

Key terms

First ionisation energy
The energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions
Successive ionisation energies
The energies to remove each further electron in turn from an atom
Shielding
The reduction in attraction between the nucleus and an outer electron caused by repulsion from inner electrons
