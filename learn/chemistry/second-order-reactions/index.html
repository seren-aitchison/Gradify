Second-order Reactions

Second-order reactions have rate = k[A]² or k[A][B], with k in dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹; half-life increases as concentration falls, 1/[A] against time is linear, and excess of one reactant gives pseudo-first-order behaviour.

Chemistry · Chemical Kinetics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the rate equations for second-order reactions
  • Interpret graphs for second-order reactions
  • Explain how half-life changes for second-order reactions

Second order

  • Rate = k[A]² or rate = k[A][B].
  • Doubling [A] in rate = k[A]² quadruples the rate.
  • Units of k: dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.
  • Rate-concentration graph: a curve; rate against [A]² is a straight line.

Half-life and examples

  • Half-life increases as concentration falls: t½ = 1 ÷ (k[A]₀).
  • Each successive half-life is double the previous one.
  • Example: 2NO₂ → 2NO + O₂.
  • Hydrolysis of primary halogenoalkanes by OH⁻: rate = k[RX][OH⁻].

Plotting 1/[A] against time gives a straight line for second-order reactions, which is one way to confirm the order. When one reactant is in large excess, a second-order reaction can appear first order, called pseudo-first-order.

Worked example

Increasing half-lives.

  1. A second-order reaction starts at 0.80 mol dm⁻³.
  2. First half-life: 10 s to reach 0.40.
  3. Second half-life: 20 s to reach 0.20.
  4. Third half-life: 40 s; each is double the last.
Common mistakeApplying t½ = 0.693 ÷ k to second-order reactions. That formula applies only to first order.

In 3 lines: Rate = k[A]². Half-life lengthens. 1/[A] is linear.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Rate = k[A]². If [A] is halved, what happens to the rate?
    • It halves
    • It doubles
    • It falls to a quarter
    • It stays the same
    Show the answer

    Answer: It falls to a quarter. (½)² = ¼, so the rate drops to one quarter.

  2. How does the half-life change during a second-order reaction?
    • It stays constant
    • It decreases as concentration falls
    • It is always zero
    • It increases as concentration falls
    Show the answer

    Answer: It increases as concentration falls. t½ = 1 ÷ k[A]₀, so lower concentration gives longer half-life.

  3. Why does a large excess of one reactant make a second-order reaction look first order?
    • Its concentration barely changes, so it is absorbed into k
    • It stops reacting entirely
    • It becomes a catalyst
    • It doubles the activation energy
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its concentration barely changes, so it is absorbed into k. A constant [B] makes rate depend only on [A].

Key terms

Second order
Rate depends on concentration squared or two concentrations
Pseudo-first-order
Apparent first order because one reactant is in excess
Integrated rate law
An equation relating concentration to time
