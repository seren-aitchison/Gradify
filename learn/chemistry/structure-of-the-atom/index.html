Structure of the Atom

An atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus holding nearly all its mass, surrounded by electrons in a space about 100 000 times wider; chemistry happens in the electrons.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the nuclear model of the atom and its scale
  • Explain how the gold foil experiment revealed the nucleus
  • Explain what holds the nucleus together despite the repulsion between protons

The nuclear atom

Almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus, made of protons and neutrons, about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across. The electrons occupy a region about 10⁻¹⁰ m across (0.1 nm). The atom is therefore mostly empty space: if the nucleus were a marble, the atom would be the size of a football stadium.

The gold foil experiment

In 1909 Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working with Ernest Rutherford, fired alpha particles at gold foil a few hundred atoms thick. Most passed straight through, but about 1 in 8000 bounced back at large angles. Thomson's "plum pudding" model, with positive charge spread through the atom, couldn't explain this. In 1911 Rutherford proposed that the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus: only alpha particles passing very close to a nucleus are strongly deflected.

Holding the nucleus together

Protons repel each other electrically, very strongly at nuclear distances. The nucleus holds together because of the strong nuclear force, which attracts protons and neutrons to each other but acts only over about 10⁻¹⁵ m. Neutrons add attraction without adding repulsion, which is why heavier nuclei need more neutrons than protons to be stable.

Electrons are held in the atom by their electrical attraction to the nucleus. Chemistry, the making and breaking of bonds, involves only the electrons, which is why an element's chemistry is set by its electron arrangement while its nucleus stays unchanged in reactions.

Investigate with the lab. Build carbon by adding six protons, six neutrons and six electrons. Then add a neutron and see what changes and what doesn't.

Common mistakePicturing electrons as tiny planets in fixed orbits close to the nucleus. Electrons occupy a large region around it, described by probabilities, as later lessons explain.

In 3 lines: The nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹⁴ m across; the atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m. Large-angle alpha scattering revealed a small, dense, positive nucleus. The strong nuclear force holds nucleons together; chemistry involves only the electrons.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What did the large-angle deflection of a few alpha particles show?
    • Electrons are in fixed orbits
    • Atoms are solid spheres
    • Positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus
    • Neutrons exist
    Show the answer

    Answer: Positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Only a small, dense, positive centre could turn back a fast alpha particle; most of the atom must be empty.

  2. Why do heavy stable nuclei such as lead-208 have more neutrons (126) than protons (82)?
    • Neutrons are needed to attract electrons
    • Protons are heavier than neutrons
    • Neutrons repel protons
    • Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrical repulsion
    Show the answer

    Answer: Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrical repulsion. As nuclei get bigger, proton–proton repulsion grows; additional neutrons provide binding without charge.

  3. If Thomson's plum pudding model had been correct, what would Geiger and Marsden have seen?
    • Almost all alpha particles passing through with only small deflections
    • Many particles bouncing straight back
    • No particles passing through
    • Particles absorbed by electrons
    Show the answer

    Answer: Almost all alpha particles passing through with only small deflections. Spread-out positive charge produces only weak forces, so no large deflections; the backscattering was the surprise.

  4. What determines which element an atom is?
    • The number of neutrons
    • The number of protons
    • The number of electrons
    • The total of protons and neutrons
    Show the answer

    Answer: The number of protons. The atomic number, the proton count, defines the element.

Key terms

Nucleus
The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons
Strong nuclear force
The short-range attraction between nucleons that holds the nucleus together
Nucleon
A proton or a neutron
