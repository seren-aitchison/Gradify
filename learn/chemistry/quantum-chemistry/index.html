Quantum Chemistry

Quantum chemistry describes electrons as wavefunctions with quantised energy; the Born-Oppenheimer approximation separates electronic and nuclear motion and methods such as Hartree-Fock and DFT approximate many-electron systems.

Chemistry · Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why quantum mechanics is needed in chemistry
  • Describe the Born-Oppenheimer approximation
  • Describe the main computational approaches

Key ideas

  • Electrons behave as waves, described by wavefunctions.
  • Energy is quantised.
  • |ψ|² gives the probability density of finding an electron.
  • Classical physics cannot explain atomic stability or spectra.

Approximations

  • Born-Oppenheimer: nuclei are much heavier and slower than electrons, so they are treated as fixed.
  • Hartree-Fock: each electron moves in the average field of the others.
  • Density functional theory (DFT): uses electron density instead of the full wavefunction.
  • Higher-level methods include electron correlation more fully.

Exact solutions exist only for one-electron systems such as the hydrogen atom, so approximations are essential. DFT, recognised by the 1998 Nobel Prize, balances accuracy and cost and is used for most molecular calculations today.

Worked example

Why Born-Oppenheimer works.

  1. A proton is about 1,836 times heavier than an electron.
  2. Electrons adjust almost instantly to nuclear positions.
  3. Electronic energy is calculated for fixed nuclei.
  4. This gives a potential energy surface for nuclear motion.

The main computational approaches

  • Ab initio methods start from the Schrödinger equation with no fitted data: Hartree-Fock, then more accurate methods such as Møller-Plesset perturbation theory and coupled cluster, which add electron correlation at a much higher computational cost.
  • Density functional theory works from the electron density with an approximate exchange-correlation functional: the usual choice for molecules of tens to hundreds of atoms.
  • Semi-empirical methods simplify the equations using parameters fitted to experiment: fast enough for large molecules, but less reliable outside the data they were fitted to.
  • Molecular mechanics leaves out electrons and treats atoms as balls joined by springs (a force field): it handles proteins and materials with many thousands of atoms but cannot describe bonds breaking or forming.
  • Choosing between these approaches is a trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, which rises steeply with the number of electrons.
Common mistakeThinking quantum chemistry solves molecules exactly. Only one-electron systems have exact solutions.

In 3 lines: Electrons as waves. Born-Oppenheimer. Hartree-Fock and DFT.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which method uses electron density rather than the full wavefunction?
    • Hartree-Fock theory
    • Classical mechanics
    • Molecular mechanics
    • Density functional theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Density functional theory. DFT builds energy from the electron density.

  2. Why can the Born-Oppenheimer approximation be used?
    • Nuclei are much heavier and slower than electrons
    • Electrons are heavier than nuclei
    • Nuclei have no mass
    • Electrons do not move
    Show the answer

    Answer: Nuclei are much heavier and slower than electrons. Electrons adjust almost instantly to nuclear positions.

  3. Why are approximations essential in quantum chemistry?
    • All molecules can be solved exactly by hand
    • Exact solutions exist only for one-electron systems
    • Electrons do not interact
    • Computers cannot do arithmetic
    Show the answer

    Answer: Exact solutions exist only for one-electron systems. Electron-electron repulsion makes exact solution impossible.

Key terms

Wavefunction
A mathematical function describing a quantum system
Born-Oppenheimer approximation
Treating nuclei as fixed while solving for electrons
Density functional theory
A method based on electron density
