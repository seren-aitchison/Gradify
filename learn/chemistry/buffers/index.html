Buffers

Buffers contain a weak acid and its conjugate base or a weak base and its conjugate acid; added H⁺ or OH⁻ is removed by one component so the ratio and pH change little, within a limited capacity, as in the blood's carbonic acid system.

Chemistry · Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define a buffer solution
  • Explain how acidic and basic buffers resist pH change
  • Describe biological and industrial uses of buffers

Composition

  • An acidic buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, such as ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate.
  • A basic buffer contains a weak base and its conjugate acid, such as ammonia and ammonium chloride.
  • Both components must be present in reasonable amounts.
  • Buffers work best when pH is near the pKa.

How they work

  • Added H⁺ reacts with the conjugate base: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA.
  • Added OH⁻ reacts with the weak acid: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O.
  • The ratio [A⁻]/[HA] changes only slightly, so pH changes little.
  • Buffers have a limited capacity.

Blood is buffered mainly by the carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate system, keeping pH at 7.35–7.45. Buffers are used in shampoos, food, fermentation and calibrating pH meters. Adding too much acid or base exhausts a buffer.

Worked example

The blood buffer.

  1. H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
  2. Extra acid from exercise reacts with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃.
  3. The lungs remove extra CO₂ derived from H₂CO₃.
  4. Blood pH stays within its narrow range.
Common mistakeThinking buffers keep pH perfectly constant. They resist change but pH still shifts slightly.

In 3 lines: Weak acid plus conjugate base. Removes added H⁺ and OH⁻. Blood and industry.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which pair forms an acidic buffer?
    • Hydrochloric acid and sodium chloride
    • Ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate
    • Sodium hydroxide and water
    • Nitric acid and nitrate
    Show the answer

    Answer: Ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate. A weak acid with its conjugate base resists pH changes.

  2. In an ethanoate buffer, what removes added OH⁻?
    • Ethanoate ions
    • Sodium ions
    • Ethanoic acid molecules
    • Water molecules only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Ethanoic acid molecules. HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O removes added hydroxide.

  3. Why does a buffer's pH change only slightly when acid is added?
    • All added H⁺ disappears without reacting
    • Buffers contain no H⁺
    • Water absorbs the acid completely
    • The ratio of conjugate base to acid changes only a little
    Show the answer

    Answer: The ratio of conjugate base to acid changes only a little. Large reserves of both components keep the ratio stable.

Key terms

Buffer
A solution resisting changes in pH
Buffer capacity
The amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb
Conjugate base
The species formed when an acid loses a proton
