Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces, including London forces, dipole-dipole forces and hydrogen bonds, act between molecules and are much weaker than covalent bonds; stronger forces raise boiling points and affect viscosity, surface tension and solubility.

Chemistry · Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish intermolecular forces from covalent bonds
  • Name the main types of intermolecular force
  • Relate intermolecular forces to boiling points and solubility

Types

  • London dispersion forces: between all molecules, from temporary dipoles.
  • Permanent dipole-dipole forces: between polar molecules.
  • Hydrogen bonds: between H on N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F.
  • Intermolecular forces are much weaker than covalent bonds.

Effects

  • Stronger intermolecular forces give higher melting and boiling points.
  • They affect viscosity, surface tension and vapour pressure.
  • Substances dissolve when solute-solvent forces are similar to those they replace.
  • Boiling separates molecules but does not break covalent bonds.

Intermolecular forces explain why water is a liquid at room temperature while methane is a gas, and why oil and water do not mix. The strength order is usually hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > London forces for molecules of similar size, but large molecules can have very strong London forces.

Worked example

Comparing boiling points.

  1. Methane (CH₄) boils at −162 °C: only London forces.
  2. Hydrogen chloride (HCl) boils at −85 °C: London and dipole-dipole forces.
  3. Water (H₂O) boils at 100 °C: hydrogen bonding as well.
  4. Stronger intermolecular forces raise the boiling point.
Common mistakeThinking boiling breaks covalent bonds. Boiling overcomes intermolecular forces between molecules.

In 3 lines: Three main types. Weaker than bonds. Control physical properties.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which force acts between all molecules?
    • Hydrogen bonds
    • Dipole-dipole forces
    • London dispersion forces
    • Ionic bonds
    Show the answer

    Answer: London dispersion forces. Temporary dipoles arise in every molecule with electrons.

  2. Why does water boil at a much higher temperature than methane?
    • Water has stronger covalent bonds
    • Methane is heavier than water
    • Methane forms hydrogen bonds
    • Water molecules form hydrogen bonds
    Show the answer

    Answer: Water molecules form hydrogen bonds. Extra energy is needed to overcome hydrogen bonding between water molecules.

  3. Why don't oil and water mix?
    • Water's hydrogen bonds are stronger than attractions to oil molecules
    • Oil molecules are ionic and repel water molecules
    • Water is non-polar
    • Oil forms hydrogen bonds with water
    Show the answer

    Answer: Water's hydrogen bonds are stronger than attractions to oil molecules. Mixing would break strong water-water attractions without replacing them.

Key terms

Intermolecular force
An attraction between molecules
London dispersion force
An attraction from temporary dipoles
Hydrogen bond
A strong dipole attraction involving H and N, O or F
