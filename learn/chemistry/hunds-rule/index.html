Hund's Rule

Electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing, which minimises repulsion.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • State Hund's rule
  • Apply it to draw orbital diagrams for p and d subshells
  • Explain the rule in terms of electron repulsion and its effect on ionisation energies

The rule

For degenerate orbitals, such as the three 2p orbitals, electrons occupy separate orbitals singly, with parallel spins, before any orbital gets a second electron. Carbon's two 2p electrons go into two different 2p orbitals, both spin-up; nitrogen's three go one in each. Only at oxygen does a 2p orbital receive a pair.

Why it happens

Electrons repel each other. Two electrons in separate orbitals are, on average, further apart than two in the same orbital, so the repulsion is smaller. Parallel spins reduce the repulsion further, a quantum effect called exchange energy. Both lower the energy of the atom.

Consequences

Hund's rule explains a dip in first ionisation energies: oxygen's (1314 kJ mol⁻¹) is lower than nitrogen's (1402 kJ mol⁻¹), although oxygen has a larger nuclear charge. Oxygen's fourth 2p electron is paired, and the extra repulsion makes it easier to remove. Half-filled subshells, such as nitrogen's 2p³ and manganese's 3d⁵, are especially stable.

Worked example

Draw the orbital diagram for the 3d electrons of Mn²⁺ ([Ar] 3d⁵).

  1. Five 3d boxes, five electrons: one in each box, all with parallel spin.
  2. Mn²⁺ therefore has five unpaired electrons and is strongly paramagnetic.

Investigate with the lab. Fill the 2p boxes for nitrogen and oxygen, and see how Hund's rule spreads the electrons out before pairing.

Common mistakePairing electrons in the first p orbital before the others are filled. In the ground state, each of the three p orbitals receives one electron first.

In 3 lines: In degenerate orbitals, electrons go in singly with parallel spins first. Fewer paired electrons means less repulsion and lower energy. Pairing explains why oxygen's first ionisation energy is lower than nitrogen's.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How many unpaired electrons are in a ground-state carbon atom?
    • 0
    • 1
    • 4
    • 2
    Show the answer

    Answer: 2. Carbon's two 2p electrons occupy two different 2p orbitals with parallel spins.

  2. Which correctly shows the 2p electrons of nitrogen in the ground state?
    • One electron in each 2p orbital, all with parallel spins
    • Two in the first orbital and one in the second
    • All three in one orbital
    • One spin-up and two spin-down in separate orbitals
    Show the answer

    Answer: One electron in each 2p orbital, all with parallel spins. Hund's rule: singly occupied, parallel spins.

  3. Why is the first ionisation energy of oxygen lower than that of nitrogen?
    • Oxygen has fewer protons
    • Oxygen's outermost electron is paired in a 2p orbital, and the extra repulsion makes it easier to remove
    • Oxygen's electron is in a higher shell
    • Nitrogen's electrons are all paired
    Show the answer

    Answer: Oxygen's outermost electron is paired in a 2p orbital, and the extra repulsion makes it easier to remove. The pairing in oxygen's 2p⁴ raises the energy of that electron, outweighing the slightly higher nuclear charge.

  4. How many unpaired electrons does a nitrogen atom have?
    • One
    • Two
    • Three
    • Five
    Show the answer

    Answer: Three. Nitrogen's three 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins.

Key terms

Hund's rule
Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing
Parallel spins
Electrons with the same spin quantum number, shown as arrows pointing the same way
Exchange energy
The lowering of energy when electrons with parallel spins occupy degenerate orbitals
