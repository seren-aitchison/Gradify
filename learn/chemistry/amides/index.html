Amides

Amides are primary, secondary or tertiary, are made from acyl chlorides or anhydrides with ammonia or amines and have a planar, delocalised C–N bond that makes them non-basic, hydrogen-bonding and hard to hydrolyse, which matters in proteins and polyamides.

Chemistry · Functional Group Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Classify amides as primary, secondary or tertiary
  • Explain the stability of the amide bond
  • Describe the importance of amides in biology and materials

Structure

  • Primary amides: RCONH₂, such as ethanamide.
  • Secondary (N-substituted): RCONHR′.
  • Tertiary: RCONR′₂.
  • Amides are made from acyl chlorides or anhydrides with ammonia or amines.

Properties

  • The N lone pair is delocalised into C=O, so the C–N bond has partial double-bond character.
  • Amides are planar at the C–N bond and are not basic.
  • Primary and secondary amides form strong hydrogen bonds: high melting points.
  • They hydrolyse only slowly.

Peptide bonds linking amino acids in proteins are amides; their planarity shapes protein structures such as α-helices. Nylon and Kevlar are polyamides whose strength comes from hydrogen bonds between chains. Paracetamol contains an amide group.

Worked example

Why amides are not basic.

  1. In amines, the N lone pair is free to accept H⁺.
  2. In amides, the lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl.
  3. It is much less available to accept a proton.
  4. So ethanamide is neutral while ethylamine is basic.
Common mistakeThinking amides are basic like amines. Delocalisation removes the nitrogen's basicity.

In 3 lines: Primary, secondary, tertiary. Planar and non-basic. Proteins and nylon.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which compound is a secondary amide?
    • Ethanamide, CH₃CONH₂
    • N-methylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₃
    • Ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂
    • N,N-dimethylethanamide, CH₃CON(CH₃)₂
    Show the answer

    Answer: N-methylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₃. One alkyl group on nitrogen makes it secondary.

  2. Why are amides much less basic than amines?
    • Amides contain no nitrogen atom at all
    • Amides carry a permanent positive charge
    • The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl
    • Amines have no lone pairs on nitrogen
    Show the answer

    Answer: The nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl. A delocalised lone pair cannot easily accept H⁺.

  3. Why is Kevlar so strong?
    • It contains only weak London forces between chains
    • It has no amide bonds anywhere in its chains
    • It is a metal alloy reinforced with carbon
    • Rigid aromatic chains line up and hydrogen bond strongly between amide groups
    Show the answer

    Answer: Rigid aromatic chains line up and hydrogen bond strongly between amide groups. Ordered hydrogen-bonded chains resist pulling apart.

Key terms

Amide
A compound containing –CONH– or –CONH₂
Peptide bond
The amide link between amino acids
Polyamide
A polymer with amide links
