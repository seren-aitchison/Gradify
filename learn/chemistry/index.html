Chemistry explained

Foundations of Chemistry

  • Matter and Its Classification All matter can be classified as a pure substance (an element or a compound) or a mixture.
  • Elements and Compounds There are 118 known elements, but millions of compounds.
  • Mixtures Mixtures can be separated by physical methods that exploit differences in the properties of their parts: particle size, solubility, boiling point or how strongly they stick to a surface.
  • Physical and Chemical Changes In a physical change the same substance remains, perhaps in a different state or shape; in a chemical change new substances form.
  • States of Matter Substances exist as solids, liquids or gases depending on the balance between the energy of their particles and the forces between them.
  • Chemical Symbols Each element has a one- or two-letter symbol, agreed internationally by IUPAC.
  • Chemical Formulae A chemical formula shows which elements are in a substance and in what ratio.
  • Chemical Equations A chemical equation summarises a reaction: reactants on the left, products on the right, with formulae, state symbols and the numbers of each particle.
  • Balancing Equations Balancing makes the number of atoms of each element the same on both sides of an equation, reflecting the conservation of mass.
  • Relative Atomic Mass Atoms are far too small to weigh individually in grams, so chemists compare their masses with carbon-12.
  • Relative Molecular Mass The relative molecular (or formula) mass of a substance is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula.
  • The Mole The mole is the chemist's counting unit.
  • Avogadro's Constant The Avogadro constant, 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, connects the atomic scale with the laboratory scale.
  • Limiting Reagents In most reactions one reactant runs out first: the limiting reagent.
  • Atom Economy Atom economy measures how much of the mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.
  • Concentration The concentration of a solution tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume.
  • Dilutions Diluting a solution adds solvent but doesn't change the amount of solute.
  • Significant Figures The number of significant figures in a result shows how precisely it is known.
  • Scientific Notation Chemistry spans numbers from 10²³ particles in a mole to 10⁻¹⁰ m across an atom.
  • Units and Measurements Every measurement in chemistry needs a number, a unit and an uncertainty.

Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

  • Structure of the Atom An atom is a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by a much larger cloud of electrons.
  • Protons, Neutrons and Electrons Atoms are built from three particles with very different masses and charges.
  • Isotopes Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
  • Atomic Number and Mass Number Two numbers identify any nucleus: the atomic number, which fixes the element, and the mass number, which identifies the isotope.
  • Electron Shells Electrons in atoms occupy shells at increasing distances and energies from the nucleus.
  • Atomic Orbitals Within each shell, electrons occupy orbitals: regions where an electron is likely to be found.
  • Quantum Numbers Four quantum numbers describe each electron in an atom: which shell, which type of orbital, which orientation and which spin.
  • Electron Configurations An electron configuration lists the subshells an atom's electrons occupy and how many are in each, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ for sodium.
  • Aufbau Principle The aufbau ("building up") principle says electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first.
  • Pauli Exclusion Principle The Pauli exclusion principle says no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers.
  • Hund's Rule Hund's rule says that when electrons fill a set of orbitals of equal energy, they occupy them singly first, with parallel spins, before pairing up.
  • Ionisation Energy Ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove electrons from atoms.
  • Electron Affinity Electron affinity describes the energy change when atoms gain electrons.
  • Atomic Spectra When atoms are given energy, they emit light at only certain wavelengths, producing a line spectrum unique to each element.
  • Emission and Absorption Atoms emit light at particular wavelengths when excited electrons fall back, and absorb the same wavelengths when electrons are lifted up.
  • Wave-particle Duality Light and electrons both behave sometimes like waves and sometimes like particles.
  • Quantum Mechanical Model The quantum mechanical model treats the electron in an atom as a wave described by the Schrödinger equation.
  • Atomic Orbitals and Shapes Each type of orbital has a characteristic shape: s orbitals are spherical, p orbitals have two lobes, and d orbitals mostly have four.
  • Periodic Trends Atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity change in regular ways across periods and down groups.
  • Electronic Structure and Chemical Properties An element's chemistry follows from its electron structure.

Periodic Table and Periodicity

  • Development of the Periodic Table The periodic table grew from attempts to organise the elements by their properties.
  • Modern Periodic Law The modern periodic table is ordered by atomic number.
  • Periods and Groups The periodic table is arranged in rows called periods and columns called groups.
  • S-block Elements The s-block contains the reactive metals of groups 1 and 2.
  • P-block Elements The p-block contains metals, metalloids and non-metals.
  • D-block Elements The d-block contains the transition elements of groups 3–12.
  • F-block Elements The f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides.
  • Group 1 Group 1, the alkali metals, are soft and very reactive.
  • Group 2 Group 2, the alkaline earth metals, are reactive metals forming 2+ ions.
  • Group 14 Group 14 shows the clearest change from non-metal to metal.
  • Group 15 Group 15 includes nitrogen and phosphorus, essential for life.
  • Group 16 Group 16, the chalcogens, includes oxygen and sulfur.
  • Group 17 Group 17, the halogens, are reactive non-metals.
  • Group 18 Group 18, the noble gases, are very unreactive.
  • Transition Metals Transition metals show characteristic properties linked to their d electrons.
  • Lanthanides The lanthanides are fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium.
  • Actinides The actinides are radioactive elements from actinium to lawrencium.
  • Atomic Radius Atomic radius describes the size of atoms.
  • Ionic Radius Ions differ in size from their atoms.
  • Ionisation Energy Trends Ionisation energies reveal electron structure and follow clear patterns.
  • Electronegativity Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts bonding electrons.
  • Reactivity Trends Reactivity varies predictably across the periodic table.
  • Oxidation States Oxidation states track electrons in compounds.
  • Periodic Relationships The periodic table reveals relationships beyond simple group and period trends.

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

  • Introduction to Chemical Bonding Chemical bonds hold atoms together in elements and compounds.
  • Ionic Bonding Ionic bonds form when electrons transfer from metal to non-metal atoms.
  • Covalent Bonding Covalent bonds form when atoms share pairs of electrons.
  • Coordinate Bonding In a coordinate bond, both shared electrons come from one atom.
  • Metallic Bonding Metallic bonding holds metal atoms together with a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Lewis Structures Lewis structures show how valence electrons are arranged in molecules.
  • Formal Charge Formal charge helps decide which Lewis structure best represents a molecule.
  • Resonance Some molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure.
  • VSEPR Theory VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes from electron pair repulsion.
  • Molecular Geometry Molecular shapes follow from the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  • Molecular Polarity A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel.
  • Electronegativity This lesson applies electronegativity to bonding: predicting bond character, comparing scales and explaining the bonding continuum.
  • Intermolecular Forces Intermolecular forces act between molecules and determine many physical properties.
  • London Dispersion Forces London dispersion forces arise from temporary dipoles in all molecules.
  • Dipole-dipole Interactions Polar molecules attract each other through permanent dipoles.
  • Hydrogen Bonding Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces in many substances.
  • Bond Enthalpy Bond enthalpy measures the energy needed to break a bond.
  • Lattice Enthalpy Lattice enthalpy measures the strength of ionic bonding.
  • Born-Haber Cycles Born-Haber cycles use Hess's law to find lattice enthalpies.
  • Molecular Orbital Theory Molecular orbital theory describes electrons spread over whole molecules.
  • Hybridisation Hybridisation explains bonding geometry by mixing atomic orbitals.
  • Sigma and Pi Bonding Covalent bonds can be sigma or pi bonds, formed by different orbital overlaps.
  • Delocalisation Delocalised electrons spread over several atoms, changing structure and properties.
  • Crystal Structures Solids are built from repeating arrangements of particles.
  • Solid-state Bonding Solids are classified by the bonding between their particles.

States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

  • Gases Gases have properties that differ sharply from liquids and solids.
  • Gas Laws The gas laws describe how pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are related.
  • Ideal Gas Equation The ideal gas equation links pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.
  • Real Gases Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour under some conditions.
  • Kinetic Molecular Theory Kinetic molecular theory explains gas behaviour from the motion of particles.
  • Liquids Liquids have particles close together but free to move.
  • Vapour Pressure Vapour pressure is the pressure of a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid.
  • Boiling and Melting Melting and boiling are changes of state that require energy.
  • Phase Diagrams Phase diagrams show which state a substance is in at any temperature and pressure.
  • Solids Solids have fixed shapes because their particles are held in place.
  • Crystalline Solids Crystalline solids have long-range order that gives them sharp melting points and flat faces.
  • Amorphous Solids Amorphous solids lack long-range order.
  • Solubility Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent.
  • Colligative Properties Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their identity.
  • Osmosis Osmosis is the movement of solvent through a semipermeable membrane.
  • Diffusion Diffusion is the spreading of particles from high to low concentration.
  • Surface Tension Surface tension makes liquid surfaces behave like a stretched skin.

Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

  • Energy in Chemical Systems Chemical reactions involve energy changes between a system and its surroundings.
  • Enthalpy of Formation The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change for making a compound from its elements.
  • Enthalpy of Combustion Enthalpy of combustion measures the energy released when a substance burns completely.
  • Enthalpy of Neutralisation Neutralisation releases energy as acid and alkali react.
  • Hess's Law Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route.
  • Bond Enthalpies This lesson compares enthalpy changes calculated from bond enthalpies with those from Hess's law and explains the differences.
  • Calorimetry Calorimetry measures energy changes by recording temperature changes.
  • Entropy Entropy measures the dispersal of energy and matter in a system.
  • Gibbs Free Energy Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict whether reactions are feasible.
  • Thermodynamic Equilibrium At equilibrium, ΔG is zero and there is no driving force for change.
  • First Law of Thermodynamics The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved.
  • Second Law of Thermodynamics The second law states that the total entropy of the universe increases in spontaneous processes.
  • Chemical Potential Chemical potential describes how Gibbs free energy changes when a substance is added.
  • Temperature Dependence Temperature changes the balance between enthalpy and entropy.
  • Thermodynamic Cycles Thermodynamic cycles combine energy terms to find quantities that cannot be measured directly.

Chemical Kinetics

  • Introduction to Reaction Rates Reaction rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products form.
  • Collision Theory Collision theory explains why reactions happen and what changes their rates.
  • Activation Energy Activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur.
  • Reaction Profiles Reaction profiles show how energy changes as a reaction proceeds.
  • Homogeneous Catalysis Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants.
  • Heterogeneous Catalysis Heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase from the reactants.
  • Enzyme Catalysis Enzymes are biological catalysts with remarkable speed and selectivity.
  • Rate Equations Rate equations show how rate depends on the concentrations of reactants.
  • Reaction Order Reaction orders can be found from initial rates or from graphs.
  • Zero-order Reactions In a zero-order reaction, rate does not depend on concentration.
  • First-order Reactions In a first-order reaction, rate is proportional to concentration.
  • Second-order Reactions In a second-order reaction, rate depends on the square of a concentration or on two concentrations.
  • Half-life Half-life is the time for a concentration to fall to half its value.
  • Integrated Rate Laws Integrated rate laws give concentration as a function of time.
  • Arrhenius Equation The Arrhenius equation links the rate constant to temperature and activation energy.
  • Temperature Effects Temperature has a strong effect on reaction rates.
  • Reaction Mechanisms A reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps by which reactants become products.
  • Rate-determining Steps The slowest step in a mechanism controls the overall rate.
  • Catalytic Cycles Catalytic cycles show how catalysts are used and regenerated in repeating loops.
  • Experimental Kinetics Kinetics experiments require careful planning and analysis.

Chemical Equilibrium

  • Reversible Reactions Many reactions can go in both directions.
  • Dynamic Equilibrium At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.
  • Equilibrium Constants The equilibrium constant expresses the position of equilibrium as a number.
  • Reaction Quotients The reaction quotient Q describes a mixture that may not be at equilibrium.
  • Le Chatelier's Principle Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to change.
  • Temperature and Equilibrium Temperature is the only factor that changes the equilibrium constant.
  • Pressure and Equilibrium Pressure changes affect equilibria involving gases.
  • Equilibrium Calculations Equilibrium calculations find unknown concentrations or constants.
  • Industrial Equilibrium Industry chooses conditions to make equilibrium reactions economical.
  • Thermodynamic Interpretation Thermodynamics explains why equilibrium positions are where they are.

Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

  • Introduction to Acids and Bases Acids and bases are among the most important classes of substance in chemistry.
  • Brønsted-Lowry Theory The Brønsted-Lowry theory defines acids and bases by proton transfer.
  • Lewis Acid-base Theory Lewis theory defines acids and bases by electron pairs, covering even more reactions.
  • Strong Acids and Bases Strong acids and bases ionise completely in water.
  • Weak Acids and Bases Weak acids and bases only partially ionise in water.
  • PH pH measures the acidity of a solution on a logarithmic scale.
  • POH pOH and the ionic product of water connect acidity and basicity.
  • Acid Dissociation Constants The acid dissociation constant Ka measures the strength of a weak acid.
  • Ka and Kb Ka and Kb of a conjugate pair are linked through Kw.
  • Buffers Buffers resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.
  • Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation calculates buffer pH.
  • Acid-base Titrations Titrations measure the concentration of an acid or base accurately.
  • Indicators Indicators change colour over a pH range.
  • Equivalence Points The equivalence point is reached when reactants have reacted in exact stoichiometric amounts.
  • Solubility Equilibria Sparingly soluble salts set up equilibria between solid and dissolved ions.
  • Ksp The solubility product Ksp quantifies the solubility of sparingly soluble salts.
  • Common-ion Effect Adding an ion already present in an equilibrium reduces solubility or ionisation.
  • Complex Ion Equilibria Metal ions form complexes with ligands in equilibrium.
  • Polyprotic Acids Polyprotic acids can donate more than one proton.

Redox and Electrochemistry

  • Oxidation and Reduction Oxidation and reduction always happen together in redox reactions.
  • Oxidation States This lesson uses oxidation states to analyse redox reactions, including disproportionation and complex compounds.
  • Reducing Agents Reducing agents cause reduction by donating electrons.
  • Electrochemical Cells Electrochemical cells interconvert chemical and electrical energy.
  • Galvanic Cells Galvanic cells generate electricity from spontaneous redox reactions.
  • Electrolytic Cells Electrolytic cells use electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions.
  • Standard Electrode Potentials Standard electrode potentials measure the tendency of half-cells to be reduced.
  • Cell Potentials The cell potential shows how strongly a cell drives electrons.
  • Electrochemical Series The electrochemical series ranks half-cells by their standard electrode potentials.
  • Nernst Equation The Nernst equation shows how electrode potentials change with concentration.
  • Electrolysis Electrolysis uses electricity to decompose compounds.
  • Faraday's Laws Faraday's laws relate the amount of product in electrolysis to the charge passed.
  • Batteries Batteries are galvanic cells that store chemical energy for later use.
  • Fuel Cells Fuel cells generate electricity continuously from a fuel and oxygen.
  • Corrosion Corrosion is the electrochemical deterioration of metals.
  • Corrosion Prevention Corrosion can be slowed or prevented by several methods.
  • Industrial Electrochemistry Electrochemistry underpins many large-scale industries.

Inorganic Chemistry

  • Main-group Chemistry Main-group elements occupy the s- and p-blocks.
  • S-block Chemistry This lesson explores s-block compounds in depth, including oxides, peroxides, nitrates and carbonates.
  • Transition Metal Chemistry This lesson explores characteristic reactions of transition metal ions, including precipitation, ligand exchange and redox.
  • Ligands Ligands donate electron pairs to metal ions.
  • Crystal Field Theory Crystal field theory explains how ligands split d-orbital energies.
  • Ligand Field Theory Ligand field theory improves on crystal field theory by including covalent bonding.
  • Magnetic Properties Unpaired electrons make complexes magnetic.
  • Colour in Transition Metal Complexes Many transition metal complexes are coloured.
  • Oxidation States Transition metals show many oxidation states.
  • Catalytic Complexes Metal complexes catalyse many reactions.
  • Metal Extraction Metals are extracted from ores by methods depending on their reactivity.
  • Industrial Inorganic Chemistry Inorganic chemicals are made on a vast scale.
  • Lanthanide Chemistry The lanthanides fill the 4f subshell.
  • Actinide Chemistry The actinides fill the 5f subshell and are all radioactive.

Organic Chemistry Foundations

  • Introduction to Organic Chemistry Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds.
  • Carbon Bonding Carbon's bonding explains the variety of organic compounds.
  • Hydrocarbons Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Benzene Structure Benzene's structure puzzled chemists for decades.
  • Constitutional Isomers Constitutional isomers differ in how atoms are connected.
  • Conformational Analysis Rotation about single bonds produces conformations.
  • Chirality Chiral molecules are not superimposable on their mirror images.
  • Optical Activity Chiral compounds rotate plane-polarised light.
  • R and S Nomenclature The Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules assign R or S to stereocentres.
  • E and Z Nomenclature E/Z nomenclature describes geometric isomers around double bonds.
  • Functional Group Interconversion Functional groups can be converted into one another.
  • Organic Reaction Classification Organic reactions can be classified by what happens and by the species involved.

Organic Reaction Mechanisms

  • Curly Arrow Notation Curly arrows are the language of organic mechanisms.
  • Electrophiles and Nucleophiles Nucleophiles and electrophiles drive most polar reactions.
  • Homolytic and Heterolytic Cleavage Bonds break in two different ways.
  • Carbanions Carbanions are negatively charged carbon species.
  • Radical Intermediates Radicals are species with unpaired electrons.
  • Reaction Energy Profiles Energy profiles show how energy changes along a reaction.
  • Substitution Reactions Substitution reactions replace one group with another.
  • SN2 Mechanisms SN2 reactions occur in one concerted step.
  • E2 Mechanisms E2 eliminations occur in one concerted step.
  • Addition Reactions Addition reactions join atoms across multiple bonds.
  • Electrophilic Addition This lesson details the mechanisms of electrophilic addition to alkenes, including bromination and hydration.
  • Nucleophilic Addition Aldehydes and ketones undergo nucleophilic addition at the carbonyl carbon.
  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution This lesson details the mechanism of electrophilic aromatic substitution and how electrophiles are generated.
  • Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution Aromatic rings can undergo nucleophilic substitution when activated.
  • Oxidation Reactions Oxidation in organic chemistry increases bonds to oxygen or decreases bonds to hydrogen.
  • Reduction Reactions Reduction in organic chemistry adds hydrogen or removes oxygen.
  • Condensation Reactions Condensation reactions join molecules with loss of a small molecule.
  • Hydrolysis Hydrolysis splits molecules using water.
  • Rearrangements Rearrangements change a molecule's carbon skeleton or connectivity.
  • Chemoselectivity Chemoselective reactions change one functional group while leaving others.

Functional Group Chemistry

  • Alcohols Alcohols contain the –OH group on a saturated carbon.
  • Phenols Phenols have –OH attached directly to a benzene ring.
  • Ethers Ethers contain an oxygen bonded to two carbon groups.
  • Aldehydes Aldehydes contain the –CHO group.
  • Ketones Ketones have a carbonyl group within the carbon chain.
  • Acyl Chlorides Acyl chlorides are highly reactive carboxylic acid derivatives.
  • Acid Anhydrides Acid anhydrides are formed from two carboxylic acid molecules.
  • Esters Esters contain the –COO– linkage.
  • Amides Amides contain the –CONH– group.
  • Amines Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia.
  • Nitriles Nitriles contain the –C≡N group.
  • Nitro Compounds Nitro compounds contain the –NO₂ group.
  • Aromatic Compounds This lesson explores substituted benzene compounds and how side chains and ring substituents react.
  • Organohalogen Compounds Organohalogen compounds are widely used but some harm the environment.
  • Carbonyl Chemistry The carbonyl group links aldehydes, ketones, acids and their derivatives.
  • Enolates and Enols Carbonyl compounds can react at the carbon next to the C=O.
  • Heterocyclic Compounds Heterocycles contain rings with atoms other than carbon.
  • Functional Group Synthesis Each functional group can be made in several ways.

Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy

  • Foundations of Analytical Chemistry Analytical chemistry finds out what substances are present and how much.
  • Qualitative Analysis Qualitative analysis identifies substances.
  • Calibration Calibration relates instrument signals to concentration.
  • Gravimetric Analysis Gravimetric analysis measures mass to find amounts.
  • UV-visible Spectroscopy UV-visible spectroscopy measures absorption of ultraviolet and visible light.
  • Nuclear Magnetic Resonance NMR spectroscopy probes atomic nuclei in a magnetic field.
  • NMR Chemical Shifts Chemical shifts reveal electronic environments.
  • Spin-spin Coupling Spin-spin coupling splits NMR signals.
  • Mass Spectrometry Mass spectrometry measures the mass-to-charge ratio of ions.
  • High-resolution Mass Spectrometry High-resolution mass spectrometry measures masses very precisely.
  • Raman Spectroscopy Raman spectroscopy studies molecular vibrations through scattered light.
  • Fluorescence Spectroscopy Fluorescent molecules emit light after absorbing it.
  • X-ray Diffraction X-ray diffraction reveals the arrangement of atoms in crystals.
  • Thin-layer Chromatography Thin-layer chromatography is a quick separation technique.
  • Gas Chromatography Gas chromatography separates volatile compounds.
  • High-performance Liquid Chromatography HPLC separates compounds in solution at high pressure.

  • Molecular Structure Molecular structure covers bond lengths, angles and symmetry.
  • Molecular Orbitals This lesson applies molecular orbital theory to diatomic molecules and conjugated systems.
  • Quantum Chemistry Quantum chemistry applies quantum mechanics to chemical systems.
  • Particle in a Box The particle in a box is the simplest quantum model.
  • Harmonic Oscillator The harmonic oscillator models molecular vibrations.
  • Rotational Spectroscopy Rotational spectroscopy measures molecular rotation using microwaves.
  • Vibrational Spectroscopy Vibrational spectroscopy studies how molecules vibrate.
  • Electronic Transitions Electronic transitions move electrons between molecular orbitals.
  • Statistical Thermodynamics Statistical thermodynamics links molecular energy levels to bulk properties.
  • Molecular Energy Distributions Molecules in a gas have a range of speeds and energies.
  • Chemical Kinetics This lesson explores advanced kinetics, including collision and transition state theories and complex mechanisms.

  • Computational Molecular Modelling Computers model molecules to predict structures and properties.
