Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy measures energy shifts in scattered laser light caused by vibrations requiring polarisability changes; it complements IR, suits symmetric bonds and aqueous samples and works through packaging.

Chemistry · Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the Raman effect
  • Compare Raman and IR spectroscopy
  • Describe applications of Raman spectroscopy

The Raman effect

  • A laser shines on a sample; most light scatters at the same energy (Rayleigh).
  • A tiny fraction scatters with changed energy (Raman).
  • The energy shift equals a vibrational energy.
  • Raman shifts are given in cm⁻¹, like IR.

Raman versus IR

  • IR needs a change in dipole moment; Raman needs a change in polarisability.
  • Symmetric vibrations such as N≡N and C=C are often strong in Raman.
  • Water gives weak Raman signals, so aqueous samples are easy.
  • Raman needs little sample preparation and works through glass.

Raman and IR give complementary information. Handheld Raman spectrometers identify drugs and explosives through packaging. Raman is used to study art pigments, minerals and carbon materials such as graphene.

Worked example

Detecting a counterfeit tablet.

  1. A handheld Raman spectrometer is aimed at a sealed blister pack.
  2. The laser passes through the plastic.
  3. The spectrum is compared with the genuine product.
  4. Missing active-ingredient peaks reveal a counterfeit.
Common mistakeThinking Raman and IR give identical spectra. Different selection rules make them complementary.

In 3 lines: Scattered light shifts. Complements IR. Works through glass.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which bond gives a strong Raman signal but no IR absorption?
    • The O–H stretch in water
    • The C=O stretch in propanone
    • The symmetric N≡N stretch in nitrogen
    • The N–H stretch in ammonia
    Show the answer

    Answer: The symmetric N≡N stretch in nitrogen. Symmetric stretches change polarisability, not dipole.

  2. Why can Raman analyse drugs through plastic packaging?
    • The Raman laser dissolves the plastic packaging
    • Plastic gives no Raman signals whatsoever
    • Raman measures only the very outer surface
    • The laser passes through and scattered light returns from the sample
    Show the answer

    Answer: The laser passes through and scattered light returns from the sample. Visible or near-IR lasers penetrate transparent containers.

  3. Why are Raman and IR described as complementary?
    • Vibrations weak in one are often strong in the other
    • They measure completely unrelated properties
    • They always give identical spectra
    • Raman measures only electronic transitions
    Show the answer

    Answer: Vibrations weak in one are often strong in the other. Different selection rules reveal different vibrations.

Key terms

Raman scattering
Inelastic scattering of light with energy change
Rayleigh scattering
Elastic scattering with no energy change
Polarisability
How easily an electron cloud is distorted
