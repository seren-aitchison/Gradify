Temperature and Equilibrium

Raising temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction, decreasing K for exothermic reactions and increasing it for endothermic ones; industry balances yield against rate with compromise temperatures.

Chemistry · Chemical Equilibrium · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Predict the effect of temperature on equilibrium position
  • Explain how K changes with temperature
  • Apply this to industrial processes

Effect of temperature

  • Raising temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction.
  • Lowering temperature shifts it in the exothermic direction.
  • For an exothermic forward reaction, raising temperature decreases K.
  • For an endothermic forward reaction, raising temperature increases K.

Examples

  • 2NO₂ (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄ (colourless), ΔH negative: heating makes it browner.
  • The Haber process is exothermic, so lower temperatures give higher yield.
  • But low temperatures make rates too slow.
  • A compromise temperature, around 400–450 °C, is used.

Temperature changes K because it changes the relative rates of forward and reverse reactions unequally: the endothermic direction has the higher activation energy and speeds up more. This is consistent with the van 't Hoff equation and ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K.

Worked example

NO₂ and N₂O₄.

  1. A sealed tube of NO₂ and N₂O₄ is placed in hot water.
  2. The gas turns darker brown.
  3. The forward reaction to N₂O₄ is exothermic, so heating favours NO₂.
  4. In ice water, the gas becomes paler.
Common mistakeThinking higher temperature always increases yield. For exothermic reactions it lowers the equilibrium yield.

In 3 lines: Heat favours endothermic. Only temperature changes K. Compromise in industry.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What happens to the colour of NO₂/N₂O₄ when heated?
    • It becomes paler
    • It becomes darker brown
    • It turns blue
    • It does not change
    Show the answer

    Answer: It becomes darker brown. Heating favours the endothermic formation of brown NO₂.

  2. Why does temperature change K when concentration does not?
    • It adds more molecules of every substance
    • It changes the stoichiometry
    • It changes forward and reverse rate constants unequally
    • K depends on concentration
    Show the answer

    Answer: It changes forward and reverse rate constants unequally. The direction with higher activation energy speeds up more when heated.

  3. Why does the Haber process not use a very low temperature despite better yield?
    • Ammonia does not form at low temperatures
    • Low temperature lowers K
    • Catalysts melt at low temperature
    • The rate would be far too slow to be economical
    Show the answer

    Answer: The rate would be far too slow to be economical. Yield must be balanced against speed of production.

Key terms

Compromise temperature
A temperature balancing yield and rate
Endothermic direction
The direction absorbing heat
Van 't Hoff equation
Relates K to temperature and ΔH
