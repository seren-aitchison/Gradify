Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry ionises, accelerates, separates by m/z and detects ions; the molecular ion gives Mr, the base peak is the most abundant and M+1 and M+2 peaks reveal carbon count, chlorine and bromine.

Chemistry · Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the stages of mass spectrometry
  • Identify the molecular ion peak
  • Use isotope patterns to identify elements

How it works

  • Ionisation: molecules become ions, by electron impact or electrospray.
  • Acceleration: ions are accelerated by an electric field.
  • Separation: ions are separated by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z).
  • Detection: ions are counted to give abundance.

Reading a spectrum

  • The molecular ion (M⁺) gives the relative molecular mass.
  • The base peak is the tallest, set to 100%.
  • M+1 peaks come from ¹³C.
  • M+2 peaks show Cl (3:1) or Br (1:1).

Mass spectrometry needs only tiny samples and is used in drug testing, forensic science and to date archaeological samples. Electrospray ionisation lets large biomolecules such as proteins be analysed, recognised by the 2002 Nobel Prize.

Worked example

A compound containing bromine.

  1. The spectrum shows peaks at m/z 108 and 110.
  2. They are of equal height.
  3. ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br occur in roughly equal amounts.
  4. The compound contains one bromine atom: C₂H₅Br.

Isotope patterns

  • Many elements have more than one common isotope, so a fragment containing them shows a pattern of peaks rather than one peak.
  • Chlorine (³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl, about 3:1) gives M and M+2 peaks in a 3:1 ratio; bromine (⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br, about 1:1) gives M and M+2 peaks of nearly equal height.
  • Two chlorine atoms give M, M+2 and M+4 in about 9:6:1; two bromine atoms give about 1:2:1.
  • The height of the M+1 peak compared with M estimates the number of carbon atoms: about 1.1% per carbon, because about 1.1% of carbon is ¹³C.
  • The spectrum of an element on its own gives each isotope's mass and abundance, from which its relative atomic mass is calculated.
Common mistakeAssuming the tallest peak is the molecular ion. The base peak is often a fragment.

In 3 lines: Ionise, accelerate, separate, detect. M⁺ gives Mr. Isotope patterns.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What is the base peak in a mass spectrum?
    • The peak with the highest m/z
    • The tallest peak, set to 100% abundance
    • The molecular ion peak always
    • The peak at m/z 0
    Show the answer

    Answer: The tallest peak, set to 100% abundance. The base peak is the most abundant ion and is set to 100%.

  2. Why do bromine compounds show M and M+2 peaks of equal height?
    • Bromine has only one isotope
    • The molecule loses two hydrogens
    • ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br occur in nearly equal amounts
    • Bromine doubles the mass
    Show the answer

    Answer: ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br occur in nearly equal amounts. The roughly 1 : 1 abundance of ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br sets the peak ratio.

  3. Why did electrospray ionisation transform biochemistry?
    • It only works on small molecules
    • It destroys all large biomolecules
    • It replaces the need for separation
    • It ionises large molecules such as proteins without breaking them
    Show the answer

    Answer: It ionises large molecules such as proteins without breaking them. Gentle ionisation keeps big molecules intact.
