Interfaces

Surface tension arises from unbalanced forces at interfaces; surfactants lower it and form micelles above the CMC, removing grease, while contact angles describe wetting, from hydrophilic to superhydrophobic surfaces.

Chemistry · Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain surface tension at liquid interfaces
  • Describe how surfactants work
  • Explain wetting and contact angles

Surface tension and surfactants

  • Molecules at a liquid surface have unbalanced forces, giving surface tension.
  • Surfactants have a polar head and non-polar tail.
  • They gather at interfaces, lowering surface tension.
  • Above the critical micelle concentration (CMC), they form micelles.

Wetting

  • The contact angle measures how a liquid spreads on a solid.
  • < 90°: the liquid wets the surface (hydrophilic).
  • > 90°: the liquid beads up (hydrophobic).
  • Superhydrophobic surfaces have angles above 150°.

Detergents remove grease by trapping it inside micelles. Lungs produce surfactants that reduce surface tension so alveoli can inflate easily; premature babies may lack them. The lotus leaf's rough, waxy surface is superhydrophobic, so water rolls off and cleans it.

Worked example

How detergent removes grease.

  1. Surfactant tails dissolve in the grease.
  2. Polar heads face the water.
  3. Grease is lifted into micelles.
  4. Micelles are rinsed away in water.
Common mistakeThinking detergents dissolve grease directly in water. They trap it inside micelles.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does a surfactant do to surface tension?
    • Raises it
    • Leaves it unchanged
    • Makes it infinite
    • Lowers it
    Show the answer

    Answer: Lowers it. Surfactants gather at the surface and reduce cohesive forces.

  2. Why do premature babies sometimes struggle to breathe?
    • Their lungs may lack surfactant, so alveoli are hard to inflate
    • Their lungs contain too much surfactant
    • Surface tension does not affect lungs
    • Babies have no alveoli
    Show the answer

    Answer: Their lungs may lack surfactant, so alveoli are hard to inflate. High surface tension resists expansion of air sacs.

  3. Why does water roll off a lotus leaf?
    • It is coated in detergent
    • Its rough, waxy surface is superhydrophobic
    • The leaf absorbs all water
    • Water wets the leaf completely
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its rough, waxy surface is superhydrophobic. Microstructure and wax give very high contact angles.

Key terms

Surfactant
A molecule with polar and non-polar parts that acts at interfaces
Micelle
A cluster of surfactant molecules with tails inside
Contact angle
The angle a liquid makes with a solid surface
