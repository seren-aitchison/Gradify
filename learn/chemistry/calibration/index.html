Calibration

Calibration uses standards to relate signal to concentration, with blanks to correct baselines, standard addition to correct matrix effects and internal standards to correct variable losses, and unknowns should fall within the linear calibrated range.

Chemistry · Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Construct and use a calibration curve
  • Explain standard addition and internal standards
  • Evaluate linearity of a calibration

Calibration curves

  • Measure signals for a series of standards of known concentration.
  • Plot signal against concentration and fit a line.
  • Read the unknown's concentration from its signal.
  • Check linearity with the correlation coefficient and residuals.

Other approaches

  • Blank: a sample without analyte, to correct the baseline.
  • Standard addition: add known amounts to the sample itself, correcting matrix effects.
  • Internal standard: a compound added in fixed amount, correcting for variable injection or losses.
  • Unknowns should fall within the calibrated range.

Extrapolating beyond the highest standard is risky because response may curve at high concentrations. Standard addition is useful when the matrix, such as blood or seawater, changes the signal. Internal standards are common in chromatography.

Worked example

Using a calibration curve.

  1. Standards of 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 mg dm⁻³ give a straight line.
  2. The equation is A = 0.050c + 0.002.
  3. The unknown has A = 0.252.
  4. c = (0.252 − 0.002) ÷ 0.050 = 5.0 mg dm⁻³.
Common mistakeExtrapolating beyond the highest standard. The response may not stay linear.

In 3 lines: Standards and a line. Blanks and matrix effects. Stay in range.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What is plotted in a calibration curve?
    • Instrument signal against known concentration
    • Time against temperature
    • Mass against volume of solvent
    • Wavelength against pH
    Show the answer

    Answer: Instrument signal against known concentration. Known standards define the signal-concentration relationship.

  2. Why should unknowns fall within the calibrated range?
    • Results outside the range are always more accurate
    • The response may not be linear outside the range
    • The range has no effect on accuracy
    • Instruments cannot read outside the range
    Show the answer

    Answer: The response may not be linear outside the range. Extrapolation assumes linearity that may fail.

  3. Why is an internal standard useful in chromatography?
    • It removes the need for any standards
    • It increases the analyte signal only
    • It corrects for variations in injection volume and sample losses
    • It changes the retention time of the analyte
    Show the answer

    Answer: It corrects for variations in injection volume and sample losses. Ratios to the internal standard cancel out variability.

Key terms

Calibration curve
A plot of signal against known concentrations
Standard addition
Adding known analyte to the sample to calibrate
Internal standard
A compound added in fixed amount to correct variability
