Electronic Structure and Chemical Properties

Outer electron configuration decides how an element bonds and reacts; group trends follow from distance and shielding, and partly filled d subshells give transition metals variable oxidation states and colour.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how outer electron configuration determines the typical ions and bonding of an element
  • Explain reactivity trends in groups 1 and 17 in terms of electron structure
  • Explain why many transition metal compounds are coloured and show variable oxidation states

Gaining, losing and sharing

Metals, on the left of the table, have few outer electrons and low ionisation energies, so they tend to lose electrons and form cations. Non-metals, on the right, have nearly full outer shells and exothermic electron affinities, so they tend to gain electrons (forming anions) or share them in covalent bonds. Noble gases, with full outer shells and very high ionisation energies, rarely react at all.

Reactivity trends

  • Group 1 reactivity increases down the group: the outer s electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily. Potassium reacts with water far more vigorously than lithium.
  • Group 17 reactivity decreases down the group: a halogen reacts by gaining an electron, and an incoming electron is attracted less strongly by a larger atom. Fluorine is the most reactive non-metal, and chlorine displaces bromide and iodide from solution.

The d-block

In transition metals the 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so different numbers can be lost: iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, and manganese shows every oxidation state from +2 to +7. Many transition metal ions have partly filled d subshells. In a complex, the surrounding ligands split the d orbitals into groups of different energy, and electrons absorb visible light when they jump between them; the colour we see is the light not absorbed. Ions with empty or full d subshells, such as Sc³⁺ (3d⁰) and Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰), are colourless.

The same idea, chemistry from configuration, is why elements in a group form similar compounds: the alkali metals all form chlorides MCl and oxides M₂O, and the halogens all form hydrogen halides HX.

Common mistakeSaying atoms react "to get a full outer shell" as if they intended it. Reactions happen when the products are lower in energy; noble gas configurations are often, not always, part of why.

In 3 lines: Metals lose electrons; non-metals gain or share them; noble gases rarely react. Group 1 reactivity rises down the group; group 17 falls. Partly filled d subshells give variable oxidation states and coloured compounds.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which ion is most likely to form from a calcium atom ([Ar] 4s²)?
    • Ca⁺
    • Ca²⁺
    • Ca²⁻
    • Ca³⁺
    Show the answer

    Answer: Ca²⁺. Losing the two 4s electrons gives the stable argon configuration; a third ionisation would remove an inner electron.

  2. Chlorine water is added to potassium bromide solution and the solution turns orange. Why?
    • Bromine is more reactive than chlorine
    • Potassium reacts with chlorine water
    • Chlorine is more reactive than bromine, so it takes electrons from bromide ions, forming bromine
    • The bromide ions gain electrons
    Show the answer

    Answer: Chlorine is more reactive than bromine, so it takes electrons from bromide ions, forming bromine. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂: chlorine's smaller atom attracts an extra electron more strongly.

  3. Scandium is in the d-block, but Sc³⁺ compounds are white and scandium is often said not to be a typical transition metal. Why?
    • Scandium has no d electrons in any state
    • Scandium is an s-block element
    • Sc³⁺ has a full 3d subshell
    • Its only common ion, Sc³⁺, has an empty 3d subshell, so it has no d electrons to give colour or variable oxidation states
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its only common ion, Sc³⁺, has an empty 3d subshell, so it has no d electrons to give colour or variable oxidation states. With 3d⁰, Sc³⁺ can't have d–d transitions; transition metal character needs a partly filled d subshell.

Key terms

Oxidation state
A number describing how many electrons an atom has lost or gained in forming a compound or ion
Transition metal
A d-block element that forms at least one stable ion with a partly filled d subshell
Ligand
A molecule or ion that donates a pair of electrons to a central metal ion in a complex
