Atomic Number and Mass Number

The atomic number defines the element and orders the periodic table; the mass number identifies the isotope; both are conserved in nuclear equations.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Use nuclide notation correctly
  • Explain why the atomic number defines an element and its place in the periodic table
  • Balance simple nuclear equations using atomic and mass numbers

The two numbers

The atomic number Z counts protons. It defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon. Since 1913, when Henry Moseley showed that X-ray frequencies increase steadily with Z, the periodic table has been ordered by atomic number rather than by mass. The mass number A counts protons plus neutrons and identifies the isotope.

Mass number is not relative atomic mass

Mass number is a whole-number count for one nucleus. Relative atomic mass is an average over an element's isotopes and is usually not a whole number (chlorine 35.45). Even an individual isotope's mass isn't exactly its mass number: chlorine-35 has a mass of 34.969, because of the binding energy of the nucleus (and carbon-12 is exactly 12 by definition).

Nuclear equations

In nuclear reactions the nucleus changes, so the element can change, but both the total mass number and the total atomic number are conserved.

  • Alpha decay emits ⁴₂He: A falls by 4 and Z by 2. ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He.
  • Beta-minus decay turns a neutron into a proton and emits an electron, ⁰₋₁e: A stays the same and Z rises by 1. ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e.
Worked example

Radium-226 (Z = 88) undergoes alpha decay. What is the product?

  1. Mass number: 226 − 4 = 222. Atomic number: 88 − 2 = 86, which is radon.
  2. The product is radon-222: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He.

Investigate with the lab. Build an atom and read off its atomic number and mass number. Then build the same element with more neutrons and compare.

Common mistakeUsing relative atomic mass as the mass number. Mass number is always a whole number for one nucleus.

In 3 lines: Z (protons) defines the element; A (protons + neutrons) identifies the isotope. Moseley showed the periodic table is ordered by atomic number. A and Z are both conserved in nuclear equations.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which number is conserved in a chemical reaction but can change in a nuclear reaction?
    • The total charge
    • The number of electrons in the universe
    • The mass number total
    • The atomic number of each atom
    Show the answer

    Answer: The atomic number of each atom. In chemistry nuclei are unchanged; in nuclear reactions atoms can become different elements, although the totals of A and Z are conserved.

  2. Thorium-234 (Z = 90) undergoes beta-minus decay. What is formed?
    • Protactinium-234 (Z = 91)
    • Thorium-230
    • Radium-230
    • Uranium-238
    Show the answer

    Answer: Protactinium-234 (Z = 91). Beta-minus decay keeps A = 234 and raises Z to 91, which is protactinium.

  3. Why did Moseley's work resolve the puzzle of argon (Ar 39.9) being placed before potassium (Ar 39.1) in the periodic table?
    • It showed argon is heavier than potassium
    • It showed the order depends on atomic number (Ar 18, K 19), not on mass
    • It showed potassium is a noble gas
    • It showed atomic masses were measured wrongly
    Show the answer

    Answer: It showed the order depends on atomic number (Ar 18, K 19), not on mass. Ordering by Z puts argon before potassium, matching their chemistry, even though argon's average mass is greater.

  4. An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. What is its mass number?
    • 11
    • 12
    • 23
    • 1
    Show the answer

    Answer: 23. Mass number is protons plus neutrons: 11 + 12 = 23.

Key terms

Nuclide
An atom with a specific number of protons and neutrons, written with its mass and atomic numbers
Alpha decay
Radioactive decay emitting a helium-4 nucleus, lowering A by 4 and Z by 2
Beta decay
Radioactive decay in which a neutron changes into a proton and an electron is emitted
