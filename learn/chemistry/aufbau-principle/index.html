Aufbau Principle

Electrons occupy the lowest-energy available orbitals; subshells fill in order of n + l, which puts 4s before 3d.

Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • State the aufbau principle
  • Use the n + l rule to predict the order in which subshells fill
  • Explain why 4s fills before 3d

Building up

Imagine building each atom by adding electrons one at a time to a nucleus. The aufbau principle says each new electron goes into the lowest-energy orbital available, as long as the Pauli principle allows. Combined with Hund's rule for orbitals of equal energy, this gives the ground-state configuration of an atom, its lowest-energy arrangement.

The order of filling

In many-electron atoms, subshell energy depends on n and l. A useful guide is the n + l rule (Madelung rule): subshells fill in order of increasing n + l, and for equal n + l, the one with lower n fills first. This gives 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p, matching the layout of the periodic table read row by row.

Worked example

Use the n + l rule to decide whether 4s or 3d fills first.

  1. 4s: n + l = 4 + 0 = 4. 3d: n + l = 3 + 2 = 5.
  2. The lower value, 4s, fills first.

Why 4s before 3d

A 4s electron spends part of its time very close to the nucleus (it penetrates the inner shells), so it is less shielded and feels a higher effective nuclear charge than a 3d electron, whose orbital lies mostly outside the 3s and 3p electrons' region but doesn't penetrate to the nucleus as much. For potassium and calcium this puts 4s below 3d in energy. Once electrons occupy 3d, the energies shift, which is why transition metal atoms lose their 4s electrons first.

The rule is a guide, not a law: there are about twenty exceptions among the transition metals, lanthanides and actinides, such as chromium, copper, silver and gold.

Investigate with the lab. Fill the orbital boxes for elements from hydrogen to argon in order, and check each against the Aufbau principle.

Common mistakeThinking 4s is always lower in energy than 3d. It is lower in potassium and calcium, but once 3d orbitals are occupied, 4s electrons are the first to be removed.

In 3 lines: Electrons fill the lowest available energy orbitals first. Subshells fill in order of n + l, lower n first when tied. Penetration puts 4s below 3d for potassium and calcium; there are exceptions such as Cr and Cu.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which subshell fills immediately after 3p?
    • 3d
    • 4p
    • 4s
    • 4d
    Show the answer

    Answer: 4s. By the n + l rule, 4s (4) comes before 3d (5).

  2. Using the n + l rule, which fills first, 4f or 5d?
    • 5d, because d always fills before f
    • They fill at the same time
    • 5d, because it has the higher n
    • 4f, because both have n + l = 7 and 4f has the lower n
    Show the answer

    Answer: 4f, because both have n + l = 7 and 4f has the lower n. For equal n + l, the subshell with lower n fills first.

  3. Why does a 4s electron in potassium have lower energy than a 3d electron would?
    • The 4s orbital penetrates closer to the nucleus, so the electron is less shielded and more strongly attracted
    • 4s orbitals are smaller than 3d orbitals overall
    • 3d orbitals don't exist in potassium
    • 4s has a lower principal quantum number
    Show the answer

    Answer: The 4s orbital penetrates closer to the nucleus, so the electron is less shielded and more strongly attracted. Penetration gives the 4s electron a higher effective nuclear charge despite its higher n.

  4. Which subshell fills after 4s?
    • 4p
    • 3d
    • 3p
    • 5s
    Show the answer

    Answer: 3d. By the Aufbau principle, 3d fills after 4s and before 4p.

Key terms

Aufbau principle
Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first
Ground state
The lowest-energy arrangement of an atom's electrons
Penetration
The extent to which an electron in an orbital spends time close to the nucleus, inside inner electrons
