Amorphous Solids

Amorphous solids have short-range but no long-range order, often forming by rapid cooling; they soften over a range at the glass transition rather than melting sharply, and differ from crystalline forms in solubility and isotropy.

Chemistry · States of Matter and Physical Chemistry · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the structure of amorphous solids
  • Explain the glass transition
  • Compare amorphous and crystalline forms of the same substance

Structure

  • Particles have short-range order but no repeating pattern.
  • Examples: glass, many polymers, amorphous silicon and candyfloss.
  • They often form when liquids are cooled rapidly, so particles cannot arrange.
  • They soften over a range of temperatures instead of melting sharply.

Glass transition and comparison

  • The glass transition temperature (Tg) is where an amorphous solid changes from hard and brittle to soft and rubbery.
  • Quartz is crystalline SiO₂; glass is amorphous SiO₂.
  • Amorphous forms are often more soluble and isotropic.
  • Some drugs are made amorphous to dissolve faster.

Glass breaks with curved conchoidal fractures rather than clean cleavage planes. Polymers such as PET can be crystalline or amorphous depending on cooling. Metallic glasses, made by very rapid cooling, can be very strong.

Worked example

Making glass.

  1. Silica is melted with sodium carbonate and limestone.
  2. The melt is cooled quickly.
  3. The atoms freeze in a disordered network.
  4. The result is transparent soda-lime glass.
Common mistakeThinking glass is a slow-flowing liquid. It is an amorphous solid; old windows are thicker at the bottom because of how they were made.

In 3 lines: No long-range order. Glass transition. Different from crystals.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is an amorphous form of SiO₂?
    • Quartz
    • Glass
    • Diamond
    • Graphite
    Show the answer

    Answer: Glass. Glass is disordered silica, while quartz is crystalline.

  2. Why do amorphous solids soften over a range of temperatures?
    • All of their bonds are identical in strength
    • They have no bonds
    • Their bonds vary in strength because of disorder
    • They are always liquids
    Show the answer

    Answer: Their bonds vary in strength because of disorder. Different local environments break down at different temperatures.

  3. Why are some drugs made in amorphous form?
    • They are always more stable
    • They have sharper melting points
    • They cannot dissolve at all
    • They dissolve faster than crystalline forms
    Show the answer

    Answer: They dissolve faster than crystalline forms. Disordered solids need less energy to dissolve.

Key terms

Amorphous solid
A solid without long-range order
Glass transition temperature
Temperature where an amorphous solid softens
Isotropic
Having the same properties in all directions
