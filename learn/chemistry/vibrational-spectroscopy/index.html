Vibrational Spectroscopy

Molecules have 3N − 6 vibrational modes (3N − 5 if linear), each an independent normal mode; modes changing the dipole are IR active and those changing polarisability are Raman active, with mutual exclusion in centrosymmetric molecules.

Chemistry · Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Calculate the number of vibrational modes of a molecule
  • Describe normal modes of vibration
  • Explain the gross selection rules for IR and Raman

Normal modes

  • A molecule with N atoms has 3N degrees of freedom.
  • Linear molecules: 3N − 5 vibrations; non-linear: 3N − 6.
  • Each normal mode is an independent pattern of motion.
  • Modes include symmetric and asymmetric stretches and bends.

Selection rules

  • IR active: the mode changes the dipole moment.
  • Raman active: the mode changes polarisability.
  • In molecules with an inversion centre, modes are IR or Raman active, not both (mutual exclusion).
  • Vibrational spectra often show rotational fine structure in the gas phase.

CO₂ has four vibrational modes: a symmetric stretch (Raman active only), an asymmetric stretch and two bends (IR active). Its IR-active modes make it a greenhouse gas. Water's three modes are all IR active.

Worked example

Counting modes of water.

  1. Water has N = 3 atoms and is non-linear.
  2. 3N − 6 = 3 vibrational modes.
  3. Symmetric stretch, asymmetric stretch and bend.
  4. All three change the dipole, so all are IR active.
Common mistakeForgetting that linear molecules have one extra vibration. They lose only two rotations, not three.

In 3 lines: 3N − 6 or 3N − 5. Normal modes. IR and Raman rules.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How many vibrational modes does CO₂ have?
    • 3
    • 2
    • 4
    • 6
    Show the answer

    Answer: 4. Linear CO₂ has 3 × 3 − 5 = 4 vibrational modes in total.

  2. Why is CO₂'s symmetric stretch not IR active?
    • It changes the dipole strongly
    • It breaks the molecule apart
    • It changes the mass of the molecule
    • It does not change the dipole moment
    Show the answer

    Answer: It does not change the dipole moment. Both C=O bonds stretch equally, keeping zero dipole.

  3. Why does the rule of mutual exclusion apply only to molecules with an inversion centre?
    • Symmetry under inversion makes dipole and polarisability changes incompatible
    • All molecules show mutual exclusion
    • It only applies to linear molecules without symmetry
    • It depends on molecular mass
    Show the answer

    Answer: Symmetry under inversion makes dipole and polarisability changes incompatible. Inversion symmetry separates the two activity types.

Key terms

Degrees of freedom
