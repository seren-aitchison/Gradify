Curly Arrow Notation

Full-headed curly arrows show electron-pair movement and half-headed arrows show single electrons; arrows start at lone pairs or bonds and end at atoms or bonding positions, and mechanisms must conserve charge and valence.

Chemistry · Organic Reaction Mechanisms · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Draw curly arrows from electron sources to electron sinks
  • Distinguish full-headed and half-headed arrows
  • Check mechanisms for conservation of charge and valence

Rules

  • A full-headed arrow shows movement of an electron pair.
  • A half-headed (fishhook) arrow shows movement of one electron.
  • Arrows start at a lone pair or a bond, never at an atom's nucleus or a positive charge.
  • Arrows end where the electrons go: an atom (forming a lone pair) or between atoms (forming a bond).

Checking

  • The overall charge must be the same on both sides of each step.
  • No atom should exceed its valence, such as carbon with five bonds.
  • Each arrow must have a clear source and sink.
  • Breaking a bond and forming a new one often happen together.

Robert Robinson popularised curly arrows in the 1920s. A correct mechanism shows why a reaction happens, not just what forms, so chemists can predict new reactions. Most mistakes come from arrows starting at positive charges or forming five bonds to carbon.

Worked example

Hydroxide attacking chloromethane.

  1. An arrow goes from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the carbon.
  2. A second arrow goes from the C–Cl bond to Cl.
  3. The new C–O bond forms as the C–Cl bond breaks.
  4. Products: CH₃OH and Cl⁻, with charge conserved.
Common mistakeDrawing an arrow from a positive charge. Positive charges have no electrons to give.

In 3 lines: Pairs or single electrons. Start at electrons. Conserve charge.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does a fishhook arrow show?
    • The movement of an electron pair
    • The transfer of a proton only
    • The movement of a single electron
    • The direction of heat flow
    Show the answer

    Answer: The movement of a single electron. Half-headed arrows are used in radical mechanisms.

  2. Why must an arrow never start at a positive charge?
    • Positive charges always carry lone pairs
    • Arrows only start at nuclei
    • Positive charges move in the opposite direction
    • A positive charge has no electrons to donate
    Show the answer

    Answer: A positive charge has no electrons to donate. Arrows track electrons, so they must begin where electrons are.

  3. In hydroxide attacking chloromethane, why are two arrows needed?
    • One forms the C–O bond and one breaks the C–Cl bond so carbon keeps four bonds
    • Hydroxide needs two arrows to lose its charge
    • One arrow is needed for each hydrogen
    • A single arrow can never show a reaction
    Show the answer

    Answer: One forms the C–O bond and one breaks the C–Cl bond so carbon keeps four bonds. Without the second arrow carbon would have five bonds.

Key terms

Curly arrow
A symbol showing electron movement in a mechanism
Fishhook arrow
A half-headed arrow for one electron
Electron sink
An atom or bond receiving electrons
