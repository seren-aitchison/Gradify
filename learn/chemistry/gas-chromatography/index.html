Gas Chromatography

GC uses an inert carrier gas and a heated column with a liquid stationary phase to separate volatile compounds by boiling point and interaction; retention times identify components and peak areas quantify them.

Chemistry · Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the parts of a gas chromatograph
  • Explain separation in GC
  • Use peak areas for quantitative analysis

The instrument

  • An inert carrier gas (helium or nitrogen) is the mobile phase.
  • A long, thin column contains a liquid stationary phase.
  • The column is in an oven whose temperature can be programmed.
  • A detector, such as a flame ionisation detector (FID), records each component.

Separation and results

  • Components separate by boiling point and interaction with the stationary phase.
  • Each gives a peak at its retention time.
  • Peak area is proportional to amount.
  • Only volatile and thermally stable compounds can be analysed.

GC is used to test blood alcohol levels, analyse fuels and detect pesticides. The relative peak areas give the percentage composition of a mixture. Temperature programming speeds up elution of high-boiling components.

Worked example

Finding ethanol in a sample.

  1. A standard of ethanol has retention time 2.3 min.
  2. The sample shows a peak at 2.3 min.
  3. Its area is compared with a calibration curve.
  4. The ethanol concentration is calculated.

The parts, in order

  • The parts of a gas chromatograph, from start to finish: the carrier gas supply; a heated injection port, where a microsyringe pushes a few microlitres of sample through a rubber septum and it vaporises; the column inside its temperature-controlled oven; the detector; and a data system that draws the chromatogram.
Common mistakeUsing GC for non-volatile substances such as sugars or proteins. They do not vaporise without decomposing.

In 3 lines: Gas carries, liquid holds. Retention time identifies. Peak area quantifies.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What type of compound can be analysed by GC?
    • Volatile, thermally stable compounds
    • Large proteins
    • Ionic salts
    • Non-volatile sugars
    Show the answer

    Answer: Volatile, thermally stable compounds. Components must vaporise without decomposing.

  2. Why is the oven temperature often increased during a GC run?
    • To cool the column down
    • To elute high-boiling components faster
    • To slow all components
    • To stop the carrier gas
    Show the answer

    Answer: To elute high-boiling components faster. Temperature programming shortens analysis time.

  3. Why must an inert carrier gas be used?
    • Inert gases are the only gases available
    • Reactive gases improve separation
    • It must not react with samples or the stationary phase
    • The carrier gas acts as the stationary phase
    Show the answer

    Answer: It must not react with samples or the stationary phase. Reactions would alter the components being measured.

Key terms

Carrier gas
Inert gas acting as the mobile phase in GC
Retention time
Time for a component to pass through the column
Flame ionisation detector
A common GC detector for organic compounds
