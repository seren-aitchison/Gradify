Carbanions

Carbanions carry a lone pair and negative charge, are stabilised by electron-withdrawing groups and resonance as in enolates, and act as strong nucleophiles and bases, with Grignard reagents, organolithiums and enolates used to form C–C bonds.

Chemistry · Organic Reaction Mechanisms · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the structure of carbanions
  • Explain factors that stabilise carbanions
  • Describe carbanion equivalents used in synthesis

Structure and stability

  • A carbanion has three bonds, a lone pair and a negative charge.
  • Simple carbanions are roughly pyramidal (sp³).
  • Stability order is the reverse of carbocations: methyl > primary > secondary > tertiary.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups and resonance stabilise carbanions, as in enolates.

In synthesis

  • Carbanions are strong nucleophiles and bases.
  • Grignard and organolithium reagents behave like carbanions.
  • Enolates form next to carbonyl groups and attack electrophiles.
  • Carbanions form new C–C bonds.

The acidity of a C–H bond shows how stable its carbanion is: hydrogens next to carbonyl groups (pKa about 20) are far more acidic than those in alkanes (pKa about 50). Carbanion chemistry underpins aldol reactions and many syntheses of medicines.

Worked example

Forming an enolate.

  1. Propanone has C–H bonds next to C=O.
  2. A strong base removes one of these protons.
  3. The negative charge spreads onto oxygen by resonance.
  4. The enolate attacks an electrophile, forming a C–C bond.
Common mistakeThinking alkyl groups stabilise carbanions as they do carbocations. They destabilise negative charge.

In 3 lines: Lone pair, negative charge. Stabilised by resonance. Makes C–C bonds.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which carbanion is least stable?
    • A methyl carbanion
    • A primary carbanion
    • A tertiary carbanion
    • An enolate ion
    Show the answer

    Answer: A tertiary carbanion. Alkyl groups push electron density onto an already negative carbon.

  2. Why are hydrogens next to a C=O group relatively acidic?
    • The C=O group donates electrons to carbon
    • Those hydrogens are bonded to oxygen
    • Carbonyl groups make carbon more electropositive
    • The carbanion formed is stabilised by resonance onto oxygen
    Show the answer

    Answer: The carbanion formed is stabilised by resonance onto oxygen. Delocalisation spreads the negative charge.

  3. Why are carbanions useful in synthesis?
    • They are nucleophiles that form new carbon-carbon bonds
    • They are electrophiles that accept electrons
    • They break C–C bonds only
    • They are always unreactive
    Show the answer

    Answer: They are nucleophiles that form new carbon-carbon bonds. Building C–C bonds assembles larger molecules.

Key terms

Carbanion
A carbon species with a negative charge and lone pair
Enolate
A resonance-stabilised anion next to a carbonyl
Organolithium
A reagent with a carbon-lithium bond
