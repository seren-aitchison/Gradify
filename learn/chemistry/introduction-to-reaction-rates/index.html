Introduction to Reaction Rates

Rate is the change in concentration or another measurable quantity per unit time, found from gradients of graphs, with initial rate from the tangent at t = 0; methods include gas volume, mass loss, colour change and precipitate formation.

Chemistry · Chemical Kinetics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define rate of reaction
  • Calculate rates from experimental data
  • Describe methods for measuring rates

Defining rate

  • Rate = change in concentration ÷ time, often in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
  • Can also be measured as change in mass, volume of gas or colour per unit time.
  • Initial rate is the rate at the start, found from the gradient of the tangent at t = 0.
  • Rate usually decreases as reactants are used up.

Measuring rates

  • Gas volume: gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder.
  • Mass loss: balance, when gas escapes.
  • Colour change: colorimeter.
  • Precipitate: time to obscure a cross ("disappearing cross").

The gradient of a concentration-time graph at any point gives the rate at that moment. Rates matter in industry, where faster reactions save time and money, and in safety, where uncontrolled fast reactions can be dangerous.

Worked example

Rate from gas volume.

  1. Magnesium reacts with acid, producing hydrogen.
  2. In the first 20 s, 40 cm³ of gas is collected.
  3. Mean rate = 40 ÷ 20 = 2.0 cm³ s⁻¹.
  4. The rate slows as the acid is used up.
Common mistakeThinking rate stays constant throughout a reaction. It usually decreases as reactants are used up.

In 3 lines: Change per unit time. Gradients give rates. Several measuring methods.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which method suits a reaction that produces a gas?
    • Timing a colour change only
    • Measuring the pH once
    • Weighing the reactants before starting
    • Collecting the gas in a gas syringe
    Show the answer

    Answer: Collecting the gas in a gas syringe. Gas volume collected over time shows the rate.

  2. Why does the rate of most reactions decrease over time?
    • Reactants are used up, so collisions become less frequent
    • Products speed up the reaction
    • Temperature always rises
    • Catalysts are consumed
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reactants are used up, so collisions become less frequent. Lower concentration means fewer collisions per second.

  3. Why is a colorimeter useful for measuring rate?
    • It measures the gas produced
    • It measures concentration changes continuously without disturbing the reaction
    • It speeds up the reaction
    • It weighs the products
    Show the answer

    Answer: It measures concentration changes continuously without disturbing the reaction. Absorbance tracks coloured species as they change.

Key terms

Rate of reaction
Change in concentration per unit time
Initial rate
The rate at the start of a reaction
Tangent
A straight line touching a curve at one point
