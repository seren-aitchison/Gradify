Uncertainty and Measurement Limitations

No measurement is exact. Every result carries an uncertainty from random scatter, systematic bias and instrument resolution, and ultimately from fundamental quantum limits. Reporting a value without its uncertainty tells only half the story.

Physics · Foundations of Physics · about 2 minutes

Sources of uncertainty

  • Random: scatter that averages down with repeats (uncertainty in the mean ∝ 1/√n).
  • Systematic: a consistent bias (zero error, calibration, parallax); repeats don't reduce it. Find it by calibration or a different method.
  • Resolution: the smallest scale division or digit; reading uncertainty is often taken as half a division.

Precision, accuracy, repeatability

  • Precise: small spread. Accurate: close to the true value. A result can be precise and inaccurate (systematic error).
  • Repeatable: same method, same person. Reproducible: different people, methods or labs.
σmean = σ/√nstandard error of the mean from n repeats
(Δz/z)2 = (Δx/x)2 + (Δy/y)2for z = xy or x/y with independent errors

Fundamental limits

Quantum mechanics sets limits no apparatus can beat: Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, and shot noise in light (√N fluctuations in photon number). Thermal (Johnson) noise limits electrical measurements. LIGO measures displacements of 10-19 m by fighting all of these, including using squeezed light.

Worked example

Five timings of a pendulum's 10 oscillations: 15.2, 15.4, 15.1, 15.3, 15.5 s. Find T with uncertainty.

  1. Mean = 15.30 s; standard deviation ≈ 0.16 s; standard error = 0.16/√5 ≈ 0.07 s
  2. 10 oscillations: T = 1.530 ± 0.007 s
Common mistakeRepeating a biased measurement 1000 times gives a precise wrong answer. Averaging only fights random error.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A zero error on a micrometer is an example of:
    • A random error
    • A resolution limit
    • A quantum limit
    • A systematic error
    Show the answer

    Answer: A systematic error. It shifts every reading by the same amount.

  2. Length 2.00 ± 0.02 m and width 1.00 ± 0.02 m. What is the percentage uncertainty in the area (adding in quadrature)?
    • About 2.2%
    • 3%
    • 1%
    • 0.04%
    Show the answer

    Answer: About 2.2%. √(12 + 22)% ≈ 2.2%.

  3. To halve the random uncertainty in a mean, you need:
    • twice as many readings
    • 4 times as many readings
    • half as many readings
    • 8 times as many readings
    Show the answer

    Answer: 4 times as many readings. σmean ∝ 1/√n.
