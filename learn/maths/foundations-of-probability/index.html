Foundations of Probability

Three axioms imply every rule; P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B); draw Venn diagrams from the centre out.

Mathematics · Probability · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • State Kolmogorov's axioms and derive basic consequences
  • Use the addition rule and inclusion–exclusion

The axioms and their consequences

  • From the axioms: P(∅) = 0; P(A′) = 1 − P(A); if A ⊆ B then P(A) ≤ P(B); P(A) ≤ 1.
  • Addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B); the overlap would otherwise be counted twice.
  • For mutually exclusive events the overlap is 0 and the probabilities simply add.

Inclusion–exclusion

  • Three events: P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A ∩ B) − P(A ∩ C) − P(B ∩ C) + P(A ∩ B ∩ C).
  • Venn diagrams make the regions visible: fill in the intersection first and work outwards.
  • The derangement problem (no letter in the right envelope, probability about 1/e) is a famous application.

Interpretations

  • Frequentist: probability is a long-run relative frequency.
  • Bayesian: probability is a degree of belief, updated by evidence.
  • Both obey the same axioms, so the mathematics is the same; they differ in what the numbers mean.
Worked example

P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Find P(A ∪ B) and the probability of neither.

  1. P(A ∪ B) = 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7
  2. Neither is the complement of A ∪ B
  3. P(neither) = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3

Investigate with the lab. Run small and large numbers of trials. Small runs can be far from the probability, which is why probabilities are long-run statements, not guarantees.

Common mistakeAdding P(A) and P(B) for events that overlap.

In 2 lines: Three axioms underpin all of probability. Subtract the overlap when adding.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which follows directly from Kolmogorov's axioms?
    • P(∅) = 0
    • P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)
    • P(A ∣ B) = P(A)
    • All outcomes are equally likely
    • P(A) = 0.5
    Show the answer

    Answer: P(∅) = 0. Ω and ∅ are mutually exclusive and Ω ∪ ∅ = Ω, so P(∅) must be 0.

  2. Can P(A) = 0.7 and P(B) = 0.6 for mutually exclusive events A and B?
    • Yes, always
    • No: their probabilities would add to 1.3
    • Yes, if they're independent
    • Only if Ω is infinite
    • Only if P(A ∩ B) = 0.3
    Show the answer

    Answer: No: their probabilities would add to 1.3. Mutually exclusive means P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B), which can't exceed 1.

  3. After 5 heads in a row with a fair coin, what is the probability the next flip is heads?
    • Less than 0.5, since tails is due
    • More than 0.5, since heads is on a streak
    • 0.5, since flips are independent
    • 0, since six heads is impossible
    • It depends on earlier flips
    Show the answer

    Answer: 0.5, since flips are independent. Independence means past results don't change the next probability (the gambler's fallacy).

Key terms

Kolmogorov's axioms
P(A) ≥ 0; P(Ω) = 1; for mutually exclusive A₁, A₂, …, P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ …) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …
Mutually exclusive
Events that can't happen together: A ∩ B = ∅
