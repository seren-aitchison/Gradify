Complex Analysis

Holomorphic ⇔ Cauchy–Riemann; holomorphic functions are analytic; ∮ f dz = 2πi Σ residues; Liouville and contour methods give powerful results.

Mathematics · Analysis · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Test functions with the Cauchy–Riemann equations
  • Evaluate integrals using Cauchy's theorem and residues

Holomorphic functions

  • f = u + iv is holomorphic iff the Cauchy–Riemann equations ux = vy, uy = −vx hold (with continuous partials).
  • z2, ez, sin z are holomorphic; conj(z) and |z| aren't.
  • Holomorphic functions are analytic (power series), and their real and imaginary parts are harmonic.

Cauchy's theorems

  • Cauchy's theorem: ∮ f dz = 0 around closed curves in a simply connected region where f is holomorphic.
  • Cauchy's integral formula: f(a) = (1/2πi) ∮ f(z)/(z − a) dz.
  • Liouville: bounded entire functions are constant, giving a quick proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Residues

  • Residue theorem: ∮ f dz = 2πi × (sum of residues inside).
  • Simple pole at a: Res = lim (z − a)f(z).
  • Evaluates real integrals such as ∫ dx/(1 + x2) and Fourier-type integrals.
Worked example

Use residues to evaluate ∫−∞∞ dx/(1 + x2).

  1. Close the contour with a large upper semicircle; 1/(1 + z2) has a simple pole at i inside
  2. Res at i = lim (z − i)/((z − i)(z + i)) = 1/(2i)
  3. Integral = 2πi × 1/(2i) = π (the arc contributes 0 as its radius grows)

Investigate with the lab. Multiply by several w with modulus 1. Each product rotates z without stretching it: the local behaviour of multiplication that makes holomorphic maps angle-preserving.

Common mistakeIncluding poles outside the contour in the sum of residues.

In 2 lines: Complex differentiability is very strong. Residues evaluate integrals.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which function is entire (holomorphic everywhere)?
    • 1/z
    • ez
    • |z|2
    • conj(z)
    • 1/(1 − z)
    Show the answer

    Answer: ez. ez has a convergent power series everywhere; the others have poles or fail Cauchy–Riemann.

  2. How does Liouville's theorem prove the fundamental theorem of algebra?
    • It shows all polynomials are linear
    • It computes roots explicitly
    • If p had no roots, 1/p would be bounded and entire, hence constant
    • It applies only to real polynomials
    • It bounds every polynomial on ℂ, forcing all of them to be constant
    Show the answer

    Answer: If p had no roots, 1/p would be bounded and entire, hence constant. A non-constant polynomial p makes 1/p non-constant, so p must have a root.

  3. Why does multiplying by a fixed non-zero complex number w preserve angles between directions?
    • It reflects directions, which preserves angles
    • It only works when w is real
    • It changes angles by arg w
    • It rotates every direction by arg w and scales every length by |w|, so angles between directions are unchanged
    • It doesn't preserve angles
    Show the answer

    Answer: It rotates every direction by arg w and scales every length by |w|, so angles between directions are unchanged. A holomorphic map with non-zero derivative acts locally like multiplication by f′(z), which is why it's conformal.

Key terms

Holomorphic function
A function complex-differentiable on an open set
Residue
The coefficient of 1/(z − a) in the Laurent series of f about a
