Risk and Probability

Use natural frequencies; compare expected cost with premiums; insure large, unaffordable losses; remember that independent events don't balance out.

Mathematics · Everyday and Practical Mathematics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Compare risks using natural frequencies and expected values
  • Decide whether insurance or a warranty is worth buying

Communicating risk

  • "3 in 1,000" is clearer than "0.3%" for most people.
  • Absolute risk tells you your chance; relative risk compares groups (see Data Interpretation in School 13).
  • A micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death, used to compare activities.

Insurance and warranties

  • Expected payout = P(claim) × (loss − excess).
  • Premiums are higher than expected payouts on average: that covers the insurer's costs and profit.
  • Insure what you couldn't afford to lose (home, car liability); self-insure small risks (phone cases, extended warranties) if you can.

Everyday probability

  • Weather forecasts: a 70% chance of rain means it rains on about 70% of such days.
  • Independent events don't "balance out" (the gambler's fallacy).
  • Rare events happen somewhere every day because there are so many opportunities.
Worked example

A £600 phone has a 5% chance a year of a claim. Insurance costs £60 a year with a £50 excess. Find the expected payout and comment.

  1. Expected payout = 0.05 × (600 − 50) = £27.50
  2. The premium is £60, more than double the expected payout
  3. It's only worth it if losing £600 would be a serious hardship

Investigate with the lab. Run short experiments several times and see how much results vary. Small samples can make risks look bigger or smaller than they are.

Common mistakeBuying insurance for small, affordable losses where premiums far exceed expected payouts.

In 2 lines: Risk decisions weigh probability and cost. Natural frequencies clarify risk.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is the clearest way to present a 0.2% risk to the public?
    • 2 in 1,000 people
    • 0.002
    • 0.2%
    • One-fifth of a per cent
    • A relative risk of 1.2
    Show the answer

    Answer: 2 in 1,000 people. Natural frequencies are easiest to picture.

  2. An extended warranty on a £300 washing machine costs £90 for 3 years. The chance of a repair in that time is 15%, and a repair costs £150. Is it good value on average?
    • Yes: £90 is less than £300
    • No: the expected cost is £22.50, well below £90
    • Yes: repairs are certain
    • No: repairs cost nothing
    • It's exactly fair
    Show the answer

    Answer: No: the expected cost is £22.50, well below £90. 0.15 × £150 = £22.50 expected; the warranty costs four times that.

  3. Why can a risk estimated from a small sample be misleading?
    • Small samples are always biased upwards
    • Probability doesn't apply to small samples
    • Small samples vary a lot by chance, so the observed frequency can be far from the true probability
    • Large samples are always wrong
    • It can't be misleading
    Show the answer

    Answer: Small samples vary a lot by chance, so the observed frequency can be far from the true probability. The law of large numbers only guarantees convergence as the sample grows.

Key terms

Natural frequency
A probability expressed as a count, such as "3 in 1,000 people"
Expected cost
Probability × cost, summed over outcomes: the long-run average cost
