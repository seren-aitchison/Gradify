Population Dynamics

Exponential and logistic models describe unlimited and limited growth; harvesting gives MSY rK/4; discrete models can be chaotic; Leslie matrices give growth rates and stable age structure.

Mathematics · Mathematical Modelling and Applied Mathematics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Analyse exponential, logistic and discrete-time population models
  • Use Leslie matrices for age-structured populations

Continuous models

  • Exponential: N′ = rN, unbounded growth.
  • Logistic: N′ = rN(1 − N/K), approaching K.
  • Allee effect: growth negative at very low densities.
  • Harvesting: N′ = rN(1 − N/K) − h; maximum sustainable yield rK/4.

Discrete models

  • Logistic map Nt+1 = rNt(1 − Nt): stable, cycling or chaotic depending on r (May 1976).
  • Ricker model for fish stocks.
  • Discrete models can oscillate where continuous ones can't.

Age structure

  • Leslie matrix L: top row fertilities, sub-diagonal survival.
  • Long-run growth rate = dominant eigenvalue λ; stable age distribution = its eigenvector.
  • λ > 1 grows, λ < 1 declines.
Worked example

A Leslie matrix [[0, 4], [0.5, 0]] describes juveniles and adults. Find the long-run growth rate.

  1. Characteristic equation λ2 − 4 × 0.5 = 0
  2. λ2 = 2
  3. λ = √2 ≈ 1.41: the population grows about 41% per time step

Investigate with the lab. Use the logistic equation: start populations at different sizes and watch them approach the carrying capacity. Start exactly at it and see nothing change.

Common mistakeAssuming a population that is growing now will keep the same age structure.

In 2 lines: Births, deaths and limits shape populations. Age structure needs matrices.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. For the Leslie matrix [[0, 2], [0.5, 0]], what happens to the population in the long run?
    • It stays constant, since λ = 1
    • It grows by 41% per step
    • It declines to zero
    • It grows by 100% per step
    • It oscillates chaotically
    Show the answer

    Answer: It stays constant, since λ = 1. The characteristic equation is λ2 = 2 × 0.5 = 1, so the dominant eigenvalue is λ = 1 and the population neither grows nor declines in the long run.

  2. Why can the discrete logistic map be chaotic when the continuous logistic equation isn't?
    • Continuous logistic models are always chaotic, so the map just inherits it
    • Discrete steps can overshoot the equilibrium, creating oscillations that become chaotic as r increases
    • The discrete map has no equilibrium, so it can never settle down
    • Chaos needs at least three variables in discrete time, so it can't happen
    • It can't be chaotic; the discrete logistic map always converges to K
    Show the answer

    Answer: Discrete steps can overshoot the equilibrium, creating oscillations that become chaotic as r increases. A one-dimensional autonomous ODE can't oscillate, but a map can jump past K.

  3. In logistic growth, what happens to a population that starts above the carrying capacity?
    • It keeps growing
    • It falls to zero
    • It declines towards the carrying capacity
    • It stays constant
    • It oscillates forever
    Show the answer

    Answer: It declines towards the carrying capacity. Above K the growth rate rN(1 − N/K) is negative, so N decreases towards K.

Key terms

Carrying capacity
K, the population size an environment can sustain in the long run
Leslie matrix
A matrix of age-specific fertilities and survival rates projecting an age-structured population one step forward
