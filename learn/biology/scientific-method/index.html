Scientific Method

Science tests falsifiable hypotheses against evidence, and its conclusions are strengthened by peer review and replication.

Biology · Foundations of Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the steps of the scientific method as a cycle
  • Distinguish a hypothesis from a theory
  • Explain why peer review and replication matter

The cycle

  • Observation: noticing something that needs explaining.
  • Question and hypothesis: a testable explanation.
  • Prediction: what should happen if the hypothesis is right.
  • Experiment or observation: collecting data under controlled conditions.
  • Analysis and conclusion: do the data support or refute the hypothesis?
  • Communication and peer review: other scientists scrutinise and try to repeat the work.

Results often raise new questions, so the process is a cycle rather than a straight line. Real research also involves creativity, chance discoveries and revision.

Hypotheses, theories and laws

A hypothesis is a tentative explanation that can be tested. A scientific theory is a broad, well-tested explanation supported by many lines of evidence, such as cell theory or the theory of evolution. In science "theory" doesn't mean a guess. Laws describe patterns (often mathematically) without necessarily explaining them.

Falsifiability

A scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable: there must be possible observations that would show it to be wrong. Evidence can support a hypothesis but never prove it absolutely; a single well-replicated contradictory result can refute it.

Worked example

Pasteur's swan-necked flask experiment (1859) tested whether microbes arise spontaneously in broth.

  1. Hypothesis: microbes in spoiled broth come from microbes in the air, not from the broth itself.
  2. Prediction: boiled broth in a flask whose curved neck traps dust will stay clear; breaking the neck will let it spoil.
  3. Result: the broth stayed clear until the neck was broken, then became cloudy with microbes.
  4. Conclusion: the evidence refuted spontaneous generation and supported the hypothesis.
Common mistakeSaying an experiment "proved" a hypothesis. Data support or fail to support it; future evidence could always revise the conclusion.

In 2 lines: Science is a cycle of observation, hypothesis, prediction, testing and revision. Theories are well-tested explanations; hypotheses must be falsifiable.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. In science, a theory is…
    • an untested guess
    • a hypothesis that has been proved true
    • a well-supported explanation based on a large body of evidence
    • the same as a law
    Show the answer

    Answer: a well-supported explanation based on a large body of evidence. Scientific theories such as cell theory are among the most reliable knowledge science has.

  2. Which hypothesis is falsifiable?
    • Plants grow because of an undetectable life force
    • Some plants may respond to music in some way
    • Plants are beautiful
    • Plants grown in red light grow taller than plants grown in blue light
    Show the answer

    Answer: Plants grown in red light grow taller than plants grown in blue light. Only the first makes a specific prediction that an experiment could show to be wrong.

  3. In Pasteur's experiment, what was the purpose of breaking the neck of a flask after the broth had stayed clear?
    • To show that the broth could still support microbial growth once dust could enter, ruling out the idea that boiling had destroyed its "vital" capacity
    • To sterilise the broth
    • To let the broth cool
    • To measure the volume of broth
    Show the answer

    Answer: To show that the broth could still support microbial growth once dust could enter, ruling out the idea that boiling had destroyed its "vital" capacity. Breaking the neck tests the alternative explanation: the broth was still able to support life, so the earlier absence of microbes was due to excluding airborne dust.

Key terms

Hypothesis
A testable, falsifiable explanation for an observation
Theory
A broad explanation supported by a large body of evidence from many sources
Peer review
Evaluation of research by independent experts before publication
