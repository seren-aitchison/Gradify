Cancer Biology

Cancer arises from accumulated mutations in oncogenes (gain of function, one allele) and tumour suppressor genes (usually two hits), plus epigenetic changes, giving hallmarks such as sustained signalling, resisting death, angiogenesis and metastasis, with risk factors such as smoking, UV and age.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how mutations in proto-oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes cause cancer
  • Describe the hallmarks of cancer
  • Identify risk factors for cancer

Genetic basis

  • Cancer develops from mutations that accumulate in a single cell lineage.
  • Oncogenes: mutated proto-oncogenes that are overactive; one mutated allele can be enough (gain of function).
  • Tumour suppressor genes: usually both alleles must be inactivated (two-hit hypothesis), such as RB and TP53.
  • Epigenetic changes, such as hypermethylation of tumour suppressor promoters, also contribute.

Hallmarks

  • Sustained growth signalling and evading growth suppressors.
  • Resisting cell death and enabling unlimited replication (telomerase).
  • Inducing angiogenesis (new blood vessel growth).
  • Activating invasion and metastasis.
  • Evading the immune system and reprogramming metabolism.

Risk factors include tobacco smoke, UV radiation, alcohol, some viruses such as HPV, obesity, inherited mutations such as BRCA1 and increasing age, because mutations accumulate over time. In the UK, about half of people will develop cancer in their lifetime, and around 40% of cases are estimated to be preventable. Treatments include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapies and immunotherapy.

Worked example

The two-hit hypothesis.

  1. A child inherits one faulty RB allele.
  2. A single mutation in the other allele in a retinal cell removes all RB function.
  3. The cell divides uncontrollably, causing retinoblastoma.
  4. People without an inherited fault need two mutations in the same cell, so the cancer is much rarer.
Common mistakeThinking one mutation is usually enough to cause cancer. Most cancers need several mutations to accumulate.

In 3 lines: Oncogenes and tumour suppressors. Hallmarks of cancer. Many risk factors.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What is an oncogene?
    • A gene that repairs DNA damage
    • A mutated proto-oncogene that promotes division
    • A gene for red blood cells
    • A gene that triggers apoptosis
    Show the answer

    Answer: A mutated proto-oncogene that promotes division. Overactive oncogenes drive uncontrolled division.

  2. Why does inheriting one faulty RB allele increase cancer risk?
    • It makes the RB gene overactive
    • It prevents all cells from dividing
    • Only one more mutation is needed to lose all RB function
    • It doubles the number of RB genes
    Show the answer

    Answer: Only one more mutation is needed to lose all RB function. With one hit inherited, a single somatic hit can cause cancer.

  3. Why does cancer risk increase with age?
    • Older people have no tumour suppressor genes
    • Cells stop dividing in old age
    • Telomerase is absent in children
    • Mutations accumulate in cells over time
    Show the answer

    Answer: Mutations accumulate in cells over time. More time allows more mutations to build up.

Key terms

Oncogene
A mutated gene that promotes uncontrolled division
Two-hit hypothesis
The idea that both tumour suppressor alleles must be inactivated
Metastasis
The spread of cancer cells to other parts of the body
