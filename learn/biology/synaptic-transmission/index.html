Synaptic Transmission

At chemical synapses, an action potential opens Ca²⁺ channels, triggering neurotransmitter release that diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors; transmitters are broken down or reabsorbed, synapses can excite or inhibit, summation integrates signals and drugs alter transmission.

Biology · Human Anatomy and Physiology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the structure of a chemical synapse
  • Explain the sequence of events in synaptic transmission
  • Explain how drugs can affect synapses

Structure and events

  • A synapse is the junction between a neurone and another cell, with a synaptic cleft about 20 nm wide.
  • An action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal.
  • Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters.
  • Vesicles release neurotransmitter, such as acetylcholine, by exocytosis.
  • Neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels.

Ending and integration

  • Neurotransmitter is broken down, such as acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase, or taken back up.
  • Excitatory synapses make the postsynaptic cell more likely to fire; inhibitory synapses less likely.
  • Summation: several inputs combine to reach threshold.
  • Synapses ensure one-way transmission, because receptors are only on the postsynaptic side.

Many drugs act at synapses. SSRIs, used for depression, block reuptake of serotonin. Nicotine mimics acetylcholine at some receptors. Some nerve agents and insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, causing continuous stimulation. Synapses allow integration and learning, as their strength can change.

Worked example

How SSRIs work.

  1. Serotonin is released into the synaptic cleft.
  2. Normally it is taken back into the presynaptic neurone by transporters.
  3. SSRIs block these transporters.
  4. Serotonin stays in the cleft longer, increasing its effect.
Common mistakeThinking the electrical impulse jumps across the synaptic cleft. At chemical synapses a neurotransmitter carries the signal.

In 3 lines: Ca²⁺ triggers release. Transmitter crosses the cleft. Drugs act at synapses.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How does a neurotransmitter cross the synaptic cleft?
    • By active transport
    • Along the axon
    • By diffusion
    • Through the myelin sheath
    Show the answer

    Answer: By diffusion. Neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the narrow gap.

  2. Why is synaptic transmission one-way?
    • Neurotransmitter is made in both neurones
    • The cleft is too wide to cross backwards
    • Calcium enters both sides equally
    • Receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane
    Show the answer

    Answer: Receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane. Only the postsynaptic side has receptors.

  3. Why do acetylcholinesterase inhibitors cause continuous muscle stimulation?
    • Acetylcholine is not broken down and keeps binding receptors
    • They block acetylcholine release
    • They destroy postsynaptic receptors
    • They speed up acetylcholine breakdown
    Show the answer

    Answer: Acetylcholine is not broken down and keeps binding receptors. The transmitter persists in the cleft and keeps stimulating the muscle.

Key terms

Synapse
A junction between a neurone and another cell
Neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger released at a synapse
Summation
Combining of inputs to reach threshold
