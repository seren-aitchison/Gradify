Sanger Sequencing

Sanger sequencing uses fluorescently labelled dideoxynucleotides that terminate chains, producing fragments of every length that are separated by capillary electrophoresis; the order of colours gives the sequence, accurately reading up to about 1,000 bases.

Biology · Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the role of dideoxynucleotides
  • Describe the steps of Sanger sequencing
  • Interpret a Sanger sequencing trace

Chain termination

  • Dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) lack a 3′-OH group.
  • When incorporated, no further nucleotide can be added, so the chain terminates.
  • The reaction contains template, primer, DNA polymerase, normal dNTPs and a small amount of ddNTPs.
  • Fragments of every length are produced, each ending at a known base.

Reading the sequence

  • Each ddNTP is labelled with a different fluorescent dye.
  • Fragments are separated by size in capillary electrophoresis.
  • A laser detects the colour of each fragment as it passes.
  • The order of colours gives the sequence, read from smallest to largest fragment.

Frederick Sanger developed the method in 1977 and won his second Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1980. Sanger sequencing reads up to about 1,000 bases with high accuracy, so it is still used to confirm specific mutations and check cloned genes. It is slower and more expensive per base than next-generation methods.

Worked example

Reading a trace.

  1. A sequencing trace shows coloured peaks in order.
  2. The first peaks are green, blue, black and red.
  3. The dyes represent A, C, G and T respectively.
  4. The sequence begins ACGT, reading from the shortest fragment.
Common mistakeThinking ddNTPs are added in large amounts. Only a small proportion is used, so termination occurs at every position.

In 3 lines: ddNTPs stop chains. Fragments separated by size. Colours give the sequence.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does each ddNTP carry so it can be identified?
    • A radioactive phosphate only
    • A magnetic bead
    • An extra 3′-OH group
    • A different fluorescent dye
    Show the answer

    Answer: A different fluorescent dye. Coloured dyes show which base ended each fragment.

  2. Why is only a small amount of ddNTP added to the reaction?
    • So chains terminate at every position, not just early
    • So no chains are terminated
    • To make the DNA longer
    • To stop the primer binding
    Show the answer

    Answer: So chains terminate at every position, not just early. Rare termination produces fragments of all lengths.

  3. Why is Sanger sequencing still used today?
    • It is the cheapest per base
    • It is highly accurate for confirming specific sequences
    • It reads whole genomes in minutes
    • It needs no primers
    Show the answer

    Answer: It is highly accurate for confirming specific sequences. Accuracy makes it ideal for checking mutations.

Key terms

Dideoxynucleotide
A nucleotide lacking a 3′-OH that stops DNA synthesis
Chain termination
Stopping DNA synthesis at a specific base
Capillary electrophoresis
Separating DNA in thin tubes by size
