Habitat Fragmentation

Habitat fragmentation creates small, isolated patches with edge effects and reduced populations; island biogeography predicts more species in larger, closer patches, and corridors and the Lawton principles of more, bigger, better and joined sites help reverse fragmentation.

Biology · Ecology and Environmental Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define habitat fragmentation
  • Explain edge effects and isolation
  • Describe solutions such as corridors

Fragmentation

  • Large habitats are split into small patches by roads, farms and buildings.
  • Small patches support fewer species and smaller populations.
  • Edge effects: edges have different light, temperature, wind and predators from the interior.
  • Species needing interior conditions decline.

Isolation and solutions

  • Island biogeography theory: larger, closer patches support more species.
  • Isolated populations lose genetic diversity through drift and inbreeding.
  • Wildlife corridors connect patches, such as hedgerows and green bridges.
  • The Lawton Review (2010) recommended "more, bigger, better and joined" sites.

In England, the Lawton Review argued that wildlife sites were too small and isolated. Hedgerows act as corridors for dormice and other species. The Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project in the Amazon has shown that small fragments lose species rapidly.

Worked example

A road through a forest.

  1. A new road splits a forest into two halves.
  2. Small mammals avoid crossing, and many are killed when they try.
  3. Populations on each side become isolated.
  4. A green bridge over the road reconnects them.
Common mistakeThinking a patch of habitat is as good as the same area spread out. Small fragments have more edge and less interior.

In 3 lines: Small, isolated patches. Edge effects and isolation. Corridors and joined sites.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What can connect isolated habitat patches?
    • New roads
    • Housing estates
    • Fences
    • Wildlife corridors
    Show the answer

    Answer: Wildlife corridors. Corridors allow animals to move between patches.

  2. Why do isolated populations lose genetic diversity?
    • Drift and inbreeding act strongly without gene flow
    • They gain alleles from other populations
    • Isolation increases mutation rates
    • They grow very large
    Show the answer

    Answer: Drift and inbreeding act strongly without gene flow. Small isolated groups lose alleles by chance.

  3. According to island biogeography, which patch supports most species?
    • A small, isolated patch
    • A large patch close to other habitat
    • A small patch next to a road
    • Any patch regardless of size
    Show the answer

    Answer: A large patch close to other habitat. Larger, closer patches have more colonisation and less extinction.

Key terms

Habitat fragmentation
Breaking a habitat into small isolated patches
Edge effect
Different conditions at a habitat's edge
Wildlife corridor
A strip of habitat connecting patches
