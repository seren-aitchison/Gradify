Protein Denaturation

Denaturation is the loss of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, leaving primary structure intact; high temperature, extreme pH, heavy metals and detergents cause it, destroying shapes such as active sites and stopping function.

Biology · Biochemistry and Biomolecules · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define denaturation
  • Explain how temperature, pH and chemicals cause denaturation
  • Explain the consequences of denaturation for function

What denaturation is

  • Denaturation is the disruption of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure.
  • The primary structure (peptide bonds) is usually unaffected.
  • Function is lost because the specific shape, such as an active site, is lost.
  • Some proteins can refold if conditions return to normal; many cannot.

Causes

  • High temperature: increased vibration breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds.
  • Extreme pH: changes the charges on R groups, disrupting ionic and hydrogen bonds.
  • Heavy metal ions, such as mercury and lead, bind to R groups.
  • Detergents and organic solvents disrupt hydrophobic interactions.

Cooking egg white is a visible example: heat denatures albumin, which becomes opaque and solid and does not return to its original form. Fever above about 41 °C is dangerous partly because of protein denaturation. Enzymes from thermophilic bacteria, such as Taq polymerase, resist denaturation at high temperatures, making them useful in PCR.

Worked example

Denaturing an enzyme.

  1. Amylase is heated to 80 °C.
  2. Hydrogen and ionic bonds holding the tertiary structure break.
  3. The active site changes shape, so starch can no longer bind.
  4. When cooled, the enzyme does not regain activity.
Common mistakeSaying denaturation breaks peptide bonds. It disrupts higher-level structure; the amino acid sequence usually remains.

In 3 lines: Shape lost; sequence kept. Heat, pH, metals, detergents. Function lost.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What happens to an enzyme's active site when it is denatured?
    • It changes shape so the substrate no longer fits
    • It becomes more specific
    • It doubles in size
    • It forms new peptide bonds
    Show the answer

    Answer: It changes shape so the substrate no longer fits. Denaturation alters the active site's shape, stopping activity.

  2. Why is Taq polymerase useful in PCR?
    • It works only at 0 °C
    • It resists denaturation at high temperatures
    • It breaks down DNA quickly
    • It is a lipid
    Show the answer

    Answer: It resists denaturation at high temperatures. Taq comes from a thermophile and stays active at PCR temperatures.

  3. Why does a cooked egg white not return to clear liquid when cooled?
    • Cooling breaks peptide bonds
    • Heat converts protein to fat
    • Albumin is irreversibly denatured and aggregated
    • Albumin is not a protein
    Show the answer

    Answer: Albumin is irreversibly denatured and aggregated. The denatured protein chains tangle and cannot refold.

Key terms

Denaturation
Loss of a protein's 3D structure and function
Thermophile
An organism adapted to high temperatures
Taq polymerase
A heat-stable DNA polymerase from Thermus aquaticus
