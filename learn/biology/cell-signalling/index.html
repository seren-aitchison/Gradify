Cell Signalling

Signals act through specific receptors; cell-surface receptors pass the message on through second messengers such as cAMP, amplifying it in a cascade.

Biology · Cell Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main types of cell signalling by the distance signals travel
  • Distinguish cell-surface and intracellular receptors
  • Explain how a second messenger amplifies a signal

Signals by distance

  • Autocrine: a cell signals to itself.
  • Paracrine: signals act on nearby cells, such as growth factors and neurotransmitters.
  • Endocrine: hormones travel in the blood to distant target cells.
  • Direct contact: molecules on one cell bind receptors on another, or small molecules pass through gap junctions.

Receptors

A cell responds only to signals it has receptors for. Water-soluble signals such as adrenaline, insulin and most growth factors can't cross the membrane and bind cell-surface receptors: G-protein-coupled receptors, receptor tyrosine kinases, or ion channels that open when the signal binds. Lipid-soluble signals such as steroid hormones diffuse through the membrane and bind intracellular receptors, which then act as transcription factors.

Transduction and amplification

At a cell-surface receptor, the signal is the first messenger; it doesn't enter the cell. Binding activates proteins that make a second messenger inside, such as cyclic AMP. Adrenaline binding to liver cells activates adenylyl cyclase, which makes cAMP, which activates protein kinase A, which sets off a chain of enzymes that breaks down glycogen into glucose. Each step activates many molecules of the next, so a few hormone molecules release vast numbers of glucose molecules.

Because a response depends on the receptor and the cell's machinery, the same signal can do different things in different cells: adrenaline speeds the heart, relaxes airway muscle and releases glucose from the liver.

Common mistakeThinking hormones enter every cell they reach. Only target cells with the right receptor respond, and water-soluble hormones never enter the cell at all.

In 3 lines: Signals can be autocrine, paracrine, endocrine or by direct contact. Water-soluble signals use surface receptors; lipid-soluble ones use receptors inside the cell. Second-messenger cascades amplify signals.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which type of signalling uses hormones carried in the blood?
    • Paracrine
    • Endocrine
    • Autocrine
    • Direct contact
    Show the answer

    Answer: Endocrine. Endocrine signals travel through the bloodstream to distant targets.

  2. Adrenaline makes liver cells release glucose but has no effect on many other cells. Why?
    • Adrenaline can only reach the liver
    • Other cells have no glycogen
    • Only cells with adrenaline receptors respond
    • Adrenaline enters only liver cells
    Show the answer

    Answer: Only cells with adrenaline receptors respond. Responsiveness depends on having the specific receptor, and on what the cell's machinery does with the signal.

  3. One molecule of adrenaline can lead to the release of millions of glucose molecules. What explains this?
    • Adrenaline is broken down into glucose
    • Glucose is pumped in by adrenaline
    • Adrenaline copies itself inside the cell
    • Each step of the second-messenger cascade activates many molecules of the next
    Show the answer

    Answer: Each step of the second-messenger cascade activates many molecules of the next. Amplification at each enzyme step multiplies the signal enormously.

Key terms

Receptor
A protein that binds a specific signal molecule and triggers a response
Second messenger
A small molecule inside the cell, such as cAMP, that relays a signal from a cell-surface receptor
Signal transduction
The conversion of an external signal into a response inside the cell
