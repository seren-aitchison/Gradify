ATP and Biological Energy

ATP consists of adenine, ribose and three phosphates; hydrolysis to ADP and Pi by ATP hydrolase releases energy in small amounts for active transport, contraction and synthesis, and ATP synthase regenerates ATP in respiration and photosynthesis.

Biology · Biochemistry and Biomolecules · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the structure of ATP
  • Explain how ATP hydrolysis releases energy for cellular processes
  • Describe how ATP is resynthesised

Structure and hydrolysis

  • Adenosine triphosphate consists of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups.
  • Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate, catalysed by ATP hydrolase (ATPase), forms ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi), releasing about 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹ under standard conditions.
  • The released energy is coupled to energy-requiring reactions.
  • Phosphate can be transferred to other molecules (phosphorylation), making them more reactive.

Uses and resynthesis

  • Uses: active transport, muscle contraction, synthesis of macromolecules, nerve impulses and cell division.
  • ATP is resynthesised from ADP and Pi by ATP synthase, in a condensation reaction.
  • This happens in respiration (mitochondria and cytoplasm) and photosynthesis (chloroplasts).
  • A typical human turns over roughly their own body mass in ATP each day.

ATP is suited to its role because it releases energy in small, manageable amounts, is easily hydrolysed in a single step and is quickly regenerated. It cannot be stored in large amounts, so cells make it continuously. ATP is not a long-term energy store; that role belongs to glucose, glycogen, starch and triglycerides.

Worked example

ATP in active transport.

  1. The sodium-potassium pump moves three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in against their gradients.
  2. ATP binds the pump and is hydrolysed to ADP and Pi.
  3. The phosphate attaches to the pump, changing its shape.
  4. Ions are moved, and the pump returns to its original shape when Pi is released.
Common mistakeCalling ATP a long-term energy store. It is an immediate energy carrier made and used continuously.

In 3 lines: Adenine, ribose, three phosphates. Hydrolysis releases energy. Resynthesised in respiration and photosynthesis.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which molecules make up ATP?
    • Glucose and three phosphates
    • Adenine, deoxyribose and one phosphate
    • Ribose and four phosphates
    • Adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups
    Show the answer

    Answer: Adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups. ATP is adenosine (adenine + ribose) with three phosphates.

  2. Why is ATP suited to being the immediate energy source in cells?
    • It releases energy in small amounts in a single hydrolysis step
    • It stores huge amounts of energy for months
    • It cannot be regenerated
    • It is insoluble in water
    Show the answer

    Answer: It releases energy in small amounts in a single hydrolysis step. Small, quick energy releases match cellular needs.

  3. Why don't cells store large amounts of ATP?
    • ATP is toxic in any amount
    • It is unstable and quickly hydrolysed, so it is made continuously instead
    • ATP is too large to fit in cells
    • ATP cannot dissolve in cytoplasm
    Show the answer

    Answer: It is unstable and quickly hydrolysed, so it is made continuously instead. Glycogen and fat store energy; ATP is constantly recycled.

Key terms

ATP
Adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency
ATP hydrolase
The enzyme that hydrolyses ATP to ADP and Pi
ATP synthase
The enzyme that makes ATP from ADP and Pi
