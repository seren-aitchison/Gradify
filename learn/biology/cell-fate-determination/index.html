Cell Fate Determination

Cells progress from specification, a reversible bias, to determination, a fixed commitment, to differentiation; fate is set by cytoplasmic determinants and signals from neighbours and locked in by master transcription factors such as MyoD and epigenetic marks.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish specification, determination and differentiation
  • Explain how cytoplasmic determinants and signals influence fate
  • Describe how transcription factors lock in cell identity

Stages of commitment

  • Specification: a cell is biased towards a fate but can still change if moved.
  • Determination: the cell is committed and will follow its fate even if moved.
  • Differentiation: the cell develops specialised structure and function.
  • Potency generally narrows as development proceeds.

Mechanisms

  • Cytoplasmic determinants: molecules unevenly distributed in the egg are inherited by some cells, as in early frog and fly embryos (autonomous specification).
  • Cell signalling from neighbours: conditional specification, common in mammals.
  • Master transcription factors, such as MyoD for muscle, switch on cell-type programmes.
  • Epigenetic marks help maintain identity through divisions.

Transplantation experiments test commitment: a cell moved to a new position that adopts its new neighbours' fate was specified but not determined. MyoD can convert some other cell types into muscle-like cells, showing the power of master regulators. Yamanaka factors reverse determination by reprogramming adult cells into iPSCs.

Worked example

Testing determination.

  1. In an early frog embryo, cells destined to form skin are moved to a region that forms neural tissue.
  2. If moved early, they form neural tissue: they were specified but not determined.
  3. If moved later, they still form skin: they were determined.
  4. Commitment increases over time.
Common mistakeThinking determined cells look specialised. Determination is a commitment that may not yet be visible; differentiation produces the specialised features.

In 3 lines: Specified, then determined, then differentiated. Determinants and signals. Transcription factors lock identity.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which stage involves a cell developing specialised structures and functions?
    • Specification
    • Determination
    • Fertilisation
    • Differentiation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Differentiation. Differentiation produces the specialised cell type.

  2. A cell moved to a new region adopts the fate of its new neighbours. What does this show?
    • It was specified but not yet determined
    • It was fully determined
    • It had already differentiated
    • It was a gamete
    Show the answer

    Answer: It was specified but not yet determined. Reversible fate shows specification without determination.

  3. What does the conversion of fibroblasts into muscle-like cells by MyoD show?
    • Fibroblasts have no genes
    • Master transcription factors can drive whole cell-type programmes
    • Muscle cells cannot be made in embryos
    • MyoD destroys DNA
    Show the answer

    Answer: Master transcription factors can drive whole cell-type programmes. One regulator can switch on many muscle genes.

Key terms

Specification
A reversible bias towards a cell fate
Determination
An irreversible commitment to a cell fate
Master transcription factor
A protein that switches on a cell-type programme
