Graphs and Data Presentation

Match the graph to the data: line graphs for continuous data, bar charts for categories, histograms for frequency distributions, scatter graphs for correlations.

Biology · Foundations of Biology · about 3 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Construct a results table with correct headings and units
  • Choose the appropriate type of graph for a data set
  • Describe and interpret trends, including rates from gradients

Results tables

Put the independent variable in the first column and the dependent variable (with repeats and means) in the next columns. Column headings give the quantity and unit, separated by a solidus: "Temperature / °C". Units go in headings, not in the body of the table. Record raw data to the instrument's resolution and means to one more decimal place at most.

Which graph?

  • Line graph: both variables continuous, showing how the dependent variable changes with the independent variable (rate against temperature).
  • Bar chart: the independent variable is categorical (blood group, species).
  • Histogram: the frequency of a continuous variable in classes, with bars touching (heights of students).
  • Scatter graph: looking for a correlation between two measured variables, often with a line of best fit.

Drawing graphs

  • Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis, both labelled with units.
  • Scales should use more than half the grid and go up in even steps.
  • Plot points precisely (small crosses), then join with straight lines point to point or draw a line or curve of best fit as appropriate.
  • Show variability with error bars or range bars where repeats exist.

Reading graphs

Describe the trend (as x increases, y increases …), quote data with units, and note changes in gradient. The gradient of a graph of amount against time gives the rate; for a curve, draw a tangent at the chosen point.

Worked example

The volume of oxygen released by an enzyme reaction is 0 cm³ at 0 s and 18 cm³ at 60 s on the straight early part of the graph. What is the initial rate?

  1. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 18 ÷ 60 = 0.3 cm³ s⁻¹.
Common mistakeJoining points on a bar chart, or drawing gaps between histogram bars. Categories aren't continuous, and histogram classes are.

In 2 lines: Tables: independent variable first, units in headings. Choose the graph by the type of data; rates come from gradients.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which variable goes on the x-axis?
    • The independent variable
    • The dependent variable
    • Whichever has the larger numbers
    • The control variable
    Show the answer

    Answer: The independent variable. Convention places the variable that is changed on the x-axis and the measured variable on the y-axis.

  2. A curve of product mass against time flattens out after 5 minutes. What does this show?
    • The reaction has sped up
    • The rate has fallen to zero, probably because a reactant has been used up
    • The enzyme has been made
    • The product is being destroyed
    Show the answer

    Answer: The rate has fallen to zero, probably because a reactant has been used up. A horizontal section means no further change in product: the reaction has stopped.

  3. On a graph of oxygen volume against time, the tangent at 30 s passes through (0 s, 4 cm³) and (60 s, 22 cm³). What is the rate at 30 s?
    • 0.73 cm³ s⁻¹
    • 0.37 cm³ s⁻¹
    • 0.30 cm³ s⁻¹
    • 18 cm³
    Show the answer

    Answer: 0.30 cm³ s⁻¹. Gradient of the tangent = (22 − 4) ÷ (60 − 0) = 18 ÷ 60 = 0.30 cm³ s⁻¹.

Key terms

Gradient
The change in y divided by the change in x; on a graph of amount against time it is the rate
Histogram
A chart of the frequency of a continuous variable in classes, with adjacent bars
Error bar
A line on a graph showing the variability or uncertainty of a value
