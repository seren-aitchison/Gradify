Statistical Analysis

Tests are chosen by data type and design: t-tests and ANOVA compare means, chi-squared compares frequencies, correlation tests association and non-parametric tests avoid normality assumptions; p-values, confidence intervals and effect sizes are interpreted together.

Biology · Experimental Biology and Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Choose an appropriate statistical test
  • Interpret p-values and confidence intervals
  • Distinguish statistical and biological significance

Choosing tests

  • t-test: compares means of two groups.
  • ANOVA: compares means of three or more groups.
  • Chi-squared test: compares observed and expected frequencies.
  • Correlation (Pearson or Spearman): tests association between two variables.
  • Mann-Whitney U: compares two groups without assuming normal distribution.

Interpreting

  • A p-value is the probability of results at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true.
  • p < 0.05 is commonly used as a threshold.
  • Confidence intervals show the range of plausible values for an effect.
  • Effect size shows how big a difference is.

A statistically significant result may be too small to matter biologically, while a large effect may not reach significance with small samples. Correlation does not show causation. Data should be checked for assumptions, such as normality, before choosing parametric tests.

Worked example

Choosing a test.

  1. A student compares mean leaf length in sun and shade plants.
  2. The data are continuous and roughly normal, with two groups.
  3. An independent t-test is chosen.
  4. p = 0.01, and the 95% confidence interval for the difference is 3–9 mm.
Common mistakeThinking p < 0.05 means the hypothesis is 95% likely to be true. A p-value is about data given the null hypothesis.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which test compares observed and expected frequencies?
    • t-test
    • ANOVA
    • Chi-squared test
    • Pearson correlation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Chi-squared test. The chi-squared test compares counts across categories.

  2. Why might a significant result be biologically unimportant?
    • Significance always means importance
    • Biological importance needs no data
    • Small effects cannot be significant
    • The effect may be too small to matter
    Show the answer

    Answer: The effect may be too small to matter. Large samples can detect trivial differences.

  3. Why does correlation not prove causation?
    • A third factor or chance could explain the association
    • Correlation values are always zero
    • Causation needs no evidence
    • Correlation measures only means
    Show the answer

    Answer: A third factor or chance could explain the association. Association alone cannot establish cause.

Key terms

p-value
Probability of results this extreme if the null is true
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for an effect
Effect size
The magnitude of a difference or relationship
