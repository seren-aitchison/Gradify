Endocytosis and Exocytosis

Endocytosis and exocytosis move large particles and molecules in vesicles, using energy; receptor-mediated endocytosis is specific.

Biology · Cell Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe phagocytosis, pinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis
  • Describe exocytosis and give examples
  • Explain why bulk transport needs energy

Endocytosis

  • Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): the membrane extends around a large particle such as a bacterium, enclosing it in a vacuole that then fuses with lysosomes. Neutrophils and macrophages do this.
  • Pinocytosis ("cell drinking"): small droplets of extracellular fluid are taken in.
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis: specific molecules bind receptors gathered in pits coated with the protein clathrin; the pit pinches off as a vesicle. Cells take in cholesterol carried by LDL particles this way.

Exocytosis

In exocytosis, a vesicle moves to the plasma membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside and adding its membrane to the cell surface. Examples include the secretion of digestive enzymes by the pancreas, release of neurotransmitter at synapses, and secretion of insulin. In regulated secretion, a signal (often a rise in Ca²⁺) triggers the fusion.

Energy and membrane balance

Both processes need ATP, to rearrange the cytoskeleton and move vesicles. They also balance each other: endocytosis removes membrane from the surface and exocytosis adds it back, so the cell's surface area stays roughly constant.

In familial hypercholesterolaemia, LDL receptors are faulty or missing, so cells can't take in cholesterol from the blood and blood cholesterol stays high, the discovery for which Brown and Goldstein shared a Nobel Prize in 1985.

Common mistakeSaying the cargo crosses the membrane in endocytosis. It never passes through the lipid; it stays inside a vesicle made of membrane.

In 3 lines: Phagocytosis, pinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis bring material in. Exocytosis releases enzymes, hormones and neurotransmitters. Both need ATP and together keep the cell surface area balanced.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which process releases neurotransmitter at a synapse?
    • Phagocytosis
    • Exocytosis
    • Osmosis
    • Facilitated diffusion
    Show the answer

    Answer: Exocytosis. Vesicles of neurotransmitter fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release their contents.

  2. A macrophage engulfs a bacterium. Which organelle will the resulting vesicle fuse with?
    • The nucleus
    • A chloroplast
    • A lysosome
    • The rough ER
    Show the answer

    Answer: A lysosome. Lysosomes deliver the hydrolytic enzymes that digest the engulfed bacterium.

  3. People with familial hypercholesterolaemia have high blood cholesterol even on a low-fat diet. Using what you know about transport, explain why.
    • Cholesterol can't diffuse through membranes
    • Their cells make no clathrin
    • Their lysosomes digest too much cholesterol
    • Faulty LDL receptors stop receptor-mediated endocytosis of cholesterol, so it stays in the blood
    Show the answer

    Answer: Faulty LDL receptors stop receptor-mediated endocytosis of cholesterol, so it stays in the blood. Cells normally remove LDL cholesterol from the blood by receptor-mediated endocytosis; without the receptors, it accumulates.

Key terms

Endocytosis
Taking material into a cell by enclosing it in a vesicle formed from the plasma membrane
Exocytosis
Releasing material from a cell by fusing a vesicle with the plasma membrane
Phagocytosis
Endocytosis of large particles such as bacteria
