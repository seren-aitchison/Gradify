Experimental Design

A good experiment starts with a testable question, changes one independent variable, measures a dependent variable, controls others and uses replication, randomisation, blinding and pilot studies, with power analysis to choose sample size.

Biology · Experimental Biology and Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Identify independent, dependent and control variables in a biology experiment
  • Explain randomisation, replication and blinding
  • Design a fair test for a biological question

Planning

  • Start from a clear, testable question.
  • Change one independent variable and measure one dependent variable.
  • Keep control variables constant.
  • Choose a suitable range and number of levels for the independent variable.

Reducing bias and error

  • Replication: repeat each condition to estimate variation.
  • Randomisation: assign samples to treatments at random.
  • Blinding: hide treatment identity from those measuring results.
  • Pilot studies test methods before the main experiment.

Biological material varies, so replication with independent samples (biological replicates) is essential. Statistical power analysis estimates how many replicates are needed to detect an effect of a given size. Poor design cannot be rescued by statistics after the experiment.

Worked example

Testing a fertiliser on seedlings.

  1. The question is whether fertiliser concentration affects seedling height.
  2. Five concentrations are tested, with ten seedlings each, randomly assigned to trays.
  3. Light, water and temperature are kept the same.
  4. The person measuring height does not know which tray received which treatment.
Common mistakeThinking more measurements of the same sample count as replicates. Independent biological samples are needed.

In 3 lines: One variable changed. Replicate, randomise, blind. Plan before you start.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. In the seedling experiment, what is the dependent variable?
    • Fertiliser concentration
    • Tray position in the room
    • Amount of light provided
    • Seedling height
    Show the answer

    Answer: Seedling height. Height is what is measured in response to the treatment.

  2. Why are seedlings randomly assigned to trays?
    • To prevent hidden differences biasing one treatment
    • To make the experiment finish faster
    • To increase the number of variables
    • To remove the need for controls
    Show the answer

    Answer: To prevent hidden differences biasing one treatment. Randomisation spreads unknown factors evenly between groups.

  3. Why can't statistics rescue a poorly designed experiment?
    • Statistics can always fix any kind of data
    • Bias and confounding built into the data cannot be removed later
    • Poor designs have no data
    • Statistics only work on large samples
    Show the answer

    Answer: Bias and confounding built into the data cannot be removed later. Design flaws distort the data themselves.

Key terms

Independent variable
The factor deliberately changed
Randomisation
Assigning samples to treatments by chance
Statistical power
The probability of detecting a real effect
