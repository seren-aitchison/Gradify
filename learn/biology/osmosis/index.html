Osmosis

Water moves by osmosis from higher to lower water potential; solutes lower ψ, and pressure from a cell wall raises it.

Biology · Cell Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define osmosis in terms of water potential
  • Predict the effects of different solutions on animal and plant cells
  • Calculate water potential from solute and pressure potentials

Water potential

Water potential (ψ, psi) measures the tendency of water to move out of a solution. Pure water at atmospheric pressure has ψ = 0; adding solute lowers it, so solutions have negative water potentials. Water moves by osmosis from a higher (less negative) to a lower (more negative) water potential, across a partially permeable membrane.

ψ = ψs + ψp

Here ψs, the solute potential, is zero or negative, and ψp, the pressure potential, is usually positive in a turgid plant cell, where the wall pushes back on the contents.

Effects on cells

  • In a solution of higher water potential (hypotonic), water enters. Animal cells swell and may burst (red blood cells undergo haemolysis); plant cells become turgid.
  • In a solution of equal water potential (isotonic), there is no net movement.
  • In a solution of lower water potential (hypertonic), water leaves. Animal cells shrink and crenate; in plant cells the membrane pulls away from the wall, called plasmolysis.
Worked example

A plant cell has ψs = −700 kPa and ψp = +300 kPa. It is placed in a solution with ψ = −500 kPa. Which way does water move?

  1. Cell ψ = −700 + 300 = −400 kPa.
  2. −400 kPa is higher than −500 kPa, so water moves out of the cell into the solution.
Common mistakeSaying water moves "to where there is more solute" without the membrane or the cause. Osmosis is net water movement down a water potential gradient across a partially permeable membrane.

In 3 lines: Osmosis is water diffusing down a water potential gradient across a partially permeable membrane. ψ = ψs + ψp; pure water has ψ = 0 and solutions are negative. Animal cells burst or shrink; plant cells become turgid or plasmolysed.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which solution has the highest water potential?
    • Pure water
    • 0.1 M sucrose
    • 0.5 M sucrose
    • 1.0 M sucrose
    Show the answer

    Answer: Pure water. Solutes lower water potential, so pure water (ψ = 0) is highest.

  2. Potato cylinders are left in a range of sucrose solutions. In which solution would they gain mass?
    • One with the same water potential
    • One with a higher water potential than the potato cells
    • One with a lower water potential
    • Any solution, because potatoes always absorb water
    Show the answer

    Answer: One with a higher water potential than the potato cells. Water enters the cells only if the solution's water potential is higher (less negative) than the cells'.

  3. Intravenous drips use saline of about 0.9% sodium chloride rather than pure water. Why?
    • Pure water is too viscous
    • Salt is needed to stop bacteria growing
    • It has about the same water potential as blood plasma, so red blood cells neither swell nor shrink
    • Red blood cells need salt to make ATP
    Show the answer

    Answer: It has about the same water potential as blood plasma, so red blood cells neither swell nor shrink. An isotonic solution prevents osmotic damage; pure water would cause haemolysis.

Key terms

Osmosis
The net movement of water from a region of higher water potential to one of lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane
Water potential
A measure of the tendency of water to move out of a solution; pure water has the highest value, zero
Plasmolysis
The pulling away of a plant cell's membrane from its wall as the cell loses water
