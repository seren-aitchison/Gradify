Population Genetics

Allele frequencies are changed by selection, drift, gene flow, mutation and non-random mating; deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations reveal these forces, such as excess homozygotes from inbreeding, and effective population size guides conservation.

Biology · Evolution and Natural Selection · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Identify the forces that change allele frequencies in populations
  • Use deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations to infer evolutionary forces
  • Explain how population genetics links to speciation and conservation

Forces of evolution

  • Natural selection: non-random, changes frequencies according to fitness.
  • Genetic drift: random, strongest in small populations.
  • Gene flow: makes populations more similar.
  • Mutation: introduces new alleles, usually slowly.
  • Non-random mating: such as inbreeding, changes genotype frequencies.

Using Hardy-Weinberg

  • Hardy-Weinberg gives expected genotype frequencies with no evolutionary forces.
  • An excess of homozygotes may indicate inbreeding or population structure.
  • Changes in allele frequency over generations indicate selection, drift, gene flow or mutation.
  • The chi-squared test compares observed and expected genotype counts.

The effective population size (Ne) is the number of individuals contributing genes to the next generation and is often much smaller than the census size; small Ne increases drift and inbreeding. Conservation genetics monitors Ne and heterozygosity to manage endangered species. Population genetics also models how quickly a beneficial allele can spread under selection.

Worked example

Detecting inbreeding.

  1. In a small island population, allele frequencies are p = 0.6 and q = 0.4.
  2. Hardy-Weinberg predicts 48% heterozygotes (2 × 0.6 × 0.4).
  3. Only 30% are heterozygous.
  4. The excess of homozygotes suggests inbreeding.
Common mistakeAssuming every deviation from Hardy-Weinberg means natural selection. Drift, gene flow, mutation and non-random mating can also cause deviations.

In 3 lines: Five forces change frequencies. Deviations reveal forces. Ne guides conservation.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which evolutionary force is random and strongest in small populations?
    • Natural selection
    • Genetic drift
    • Gene flow from migrants
    • Mutation alone
    Show the answer

    Answer: Genetic drift. Drift reflects chance and has most effect when numbers are small.

  2. Which force tends to make separate populations more genetically similar?
    • Genetic drift
    • Inbreeding
    • Gene flow
    • Disruptive selection
    Show the answer

    Answer: Gene flow. Exchange of alleles between populations reduces their genetic differences.

  3. Why is effective population size often smaller than census size?
    • Effective size counts only juveniles
    • Census size excludes adults
    • Effective size is always larger
    • Not all individuals breed, and breeding success varies
    Show the answer

    Answer: Not all individuals breed, and breeding success varies. Unequal reproduction reduces the number contributing genes.

Key terms

Effective population size
The number of individuals contributing genes to the next generation
Inbreeding
Mating between relatives, increasing homozygosity
Census size
The total number of individuals counted
