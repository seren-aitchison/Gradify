Sampling Methods

Sample randomly (or systematically along gradients), take enough samples, and know the assumptions behind each method.

Biology · Foundations of Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why samples must be representative and large enough
  • Describe random, systematic and stratified sampling
  • Estimate population size using quadrats and mark–release–recapture

Why sampling matters

A sample should be representative (unbiased) and large enough to reflect the variation in the population. Bias creeps in when samples are chosen for convenience, such as only surveying the easy-to-reach edge of a field.

Sampling strategies

  • Random sampling: every individual or location has an equal chance of selection; positions chosen with random-number coordinates.
  • Systematic sampling: samples taken at regular intervals, for example along a transect, useful where conditions change across an area (from a hedge into a field).
  • Stratified sampling: the area is divided into zones (strata) and each is sampled in proportion to its size.

Ecological methods

  • Quadrats (often 0.25 m² or 1 m² frames) to measure density, frequency or percentage cover of plants and slow-moving animals.
  • Transects: lines along which quadrats are placed at intervals (belt transect) or species touching the line are recorded (line transect).
  • Mark–release–recapture for mobile animals.
N = (M × C) / RLincoln index: M marked in the first sample, C caught in the second, R marked ones recaptured
Worked example

Twenty-five woodlice are caught, marked and released. A week later 30 are caught, 5 of them marked. Estimate the population.

  1. N = (25 × 30) ÷ 5 = 150 woodlice.

The estimate assumes no migration, births or deaths between samples, that marks don't come off or affect survival, and that marked animals mix randomly.

Common mistakePlacing quadrats where the plants look interesting. That introduces bias; use random coordinates.

In 3 lines: Representative, adequately sized samples avoid bias. Random, systematic and stratified sampling suit different situations. N = MC/R estimates population size from recaptures.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which method is best for estimating the population of daisies on a school field?
    • Mark–release–recapture
    • A line transect from a hedge
    • Random quadrats
    • Counting every daisy in one corner
    Show the answer

    Answer: Random quadrats. Random quadrats give unbiased estimates of plant density across a uniform area.

  2. A survey of a rocky shore records species every 2 m from the low-tide line to the top of the shore. This is…
    • random sampling
    • stratified sampling
    • mark–release–recapture
    • systematic sampling with a transect
    Show the answer

    Answer: systematic sampling with a transect. Regular intervals along a line across a gradient is systematic transect sampling.

  3. In a mark–release–recapture study the marks make beetles more visible to birds. How does this affect the estimate?
    • Fewer marked beetles survive to be recaptured, so R is too small and the population is overestimated
    • The estimate is unaffected
    • The population is underestimated
    • The method can't be used for beetles
    Show the answer

    Answer: Fewer marked beetles survive to be recaptured, so R is too small and the population is overestimated. If marked individuals die more often, the recaptured proportion falls and N = MC/R becomes too large.

Key terms

Representative sample
A sample whose characteristics reflect those of the whole population
Transect
A line across a habitat along which samples are taken at intervals
Mark–release–recapture
A method of estimating the size of a mobile animal population from the proportion of marked individuals recaptured
