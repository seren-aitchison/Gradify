Genome-wide Association Studies

GWAS compare millions of SNPs between people with and without a trait, using a threshold of p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ and Manhattan plots; they show most traits are polygenic, mark regions via linkage disequilibrium and feed polygenic scores, but have small effects, limited diversity and need follow-up for causation.

Biology · Genetics and Molecular Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe how a genome-wide association study is carried out
  • Interpret GWAS results, including Manhattan plots
  • Evaluate the strengths and limitations of GWAS

How GWAS work

  • Genotype hundreds of thousands to millions of SNPs in large groups of people.
  • Compare SNP frequencies between people with and without a condition, or across a range of trait values.
  • A strict significance threshold, typically p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, accounts for testing millions of SNPs.
  • Results are shown in Manhattan plots, with peaks marking associated regions.

What they find

  • Most traits and common diseases are polygenic, influenced by many variants with small effects.
  • Associated SNPs often mark a region rather than the causal variant itself, because of linkage disequilibrium.
  • Results can be combined into polygenic scores that estimate genetic risk.
  • Large biobanks such as UK Biobank, with about 500,000 participants, power many studies.

GWAS have identified thousands of associations, for example with type 2 diabetes and height, and revealed new biological pathways. Limitations include small effect sizes, difficulty identifying causal variants, and under-representation of non-European populations, which reduces accuracy of polygenic scores for other groups. Association does not prove causation, so follow-up experiments are needed.

Worked example

Reading a Manhattan plot.

  1. The x-axis shows positions along each chromosome.
  2. The y-axis shows −log₁₀(p) for each SNP.
  3. A tall peak on chromosome 10 crosses the significance line.
  4. SNPs in this region are associated with the trait and are followed up.
Common mistakeAssuming a GWAS hit is the causal mutation. It usually marks a region containing the causal variant.

In 3 lines: Scan millions of SNPs. Strict significance thresholds. Many small effects; limits remain.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does a genome-wide association study compare?
    • Protein shapes between different species
    • The number of chromosomes in each cell
    • Enzyme activity at different temperatures
    • SNP frequencies between people with and without a trait
    Show the answer

    Answer: SNP frequencies between people with and without a trait. GWAS look for variants more common in people with a trait.

  2. Why is a very strict significance threshold used in GWAS?
    • Millions of SNPs are tested, so false positives must be limited
    • Each SNP is tested only once in a lifetime
    • Strict thresholds increase effect sizes
    • It ensures every SNP is significant
    Show the answer

    Answer: Millions of SNPs are tested, so false positives must be limited. Multiple testing would otherwise produce many chance results.

  3. Why might polygenic scores be less accurate for people of non-European ancestry?
    • Non-European genomes have no SNPs
    • Most GWAS have studied people of European ancestry
    • Polygenic scores ignore genes entirely
    • Accuracy never depends on ancestry
    Show the answer

    Answer: Most GWAS have studied people of European ancestry. Under-representation limits how well results transfer to other groups.

Key terms

GWAS
A study scanning the genome for variants associated with a trait
Manhattan plot
A graph showing association strength for SNPs across the genome
Polygenic score
An estimate of genetic risk combining many variants
