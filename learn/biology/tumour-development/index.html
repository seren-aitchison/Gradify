Tumour Development

Benign tumours grow slowly without invading or spreading, while malignant tumours invade and metastasise; progression involves accumulating mutations, angiogenesis and metastasis through blood or lymph, and early detection through screening improves outcomes.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish benign and malignant tumours
  • Describe the stages of tumour progression
  • Explain how metastasis occurs

Benign and malignant

  • Benign tumours: grow slowly, are usually enclosed in a capsule, do not invade and do not spread; often removable.
  • Malignant tumours (cancers): grow faster, invade surrounding tissue and can spread (metastasise).
  • Malignant cells often look abnormal, with large nuclei and loss of specialised features.

Progression and metastasis

  • Progression usually involves multiple mutations over years, as in colorectal cancer from polyp to carcinoma.
  • Tumours induce angiogenesis to obtain blood supply beyond about 1–2 mm.
  • Metastasis: cells detach, invade, enter blood or lymph, survive in circulation and form secondary tumours.
  • Secondary tumours are named after the primary site, such as breast cancer in bone.

Early detection improves outcomes because cancers are easier to treat before they spread. NHS screening programmes, such as bowel, breast and cervical screening, aim to detect cancers or precancerous changes early. Most cancer deaths result from metastases rather than the primary tumour.

Worked example

Colorectal cancer progression.

  1. A mutation in APC causes a small benign polyp.
  2. Further mutations, such as in KRAS, enlarge the polyp.
  3. Loss of TP53 allows malignant transformation into a carcinoma.
  4. The carcinoma invades and may metastasise to the liver.
Common mistakeThinking all tumours are cancers. Benign tumours do not invade or spread.

In 3 lines: Benign or malignant. Multistep progression. Metastasis causes most deaths.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which feature describes a benign tumour?
    • It does not invade tissue or spread
    • It spreads rapidly through the blood
    • It always causes death
    • It contains no cells at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: It does not invade tissue or spread. Benign tumours stay localised and do not invade tissue or metastasise.

  2. How do cancer cells usually spread to distant organs?
    • By dividing along nerves only
    • Through the blood or lymphatic system
    • Through the air in the lungs
    • By fusing with red blood cells
    Show the answer

    Answer: Through the blood or lymphatic system. Metastasising cells travel in blood or lymph.

  3. Why do screening programmes improve cancer outcomes?
    • They prevent all mutations
    • They cure metastatic cancer
    • They detect cancers or precancerous changes before spread
    • They stop angiogenesis completely
    Show the answer

    Answer: They detect cancers or precancerous changes before spread. Early detection allows treatment before metastasis.

Key terms

Benign tumour
A non-invasive tumour that does not spread
Malignant tumour
A tumour that invades and can metastasise
Secondary tumour
A tumour formed by spread from a primary site
