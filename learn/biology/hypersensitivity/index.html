Hypersensitivity

Hypersensitivity is a harmful immune response: type I involves IgE and mast cells, type II antibodies against cells, type III immune complexes and type IV T cells acting over days.

Biology · Immunology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define hypersensitivity
  • Describe types I to IV hypersensitivity
  • Give examples of each type

Four types

  • Type I (immediate): IgE on mast cells triggers histamine release within minutes, as in hay fever and anaphylaxis.
  • Type II (antibody-mediated): IgG or IgM bind cell surfaces, as in transfusion reactions and haemolytic disease of the newborn.
  • Type III (immune complex): antigen-antibody complexes deposit in tissues, as in lupus nephritis.
  • Type IV (delayed): T cells cause reactions over 24–72 hours, as in contact dermatitis from nickel and the tuberculin test.

Features

  • Hypersensitivity is an exaggerated or inappropriate immune response.
  • Types I–III involve antibodies; type IV involves T cells.
  • Reactions can be local or systemic.
  • Prior sensitisation is usually needed.

Haemolytic disease of the newborn occurs when a Rhesus-negative mother makes antibodies against a Rhesus-positive fetus's red cells; anti-D injections given to Rh-negative mothers prevent this. The Mantoux test injects tuberculin into the skin; a raised area after 48–72 hours shows previous exposure to TB.

Worked example

Nickel allergy.

  1. A person wears a nickel earring for the first time; no reaction occurs.
  2. Their T cells become sensitised to nickel-modified proteins.
  3. On later exposure, an itchy rash appears after about two days.
  4. This is a type IV, delayed hypersensitivity.
Common mistakeThinking all allergic-type reactions are immediate. Type IV reactions take one to three days.

In 3 lines: Type I: IgE. Types II and III: antibodies. Type IV: T cells.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is a type IV hypersensitivity reaction?
    • Anaphylaxis to peanuts
    • Contact dermatitis from nickel
    • Hay fever from pollen
    • A mismatched transfusion
    Show the answer

    Answer: Contact dermatitis from nickel. T cells cause delayed skin reactions to nickel.

  2. What causes haemolytic disease of the newborn?
    • Fetal T cells attacking the mother
    • Lack of IgE in the fetus
    • Maternal antibodies attacking fetal red cells
    • Bacteria in the placenta
    Show the answer

    Answer: Maternal antibodies attacking fetal red cells. Anti-Rhesus antibodies cross the placenta.

  3. How do anti-D injections prevent haemolytic disease in later pregnancies?
    • They boost the mother's anti-Rh antibodies
    • They change the baby's blood group
    • They stop all antibody production
    • They remove fetal Rh-positive cells before the mother is sensitised
    Show the answer

    Answer: They remove fetal Rh-positive cells before the mother is sensitised. Preventing sensitisation stops harmful antibodies forming.

Key terms

Hypersensitivity
A harmful exaggerated immune response
Immune complex
A cluster of antigen bound to antibody
Anaphylaxis
A severe, whole-body type I reaction
