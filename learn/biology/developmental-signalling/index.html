Developmental Signalling

Morphogens form concentration gradients that give cells different fates, as Bicoid and Sonic hedgehog do; conserved pathways such as Hedgehog, Wnt, Notch, BMP and FGF control development, and their misregulation causes developmental defects and cancer.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how morphogen gradients specify cell fates
  • Describe major developmental signalling pathways
  • Explain the consequences of faulty developmental signalling

Morphogens

  • A morphogen is a signalling molecule that forms a concentration gradient across tissue.
  • Cells respond differently depending on the concentration they receive, adopting different fates.
  • Example: Bicoid protein in the fruit fly egg forms a head-to-tail gradient.
  • Example: Sonic hedgehog (Shh) from the zone of polarising activity patterns the digits of the limb.

Major pathways

  • Hedgehog, Wnt, Notch, TGF-β/BMP and FGF pathways are used repeatedly in development.
  • They typically involve a ligand binding a receptor, triggering a cascade that changes gene expression.
  • Notch signalling between neighbouring cells can make them adopt different fates (lateral inhibition).
  • The same pathways are used in many tissues and species.

Developmental pathways are often reused in adults for tissue maintenance, and their misregulation can cause cancer: for example, overactive Wnt signalling occurs in many colorectal cancers. Mutations in Sonic hedgehog signalling can cause holoprosencephaly, a failure of the forebrain to divide. Because these pathways are conserved, findings in model organisms such as fruit flies, zebrafish and mice apply to humans.

Worked example

The French flag model.

  1. A morphogen is released from one end of a row of cells.
  2. Cells near the source receive a high concentration and adopt fate A.
  3. Cells further away receive a medium concentration and adopt fate B.
  4. Distant cells receive a low concentration and adopt fate C, like the three stripes of a flag.
Common mistakeAssuming each tissue uses unique signalling pathways. A small set of conserved pathways is reused in many contexts.

In 3 lines: Gradients specify fates. A few conserved pathways. Faults cause defects and cancer.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How do cells respond to a morphogen gradient?
    • They adopt different fates depending on concentration
    • They all adopt exactly the same fate
    • They ignore the signalling molecule
    • They divide by meiosis
    Show the answer

    Answer: They adopt different fates depending on concentration. Concentration-dependent responses create patterns.

  2. What does Notch signalling between neighbouring cells often achieve?
    • All cells become identical
    • Neighbouring cells adopt different fates
    • Cells fuse together
    • Cells stop expressing genes
    Show the answer

    Answer: Neighbouring cells adopt different fates. Lateral inhibition creates differences between neighbours.

  3. Why do findings in fruit flies and zebrafish often apply to humans?
    • Humans and flies have identical genomes
    • Flies use no signalling pathways
    • Developmental signalling pathways are highly conserved
    • Zebrafish develop exactly like humans in every detail
    Show the answer

    Answer: Developmental signalling pathways are highly conserved. Shared pathways allow insights from model organisms.

Key terms

Morphogen
A signalling molecule acting through a concentration gradient
Sonic hedgehog
A morphogen that patterns limbs and the neural tube
Lateral inhibition
Signalling that makes neighbouring cells adopt different fates
