Meiosis

Meiosis I separates homologous pairs after pairing and crossing over, and meiosis II separates sister chromatids, producing four genetically different haploid cells; halving the chromosome number lets fertilisation restore diploidy.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main events of meiosis I and meiosis II
  • Explain how meiosis halves the chromosome number
  • Explain why meiosis is needed in sexual reproduction

Meiosis I

  • Homologous chromosomes pair up in prophase I, and crossing over occurs.
  • Pairs line up at the equator in metaphase I.
  • Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles in anaphase I.
  • Two haploid cells form, each with one chromosome from each pair, still with two chromatids.

Meiosis II

  • Similar to mitosis.
  • Chromosomes line up singly in metaphase II.
  • Sister chromatids separate in anaphase II.
  • Four haploid cells result, each genetically different.

Meiosis halves the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n), so that fertilisation restores the diploid number: in humans, gametes have 23 chromosomes and the zygote 46. Without halving, the chromosome number would double each generation. In humans, meiosis in testes produces four sperm, while in ovaries it produces one egg and polar bodies.

Worked example

Chromosome numbers in meiosis.

  1. A human germ cell has 46 chromosomes (2n).
  2. After meiosis I, two cells each have 23 chromosomes, each with two chromatids.
  3. After meiosis II, four cells each have 23 single-chromatid chromosomes.
  4. Fertilisation of an egg (23) by a sperm (23) restores 46.
Common mistakeSaying sister chromatids separate in meiosis I. Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I; chromatids separate in meiosis II.

In 3 lines: Meiosis I separates homologues. Meiosis II separates chromatids. Four different haploid cells.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What is the chromosome number in a human gamete?
    • 46
    • 92
    • 23
    • 12
    Show the answer

    Answer: 23. Gametes are haploid, with one set of 23 chromosomes.

  2. Why must gametes be haploid?
    • So zygotes have twice the normal chromosomes
    • So gametes can divide by mitosis only
    • So chromosome number doubles each generation
    • So fertilisation restores the diploid number
    Show the answer

    Answer: So fertilisation restores the diploid number. Fusion of two haploid gametes gives a diploid zygote.

  3. Why are the four cells from meiosis genetically different?
    • Crossing over and independent assortment mix alleles
    • They have identical chromosomes
    • Mitosis follows meiosis
    • They come from different organisms
    Show the answer

    Answer: Crossing over and independent assortment mix alleles. Recombination in meiosis I creates new combinations.

Key terms

Haploid
Having one set of chromosomes (n)
Diploid
Having two sets of chromosomes (2n)
Homologous chromosomes
A matching pair of chromosomes, one from each parent
