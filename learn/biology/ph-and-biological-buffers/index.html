PH and Biological Buffers

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], each unit is a tenfold change, blood is kept near pH 7.4, and buffers of weak acids and conjugate bases, such as carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate, phosphate and proteins, resist pH changes.

Biology · Biochemistry and Biomolecules · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define pH and calculate it from hydrogen ion concentration
  • Explain how buffers resist changes in pH
  • Describe important biological buffer systems

pH

  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is in mol dm⁻³.
  • pH 7 is neutral at 25 °C; below 7 is acidic; above 7 is alkaline.
  • Each pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
  • Human blood is kept at about pH 7.35–7.45.

Buffers

  • A buffer is a solution of a weak acid and its conjugate base that resists pH change.
  • Added H⁺ combines with the base; added OH⁻ reacts with the acid.
  • Carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate buffer in blood: H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
  • Phosphate buffers inside cells, and proteins such as haemoglobin also buffer.

pH matters because it affects the charges on amino acid side chains, which change protein shape and enzyme activity. The blood buffer is linked to breathing: carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, so faster breathing removes CO₂ and raises pH, while kidneys adjust hydrogencarbonate. Different compartments have different optimum pH values, such as the stomach at about pH 2 and lysosomes at about pH 5.

Worked example

Calculating pH.

  1. A solution has [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.
  2. pH = −log₁₀(1 × 10⁻⁴) = 4.
  3. If [H⁺] rises tenfold to 1 × 10⁻³, the pH falls to 3.
  4. A one-unit change in pH means a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.
Common mistakeThinking pH 4 is twice as acidic as pH 5. It has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration.

In 3 lines: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. One unit = tenfold. Buffers keep pH stable.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What does a lower pH indicate?
    • A higher hydrogen ion concentration
    • A lower hydrogen ion concentration
    • More hydroxide ions only
    • No hydrogen ions at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: A higher hydrogen ion concentration. Lower pH means more H⁺, so a more acidic solution.

  2. How does the hydrogencarbonate buffer respond to added H⁺ in blood?
    • H₂CO₃ releases more H⁺
    • HCO₃⁻ combines with H⁺ to form H₂CO₃
    • Water splits into ions
    • The buffer is destroyed
    Show the answer

    Answer: HCO₃⁻ combines with H⁺ to form H₂CO₃. The conjugate base removes excess hydrogen ions.

  3. Why can faster breathing raise blood pH?
    • It adds hydrogen ions to blood
    • It increases carbonic acid production
    • It removes CO₂, shifting the equilibrium away from carbonic acid
    • It removes hydrogencarbonate ions
    Show the answer

    Answer: It removes CO₂, shifting the equilibrium away from carbonic acid. Less CO₂ means less carbonic acid and fewer H⁺ ions.

Key terms

pH
−log₁₀ of the hydrogen ion concentration
Buffer
A solution that resists changes in pH
Conjugate base
The species formed when an acid loses H⁺
