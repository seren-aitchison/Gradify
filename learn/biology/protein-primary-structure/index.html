Protein Primary Structure

Primary structure is the amino acid sequence encoded by a gene; it determines all higher levels of structure through R group interactions, so a single change, such as glutamate to valine in sickle cell haemoglobin, can change function.

Biology · Biochemistry and Biomolecules · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define primary structure
  • Explain how primary structure determines higher levels of structure
  • Describe how changes in primary structure affect function

Sequence

  • Primary structure is the order of amino acids, from N- to C-terminus.
  • It is encoded by the base sequence of a gene.
  • With 20 amino acids, the number of possible sequences is enormous: a chain of 100 has 20¹⁰⁰ possibilities.
  • Insulin was the first protein sequenced, by Frederick Sanger in the 1950s.

Sequence and function

  • The R groups in the sequence determine where hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions form.
  • So primary structure determines secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure.
  • A single amino acid change can alter function.
  • In sickle cell anaemia, glutamate is replaced by valine at position 6 of the β-globin chain.

Not every change harms function: substitutions of chemically similar amino acids, or changes away from the active site, may have little effect. Comparing sequences between species reveals evolutionary relationships, as related species have more similar sequences. Modern methods determine sequences mainly from DNA sequences or by mass spectrometry.

Worked example

Sickle cell haemoglobin.

  1. In normal β-globin, position 6 is glutamate, a charged amino acid.
  2. In sickle cell β-globin, it is valine, which is non-polar.
  3. Valine creates a sticky patch, so haemoglobin molecules stick together when oxygen is low.
  4. Red blood cells become sickle-shaped and block capillaries.
Common mistakeAssuming every amino acid change destroys a protein. Some substitutions have little effect, depending on the amino acid and position.

In 3 lines: Primary structure is the sequence. Sequence determines shape. One change can alter function.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What is the primary structure of a protein?
    • Its overall 3D shape
    • Its number of polypeptide chains
    • Its sequence of amino acids
    • Its coiling into α-helices
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its sequence of amino acids. Primary structure is the order of amino acids in the chain.

  2. Why does primary structure determine a protein's 3D shape?
    • Primary structure has nothing to do with shape
    • Only the N-terminus affects folding
    • Shape is decided only by temperature
    • R groups in the sequence decide which bonds and interactions form
    Show the answer

    Answer: R groups in the sequence decide which bonds and interactions form. The positions of different R groups determine folding.

  3. Why might replacing leucine with isoleucine have little effect on a protein?
    • Both are similar non-polar amino acids
    • Leucine and isoleucine are both charged
    • Isoleucine cannot fit in proteins
    • It always destroys the active site
    Show the answer

    Answer: Both are similar non-polar amino acids. Chemically similar substitutions often preserve shape and function.

Key terms

Primary structure
The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide
Substitution
Replacement of one amino acid with another
Sickle cell anaemia
A disease caused by one amino acid change in β-globin
