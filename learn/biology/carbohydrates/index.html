Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates contain C, H and O, are classified as monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides, and provide energy, energy storage, structure and cell recognition, with structure matched to function.

Biology · Biochemistry and Biomolecules · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the general structure and formula of carbohydrates
  • Classify carbohydrates into monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides
  • Relate carbohydrate structure to functions in organisms

General features

  • Contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, often in the ratio Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ for simple sugars.
  • Have many hydroxyl groups, making small carbohydrates soluble.
  • Monosaccharides: single sugar units, such as glucose and fructose.
  • Disaccharides: two units, such as sucrose and lactose.
  • Polysaccharides: many units, such as starch, glycogen and cellulose.

Functions

  • Energy source: glucose is respired to make ATP.
  • Energy storage: starch in plants and glycogen in animals.
  • Structure: cellulose in plant cell walls; chitin in fungal walls and arthropod exoskeletons.
  • Recognition: glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces.

Carbohydrate structure is closely matched to function. Small sugars are soluble and easily transported but affect water potential, so organisms store energy in large, insoluble polysaccharides. Storage polysaccharides are compact and easily hydrolysed, while structural polysaccharides form strong fibres that resist digestion. Glycosidic bonds link sugar units, and their type determines the polymer's shape.

Worked example

Matching structure to function.

  1. A potato stores energy as starch, which is insoluble and does not affect water potential.
  2. Its cell walls contain cellulose, which forms strong fibres.
  3. Both are glucose polymers, but different bonds give different shapes.
  4. Soluble glucose and sucrose are used for transport and respiration.
Common mistakeThinking all carbohydrates are sweet and soluble. Polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose are neither.

In 3 lines: C, H and O. Mono, di and polysaccharides. Energy, storage, structure, recognition.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is a polysaccharide?
    • Glucose
    • Sucrose
    • Fructose
    • Starch
    Show the answer

    Answer: Starch. Starch is a polymer of many glucose units.

  2. Why do organisms store energy as polysaccharides rather than glucose?
    • Polysaccharides are insoluble and do not affect water potential
    • Polysaccharides release energy faster than glucose
    • Glucose cannot be respired
    • Polysaccharides are smaller than glucose
    Show the answer

    Answer: Polysaccharides are insoluble and do not affect water potential. Insoluble storage avoids osmotic effects of many glucose molecules.

  3. Starch and cellulose are both glucose polymers. Why do they have different functions?
    • Starch contains nitrogen and cellulose does not
    • Different glycosidic bonds give different shapes and properties
    • Cellulose is made of fructose
    • Starch is a protein
    Show the answer

    Answer: Different glycosidic bonds give different shapes and properties. Bond type determines whether chains coil or form straight fibres.

Key terms

Monosaccharide
A single sugar unit
Polysaccharide
A polymer of many sugar units
Glycosidic bond
The covalent bond linking sugar units
