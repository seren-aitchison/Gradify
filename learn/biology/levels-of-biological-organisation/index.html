Levels of Biological Organisation

Each level is built from the one below and has emergent properties of its own.

Biology · Foundations of Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Put the levels of biological organisation in order
  • Explain what an emergent property is, with examples
  • Relate a process at one level to its effects at another

The hierarchy

  • Atoms and molecules: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen form biological molecules such as proteins and DNA.
  • Organelles: structures inside cells, such as mitochondria.
  • Cells: the smallest units that can carry out all life processes.
  • Tissues: groups of similar cells working together (muscle tissue).
  • Organs: several tissues forming a structure with a function (the heart).
  • Organ systems: organs working together (the circulatory system).
  • Organisms: individual living things.
  • Populations: individuals of one species in an area.
  • Communities: all the populations in an area.
  • Ecosystems: a community plus its non-living environment.
  • The biosphere: all ecosystems on Earth.

Emergent properties

New properties emerge as parts are combined. A single heart muscle cell contracts, but only the organised tissue of the heart pumps blood; a single neurone fires, but thought emerges from networks of billions. Studying parts alone (reductionism) can't explain everything, so biologists also study whole systems.

Worked example

Trace oxygen transport through the levels of organisation.

  1. Molecule: haemoglobin binds oxygen in the lungs.
  2. Cell: red blood cells, packed with haemoglobin and without a nucleus, carry it.
  3. Tissue and organ: blood is pumped by the heart through vessels.
  4. Organ system: the circulatory and respiratory systems together deliver oxygen to every cell.
  5. Organism: aerobic respiration in all tissues depends on the whole chain.

A change at one level can show up at others. In sickle-cell disease, one amino acid change in haemoglobin makes red cells deform at low oxygen, blocking capillaries and damaging organs; and because carriers are partly protected from malaria, the allele is common in some populations.

Common mistakeConfusing a tissue with an organ. A tissue is one type of cell working together; an organ contains several tissues.

In 2 lines: Atoms → molecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → systems → organisms → populations → communities → ecosystems → biosphere. Emergent properties appear at each level.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is an organ?
    • A muscle cell
    • Blood plasma
    • A mitochondrion
    • The stomach
    Show the answer

    Answer: The stomach. The stomach contains several tissues (muscle, epithelium, glands) working together; a muscle cell is a cell and a mitochondrion an organelle.

  2. All the trout, algae, insects and plants in a lake, but not the water or rocks, make up a…
    • community
    • population
    • ecosystem
    • biosphere
    Show the answer

    Answer: community. A community is all the living populations in an area; adding the non-living environment would make an ecosystem.

  3. Why can't the behaviour of an ecosystem be predicted just by studying each species separately?
    • Species never interact
    • Interactions between species and with the environment create emergent properties such as nutrient cycling and food-web stability
    • Ecosystems contain no cells
    • Each species behaves randomly
    Show the answer

    Answer: Interactions between species and with the environment create emergent properties such as nutrient cycling and food-web stability. Feeding relationships, competition and cycling of matter arise from interactions between parts, which studying parts in isolation misses.

Key terms

Tissue
A group of similar cells that work together to carry out a function
Emergent property
A property of a whole system that its separate parts do not have
Ecosystem
A community of organisms together with their physical environment
