Restriction Enzymes

Restriction enzymes from bacteria cut DNA at specific, often palindromic, recognition sites, producing sticky or blunt ends; cutting gene and vector with the same enzyme creates complementary sticky ends that ligase can join.

Biology · Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the origin and function of restriction enzymes
  • Distinguish sticky ends and blunt ends
  • Explain how restriction enzymes are used in genetic engineering

What they are

  • Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) are made by bacteria to cut viral DNA.
  • Each recognises a specific recognition site, often 4–8 base pairs long.
  • Many sites are palindromic: they read the same on both strands in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
  • Bacteria protect their own DNA by methylating it.

Cutting DNA

  • Sticky ends: staggered cuts leave short single-stranded overhangs, such as EcoRI (G^AATTC).
  • Blunt ends: straight cuts leave no overhangs, such as SmaI.
  • Cutting a gene and vector with the same enzyme gives complementary sticky ends.
  • Complementary ends base-pair, then ligase seals them.

Werner Arber, Daniel Nathans and Hamilton Smith shared the 1978 Nobel Prize for discovering restriction enzymes. Hundreds are now available commercially. Restriction mapping and DNA fingerprinting also use restriction enzymes to cut DNA into fragments of characteristic sizes.

Worked example

EcoRI cutting.

  1. EcoRI recognises GAATTC on one strand and CTTAAG on the other.
  2. It cuts between G and A on each strand.
  3. This leaves single-stranded AATT overhangs.
  4. Any DNA cut with EcoRI can join to these sticky ends.
Common mistakeThinking restriction enzymes cut DNA randomly. They cut only at specific recognition sequences.

In 3 lines: Bacterial defence enzymes. Sticky and blunt ends. Same enzyme, matching ends.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How do bacteria protect their own DNA from restriction enzymes?
    • By having no DNA at all
    • By making extra ligase
    • By coating DNA in lipid
    • By methylating their recognition sites
    Show the answer

    Answer: By methylating their recognition sites. Methyl groups block the enzyme from cutting.

  2. Why are the gene and vector cut with the same restriction enzyme?
    • To produce complementary sticky ends that can pair
    • To destroy the vector completely
    • To make blunt ends only
    • To remove the marker genes
    Show the answer

    Answer: To produce complementary sticky ends that can pair. Matching overhangs base-pair before ligation.

  3. What does "palindromic" mean for a recognition site?
    • It is found only once in every genome
    • It reads the same 5′ to 3′ on both strands
    • It contains only A and T bases
    • It cannot be cut by any enzyme
    Show the answer

    Answer: It reads the same 5′ to 3′ on both strands. Both strands have the same sequence in the same direction.

Key terms

Restriction enzyme
An enzyme cutting DNA at a specific sequence
Sticky end
A short single-stranded overhang after a staggered cut
Recognition site
The DNA sequence a restriction enzyme cuts
