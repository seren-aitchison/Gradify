Phloem

Phloem consists of living sieve tube elements with sieve plates and companion cells; translocation moves sucrose from sources to sinks by mass flow, driven by active loading and osmosis.

Biology · Plant Biology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the structure of sieve tube elements and companion cells
  • Explain translocation from sources to sinks
  • Describe the mass flow hypothesis

Structure

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells joined end to end, with perforated sieve plates.
  • They have few organelles and no nucleus.
  • Companion cells: connected by plasmodesmata, with many mitochondria, supporting the sieve tubes.
  • Phloem also contains parenchyma and fibres.

Translocation

  • Translocation is movement of sugars (mainly sucrose) and amino acids.
  • Sources produce or release sugars, such as leaves.
  • Sinks use or store sugars, such as roots, fruits and growing tips.
  • Mass flow: sucrose is loaded at the source, water follows by osmosis, pressure builds and sap flows to the sink.

Companion cells use ATP to load sucrose by active transport, often via proton co-transport. Evidence for mass flow comes from aphids, whose mouthparts tap phloem sap under pressure, and from radioactive tracers. Some details, such as how sieve plates affect flow, are still investigated. Sinks can change with season: roots may be sinks in summer and sources in spring.

Worked example

Aphid evidence.

  1. An aphid inserts its stylet into a single sieve tube.
  2. The aphid is removed, leaving the stylet in place.
  3. Sap flows out under pressure.
  4. This shows phloem contents are under pressure, supporting mass flow.
Common mistakeThinking sieve tube elements are dead like xylem. They are living, supported by companion cells.

In 3 lines: Sieve tubes and companion cells. Sources to sinks. Mass flow.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which cells support sieve tube elements?
    • Guard cells
    • Companion cells
    • Root hair cells
    • Xylem vessels
    Show the answer

    Answer: Companion cells. Companion cells carry out metabolism for the sieve tubes.

  2. How does sucrose loading at a source cause sap to flow?
    • Sucrose loading lowers pressure at the source
    • Gravity pulls sap downwards only
    • Water follows by osmosis, raising pressure that drives flow
    • Xylem pumps sap into the phloem
    Show the answer

    Answer: Water follows by osmosis, raising pressure that drives flow. Higher pressure at the source pushes sap to sinks.

  3. Why do companion cells contain many mitochondria?
    • To store sucrose for the plant
    • To carry water upwards
    • To produce chlorophyll
    • To provide ATP for active loading of sucrose
    Show the answer

    Answer: To provide ATP for active loading of sucrose. Active transport of sucrose into sieve tubes requires a lot of ATP.

Key terms

Translocation
Movement of organic substances in phloem
Source
A part of a plant that produces or releases sugars
Sink
A part of a plant that uses or stores sugars
