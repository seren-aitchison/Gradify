Cellular Senescence

Senescence is permanent arrest of division; telomeres shorten with each division until the Hayflick limit triggers senescence, telomerase maintains telomeres in stem, germ and most cancer cells, and senescence protects against cancer while contributing to ageing.

Biology · Cell Division and Development · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define cellular senescence
  • Explain how telomere shortening limits cell division
  • Describe the roles of senescence in ageing and cancer protection

Senescence and telomeres

  • Senescence is a permanent arrest of cell division in living cells.
  • Telomeres are repeated sequences (TTAGGG in humans) at chromosome ends.
  • Telomeres shorten with each division because DNA polymerase cannot copy the very end of linear chromosomes.
  • After roughly 40 to 60 divisions in cultured human cells, short telomeres trigger senescence: the Hayflick limit.

Roles

  • Senescence prevents damaged cells from dividing, protecting against cancer.
  • Telomerase adds telomere repeats; it is active in stem cells, germ cells and most cancer cells.
  • Senescent cells accumulate with age and release inflammatory signals, contributing to ageing tissues.
  • Other triggers include DNA damage and oncogene activation.

Leonard Hayflick showed in the 1960s that normal human cells have a limited number of divisions in culture. Blackburn, Greider and Szostak won the 2009 Nobel Prize for discovering how telomeres and telomerase protect chromosomes. Research into senolytic drugs, which remove senescent cells, aims to improve healthy ageing, but this remains an active area of study.

Worked example

Cancer cells and telomerase.

  1. Normal skin cells in culture stop dividing after about 50 divisions.
  2. Most cancer cells reactivate telomerase.
  3. Telomerase maintains telomere length, so the cells avoid senescence.
  4. The cancer cells can divide indefinitely.
Common mistakeSaying senescent cells are dead. They are alive but no longer divide.

In 3 lines: Senescent cells stop dividing. Telomeres shorten with divisions. Telomerase in stem and cancer cells.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Why do telomeres shorten with each cell division?
    • Telomeres are digested by lysosomes
    • Telomeres are copied twice in S phase
    • DNA polymerase cannot fully copy the ends of linear chromosomes
    • Mitosis removes them deliberately
    Show the answer

    Answer: DNA polymerase cannot fully copy the ends of linear chromosomes. The end-replication problem shortens telomeres.

  2. How do most cancer cells avoid senescence?
    • They stop replicating their DNA
    • They lose all their chromosomes
    • They enter G₀ permanently
    • They reactivate telomerase to maintain telomeres
    Show the answer

    Answer: They reactivate telomerase to maintain telomeres. Telomerase lets cancer cells divide indefinitely.

  3. How does senescence protect against cancer?
    • It stops cells with damage or short telomeres from dividing
    • It makes cells divide faster
    • It activates telomerase in all cells
    • It causes immediate necrosis
    Show the answer

    Answer: It stops cells with damage or short telomeres from dividing. Permanent arrest prevents damaged cells from proliferating.

Key terms

Senescence
Permanent arrest of cell division in a living cell
Telomere
A repetitive DNA sequence protecting chromosome ends
Telomerase
An enzyme that adds telomere repeats
