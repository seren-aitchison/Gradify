Stigma

Stigma includes public, self and structural forms; it discourages help-seeking and causes discrimination; Time to Change and social contact approaches reduced some discrimination.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish public stigma, self-stigma and structural stigma
  • Explain the effects of stigma on help-seeking and recovery
  • Evaluate evidence on anti-stigma campaigns
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Types of stigma

Public stigma involves stereotypes (such as "dangerous" or "weak"), prejudice and discrimination by others. Self-stigma occurs when people apply these stereotypes to themselves, lowering self-esteem and hope. Structural stigma involves policies and practices in institutions that disadvantage people with mental health difficulties. Link and Phelan (2001) argued stigma depends on power to label and discriminate.

Effects

Stigma can discourage help-seeking, as people fear being labelled. It can lead to discrimination in employment, housing and relationships, and to diagnostic overshadowing, where physical symptoms in people with mental health diagnoses are wrongly attributed to their mental health. Self-stigma can produce a "why try" effect, reducing motivation to pursue goals.

Worked example

Time to Change

  1. Time to Change was a national anti-stigma programme in England that ran from 2007 to 2021, using social contact and campaigns.
  2. Corker et al. (2013) reported that discrimination experienced by mental health service users declined between 2008 and 2011.
  3. Research suggests social contact, meeting people with lived experience, is one of the most effective ways to reduce stigma.

Evaluation

Anti-stigma work has had measurable effects, but stigma remains, especially towards conditions such as psychosis. Some campaigns based on biological explanations reduced blame but increased perceptions of dangerousness or permanence. Tackling structural stigma requires changes to laws, services and employment practices, not only attitudes.

Common mistakeAssuming stigma is only about individual attitudes. Structural stigma in institutions also matters.

In 2 lines: Public, self and structural stigma. Stigma deters help-seeking; social contact and Time to Change reduced some discrimination.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Institutional policies that disadvantage people with mental health difficulties are…
    • Self-stigma
    • Public stigma only
    • Resilience
    • Structural stigma
    Show the answer

    Answer: Structural stigma. Structural stigma operates through institutions.

  2. A woman with a mental health diagnosis reports chest pain, and staff assume it is anxiety without checking her heart. This is…
    • Diagnostic overshadowing
    • Self-stigma in the patient
    • Comorbidity of conditions
    • Resilience under pressure
    Show the answer

    Answer: Diagnostic overshadowing. Physical symptoms are wrongly attributed to mental health.

  3. Why have some biologically focused anti-stigma messages backfired?
    • They always eliminated stigma completely
    • They cut blame but raised views of danger and permanence
    • They increased blame on individuals
    • They had no effects on attitudes at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: They cut blame but raised views of danger and permanence. Biological framing can make difficulties seem fixed.

Key terms

Self-stigma
Applying negative stereotypes to oneself
Structural stigma
Institutional policies and practices that disadvantage a group
Diagnostic overshadowing
Attributing physical symptoms wrongly to a mental health condition
