Human Factors and Ergonomics

Human factors designs systems to fit people; errors include slips, lapses, mistakes and violations; the Swiss cheese model shows system causes; good design and checklists reduce error.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define human factors and ergonomics
  • Explain types of human error, including slips and mistakes
  • Apply human factors principles to safety-critical design

Fitting the task to the person

Human factors (also called ergonomics) studies how people interact with systems and designs those systems to suit human physical and cognitive capabilities. It covers physical ergonomics (posture, workstation design), cognitive ergonomics (attention, memory, decision-making) and organisational ergonomics (teamwork, procedures). The aim is safety, efficiency and wellbeing.

Human error

Reason (1990) distinguished slips and lapses (errors in carrying out a correct plan, such as pressing the wrong button or forgetting a step) from mistakes (errors in the plan itself) and violations (deliberate deviations from rules). His Swiss cheese model describes how accidents occur when weaknesses (holes) in several layers of defence line up, so focusing only on the person at the "sharp end" misses system failures.

Worked example

Checklists in surgery

  1. Haynes et al. (2009) introduced the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist in eight hospitals around the world.
  2. Rates of death and complications after surgery fell substantially after its introduction.
  3. Simple design tools that support memory and communication can reduce errors in complex work.

Designing for people

Good design uses principles such as visibility, feedback, consistency and constraints that prevent errors (Norman, 1988). For example, different-shaped connectors stop medical tubes being connected to the wrong system. Human factors is used in aviation, healthcare, transport and nuclear power.

Common mistakeBlaming accidents only on individual carelessness. Most accidents involve system weaknesses lining up.

In 2 lines: Human factors covers physical, cognitive and organisational design. Errors take several forms; Reason's model shows system causes; checklists and design reduce error.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Pressing the wrong button while intending the right one is a…
    • Mistake
    • Slip
    • Violation
    • Lapse of planning
    Show the answer

    Answer: Slip. Correct intention but wrong action is a slip.

  2. Medical tubes are given different-shaped connectors so they can't be joined wrongly. This uses…
    • Violation
    • Burnout
    • Design constraints
    • Hawthorne effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Design constraints. Physical constraints prevent errors by design.

  3. Why does the Swiss cheese model discourage simply blaming the operator?
    • Operators never make errors
    • Systems are always perfect
    • Blame always prevents error
    • Several failures line up
    Show the answer

    Answer: Several failures line up. Focusing on one person misses latent system weaknesses.

Key terms

Ergonomics
Designing systems and tools to fit human abilities and limits
Slip
An error in carrying out a correct plan
Swiss cheese model
Reason's model of accidents as aligned weaknesses in layers of defence
