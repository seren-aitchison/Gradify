The Aims of Psychological Research

The aims build on each other: good description makes explanation possible; explanations allow prediction; reliable prediction makes effective, evidence-based change possible.

Psychology · Foundations of Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define the four aims of psychological research
  • Apply the aims to classify research questions
  • Evaluate the ethical issues raised by the aim of controlling behaviour

Four aims

  • Description: recording what happens and how often. For example, how many hours a night do teenagers sleep?
  • Explanation: identifying causes and mechanisms. Why do teenagers fall asleep later? (Pubertal changes in the circadian rhythm are part of the answer.)
  • Prediction: forecasting when behaviour will occur. Which students are most likely to be sleep-deprived during exams?
  • Control, often better called change or application: using knowledge to influence behaviour, for example by moving school start times later.

From description to change

Worked example

Classify each research question by its main aim.

  1. "What percentage of UK adults report feeling lonely?" Description.
  2. "Does social media use increase loneliness, or do lonely people use it more?" Explanation (of causal direction).
  3. "Can we identify which older adults are at risk of loneliness after bereavement?" Prediction.
  4. "Does a befriending scheme reduce loneliness?" Control or change, tested by an intervention study.

Each aim needs different methods: surveys and observation describe; experiments test explanations; longitudinal studies and statistical models predict; and randomised trials test whether an intervention changes behaviour.

The ethics of "control"

"Control" sounds sinister, and the history of psychology contains misuse, such as intelligence testing used to justify discrimination in the early 20th century. Modern psychology applies knowledge with consent and for people's benefit, under ethical codes. Debates continue about large-scale behaviour change by governments and companies ("nudging": Thaler and Sunstein, 2008), and about manipulative design in technology.

Common mistakeAssuming a good prediction means we understand the cause. A variable can predict an outcome without causing it (for example, if both depend on a third factor).

In 2 lines: Psychology aims to describe, explain, predict and control or change behaviour. Applying psychology to change behaviour raises questions of consent, autonomy and misuse.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Recording how many hours of sleep teenagers get each night is mainly which aim?
    • Explanation
    • Description
    • Prediction
    • Control
    Show the answer

    Answer: Description. Measuring what happens and how often is description, the base for the other aims.

  2. Researchers test whether a mindfulness course reduces exam anxiety. Which aim is this mainly?
    • Description
    • Prediction
    • Control or change
    • Explanation of history
    Show the answer

    Answer: Control or change. The study applies knowledge to change behaviour and tests whether it works.

  3. Which is the strongest ethical concern about the aim of controlling behaviour?
    • Behaviour can never actually be changed, so the aim wastes research funding
    • Making predictions about people is unethical in itself, whatever is done with them
    • Describing behaviour always invades participants' privacy, whatever the safeguards
    • Knowledge can be used to change behaviour without people's consent or against their interests
    Show the answer

    Answer: Knowledge can be used to change behaviour without people's consent or against their interests. The concern is about how and by whom knowledge is applied: consent, autonomy and benefit, as debates about nudging and manipulative design show.

Key terms

Description
Recording what behaviour occurs and how often
Prediction
Forecasting when or in whom a behaviour will occur
Nudge
Changing how choices are presented to steer behaviour while leaving options open (Thaler and Sunstein)
