Risk and Resilience

Resilience is a process supported by ordinary protective factors; cumulative adversity raises risk, but most people's outcomes aren't determined by early adversity.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define risk factors, protective factors and resilience
  • Explain research on adverse childhood experiences and cumulative risk
  • Evaluate evidence on what promotes resilience

Risk and protection

Risk factors increase the likelihood of poor outcomes: poverty, maltreatment, parental mental illness, family conflict. Protective factors reduce the impact of risk: a warm relationship with at least one caring adult, good problem-solving skills, self-regulation, supportive schools and communities. Resilience is not a fixed trait but a process: positive adaptation in the face of adversity. Masten (2001) called it "ordinary magic" because it usually comes from ordinary resources such as relationships and competent caregiving.

Worked example

The Kauai study

  1. Werner and Smith followed all 698 children born in 1955 on the Hawaiian island of Kauai into adulthood.
  2. About a third were classed as high risk (poverty, perinatal stress, family conflict, parental mental illness).
  3. About a third of those high-risk children grew into competent, caring adults; protective factors included a close bond with a caregiver and supportive adults outside the family (Werner, 1993).

Adverse childhood experiences

Felitti et al. (1998) surveyed over 9,000 adults in the US and found that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), such as abuse, neglect and household dysfunction, were common and showed a dose–response relationship with later health risks: the more ACEs, the higher the risk. ACE scores are useful at the population level but poor at predicting outcomes for individuals, and screening individuals with ACE scores has been criticised (Baldwin et al., 2021).

Interventions that build protective factors include high-quality early childhood education, parenting support and mentoring. The Perry Preschool Project, a randomised study in Michigan in the 1960s, found children given a high-quality preschool programme had higher earnings and fewer arrests by age 40 than controls (Schweinhart et al., 2005).

Common mistakeTreating ACE scores as predicting an individual's future. They describe population risk, and many people with high scores do well.

In 2 lines: Risk factors raise and protective factors lower the chance of poor outcomes; resilience is a process. The Kauai study showed many high-risk children did well; ACEs describe population risk, not individual destiny.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A warm relationship with a caring adult is an example of a…
    • Risk factor
    • Adverse childhood experience
    • Protective factor
    • Diagnosis
    Show the answer

    Answer: Protective factor. A caring adult relationship reduces the impact of adversity, so it is protective.

  2. Adults with four or more ACEs had higher health risks than those with none. This illustrates…
    • Resilience
    • A protective factor
    • Fast mapping
    • A dose–response relationship
    Show the answer

    Answer: A dose–response relationship. Risk rose step by step with each additional ACE: a dose–response relationship.

  3. Why did Masten describe resilience as "ordinary magic"?
    • It comes from everyday resources
    • It is extremely rare
    • It is genetic only
    • It needs therapy
    Show the answer

    Answer: It comes from everyday resources. Relationships and competent care usually drive resilience.

Key terms

Resilience
Positive adaptation despite significant adversity
Protective factor
Something that reduces the impact of risk
Adverse childhood experiences
Abuse, neglect and household dysfunction in childhood
