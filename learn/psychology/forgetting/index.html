Forgetting

Forgetting is rapid at first and then slows; decay, interference, retrieval failure and motivated forgetting are its main explanations.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the forgetting curve and the main theories of forgetting
  • Explain how savings and relearning reveal memory that seems lost
  • Evaluate trace decay as an explanation of forgetting

The forgetting curve

Ebbinghaus (1885) taught himself lists of nonsense syllables (such as "DAX", "BOK") and measured how much effort it took to relearn them after various delays. He found forgetting was rapid at first and then levelled off: the forgetting curve. He measured savings: relearning a list took less time than learning it originally, even when he could no longer recall it, showing some memory remained.

Worked example

Reading the curve

  1. Ebbinghaus's savings fell steeply within the first hour or so and then declined slowly over days.
  2. A replication by Murre and Dros (2015) found a similar curve.
  3. The practical lesson: early review, soon after learning, prevents much of the loss.

Theories of forgetting

  • Trace decay: memory traces fade with time if not used. Hard to test, because time is always filled with other events.
  • Interference: other memories disrupt recall (next lesson).
  • Retrieval failure: the memory is stored but can't be accessed without the right cues.
  • Motivated forgetting: distressing memories are pushed out of awareness, as Freud proposed with repression; evidence for repression is disputed.

Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924) found participants who slept after learning remembered more than those who stayed awake, suggesting events in waking time, not time itself, cause forgetting, which challenges pure decay theory.

Investigate with the lab. Recall the list after a distractor task and see which part of your curve disappears.

Common mistakeAssuming forgotten means gone. Savings and cued recall show much forgotten information is still stored.

In 2 lines: Ebbinghaus showed forgetting is fast at first and then slows, and that savings reveal stored memory. Decay, interference, retrieval failure and motivated forgetting explain forgetting; decay is hard to test.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Ebbinghaus studied his own memory for…
    • Nonsense syllables
    • Faces
    • Childhood events
    • Song lyrics
    Show the answer

    Answer: Nonsense syllables. Nonsense syllables limited the effect of prior meaning.

  2. Participants who sleep after learning remember more than those who stay awake. What does this suggest?
    • Sleep erases memory
    • Waking events cause interference
    • Time alone causes forgetting
    • Memory improves at night only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Waking events cause interference. Fewer intervening events while asleep means less interference.

  3. What is a limitation of Ebbinghaus's research?
    • It used too many participants
    • It ignored time
    • One participant and meaningless material
    • Its results were never replicated
    Show the answer

    Answer: One participant and meaningless material. Single-participant data with nonsense syllables limits generalisation.

  4. What happens to the recency effect after a distractor task?
    • It gets stronger
    • It moves to the start of the list
    • It is unaffected
    • It disappears
    Show the answer

    Answer: It disappears. The distractor displaces the last items from short-term memory.

Key terms

Forgetting curve
The rapid early and slower later decline in retention over time
Savings
The reduction in time needed to relearn material compared with learning it originally
Trace decay
The idea that memory traces fade with time if not used
