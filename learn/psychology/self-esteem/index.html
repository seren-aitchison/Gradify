Self-esteem

Self-esteem is a global self-evaluation; it predicts happiness and is a depression risk factor, but programmes to boost it rarely improve performance.

Psychology · Personality and Individual Differences · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define self-esteem and describe how it is measured
  • Explain evidence on what self-esteem predicts
  • Evaluate attempts to raise self-esteem and the alternative of self-compassion

Measuring self-esteem

Self-esteem is a person's overall evaluation of their worth. The most widely used measure is the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (1965): ten statements such as "On the whole, I am satisfied with myself", rated for agreement. Self-esteem can be global or domain-specific (academic, social, physical). Self-esteem tends to dip in adolescence, rise through adulthood and fall in old age (Robins et al., 2002).

What does it predict?

Worked example

A self-esteem review

  1. Baumeister et al. (2003) reviewed decades of research for the American Psychological Society.
  2. High self-esteem was linked to happiness and persistence after failure, but only weakly to school performance and job performance.
  3. It didn't prevent children from smoking, drinking or early sexual activity, and some people with high self-esteem were more aggressive when their ego was threatened.

Longitudinal studies suggest low self-esteem is a risk factor for later depression (Sowislo and Orth, 2013). The direction is debated: success may raise self-esteem more than self-esteem causes success.

Raising self-esteem

The "self-esteem movement" of the 1980s and 1990s promoted praising children to raise self-esteem. Evidence suggests this rarely improves performance. Self-compassion (Neff, 2003), treating oneself kindly when failing, is linked to well-being and resilience without the defensiveness sometimes found in high self-esteem.

Common mistakeAssuming raising self-esteem causes success. Much of the link runs from success to self-esteem.

In 2 lines: Self-esteem is measured by scales such as Rosenberg's and changes across the lifespan. It predicts happiness and depression risk but not performance; self-compassion is an alternative focus.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A person's overall evaluation of their own worth is…
    • Self-esteem
    • Self-concept
    • Self-efficacy
    • Self-schema
    Show the answer

    Answer: Self-esteem. Self-esteem is an evaluation of worth, while self-concept is a set of beliefs.

  2. A school praises all pupils' work to raise self-esteem, but grades don't improve. Which finding does this fit?
    • Self-esteem always improves grades
    • Boosting self-esteem rarely raises performance
    • Praise always harms pupils
    • Grades cause no self-esteem
    Show the answer

    Answer: Boosting self-esteem rarely raises performance. Baumeister et al. found weak links to performance.

  3. Why might the link between self-esteem and success be misleading?
    • Self-esteem can't be measured
    • Success never affects people
    • Success may raise self-esteem
    • All studies are experiments
    Show the answer

    Answer: Success may raise self-esteem. The causal direction may run from success to self-esteem.
