Psychology in Everyday Life

Psychology is applied across education, health, design, law and policy, but only claims backed by good evidence should be trusted.

Psychology · Foundations of Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe examples of psychology applied to everyday life
  • Apply psychological findings to an everyday situation
  • Evaluate popular psychological claims using the principles of evidence

Psychology all around

  • Learning and study: spaced practice and retrieval practice (testing yourself) improve long-term memory more than rereading (Dunlosky et al., 2013).
  • Health: behaviour change techniques in stop-smoking services and public health campaigns.
  • Design: road signs, cockpits and apps designed around attention and memory limits.
  • Law: guidelines on eyewitness identification informed by memory research (e.g. Loftus and Palmer, 1974).
  • Policy: the UK Behavioural Insights Team, set up in government in 2010, applies behavioural science to public services.
Worked example

Applying retrieval practice to revision

  1. Instead of rereading notes, close the book and write down everything you remember, then check.
  2. Space the sessions out over days and weeks rather than cramming.
  3. Both techniques were rated as having high utility in a major review of learning techniques (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

Applying findings well means checking that they were tested in conditions like the ones they will be used in, with people like the ones who will use them.

Judging psychological claims

Popular psychology spreads quickly, and not all of it is accurate. Learning styles (matching teaching to visual, auditory or kinaesthetic learners) is widely believed, yet a major review found little evidence that matching improves learning (Pashler et al., 2008). Useful questions: what is the evidence and who produced it? Was there a control group? Has it been replicated? Is it correlation presented as causation? Is someone selling something?

Common mistakeAssuming a claim is scientific because it uses brain images or technical words. Neuroscience language can make weak explanations seem more convincing.

In 2 lines: Psychology is applied in learning, health, design, law and policy. Popular claims such as learning styles need checking against controlled, replicated evidence.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Testing yourself on material to strengthen memory is called…
    • Retrieval practice
    • Massed practice
    • Rereading
    • Learning styles
    Show the answer

    Answer: Retrieval practice. Retrieval practice strengthens memory more than passive rereading.

  2. A student has an exam in three weeks. Which plan best applies research on learning?
    • Rereading the notes many times the night before
    • Short self-testing sessions spread over the three weeks
    • Highlighting the textbook once in colour
    • Studying only in their preferred learning style
    Show the answer

    Answer: Short self-testing sessions spread over the three weeks. Spaced retrieval practice has strong evidence; massed rereading, highlighting and style-matching don't.

  3. An advert claims a supplement "boosts brain power" and cites customer reviews. What is the main weakness of this evidence?
    • Customer reviews are always written by people paid to lie about products
    • No substance taken by mouth can ever affect how the brain works
    • There's no control group, so improvements could be expectation, chance or other factors
    • The claim uses the word "brain", which makes any statement unscientific
    Show the answer

    Answer: There's no control group, so improvements could be expectation, chance or other factors. Without a controlled comparison, apparent benefits can't be attributed to the supplement.

Key terms

Retrieval practice
Recalling information from memory (self-testing) to strengthen learning
Spaced practice
Spreading study sessions over time rather than massing them together
