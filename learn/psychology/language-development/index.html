Language Development

Infants tune into their language through perception and statistical learning; vocabulary and grammar grow rapidly; conversational interaction supports development.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe how infants perceive and segment speech
  • Explain vocabulary and grammar development, including fast mapping
  • Evaluate evidence on how caregiver speech supports language development

Tuning in

Young infants can distinguish speech sounds from many languages. By about 10–12 months, they lose sensitivity to many contrasts not used in their own language: English-learning infants stop distinguishing some Hindi sounds (Werker and Tees, 1984). Infants also use statistical learning to find words: Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) found 8-month-olds could pick out "words" from a continuous stream of syllables after two minutes, using how often syllables followed each other.

Words and grammar

First words appear around 12 months. The vocabulary spurt often follows at around 18 months to 2 years. Children use fast mapping to link a new word to a meaning after very little exposure. Early errors include overextension (calling all men "daddy") and underextension (using "cat" only for the family cat). Grammar develops through two-word combinations to complex sentences, with predictable morphemes such as "-ing" and plural "-s" appearing in a typical order (Brown, 1973).

Worked example

Words heard

  1. Hart and Risley (1995) recorded talk in 42 US families and reported large differences in words children heard, the much-cited "30 million word gap".
  2. A larger replication found much smaller differences, partly because overheard speech was counted (Sperry, Sperry and Miller, 2019).
  3. Later studies suggest the quality of interaction, such as back-and-forth conversational turns, predicts language growth more than the raw number of words (Romeo et al., 2018).

Bilingual children learn two languages along broadly similar timelines; their vocabulary in each language may be smaller, but combined vocabulary is typical. There is no good evidence that bilingualism causes language delay.

Investigate with the lab. Find the language milestones on the timeline, from babbling to two-word sentences.

Common mistakeRepeating the "30 million word gap" as settled fact. Replications found smaller differences, and interaction quality matters more.

In 2 lines: Infants tune into native sounds and segment words by statistical learning. Vocabulary and grammar grow fast; conversational turns support language; the word gap is debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Calling every man "daddy" is an example of…
    • Underextension
    • Fast mapping
    • Telegraphic speech
    • Overextension
    Show the answer

    Answer: Overextension. Using a word too broadly is overextension.

  2. A 12-month-old English learner no longer distinguishes two Hindi sounds she could tell apart at 6 months. This shows…
    • Perceptual tuning
    • Hearing loss after illness
    • Overextension of words
    • Fast mapping of new words
    Show the answer

    Answer: Perceptual tuning. Infants lose sensitivity to non-native contrasts.

  3. Why is the "30 million word gap" contested?
    • It was never measured
    • Replications found smaller differences
    • Words don't matter
    • It studied only bilinguals
    Show the answer

    Answer: Replications found smaller differences. Larger samples and broader counting reduced the gap.

  4. At about what age do children typically produce two-word sentences?
    • Around 3 months
    • Around 5 years
    • Around 18 to 24 months
    • Around 10 years
    Show the answer

    Answer: Around 18 to 24 months. The two-word stage typically begins around 18 to 24 months.

Key terms

Statistical learning
Detecting patterns, such as syllable sequences, from input
Fast mapping
Learning a word's meaning from very little exposure
Overextension
Using a word too broadly, as in calling all animals "doggy"
