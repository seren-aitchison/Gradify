Kin Selection and Inclusive Fitness

Kin selection explains helping relatives through inclusive fitness; Hamilton's rule (rB > C) predicts when helping evolves; human evidence is consistent but shaped by culture.

Psychology · Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain inclusive fitness and Hamilton's rule
  • Apply Hamilton's rule to examples of helping
  • Evaluate evidence for kin selection in humans

Helping relatives

Natural selection seems to favour selfishness, yet many animals help others. Hamilton (1964) explained helping relatives with inclusive fitness: an individual's genes can be passed on through its own reproduction (direct fitness) and through helping relatives who share genes (indirect fitness). Full siblings share on average half their genes by descent, so helping a sibling reproduce can spread one's genes.

Hamilton's rule

Hamilton's rule states that a gene for helping will spread if rB > C, where r is the coefficient of relatedness between helper and recipient, B is the benefit to the recipient (in extra offspring) and C is the cost to the helper. Haldane is said to have joked he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins.

Worked example

Alarm calls

  1. Sherman (1977) studied Belding's ground squirrels and found females were more likely to give alarm calls when relatives were nearby.
  2. Callers were more likely to be attacked by predators, so calling was costly.
  3. Calling patterns fitted kin selection: helping close relatives offset the cost.

Humans

Burnstein, Crandall and Kitayama (1994) found people said they were more willing to help close relatives than distant ones, especially in life-or-death situations. Wills and inheritance also tend to favour close kin. Critics note that cultural norms and social closeness also shape helping, and that adoptive and step-relationships involve strong care without genetic relatedness.

Common mistakeThinking kin selection requires individuals to calculate relatedness. Selection shapes tendencies; no conscious calculation is needed.

In 2 lines: Inclusive fitness includes direct and indirect fitness. Hamilton's rule predicts helping; ground squirrels and human studies support kin selection.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Fitness gained by helping relatives reproduce is…
    • Indirect fitness
    • Direct fitness
    • Reciprocal altruism
    • Sexual selection
    Show the answer

    Answer: Indirect fitness. Helping kin spreads shared genes indirectly.

  2. Squirrels give alarm calls mostly when relatives are nearby. This supports…
    • Group selection
    • Kin selection
    • Sexual selection
    • Mismatch
    Show the answer

    Answer: Kin selection. Costly alarm calls benefit nearby relatives, which fits kin selection.

  3. Why do adoptive families challenge purely genetic explanations of care?
    • Adoptive parents don't care
    • Genes explain all care
    • Strong care occurs without relatedness
    • Adoption is rare
    Show the answer

    Answer: Strong care occurs without relatedness. Care without genetic ties shows other influences.

Key terms

Inclusive fitness
Fitness from one's own reproduction plus effects on relatives' reproduction
Hamilton's rule
Helping evolves if rB > C
Coefficient of relatedness
The probability that two individuals share a gene by descent
