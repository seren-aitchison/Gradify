Habit Formation

Habits form through repetition of a behaviour in a consistent context, until the context triggers it automatically; this takes weeks to months.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define a habit and describe how habits differ from goal-directed behaviour
  • Explain the role of context, repetition and reward in habit formation
  • Apply habit research to design a strategy for behaviour change

What a habit is

A habit is a behaviour that is triggered automatically by a cue in a stable context, after repeated performance in that context. Unlike goal-directed behaviour, which depends on wanting an outcome, a habit continues even when the outcome no longer matters, such as reaching for a phone without wanting to check anything. Research in animals and humans links the shift from goal-directed to habitual control with different circuits in the basal ganglia.

How long do habits take?

Lally and colleagues (2010) asked 96 volunteers to repeat a new daily behaviour (such as eating fruit at lunch) and rate its automaticity. Automaticity rose in a curve that levelled off; the median time to reach the plateau was 66 days, with a very wide range (18 to 254 days). Missing a single day didn't seriously harm the process.

Worked example

Building a revision habit

  1. Choose a stable cue: after dinner at the desk.
  2. Make the behaviour small and easy: 20 minutes of self-testing.
  3. Repeat daily in the same context; don't worry about missing one day.
  4. Over weeks, sitting at the desk after dinner starts to trigger revision automatically.

Breaking habits

Because habits are cued by context, they are hard to break by willpower alone. Strategies include changing or avoiding the cue, making the behaviour harder (friction), and replacing it with a new behaviour in response to the same cue. Changes of context, such as moving house, are windows when habits can change more easily (Wood and Neal, 2007).

Common mistakeBelieving it takes "21 days" to form a habit. This popular figure has no solid basis; research shows it varies widely and often takes longer.

In 2 lines: Habits are context-cued automatic behaviours, formed by repetition. Formation took a median of 66 days in one study, with wide variation; breaking habits works best by changing cues.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. What triggers a habit?
    • A conscious goal every time
    • A cue in a stable context
    • A new, unfamiliar setting
    • Punishment after the behaviour
    Show the answer

    Answer: A cue in a stable context. Habits are cued automatically by their usual context.

  2. Someone wants to stop snacking while watching TV in the evening. Which strategy fits habit research best?
    • Rely on willpower each evening
    • Watch more TV to get used to it
    • Remove snacks from beside the sofa
    • Snack more to satisfy the craving
    Show the answer

    Answer: Remove snacks from beside the sofa. Habits are cue-driven, so changing the context or adding friction is effective.

  3. Why is the claim that habits take 21 days to form misleading?
    • Habits form instantly after one repetition
    • Habits can never be formed deliberately
    • Research shows exactly 21 days for everyone
    • Evidence shows formation varies widely and often takes much longer
    Show the answer

    Answer: Evidence shows formation varies widely and often takes much longer. Lally et al. found a median of 66 days and a range of 18 to 254.

Key terms

Habit
A behaviour triggered automatically by a context cue after repeated performance
Goal-directed behaviour
Behaviour controlled by wanting a particular outcome
