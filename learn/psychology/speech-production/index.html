Speech Production

Speech production moves from concept to words to sounds; errors and aphasia reveal the underlying stages and brain systems.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the stages of speech production
  • Explain what speech errors and tip-of-the-tongue states reveal
  • Evaluate evidence from aphasia about the brain basis of speech

From idea to sound

Levelt's (1989) model describes three broad stages: conceptualisation (deciding what to say), formulation (selecting words and grammatical structure, then their sounds) and articulation (producing the speech with the vocal tract). Speakers produce about two to three words per second.

What errors reveal

Speech errors follow patterns that suggest these stages. Spoonerisms swap sounds between words ("you have hissed all my mystery lectures" for "missed all my history lectures"). Word substitutions usually replace a word with one of the same grammatical category. Tip-of-the-tongue states show that the meaning of a word can be retrieved without its sound: people often know the first letter or number of syllables (Brown and McNeill, 1966).

Worked example

Aphasia

  1. Broca's aphasia (frontal damage): slow, effortful speech with simplified grammar, but comprehension relatively spared.
  2. Wernicke's aphasia (temporal damage): fluent speech that lacks meaning, with poor comprehension.
  3. These dissociations suggest separable processes, though modern imaging shows language relies on broader networks.

Speakers also monitor their own speech and repair errors, often within a fraction of a second, suggesting an internal check before and during articulation. Gesture is closely linked to speech: people gesture even on the phone, and gesture may help retrieve words.

Common mistakeAssuming speech errors are random slips. They follow systematic patterns that reveal how speech is planned.

In 2 lines: Speech production involves conceptualisation, formulation and articulation. Speech errors, tip-of-the-tongue states and aphasia reveal its stages and brain basis.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. "You have hissed all my mystery lectures" is an example of a…
    • Spoonerism
    • Garden-path sentence
    • Aphasia
    • Prime
    Show the answer

    Answer: Spoonerism. The first sounds of two words have been swapped: a spoonerism.

  2. A patient speaks fluently but makes little sense and struggles to understand others. Which condition fits?
    • Broca's aphasia
    • Wernicke's aphasia
    • Prosopagnosia
    • Amnesia
    Show the answer

    Answer: Wernicke's aphasia. Fluent but meaningless speech with poor comprehension suggests Wernicke's aphasia.

  3. What do systematic speech errors suggest about speech planning?
    • Speech is unplanned
    • Errors are always random
    • Words and sounds are planned in stages
    • Grammar plays no role
    Show the answer

    Answer: Words and sounds are planned in stages. Patterned errors reflect separate planning levels.

Key terms

Spoonerism
A speech error in which sounds are swapped between words
Tip-of-the-tongue state
Knowing a word but being temporarily unable to produce it
Aphasia
A language disorder caused by brain damage
