Cognitive Neuroscience Today

Cognitive neuroscience uses imaging, stimulation, lesions and models; research has moved from localisation to networks; small samples undermine reliability; reverse inference needs caution.

Psychology · Contemporary Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe current methods in cognitive neuroscience
  • Explain the problem of small samples and reproducibility in brain imaging
  • Evaluate claims about localisation and brain networks

Methods and approaches

Cognitive neuroscience combines imaging (fMRI, EEG, MEG), brain stimulation (TMS), lesion studies and computational modelling. Research has shifted from finding single brain "centres" for functions towards studying networks: distributed sets of regions that work together, such as the default mode network and attention networks. Large projects, such as the Human Connectome Project and UK Biobank, scan thousands of people.

Sample size and reproducibility

Button et al. (2013) showed that many neuroscience studies had low statistical power because of small samples, making findings less likely to be true and effect sizes likely to be inflated. Marek et al. (2022) found that brain-wide association studies, linking individual differences in brain structure or function to traits, need thousands of participants to produce reproducible results.

Worked example

Reverse inference

  1. Reverse inference reasons from brain activity to mental state: "the amygdala was active, so participants were afraid".
  2. Because most regions are involved in many functions, this reasoning is often weak.
  3. Poldrack (2006) argued reverse inference should be used cautiously and checked against databases of many tasks.

Evaluation

Cognitive neuroscience has advanced understanding of memory, attention and emotion, and network approaches better reflect how the brain works. But small samples, flexible analysis and reverse inference have produced unreliable claims. Larger samples, preregistration and data sharing are improving the field.

Common mistakeTreating brain activity as direct proof of a mental state. Most regions serve many functions.

In 2 lines: Methods include imaging, stimulation, lesions and models; networks over centres. Small samples undermine reliability; reverse inference needs caution.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The probability that a study detects a real effect is its…
    • Reverse inference
    • Statistical power
    • Effect size
    • Reliability coefficient
    Show the answer

    Answer: Statistical power. Statistical power is the chance of detecting a real effect and depends heavily on sample size.

  2. A news story claims "scientists found the love centre" because one region lit up when people saw partners. This relies on…
    • Lesion evidence
    • Brain stimulation
    • Reverse inference
    • A large sample
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reverse inference. Inferring love from activity is reverse inference.

  3. Why has research shifted from brain "centres" to networks?
    • Each function has exactly one region
    • Brain networks don't exist at all
    • Imaging can't detect any regions
    • Functions depend on distributed regions working together
    Show the answer

    Answer: Functions depend on distributed regions working together. Distributed activity better explains cognition.

Key terms

Statistical power
The probability that a study detects a real effect
Reverse inference
Inferring a mental state from observed brain activity
Brain network
A set of regions that work together
