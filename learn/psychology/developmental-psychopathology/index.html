Developmental Psychopathology

Developmental psychopathology traces pathways to and from difficulties; equifinality, multifinality and timing are key, and specific gene–environment findings need large replications.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the developmental psychopathology approach and its key principles
  • Describe Moffitt's developmental taxonomy of antisocial behaviour
  • Evaluate gene–environment interaction research

Not a diagnosis

This lesson explains research about how difficulties develop, for learning only. It can't be used to judge anyone's mental health. If you are worried about yourself or someone else, speak to a GP, school or university wellbeing service or another qualified professional; in an emergency in the UK, call 999 or contact Samaritans on 116 123.

A developmental approach

Developmental psychopathology (Cicchetti, 1984; Sroufe and Rutter, 1984) studies typical and atypical development together, asking how pathways lead towards or away from difficulties. Two principles are central:

  • Equifinality: different pathways can lead to the same outcome (depression may follow bereavement, bullying or chronic illness).
  • Multifinality: the same starting point can lead to different outcomes (maltreated children may develop anxiety, conduct problems or show resilience).

Timing matters: Kessler et al. (2005) estimated that half of all lifetime mental disorders in the US had started by age 14 and three-quarters by age 24, highlighting the importance of childhood and adolescence.

Worked example

Two pathways to antisocial behaviour

  1. Moffitt (1993) proposed that a small group shows life-course-persistent antisocial behaviour from early childhood, linked to early neurodevelopmental difficulties and adverse environments.
  2. A larger group shows adolescence-limited antisocial behaviour, starting in adolescence and usually stopping in early adulthood.
  3. The Dunedin longitudinal study in New Zealand supported the distinction, though some "adolescence-limited" individuals still had difficulties in their 30s (Moffitt et al., 2002).

Genes and environments

Caspi et al. (2003) reported that a serotonin transporter gene variant moderated the link between stressful life events and depression, a gene–environment interaction. Later large studies and meta-analyses failed to replicate this specific finding (Border et al., 2019), illustrating the risks of small candidate-gene studies. Researchers now use genome-wide methods and very large samples.

Common mistakeAssuming a single risk factor leads to a single outcome. Multifinality and equifinality show pathways are varied.

In 2 lines: Developmental psychopathology studies pathways; equifinality and multifinality describe their variety. Moffitt distinguished persistent and adolescence-limited pathways; candidate-gene findings need caution. Not diagnostic.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Different pathways leading to the same outcome is called…
    • Multifinality
    • Resilience
    • Comorbidity
    • Equifinality
    Show the answer

    Answer: Equifinality. Equifinality means many routes to one outcome.

  2. A teenager starts shoplifting at 15 with friends and stops by 20. Moffitt would classify this as…
    • Adolescence-limited
    • Life-course-persistent
    • Equifinality
    • Neurodevelopmental
    Show the answer

    Answer: Adolescence-limited. Antisocial behaviour confined to adolescence is adolescence-limited.

  3. Why have many candidate-gene findings failed to replicate?
    • Genes don't affect behaviour
    • Early studies were small and underpowered
    • Replications used no data
    • Depression has no causes
    Show the answer

    Answer: Early studies were small and underpowered. Small samples produce unstable, inflated findings.

Key terms

Equifinality
Different pathways leading to the same outcome
Multifinality
The same starting point leading to different outcomes
Life-course-persistent
Antisocial behaviour beginning early and continuing into adulthood
