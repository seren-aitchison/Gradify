Wellbeing at Work

Work stress arises from high demands, low control, poor support and effort–reward imbalance; the HSE standards address work design; organisational changes matter more than individual programmes.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes · sources checked 2026-11

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the demand–control–support and effort–reward imbalance models
  • Describe the HSE management standards for work-related stress
  • Evaluate workplace wellbeing interventions

Models of work stress

Karasek's (1979) demand–control model proposes that high-strain jobs, with high demands and low control (decision latitude), are most stressful; adding social support created the demand–control–support model (Karasek and Theorell, 1990). Siegrist's (1996) effort–reward imbalance model proposes that stress arises when high effort isn't matched by rewards such as pay, esteem and job security.

The HSE management standards

The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) identifies six areas of work design that, if not managed, are linked to poor health and lower productivity: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. HSE annual statistics consistently show stress, depression or anxiety as a leading cause of work-related ill health and working days lost in Great Britain.

Worked example

The Whitehall II study

  1. The Whitehall II study followed British civil servants from 1985.
  2. Low job control was associated with higher risk of coronary heart disease, independent of grade and other risk factors (Bosma et al., 1997).
  3. The findings support the demand–control model and show work design affects physical health.

Interventions

Organisational interventions change work design (workload, control, support); individual interventions teach coping (mindfulness, stress management). Evidence suggests individual programmes have small, short-term effects; Fleming (2024) found little benefit of individual wellbeing programmes such as apps and resilience training for UK workers, suggesting changes to working conditions matter more.

Common mistakeAssuming wellbeing apps or resilience training solve work stress. Evidence favours changing working conditions.

In 2 lines: Demand–control–support and effort–reward imbalance models explain work stress. The HSE standards cover six areas; organisational change matters more than individual programmes.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A job with high demands and low control is described by Karasek as…
    • Low strain
    • Active
    • Passive
    • High strain
    Show the answer

    Answer: High strain. High demands with low control create high strain.

  2. A nurse works very hard but feels unrecognised and poorly paid. Which model fits best?
    • Effort–reward imbalance
    • Demand–control only
    • Two-factor theory
    • Scientific management
    Show the answer

    Answer: Effort–reward imbalance. Siegrist's model focuses on effort exceeding rewards.

  3. Why might organisational interventions work better than individual ones?
    • Individuals cause all stress
    • They change the causes of stress
    • Work design never matters
    • Coping skills are harmful
    Show the answer

    Answer: They change the causes of stress. Changing demands, control and support tackles root causes.
