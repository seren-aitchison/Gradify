Decision-making

People decide with bounded rationality, are loss-averse and sensitive to framing; these findings inform policy nudges.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe expected utility theory and bounded rationality
  • Explain prospect theory, loss aversion and framing effects
  • Evaluate applications of decision research, such as nudges

Rational choice and its limits

Expected utility theory says a rational person chooses the option with the highest expected value, weighting each outcome by its probability. Simon (1955) argued people have bounded rationality: limited time, information and processing capacity, so they satisfice, choosing an option that is good enough rather than the best.

Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky's (1979) prospect theory proposes that people judge outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and that losses loom larger than gains: losing £10 feels worse than finding £10 feels good (loss aversion). People also overweight small probabilities, which helps explain both buying lottery tickets and buying insurance.

Worked example

The Asian disease problem

  1. Participants imagined 600 people at risk from a disease (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981).
  2. Framed as gains ("200 people will be saved" versus a one-third chance all 600 are saved), 72% chose the certain option.
  3. Framed as losses ("400 people will die" versus a one-third chance nobody dies), 78% chose the risky option.
  4. The options were identical; only the framing changed.

Nudges

Thaler and Sunstein (2008) argued that "choice architecture" can nudge people toward better decisions without removing choice: for example, automatic enrolment in pension schemes greatly increases saving (Madrian and Shea, 2001). Critics question whether nudging is manipulative, and replications show many nudges have smaller effects than first reported.

Investigate with the lab. Answer the framing, anchoring and base-rate problems before reading the original findings, then try the reaction-time and signal-detection labs.

Common mistakeAssuming that a framing effect means people are irrational in every case. Reference points and loss aversion can be sensible in context.

In 2 lines: Expected utility describes ideal choice; bounded rationality and satisficing describe real choice. Prospect theory explains loss aversion and framing; nudges apply these findings but are debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who proposed bounded rationality?
    • Daniel Kahneman
    • Herbert Simon
    • Peter Wason
    • Richard Thaler
    Show the answer

    Answer: Herbert Simon. Simon argued real decision-makers satisfice.

  2. A shop says a product has "a 90% success rate" rather than "a 10% failure rate", and sales rise. This is…
    • Loss aversion only
    • Satisficing
    • A framing effect
    • Mental set
    Show the answer

    Answer: A framing effect. Equivalent information framed positively changes choices.

  3. Which is the strongest ethical criticism of nudging?
    • It removes all choices
    • It has no effects
    • It uses expected utility
    • It may steer people without awareness
    Show the answer

    Answer: It may steer people without awareness. Critics argue nudges can be manipulative even if choice remains.

  4. What is the framing effect?
    • Choices change depending on whether options are described as gains or losses
    • People always choose the safest option
    • People ignore all numbers in decisions
    • Decisions depend only on the final outcome
    Show the answer

    Answer: Choices change depending on whether options are described as gains or losses. Tversky and Kahneman showed choices reverse when the same outcome is framed differently.

Key terms

Bounded rationality
Decision-making limited by time, information and capacity
Loss aversion
The tendency for losses to feel worse than equivalent gains feel good
Framing effect
Different choices depending on how equivalent options are described
