Self-concept

The self-concept is a person's beliefs about themselves, built through reflected appraisals, social comparison and culture.

Psychology · Personality and Individual Differences · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define self-concept and distinguish it from self-esteem
  • Explain how the self-concept develops through reflected appraisals and social comparison
  • Explain cultural differences in self-concept

Who am I?

The self-concept is the organised set of beliefs about oneself: traits ("I'm shy"), roles ("I'm a student"), relationships and values. It differs from self-esteem, which is how positively a person evaluates themselves. The Twenty Statements Test (Kuhn and McPartland, 1954) asks people to complete "I am…" twenty times, revealing which aspects are most important to them.

How it develops

Cooley's (1902) looking-glass self proposed we come to see ourselves through how we think others see us (reflected appraisals). Festinger's (1954) social comparison theory proposed we evaluate our abilities by comparing ourselves with others, especially similar others. Self-schemas (Markus, 1977) are organised beliefs about the self in particular domains that guide attention and memory.

Worked example

Big fish, little pond

  1. Marsh and Parker (1984) found pupils of similar ability had higher academic self-concepts in lower-ability schools than in high-ability schools.
  2. Comparison with classmates shapes how able pupils think they are.
  3. This "big-fish-little-pond effect" has been replicated across many countries (Marsh and Hau, 2003).

Culture and the self

Markus and Kitayama (1991) contrasted independent self-construals (common in individualist cultures), describing the self by personal attributes, with interdependent self-construals (common in collectivist cultures), describing the self through relationships and roles. In the Twenty Statements Test, American students tend to list more traits and Japanese students more social roles.

Common mistakeEquating self-concept with self-esteem. One is what you think you are; the other is how you feel about it.

In 2 lines: The self-concept is distinct from self-esteem and includes traits, roles and values. It develops through reflected appraisals and comparison, and varies with culture.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Completing "I am…" twenty times is the…
    • Rorschach test
    • Twenty Statements Test
    • NEO-PI-R
    • Strange Situation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Twenty Statements Test. It reveals the content of the self-concept.

  2. A strong student moves to a selective school and starts to think she isn't clever. Which effect explains this?
    • The halo effect in judgement
    • The spotlight effect
    • Big-fish-little-pond effect
    • The false consensus effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Big-fish-little-pond effect. Comparison with abler classmates lowers academic self-concept.

  3. Why might Japanese students list more social roles than American students?
    • They have no personality traits
    • Roles are easier to write down
    • American students are more modest
    • Interdependent selves
    Show the answer

    Answer: Interdependent selves. Interdependent self-construals define the self through relationships and roles.

Key terms

Self-concept
The organised set of beliefs a person holds about themselves
Looking-glass self
Cooley's idea that we see ourselves through others' eyes
Social comparison
Evaluating oneself by comparison with others
