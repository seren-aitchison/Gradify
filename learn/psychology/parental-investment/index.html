Parental Investment

Parental investment theory explains sex differences in care and parent–offspring conflict; the Cinderella effect and grandmother hypothesis are debated applications.

Psychology · Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain parental investment and its link to sex differences
  • Explain parent–offspring conflict
  • Evaluate evidence on paternal investment and family relationships in humans

Investment and its costs

Trivers (1972) defined parental investment as any investment by a parent in an offspring that increases the offspring's chance of surviving at the cost of the parent's ability to invest in other offspring. Human children need many years of care, so investment is high. Mothers' minimum investment (pregnancy, birth, breastfeeding) is greater than fathers', which helps explain why mothers on average provide more direct care across cultures.

Parent–offspring conflict

Trivers (1974) argued parents and offspring don't share identical genetic interests. A parent is equally related to all its children (r = 0.5), but each child is more related to itself (r = 1) than to its siblings (r = 0.5). So children are expected to demand more investment than parents want to give, producing conflict over weaning, attention and resources. Haig (1993) proposed that conflict even occurs in pregnancy, between fetal and maternal interests over nutrients.

Worked example

Grandmothers and stepchildren

  1. The "grandmother hypothesis" (Hawkes et al., 1998) proposes that women's long post-reproductive lifespan evolved because grandmothers helped their grandchildren survive.
  2. Daly and Wilson (1988) found children in Canada living with a step-parent were at much higher risk of fatal abuse than those living with two genetic parents, which they called the Cinderella effect.
  3. Critics note most step-parents are loving, and other factors such as family stress, poverty and selection into step-families contribute.

Fathers

Human fathers invest more than most male mammals, through providing resources, protection and care. Paternal investment varies widely across cultures and with paternity certainty, economic conditions and social norms. Modern research finds fathers' involvement benefits child development (Sarkadi et al., 2008).

Common mistakeInterpreting the Cinderella effect as saying step-parents are generally harmful. The absolute risk is very low and most step-parents care well.

In 2 lines: Parental investment explains sex differences in care. Parent–offspring conflict, the grandmother hypothesis and the Cinderella effect apply the theory; paternal investment varies with conditions.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who proposed parental investment theory?
    • Martin Daly
    • Kristen Hawkes
    • David Haig
    • Robert Trivers
    Show the answer

    Answer: Robert Trivers. Robert Trivers set out parental investment theory in 1972.

  2. A toddler demands more attention than a parent with several children wants to give. This illustrates…
    • Parent–offspring conflict
    • The Cinderella effect
    • The grandmother hypothesis
    • Kin selection only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Parent–offspring conflict. Each child seeks more than the parent's optimal share.

  3. Why might women live long after they stop reproducing, according to the grandmother hypothesis?
    • Menopause has no function
    • Grandmothers helped grandchildren survive
    • Men live longer
    • Grandmothers had more children
    Show the answer

    Answer: Grandmothers helped grandchildren survive. Help to grandchildren boosted inclusive fitness.

Key terms

Parental investment
Investment in an offspring that reduces a parent's ability to invest in others
Parent–offspring conflict
Conflict arising because parents and offspring have different genetic interests
Cinderella effect
Higher risk of abuse to children living with a step-parent
