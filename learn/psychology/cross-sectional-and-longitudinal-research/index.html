Cross-sectional and Longitudinal Research

Cross-sectional designs are fast but confound age with cohort; longitudinal designs track real change but are slow and lose participants.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe cross-sectional and longitudinal designs
  • Explain cohort effects and attrition
  • Evaluate which design suits a research question about development or change

Two ways to study change

  • Cross-sectional: compare different groups (e.g. 20-, 40- and 60-year-olds) at the same time. Quick and cheap, but differences may reflect cohort effects: the groups grew up in different times, with different schooling, diet and technology.
  • Longitudinal: follow the same people over time, measuring them repeatedly. Shows individual change and the order of events, but is slow and costly, and suffers attrition (people drop out, often not at random).

Cohort-sequential designs combine both: several cohorts are followed for a shorter period, separating age effects from cohort effects.

Worked example

The Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study

  1. About 1,000 people born in Dunedin, New Zealand, in 1972–73 have been assessed repeatedly from birth into middle age.
  2. Retention has been unusually high, reducing attrition bias.
  3. It has shown, for example, that childhood self-control predicts adult health and wealth (Moffitt et al., 2011), though one town's cohort may not represent other places or generations.

Choosing a design

Questions about how individuals change, or what predicts later outcomes, need longitudinal data; quick descriptions of age differences can use cross-sectional data, interpreted cautiously.

Common mistakeConcluding from a cross-sectional study that people change as they age. Differences between age groups may reflect when people were born, not ageing.

In 2 lines: Cross-sectional studies compare age groups at one time; longitudinal studies follow the same people. Cohort effects threaten cross-sectional studies; attrition, cost and time threaten longitudinal studies.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Comparing 20-year-olds and 70-year-olds on a memory test this week is a…
    • Longitudinal design
    • Case study
    • Field experiment
    • Cross-sectional design
    Show the answer

    Answer: Cross-sectional design. Different age groups are compared at one point in time.

  2. In a cross-sectional study, older adults score lower on a computer-based reasoning test. Which alternative explanation is most important?
    • Cohort effects such as less experience with computers
    • Attrition of younger participants
    • Practice effects from repeated testing
    • The test was given longitudinally
    Show the answer

    Answer: Cohort effects such as less experience with computers. Older cohorts grew up with less computer use, which could explain lower scores.

  3. Which design best separates the effects of ageing from cohort effects?
    • A single cross-sectional study
    • A cohort-sequential design following several cohorts
    • A single case study
    • A one-off survey of one age group
    Show the answer

    Answer: A cohort-sequential design following several cohorts. Following several cohorts over time lets researchers compare same-age people from different cohorts.

Key terms

Cohort effect
Differences between age groups caused by the different times in which they grew up
Attrition
Participants dropping out of a longitudinal study
Longitudinal design
Studying the same participants repeatedly over time
