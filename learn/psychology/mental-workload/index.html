Mental Workload

Mental workload reflects demands relative to resources; it can be measured subjectively, by performance or physiologically; both overload and underload impair safety.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define mental workload and explain its link to attention and resources
  • Describe subjective, performance and physiological measures of workload
  • Apply workload research to design and safety

Limited resources

Mental workload reflects the relationship between task demands and a person's available mental resources. Wickens's multiple resource theory suggests tasks interfere most when they compete for the same resources (for example, two visual tasks). Both overload (too many demands) and underload (too few, leading to boredom and reduced vigilance) can impair performance.

Measuring workload

  • Subjective: rating scales such as the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX; Hart and Staveland, 1988), which rates mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort and frustration.
  • Performance: secondary task measures (performance on an extra task drops as primary workload rises).
  • Physiological: heart rate variability, pupil dilation and brain activity.
Worked example

Automation and underload

  1. Automation in cockpits and cars can reduce workload, but may leave operators under-loaded and out of the loop.
  2. Bainbridge (1983) described the "ironies of automation": automation removes easy tasks but leaves humans to monitor and take over in rare, difficult situations, for which they may be unpractised.
  3. Partially automated driving raises similar concerns about drivers' readiness to take control.

Applications

Workload research informs the design of control rooms, cockpits, medical devices and shift patterns, and decisions about task allocation between humans and machines. Keeping workload in a moderate range helps maintain performance and safety.

Common mistakeAssuming lower workload is always safer. Underload and automation can reduce vigilance and readiness.

In 2 lines: Workload balances demands and resources; overload and underload both harm performance. NASA-TLX, secondary tasks and physiology measure it; automation creates ironies.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which tool rates mental, physical and temporal demand, effort and frustration?
    • NASA-TLX
    • Maslach Burnout Inventory
    • NEO-PI-R
    • SRRS
    Show the answer

    Answer: NASA-TLX. The NASA Task Load Index has six subscales.

  2. A driver in a partly self-driving car is slow to react when asked to take control. This illustrates…
    • Overload from too many tasks
    • Ironies of automation
    • A simple slip of action
    • Psychological safety
    Show the answer

    Answer: Ironies of automation. Low engagement reduces readiness to take over.

  3. Why might secondary task performance measure workload?
    • It always improves
    • It measures personality
    • Spare capacity shrinks
    • It ignores attention entirely
    Show the answer

    Answer: Spare capacity shrinks. Fewer spare resources leave less capacity for the second task.

Key terms

Mental workload
The demand a task places on limited mental resources
NASA-TLX
A subjective workload rating scale with six dimensions
Ironies of automation
Bainbridge's observation that automation can make human roles harder
