Social Identity Theory

Social identity comes from group memberships; even minimal groups produce in-group favouritism as people seek positive distinctiveness.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe social categorisation, identification and comparison
  • Explain the minimal group paradigm findings
  • Explain how people pursue a positive social identity

The theory

Tajfel and Turner (1979) proposed that part of our self-concept, our social identity, comes from the groups we belong to. Three processes are involved:

  • Social categorisation: sorting people, including ourselves, into groups.
  • Social identification: adopting the identity and norms of our group.
  • Social comparison: comparing our group with others to maintain positive distinctiveness and self-esteem.
Worked example

Minimal groups

  1. Tajfel et al. (1971) assigned Bristol schoolboys to groups on trivial grounds, such as supposedly preferring paintings by Klee or Kandinsky.
  2. Boys allocated points to anonymous members of their own group and the other group.
  3. They favoured their in-group, and often chose to maximise the difference between groups even when this meant their own group got fewer points in total.

Strategies for positive identity

When a group's status is low, members may use individual mobility (leaving the group, if boundaries are permeable), social creativity (comparing on a different dimension, revaluing the group's traits, or comparing with a lower-status group) or social competition (directly challenging the higher-status group, more likely when status differences seem unstable and illegitimate).

Social identity research has been applied to football fans, political movements, health and well-being: belonging to multiple valued groups is linked to better mental health (Haslam et al., 2009).

Common mistakeThinking in-group favouritism needs real conflict or meaningful groups. Even trivial categories can produce it.

In 2 lines: Social identity theory involves categorisation, identification and comparison. Minimal groups produce in-group favouritism; low-status groups use mobility, creativity or competition.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Tajfel's minimal groups were formed on the basis of…
    • Football teams
    • Religion
    • Preferences for paintings
    • Family background
    Show the answer

    Answer: Preferences for paintings. Trivial painting preferences created the groups.

  2. Fans of a losing team say "at least our fans are the most loyal". This is…
    • Individual mobility
    • Social competition
    • Deindividuation
    • Social creativity
    Show the answer

    Answer: Social creativity. Comparing on a new dimension is social creativity.

  3. When is social competition most likely?
    • When status seems illegitimate
    • When boundaries are permeable
    • When groups are equal
    • When members are alone
    Show the answer

    Answer: When status seems illegitimate. Perceived illegitimate, unstable status prompts collective challenge.

Key terms

Social identity
The part of the self-concept that comes from group memberships
Minimal group paradigm
Creating groups on trivial grounds to study intergroup behaviour
Positive distinctiveness
Seeing one's group as positively different from others
