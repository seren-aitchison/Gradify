Measuring Attachment

Attachment is measured by observation in childhood and by interview or self-report in adulthood; different measures capture different aspects.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe observational measures of attachment in infancy and childhood
  • Explain the Adult Attachment Interview and self-report measures
  • Evaluate the reliability and validity of attachment measures

In infancy and childhood

The Strange Situation is the best-known laboratory measure for infants of about 12–18 months. The Attachment Q-sort (Waters and Deane, 1985) is an alternative: an observer watches a child at home for several hours and sorts 90 statements (such as "uses mother as a base for exploration") from most to least characteristic. It can be used with a wider age range and in natural settings. For older children, researchers use reunion observations and story-completion tasks with dolls.

In adulthood

The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI; George, Kaplan and Main, 1985) asks adults about childhood relationships with parents. It is scored not on what happened but on how coherently people discuss it, classifying adults as secure-autonomous, dismissing, preoccupied or unresolved. Parents' AAI classifications predict their infants' Strange Situation classifications above chance (van IJzendoorn, 1995).

Self-report questionnaires, such as the Experiences in Close Relationships scale (Brennan, Clark and Shaver, 1998), measure adult romantic attachment on two dimensions: anxiety (fear of rejection) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness). Hazan and Shaver (1987) first applied attachment types to adult love with a newspaper "love quiz".

Worked example

Two measures, different results

  1. The AAI and romantic self-report questionnaires correlate only weakly (Roisman et al., 2007).
  2. The AAI captures unconscious organisation of thoughts about parents; questionnaires capture conscious feelings about partners.
  3. Researchers need to be clear which aspect of attachment a measure assesses.

Investigate with the lab. Classify each infant from the Strange Situation, then evaluate the method across cultures.

Common mistakeAssuming all attachment measures measure the same thing. Interviews and questionnaires correlate only weakly.

In 2 lines: Infant and child attachment is measured by the Strange Situation, Q-sort and story tasks. Adult attachment is measured by the AAI and self-report; these capture different aspects.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. An observer sorts 90 statements about a child's behaviour at home. Which measure is this?
    • Strange Situation
    • Adult Attachment Interview
    • Attachment Q-sort
    • Love quiz
    Show the answer

    Answer: Attachment Q-sort. The Attachment Q-sort ranks 90 descriptive statements about a child's behaviour.

  2. An adult describes a difficult childhood clearly and reflectively. How might the AAI classify them?
    • Dismissing
    • Preoccupied
    • Unresolved
    • Secure-autonomous
    Show the answer

    Answer: Secure-autonomous. Coherence, not events, leads to a secure classification.

  3. What is an advantage of the Q-sort over the Strange Situation?
    • Natural settings, wider ages
    • It takes only 20 minutes
    • It needs no trained observer
    • It is designed for adults
    Show the answer

    Answer: Natural settings, wider ages. Home observation across ages increases ecological validity.

  4. Which attachment type shows distress at separation and is easily comforted on reunion?
    • Insecure-avoidant
    • Secure
    • Insecure-resistant
    • Disorganised
    Show the answer

    Answer: Secure. Securely attached infants seek and accept comfort from the caregiver.

Key terms

Attachment Q-sort
An observational measure in which statements are sorted by how well they describe a child
Adult Attachment Interview
An interview assessing the coherence of adults' accounts of childhood attachment
Attachment anxiety
Fear of rejection and abandonment in relationships
