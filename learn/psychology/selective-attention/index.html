Selective Attention

Selective attention filters information; theories differ on how early and how completely unattended information is filtered, and load theory reconciles them.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe early-selection, attenuation and late-selection theories of attention
  • Explain the evidence from dichotic listening and inattentional blindness
  • Evaluate theories of selective attention, including load theory

The cocktail party problem

Cherry (1953) used dichotic listening: participants heard two messages, one in each ear, and shadowed (repeated aloud) one of them. They recalled little of the unattended message, noticing only physical changes such as a switch from a male to a female voice. Yet at a party we notice our own name in a nearby conversation, the cocktail party effect, so some unattended information is processed.

Theories

  • Early selection (Broadbent, 1958): a filter selects one channel on physical features before meaning is analysed.
  • Attenuation (Treisman, 1964): unattended messages are weakened, not blocked, so important or expected words (your name) can still break through.
  • Late selection (Deutsch and Deutsch, 1963): all input is analysed for meaning; selection happens just before response.
  • Load theory (Lavie, 1995): when a task demands a lot of perceptual capacity, distractors aren't processed (early selection); when load is low, spare capacity processes distractors (late selection).
Worked example

Inattentional blindness

  1. Participants counted basketball passes by one team in a video (Simons and Chabris, 1999).
  2. A person in a gorilla suit walked through the scene; about half the participants didn't notice.
  3. Without attention, even a salient, unexpected object can go unseen.

Attention can also be studied visually. In visual search, finding a target defined by one feature (a red item among green) is fast regardless of how many distractors there are, while finding a target defined by a combination of features takes longer as distractors are added. Treisman and Gelade (1980) argued that combining features needs focused attention.

Investigate with the lab. Do the Stroop task and measure your interference effect: name the ink colour, not the word.

Common mistakeAssuming unattended information is never processed. Breakthrough of personally relevant words shows some processing occurs.

In 2 lines: Dichotic listening and the cocktail party effect show both filtering and breakthrough. Early, attenuation and late selection theories differ; load theory reconciles them.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who proposed the early-selection filter model of attention?
    • Anne Treisman
    • Nilli Lavie
    • Donald Broadbent
    • Colin Cherry
    Show the answer

    Answer: Donald Broadbent. Broadbent's 1958 model filtered information by physical features.

  2. While shadowing one ear, a participant hears their name in the other ear. Which theory explains this best?
    • Broadbent's strict filter
    • Hick's law
    • Signal detection theory
    • Attenuation theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Attenuation theory. Attenuation lets important words break through.

  3. How does load theory reconcile early and late selection?
    • Selection depends on how demanding the main task is
    • It rejects all evidence on attention
    • It says attention never filters
    • It applies only to hearing
    Show the answer

    Answer: Selection depends on how demanding the main task is. High load produces early selection; low load allows late processing.

  4. Why are people slower to name the ink colour in the Stroop task when it spells a different colour?
    • The colours are hard to see
    • Reading the word is automatic and interferes
    • People read more slowly under time pressure
    • The words are printed too small
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reading the word is automatic and interferes. Automatic reading competes with the slower colour-naming task.

Key terms

Dichotic listening
Presenting different messages to each ear, often asking participants to shadow one
Attenuation
Treisman's idea that unattended information is weakened rather than blocked
Inattentional blindness
Failing to notice an unexpected object when attention is focused elsewhere
