Network Approaches to Mental Health

Network theory treats disorders as interacting symptoms; central symptoms and bridges explain maintenance and comorbidity; replicability and causal inference are challenges.

Psychology · Contemporary Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the network theory of mental disorders
  • Describe how symptom networks are analysed
  • Evaluate the strengths and limitations of network approaches
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Questions about mental health should be discussed with a GP or another qualified professional.

Symptoms as a network

Traditional models treat symptoms as caused by an underlying disorder, as a virus causes fever. Borsboom (2017) proposed a network theory: symptoms directly influence each other. For example, insomnia may cause fatigue, which causes concentration problems, which cause worry, which worsens insomnia. A disorder is a stable state of a strongly connected symptom network.

Analysing networks

Researchers use statistical methods to estimate symptom networks from data, where symptoms are nodes and associations between them are edges. Central symptoms are strongly connected and may be targets for intervention. Networks can also explain comorbidity, since symptoms shared by different conditions act as bridges between them.

Worked example

Replicability concerns

  1. Forbes et al. (2017) found that symptom networks estimated in different samples often differed, raising concerns about replicability.
  2. Network analyses depend on sample size and estimation methods.
  3. Supporters responded that newer methods and larger samples improve stability.

Evaluation

Network approaches offer a fresh way to understand comorbidity and how symptoms maintain each other. But most network estimates come from cross-sectional data, which can't show causal direction, and replicability has been questioned. Whether centrality guides effective treatment remains to be tested.

Common mistakeAssuming network analysis shows that one symptom causes another. Cross-sectional associations don't show direction.

In 2 lines: Symptoms influence each other in networks. Central and bridge symptoms; replicability and causal limits.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. How strongly a symptom is connected within a network is its…
    • Prior
    • Heritability
    • Centrality
    • Precision
    Show the answer

    Answer: Centrality. Central symptoms have many strong connections.

  2. Poor sleep leads to tiredness, which leads to low mood, which worsens sleep. A network approach sees this as…
    • A single hidden cause
    • Reverse inference
    • Loss aversion
    • Symptoms maintaining each other
    Show the answer

    Answer: Symptoms maintaining each other. Mutually reinforcing symptoms form a network.

  3. Why can't most network analyses show causal direction?
    • They often use cross-sectional data
    • They always use experiments
    • Symptoms never change
    • Networks have no edges
    Show the answer

    Answer: They often use cross-sectional data. Associations at one time don't show what causes what.

Key terms

Symptom network
Symptoms as nodes connected by associations
Centrality
How strongly a node is connected within a network
Bridge symptom
A symptom linking clusters of different conditions
