Generalisation and Discrimination

Generalisation spreads a learned response to similar stimuli; discrimination narrows it to the stimulus that actually predicts the outcome.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define stimulus generalisation and discrimination
  • Explain the generalisation gradient and how discrimination training works
  • Apply generalisation and discrimination to anxiety and everyday behaviour

Generalisation

After conditioning, stimuli similar to the CS also produce the CR, more weakly the less similar they are. Plotting response strength against similarity gives a generalisation gradient. Little Albert's fear spread from the rat to a rabbit and a fur coat. Generalisation is adaptive: a predator rarely looks exactly the same twice.

Discrimination

In discrimination training, one stimulus is followed by the UCS (CS+) and a similar one is not (CS−). The organism learns to respond to the CS+ but not the CS−. Pavlov trained dogs to tell apart tones of different pitch; when the discrimination became too hard, some dogs became agitated, which he called "experimental neurosis".

Generalisation also occurs in operant learning: a child praised for tidying their bedroom may also tidy the living room.

Investigate with the lab. Run acquisition and then extinction in the Rescorla–Wagner model and see how a similar stimulus produces a weaker response.

Worked example

Overgeneralised fear

  1. After a car accident on a motorway, someone fears all motorways (generalisation).
  2. Fear then spreads to dual carriageways and being a passenger.
  3. Over-generalised fear is a feature of anxiety disorders; treatment helps people learn which situations are truly dangerous (discrimination).
Common mistakeConfusing generalisation with spontaneous recovery. Generalisation is about similar stimuli; spontaneous recovery is about time after extinction.

In 2 lines: Generalisation spreads responses to similar stimuli; discrimination limits them to the predictive one. Overgeneralisation of fear is linked to anxiety; discrimination learning helps reverse it.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Little Albert's fear of a fur coat is an example of…
    • Stimulus discrimination
    • Stimulus generalisation
    • Extinction
    • Spontaneous recovery
    Show the answer

    Answer: Stimulus generalisation. The fear spread from the rat to a similar furry object.

  2. A dog salivates to a high tone that predicts food but not to a low tone that never does. This shows…
    • Generalisation
    • Habituation
    • Discrimination
    • Extinction
    Show the answer

    Answer: Discrimination. Responding to the predictive stimulus but not a similar one is discrimination.

  3. Why might overgeneralisation of fear be a problem in anxiety disorders?
    • It makes fear disappear too quickly
    • It prevents any learning about danger
    • It only affects fears of animals
    • It spreads fear to safe situations
    Show the answer

    Answer: It spreads fear to safe situations. Fear spreading to safe situations widens avoidance and distress, a pattern seen in anxiety disorders.

  4. What is stimulus generalisation?
    • Responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus
    • Learning to tell two stimuli apart
    • Forgetting a conditioned response
    • Responding only to the exact conditioned stimulus
    Show the answer

    Answer: Responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus. Generalisation spreads the response to similar stimuli; discrimination narrows it.

Key terms

Stimulus generalisation
Responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus
Stimulus discrimination
Responding to the conditioned stimulus but not to similar stimuli
Generalisation gradient
The decline in response strength as stimuli become less similar to the CS
