Professional Boundaries

Boundaries protect clients given power imbalance and vulnerability; crossings may be harmless, violations exploit clients; dual relationships and digital contact need careful management; supervision helps with grey areas.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define professional boundaries in therapy
  • Distinguish boundary crossings from boundary violations
  • Evaluate how boundaries protect clients and practitioners
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to therapy. Psychological therapies should only be delivered by qualified, registered professionals.

Why boundaries matter

Professional boundaries define the limits of the therapeutic relationship, such as its purpose, time, place, contact and the roles of client and therapist. The relationship involves a power imbalance and the client's vulnerability, so boundaries protect clients from exploitation and help therapy stay focused on their needs. The HCPC standards require registrants to maintain appropriate professional relationships.

Crossings and violations

A boundary crossing is a departure from usual practice that may be harmless or even helpful, such as accepting a small card at the end of therapy. A boundary violation is harmful and exploits the client, such as financial exploitation or any sexual relationship with a client, which is always unacceptable. Dual relationships, where the therapist has another role with the client, such as friend or employer, risk conflicts of interest.

Worked example

Digital boundaries

  1. Clients may find or contact therapists on social media.
  2. Practitioners usually decline personal social media connections with clients to protect the relationship.
  3. Clear policies about contact between sessions, such as email response times, help keep boundaries consistent. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Clear boundaries protect clients and support effective, trusting therapy. But overly rigid boundaries can feel cold, and some approaches and cultures involve more flexibility, such as in rural communities where dual relationships are hard to avoid. Supervision helps practitioners think through grey areas.

Common mistakeAssuming every boundary crossing is a violation. Some small crossings can be harmless; exploitation never is.

In 2 lines: Boundaries protect clients given power imbalance and vulnerability. Crossings differ from violations; dual and digital relationships need care.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A therapist also employing a client in their business is a…
    • Dual relationship
    • Boundary crossing that is harmless
    • Informed consent
    • Formulation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Dual relationship. Holding two roles creates a dual relationship.

  2. At the last session, a client gives their therapist a small handmade card, which the therapist accepts. This is best described as…
    • A boundary violation
    • A boundary crossing
    • A dual relationship
    • A safeguarding concern
    Show the answer

    Answer: A boundary crossing. A small, harmless departure is a crossing.

  3. Why can dual relationships be hard to avoid in rural communities?
    • Rural clients never meet anyone
    • Boundaries don't apply there
    • Practitioners and clients often share local networks
    • Dual relationships are always helpful
    Show the answer

    Answer: Practitioners and clients often share local networks. Small communities make overlapping roles likely.

Key terms

Boundary crossing
A departure from usual practice that may be harmless
Boundary violation
A harmful breach that exploits the client
Dual relationship
Having another role with a client besides therapist
