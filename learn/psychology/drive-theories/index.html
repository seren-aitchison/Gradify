Drive Theories

Drive theories explain motivation as reducing tension from unmet needs; arousal theory adds that people seek optimal stimulation; neither explains all motivation.

Psychology · Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain homeostasis and drive-reduction theory
  • Explain optimal arousal and the Yerkes–Dodson law
  • Evaluate drive theories as explanations of motivation

Homeostasis and drives

The body maintains homeostasis: stable internal conditions such as temperature, blood sugar and fluid balance. Hull (1943) proposed that needs (such as for food or water) create drives: unpleasant states of tension that push behaviour to reduce them. Behaviour that reduces a drive is reinforced, so drive reduction explains learning as well as motivation.

Arousal theory

Not all motivation reduces tension: people seek novelty, play and thrills. Optimal arousal theory proposes we are motivated to maintain an ideal level of arousal, seeking stimulation when bored and calm when overstimulated. The Yerkes–Dodson law (1908) describes an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: moderate arousal is best, and the optimum is lower for complex tasks.

Worked example

Curiosity without a drive

  1. Harlow, Harlow and Meyer (1950) gave monkeys mechanical puzzles (latches and hooks) with no food reward.
  2. The monkeys solved them repeatedly, apparently for the activity itself.
  3. This challenged pure drive-reduction accounts and pointed to curiosity and exploration as motives.

Evaluation

Drive theories explain basic biological motives such as hunger and thirst, and link motivation to physiology. They struggle to explain curiosity, risk-taking, eating when not hungry, and motives that increase tension, such as watching horror films. Later theories added incentives, cognition and social motives.

Common mistakeAssuming all behaviour reduces tension. People often seek stimulation, novelty and challenge.

In 2 lines: Drives arise from needs and push behaviour to restore homeostasis. Optimal arousal and the Yerkes–Dodson law explain stimulation-seeking; curiosity challenges drive theory.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Maintaining a stable body temperature is an example of…
    • Incentive
    • Homeostasis
    • Flow
    • Overjustification
    Show the answer

    Answer: Homeostasis. Keeping internal conditions stable is homeostasis.

  2. A student performs best in an exam when moderately nervous, but freezes when very anxious. This fits…
    • Drive reduction only
    • Incentive theory
    • The Yerkes–Dodson law
    • Self-determination
    Show the answer

    Answer: The Yerkes–Dodson law. Moderate arousal is optimal for performance, while very high arousal impairs it.

  3. Why do horror films challenge drive-reduction theory?
    • They reduce hunger
    • They lower arousal
    • They satisfy thirst
    • People seek increased tension
    Show the answer

    Answer: People seek increased tension. Seeking fear contradicts tension reduction.
