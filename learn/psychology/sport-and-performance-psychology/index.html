Sport and Performance Psychology

Moderate arousal and positive interpretation of anxiety support performance; catastrophe theory explains sudden collapses; imagery, goal setting and self-talk improve performance.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the relationship between arousal, anxiety and performance
  • Describe mental skills such as imagery, goal setting and self-talk
  • Evaluate evidence for psychological skills training

Arousal and anxiety

The inverted-U hypothesis proposes performance is best at moderate arousal. Multidimensional anxiety theory (Martens et al., 1990) distinguishes cognitive anxiety (worry) from somatic anxiety (bodily symptoms). Catastrophe theory (Hardy, 1990) suggests that when cognitive anxiety is high, increasing arousal beyond a point can cause a sudden, dramatic drop in performance rather than a gradual decline. How athletes interpret anxiety (as helpful or harmful) also matters.

Choking under pressure

Choking is performing worse than expected under pressure. Baumeister (1984) proposed self-focus theory: pressure makes performers attend to skills that are usually automatic, disrupting them. Training under pressure and using pre-performance routines can reduce choking.

Worked example

Mental imagery

  1. Imagery is mentally rehearsing performance using senses and emotions.
  2. Feltz and Landers (1983) meta-analysed studies and found mental practice improved performance compared with no practice, though less than physical practice.
  3. Combining mental and physical practice is common in elite sport.

Mental skills

Psychological skills training includes goal setting, imagery, self-talk (instructional or motivational), relaxation and concentration techniques. A meta-analysis found self-talk improved sport performance with moderate effects (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011). Sport psychology also addresses athletes' mental health, injury recovery and burnout.

Common mistakeAssuming lower anxiety always means better performance. Interpretation of anxiety and moderate arousal matter.

In 2 lines: Arousal, cognitive and somatic anxiety, and interpretation affect performance. Choking reflects self-focus; imagery and self-talk improve performance.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Mentally rehearsing a penalty kick is an example of…
    • Self-talk
    • Catastrophe
    • Imagery
    • Choking
    Show the answer

    Answer: Imagery. Mental rehearsal using the senses is imagery.

  2. A worried gymnast's performance suddenly collapses as arousal rises in a final. This fits…
    • The inverted-U only
    • Drive theory
    • Equity theory
    • Catastrophe theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Catastrophe theory. High cognitive anxiety plus rising arousal can cause sudden collapse.

  3. Why might pre-performance routines reduce choking?
    • They limit self-focus
    • They increase self-focus
    • They raise anxiety levels
    • They remove the need for practice
    Show the answer

    Answer: They limit self-focus. Routines limit disruptive conscious monitoring.
