Levels of Explanation

Behaviour can be explained at several levels at once; good explanations specify their level and how it connects to others.

Psychology · Foundations of Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main levels at which behaviour can be explained
  • Apply Tinbergen's four questions or Marr's three levels to a behaviour
  • Analyse how explanations at different levels relate to one another

Many levels, one behaviour

Explanations range from the molecular (neurotransmitters and genes), through the neural (brain systems) and cognitive (mental representations and processes), to the individual (personality, learning history), social (relationships, groups) and cultural (norms, institutions). These are not rival accounts; they answer different questions about the same thing.

Two frameworks

Tinbergen's four questions (1963), from biology, ask about mechanism (how does it work?), development (how does it arise in the individual?), function (what is it for?) and evolution (how did it evolve?). Marr's three levels (1982), from vision science, distinguish the computational level (what problem is solved and why), the algorithmic level (what procedure solves it) and the implementational level (how the brain physically does it).

Worked example

Explaining why we feel fear on seeing a snake, using Tinbergen's questions

  1. Mechanism: the amygdala rapidly detects threatening stimuli and triggers a stress response.
  2. Development: fear can be learned through experience, observation or instruction.
  3. Function: it prompts avoidance of danger.
  4. Evolution: ancestors prepared to fear snakes were more likely to survive.

How levels relate

Higher levels may be reduced to lower ones (reductionism), or may have properties that only appear at that level (emergence). Most psychologists take a pragmatic view: lower levels constrain higher ones, higher levels guide what to look for at lower ones, and the most useful level depends on the question and the intervention. A therapist and a pharmacologist may both effectively treat anxiety, working at different levels.

Common mistakeAssuming a lower-level explanation is automatically more scientific or more "real". A neural explanation of prejudice wouldn't replace social explanations of where prejudiced attitudes come from.

In 2 lines: Behaviour can be explained at many levels, which answer different questions and can complement each other. Tinbergen's four questions and Marr's three levels help organise explanations; how levels relate (reduction or emergence) is debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. In Tinbergen's framework, "what is the behaviour for?" is a question about…
    • Mechanism
    • Function
    • Development
    • Implementation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Function. Function asks about the adaptive purpose of a behaviour.

  2. A researcher describes the step-by-step procedure the visual system uses to detect edges. Which of Marr's levels is this?
    • Computational
    • Implementational
    • Algorithmic
    • Cultural
    Show the answer

    Answer: Algorithmic. The algorithmic level specifies the procedure and representations used, separate from the neurons that implement it.

  3. Which statement best describes how levels of explanation relate?
    • Only the lowest, molecular level gives a true explanation
    • Higher-level explanations are always opinions rather than science
    • Each behaviour can be explained at only one level
    • Different levels can be valid together, and the most useful depends on the question being asked
    Show the answer

    Answer: Different levels can be valid together, and the most useful depends on the question being asked. Levels are complementary; whether higher levels reduce to lower ones is debated, but useful explanations are found at many levels.

Key terms

Levels of explanation
The idea that behaviour can be explained at molecular, neural, cognitive, individual, social and cultural levels
Emergence
Properties of a whole that aren't present in or predictable from its parts alone
