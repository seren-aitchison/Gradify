Predictive Processing

Predictive processing sees the brain as predicting input and updating on prediction errors weighted by precision; it explains expectation effects and has been applied to hallucinations; critics question its falsifiability.

Psychology · Contemporary Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain the core ideas of predictive processing
  • Apply predictive processing to perception and illusions
  • Evaluate the strengths and limits of the framework

The predictive brain

Predictive processing (Clark, 2013) proposes that the brain is a prediction machine. Higher levels generate predictions about sensory input; lower levels send up prediction errors when input doesn't match. Perception is the brain's best guess about the causes of its sensory signals. Friston's (2010) free-energy principle frames this as the brain minimising surprise or prediction error.

Precision weighting

The brain weighs predictions and errors by their precision (reliability). When sensory signals are noisy, prior expectations dominate; when signals are clear, errors drive updating. This explains why expectations shape perception, as when we "hear" a word in noisy speech because we expect it.

Worked example

Predictions and hallucinations

  1. Powers et al. (2017) trained people to associate a light with a tone, then presented the light alone.
  2. Some participants reported hearing the tone even when it wasn't played, a conditioned hallucination.
  3. People who hear voices showed this effect more strongly, consistent with stronger reliance on prior expectations.

Evaluation

Predictive processing unifies perception, action and learning within one framework and has inspired research on illusions, psychosis and autism. Critics argue it is so flexible that it can explain almost any result, making it hard to falsify, and that its neural mechanisms are not yet established.

Common mistakeTreating perception as a passive recording of the world. Predictions actively shape what we perceive.

In 2 lines: The brain predicts input and updates on precision-weighted errors. Explains expectation effects and hallucinations; falsifiability is debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Friston's proposal that brains minimise surprise is the…
    • Dual-process theory
    • Free-energy principle
    • Prospect theory
    • Reverse inference
    Show the answer

    Answer: Free-energy principle. Friston framed perception as minimising free energy.

  2. In a noisy café, you clearly "hear" a friend say your name because you expected it. Predictive processing explains this by…
    • Prediction errors only
    • Reverse inference
    • Strong priors shaping perception
    • Loss aversion
    Show the answer

    Answer: Strong priors shaping perception. Expectations dominate when signals are noisy.

  3. Why is predictive processing criticised as hard to falsify?
    • It makes no claims about the brain
    • It has been fully disproved
    • It only ever explains vision
    • Its flexibility lets it explain almost any result
    Show the answer

    Answer: Its flexibility lets it explain almost any result. Very flexible frameworks are hard to test.

Key terms

Predictive processing
The view that the brain predicts input and updates on prediction errors
Precision weighting
Weighting signals by their estimated reliability
Free-energy principle
Friston's proposal that brains minimise surprise
