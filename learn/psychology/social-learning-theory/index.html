Social Learning Theory

Social learning theory explains learning through observation filtered by four mediational processes, so cognition sits between environment and behaviour.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main assumptions of social learning theory
  • Explain the four mediational processes: attention, retention, reproduction and motivation
  • Evaluate social learning theory as a bridge between behaviourism and cognitive psychology

Assumptions

Bandura's social learning theory (1977) accepts that learning is shaped by the environment and consequences, but argues that much learning is observational and depends on cognitive processes. People don't simply react to reinforcement; they form expectations about the consequences of behaviour.

Mediational processes

  • Attention: the learner must notice the model's behaviour.
  • Retention: the behaviour must be remembered.
  • Reproduction: the learner must be physically and mentally able to perform it.
  • Motivation: the learner must expect the behaviour to be rewarded (or not punished).

Bandura distinguished learning (acquiring a behaviour) from performance (doing it): children may learn an aggressive act but perform it only if they expect reward.

Worked example

Learning a football skill from a video

  1. Attention: the player watches a professional's free kick closely.
  2. Retention: they remember the run-up and foot position.
  3. Reproduction: they need enough skill and strength to try it.
  4. Motivation: they practise because they expect to score and be praised.

Self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism

Bandura later emphasised self-efficacy, belief in one's ability to succeed at a task, as a key influence on what people attempt and persist with (Bandura, 1977). He also proposed reciprocal determinism: behaviour, personal factors and the environment influence each other.

Evaluation

Strengths: explains learning without direct reinforcement; supported by experiments; widely applied in education, health promotion and media research. Limitations: it can't fully explain abilities that seem biologically prepared; the mediational processes are hard to measure directly; and it gives less weight to biological and developmental factors.

Common mistakeThinking social learning theory rejects reinforcement. It keeps reinforcement but adds observation and expectation.

In 2 lines: Social learning theory combines observation, reinforcement and cognition through four mediational processes. Self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism extend the theory; it underplays biology.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which mediational process requires the learner to remember the observed behaviour?
    • Retention
    • Attention
    • Reproduction
    • Motivation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Retention. Retention is storing the behaviour in memory for later.

  2. A child watches a gymnast's routine but can't copy it because they lack the strength. Which process has failed?
    • Attention
    • Reproduction
    • Retention
    • Motivation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reproduction. The child noticed and remembered, but lacks the physical ability to reproduce it.

  3. Why is social learning theory described as a bridge between behaviourism and cognitive psychology?
    • It rejects all environmental influences
    • It explains behaviour only through genes
    • It keeps reinforcement but adds expectation
    • It denies that people learn from others
    Show the answer

    Answer: It keeps reinforcement but adds expectation. It combines environmental learning with cognitive mediation.

Key terms

Mediational processes
Attention, retention, reproduction and motivation: cognitive steps between observing and imitating
Self-efficacy
A person's belief in their ability to succeed at a particular task
Reciprocal determinism
Bandura's view that behaviour, personal factors and environment influence each other
