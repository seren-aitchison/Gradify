Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation

Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself; extrinsic from separate outcomes; expected tangible rewards can undermine interest in enjoyable tasks.

Psychology · Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define intrinsic and extrinsic motivation with examples
  • Explain the overjustification and undermining effects
  • Apply the distinction to designing rewards in education and work

Two sources of motivation

Intrinsic motivation is doing an activity for its own sake: because it is interesting, enjoyable or satisfying. Extrinsic motivation is doing it for a separate outcome: money, grades, praise or avoiding punishment. Most behaviour involves both, and the same activity can be intrinsically motivating for one person and extrinsically for another.

When rewards backfire

Worked example

Markers and drawing

  1. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973) observed nursery children who enjoyed drawing with felt-tip markers.
  2. Some were promised a "Good Player Award" for drawing; others got an unexpected award or none.
  3. Two weeks later, children who had expected the award spent less free time drawing than the other groups: the overjustification effect.

Deci, Koestner and Ryan's (1999) meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected, tangible rewards for interesting tasks undermined intrinsic motivation, while verbal praise that conveyed competence tended to increase it. Rewards for dull tasks, which people wouldn't do for fun anyway, don't have this undermining effect.

Applying the distinction

For tasks that are already interesting, offering choice, meaningful feedback and challenge supports intrinsic motivation. For tasks that are dull but necessary, explaining their purpose helps people internalise their value. Pay and grades remain important; the issue is how they are framed: as controlling or as information about competence.

Common mistakeAssuming rewards always increase motivation. For enjoyable tasks, expected tangible rewards can reduce interest.

In 2 lines: Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation differ in source; most behaviour involves both. Expected tangible rewards can undermine interest; praise and choice can support it.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Reading a book because you enjoy the story shows…
    • Extrinsic motivation
    • Overjustification
    • Intrinsic motivation
    • Drive reduction
    Show the answer

    Answer: Intrinsic motivation. Enjoyment of the activity itself is intrinsic.

  2. Children promised an award for drawing later draw less in free time. This is…
    • Positive reinforcement
    • Flow
    • Drive reduction
    • The overjustification effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: The overjustification effect. Expecting an award reduced the children's later free-choice interest in drawing.

  3. Why might the same reward have different effects?
    • It can feel controlling or informative
    • Rewards are always identical
    • People ignore rewards
    • Only money matters
    Show the answer

    Answer: It can feel controlling or informative. Framing determines whether a reward undermines or supports motivation.

Key terms

Intrinsic motivation
Doing an activity for its own sake
Extrinsic motivation
Doing an activity for a separate outcome such as a reward
Overjustification effect
Reduced interest after an external reward is given for an enjoyable activity
