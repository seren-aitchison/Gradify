Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis pools effect sizes from many studies to estimate an overall effect and explore why results differ.

Psychology · Statistics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the steps of a systematic review and meta-analysis
  • Interpret a forest plot and measures of heterogeneity
  • Evaluate meta-analysis, including publication bias and study quality

Combining studies

Glass (1976) coined "meta-analysis" for the statistical analysis of a collection of studies. Smith and Glass (1977) combined 375 studies of psychotherapy and estimated an average effect of about two-thirds of a standard deviation over untreated controls. A meta-analysis follows a systematic review: a pre-specified search, explicit inclusion criteria, data extraction and quality assessment, often reported using the PRISMA guidelines.

The analysis

Each study's effect size is weighted by its precision (larger, more precise studies count more), and the weighted average is calculated. A forest plot shows each study's effect and confidence interval, plus the pooled estimate. Heterogeneity (e.g. the I² statistic) shows how much effects vary beyond chance; moderator analyses ask why.

Worked example

Reading a forest plot

  1. Twelve studies of an intervention: most show small positive effects, two show none.
  2. The pooled effect is d = 0.3, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.4; I² = 60% indicates substantial heterogeneity.
  3. Moderator analysis finds larger effects in studies with lower quality ratings, a warning sign.

Limitations

"Garbage in, garbage out": pooling biased studies gives a precise biased answer. Publication bias can inflate pooled effects; funnel plots and other tests help detect it but can't fully correct it. Combining very different studies ("apples and oranges") can obscure important differences.

Common mistakeTreating a meta-analysis as automatically definitive. Its conclusions are only as good as the studies and methods it includes.

In 2 lines: Meta-analysis pools weighted effect sizes from a systematic review. Forest plots, heterogeneity and moderators aid interpretation; bias and study quality limit conclusions.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who coined the term "meta-analysis"?
    • Jacob Cohen
    • Gene Glass
    • Karl Pearson
    • John Ioannidis
    Show the answer

    Answer: Gene Glass. Gene Glass introduced the term in 1976 in Educational Researcher.

  2. A meta-analysis gives larger studies more weight. Why?
    • They are always better designed
    • They were published earlier
    • Their estimates are more precise
    • They have larger effects
    Show the answer

    Answer: Their estimates are more precise. Weighting by precision gives more influence to less noisy estimates.

  3. A funnel plot is asymmetrical, with small studies showing only large positive effects. What does this suggest?
    • Perfect consistency between studies
    • That small studies are more accurate
    • No heterogeneity
    • Possible publication bias
    Show the answer

    Answer: Possible publication bias. Missing small null studies suggest selective publication.

Key terms

Meta-analysis
The statistical combination of results from many studies to estimate an overall effect
Forest plot
A graph showing each study's effect size and confidence interval, and the pooled estimate
Heterogeneity
Variation in effect sizes between studies beyond what chance would produce
