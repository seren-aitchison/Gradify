Adaptation

Adaptations are traits shaped by selection for ancestral problems; not all traits are adaptations, and claims need evidence of special design.

Psychology · Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define adaptation and distinguish it from by-products and exaptations
  • Explain the criteria used to identify psychological adaptations
  • Evaluate the risk of "adaptationist" storytelling

What is an adaptation?

An adaptation is an inherited trait that exists because it solved a recurrent problem of survival or reproduction for ancestors, and so was favoured by natural selection. Examples include the eye, the immune system and, evolutionary psychologists argue, some psychological mechanisms such as fear of snakes or preferences for sweet, fatty foods. Adaptations are designed for the environments in which they evolved, not necessarily for modern ones.

Adaptations, by-products and exaptations

Not every trait is an adaptation. By-products are side effects of adaptations: the whiteness of bones is a by-product of calcium's use for strength. Exaptations are traits that evolved for one function and were later co-opted for another: feathers may have first evolved for insulation and later been used for flight (Gould and Vrba, 1982). Noise refers to random variation without function.

Worked example

Sweet tooth in a modern world

  1. In ancestral environments, ripe fruit and honey were rare, valuable sources of energy.
  2. A strong preference for sweet foods would have helped survival.
  3. Today, cheap sugary food is abundant, so the same preference contributes to tooth decay and obesity: a mismatch between evolved preferences and modern environments.

Identifying adaptations

Researchers look for evidence of special design: a trait is efficient, economical and reliable at solving a specific adaptive problem (Williams, 1966). Cross-cultural universality, early development and evidence from other species can also support a claim. Gould and Lewontin (1979) warned against "just-so stories": inventing plausible adaptive explanations for any trait without testing them.

Common mistakeAssuming every human trait is an adaptation. Many are by-products, exaptations or culturally learned.

In 2 lines: Adaptations solve ancestral problems; by-products and exaptations are different. Evidence of special design is needed to avoid just-so stories; mismatch explains some modern problems.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The whiteness of bones is an example of a…
    • Adaptation
    • By-product
    • Exaptation
    • Mismatch
    Show the answer

    Answer: By-product. Colour is a side effect of calcium's use.

  2. A preference for sugary food now contributes to obesity. This illustrates…
    • Exaptation
    • A just-so story
    • Evolutionary mismatch
    • Noise
    Show the answer

    Answer: Evolutionary mismatch. An ancestral preference misfits a modern environment.

  3. What counts as evidence of special design?
    • Any plausible story at all
    • A trait being common
    • A trait being recent
    • Efficient, reliable problem-solving
    Show the answer

    Answer: Efficient, reliable problem-solving. Williams set criteria of efficient, economical, reliable design.

Key terms

Adaptation
A trait shaped by natural selection because it solved an ancestral problem
By-product
A side effect of an adaptation that has no function itself
Mismatch
When evolved traits fit ancestral but not modern environments
