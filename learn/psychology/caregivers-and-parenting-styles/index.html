Caregivers and Parenting Styles

Infants form multiple attachments based on sensitive care; authoritative parenting is often linked to good outcomes, but culture and child effects complicate cause and effect.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the formation of attachments to multiple caregivers, including fathers
  • Describe Baumrind's parenting styles and their associated outcomes
  • Evaluate research on parenting styles, including cultural variation and child effects

Multiple attachments

Schaffer and Emerson (1964) followed 60 babies in Glasgow for their first 18 months, visiting monthly. Most formed a first specific attachment at around 7–9 months, and multiple attachments soon followed: by 18 months most infants had several. The strongest attachment was usually to the caregiver who responded most sensitively, not necessarily the one who spent most time with them.

Most infants form attachments to fathers, who in many studies engage in more physical, playful interaction. Research suggests that what matters is sensitive, responsive care, whoever provides it; children of same-sex parents show similar development to others (Golombok et al., 2003).

Parenting styles

Baumrind (1966) described parenting styles by combining warmth (responsiveness) and control (demandingness); Maccoby and Martin (1983) added a fourth:

  • Authoritative: warm with clear, explained rules. Associated with good social and academic outcomes in many Western samples.
  • Authoritarian: strict, controlling, low warmth.
  • Permissive: warm but few demands.
  • Neglectful (uninvolved): low warmth and low control, associated with the poorest outcomes.
Worked example

Culture and parenting

  1. In some studies of Chinese American and African American families, strict parenting didn't show the negative associations found in European American samples (Chao, 1994).
  2. Strictness may be understood as care and involvement in some cultural contexts.
  3. The meaning of a parenting practice depends on its cultural and social setting.

Child effects

Parenting research is mostly correlational. Children also influence parents: easy-going children may elicit warm, authoritative parenting, while challenging temperaments may elicit more controlling responses. Genes shared by parents and children can also produce correlations.

Common mistakeAssuming parenting style alone causes child outcomes. Children's temperaments and shared genes also shape the association.

In 2 lines: Infants form multiple attachments; sensitivity, not time or gender of caregiver, matters most. Parenting styles combine warmth and control; outcomes depend on culture and child effects.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Warm parenting with clear, explained rules is…
    • Authoritarian
    • Authoritative
    • Permissive
    • Neglectful
    Show the answer

    Answer: Authoritative. Authoritative parents combine warmth and control.

  2. A baby's strongest attachment is to a grandmother who responds quickly to cries, though the mother spends more time with her. Which finding fits?
    • Time with the infant decides attachment
    • Mothers are always the primary attachment
    • Sensitivity matters more than time
    • Babies attach to only one person
    Show the answer

    Answer: Sensitivity matters more than time. Schaffer and Emerson found responsiveness was key.

  3. Why is it hard to conclude that authoritative parenting causes good outcomes?
    • Parenting has no effects at all
    • All the studies are experiments
    • Outcomes are never measured
    • Child effects and shared genes
    Show the answer

    Answer: Child effects and shared genes. Correlations may reflect child effects and shared genes.
