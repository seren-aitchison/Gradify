Vulnerable Participants

Vulnerable participants need extra safeguards: appropriate consent or assent, approval, minimised risk and protection from pressure.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Identify groups who may be vulnerable in research and why
  • Explain the additional safeguards required for research with children and adults lacking capacity
  • Evaluate the balance between protecting vulnerable people and including them in research

Who may be vulnerable

Vulnerability can come from limited capacity to consent (young children, some people with dementia or learning disabilities), increased risk of harm (people in mental health crisis), or dependent relationships that make refusal hard (patients asked by their clinician, students by their lecturer, prisoners).

Safeguards

  • Children: consent from a parent or guardian for those under 16 (as the BPS Code advises), plus the child's own assent in age-appropriate language; respecting a child's wish to stop.
  • Adults who may lack capacity: in England and Wales, research involving adults lacking capacity is governed by the Mental Capacity Act 2005, which requires approval by an appropriate body and consultation with someone close to the person.
  • Dependent relationships: recruitment by someone without power over the participant.
  • Additional ethical review, minimised risk and access to support.
Worked example

Research with people with dementia

  1. Capacity is assessed for each decision, not assumed lost.
  2. Where someone lacks capacity, a personal consultee (such as a family member) is asked for advice.
  3. Any sign of distress or objection means stopping, whatever was agreed earlier.

Protection versus inclusion

Excluding vulnerable groups entirely protects them from research risks but leaves them without evidence about their own needs and treatments. Ethics codes aim to include them safely rather than exclude them.

Common mistakeAssuming everyone with a diagnosis lacks capacity. Capacity is decision-specific and must be assessed, not assumed.

In 2 lines: Vulnerability arises from limited capacity, higher risk or dependent relationships. Safeguards include parental consent and child assent, legal procedures for adults lacking capacity, and independent recruitment.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Why may prisoners be considered vulnerable participants?
    • They may find it hard to refuse because of their dependent situation
    • They are always unable to understand information
    • They are under 16
    • They have no legal rights
    Show the answer

    Answer: They may find it hard to refuse because of their dependent situation. Dependent relationships can undermine voluntary consent.

  2. A 9-year-old's parent consents, but the child says they don't want to continue. What should happen?
    • Continue because the parent consented
    • Stop: the child's wish to withdraw should be respected
    • Offer a larger reward to continue
    • Ask the parent to persuade them
    Show the answer

    Answer: Stop: the child's wish to withdraw should be respected. Assent and the right to withdraw apply to children too.

  3. What is the main argument for including vulnerable groups in research rather than excluding them?
    • They are easier to recruit than other groups
    • Research with them needs no ethical review
    • Otherwise they lack evidence about their care
    • They can't experience harm
    Show the answer

    Answer: Otherwise they lack evidence about their care. Exclusion can leave groups without evidence-based care; ethics aims for safe inclusion.

Key terms

Assent
A child's or vulnerable person's agreement to take part, alongside formal consent from another
Capacity
The ability to understand, retain and weigh information to make a particular decision
