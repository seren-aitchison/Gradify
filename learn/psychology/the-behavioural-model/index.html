The Behavioural Model

The behavioural model explains distress through conditioning; Mowrer's two-process model explains how avoidance maintains phobias; learned helplessness links uncontrollability to depression; preparedness and cognition limit the model.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain classical and operant conditioning accounts of difficulties
  • Describe Mowrer's two-process model of phobias
  • Evaluate the behavioural model
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Learning distress

The behavioural model argues that maladaptive behaviour is learned. Watson and Rayner (1920) conditioned fear in an infant, "Little Albert", by pairing a white rat with a loud noise, an early example of classical conditioning of fear, though the study raises serious ethical concerns. Operant conditioning explains how behaviours are maintained by reinforcement.

The two-process model

Mowrer (1960) proposed a two-process model of phobias. Fear is acquired through classical conditioning (a neutral stimulus is paired with something frightening), and maintained through operant conditioning: avoiding the feared object reduces anxiety, which negatively reinforces avoidance. Because avoidance prevents new learning, fear persists.

Worked example

Learned helplessness

  1. Seligman and Maier (1967) found that dogs exposed to inescapable shocks later failed to escape avoidable shocks.
  2. Seligman proposed that learned helplessness, learning that one's actions don't matter, may contribute to depression in humans.
  3. The theory was later revised to emphasise how people explain events (attributional style).

Evaluation

The behavioural model is testable and led to effective treatments such as exposure therapy. But many people develop phobias without a traumatic conditioning event, and some fears, such as of snakes, seem easier to learn than others, suggesting biological preparedness (Seligman, 1971). The model also underplays thoughts and meaning, which cognitive models address.

Common mistakeAssuming all fears come from a frightening experience. Many arise without one, and preparedness plays a role.

In 2 lines: Conditioning explains how distress is learned and maintained. Two-process model and learned helplessness; preparedness and cognition limit it.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Strengthening a behaviour by removing something unpleasant is…
    • Negative reinforcement
    • Positive reinforcement
    • Punishment
    • Extinction
    Show the answer

    Answer: Negative reinforcement. Removing an unpleasant feeling, such as anxiety, strengthens the behaviour that removed it.

  2. A person who was bitten by a dog avoids all dogs, and each time feels relief. The relief maintains avoidance through…
    • Positive punishment
    • Negative reinforcement
    • Extinction
    • Systematic desensitisation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Negative reinforcement. Relief from anxiety negatively reinforces avoidance, so the fear persists.

  3. What does biological preparedness add to the behavioural model?
    • All fears are equally easy to learn
    • Learning plays no role in fear at all
    • Some fears are learned more easily for evolutionary reasons
    • Fear can never be conditioned
    Show the answer

    Answer: Some fears are learned more easily for evolutionary reasons. Preparedness explains why some phobias are common.

Key terms

Two-process model
Fear acquired through classical conditioning and maintained through operant conditioning
Negative reinforcement
Strengthening a behaviour by removing something unpleasant
Learned helplessness
Learning that one's actions don't affect outcomes
