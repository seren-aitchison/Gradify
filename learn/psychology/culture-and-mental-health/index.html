Culture and Mental Health

Culture shapes idioms of distress, somatisation and explanatory models; these affect help-seeking; debates continue over exporting Western models of mental health.

Psychology · Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how culture shapes the experience and expression of distress
  • Describe cultural explanations of illness and help-seeking
  • Evaluate the global export of Western models of mental health
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone worried about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Expressing distress

Kleinman's research in China in the 1970s and 1980s found that many patients presented distress through bodily symptoms, such as fatigue, headaches and dizziness, a pattern called somatisation. Some later researchers argued Western patients also somatise more than is recognised. The idioms of distress that people use, the words and symptoms through which suffering is expressed, vary between cultures.

Explanatory models and help-seeking

Kleinman described explanatory models: people's beliefs about what caused an illness, what it means and how it should be treated. These may be biological, psychological, social, moral or spiritual. Explanatory models shape whether people seek help from doctors, family, religious leaders or traditional healers. Stigma can discourage help-seeking in many communities.

Worked example

Exporting Western models

  1. Watters (2010) argued that Western diagnostic categories and treatments have spread globally, sometimes displacing local understandings of distress.
  2. Supporters of global mental health argue that many people worldwide lack access to effective care.
  3. Critics argue interventions should be adapted to local meanings and involve communities.

Evaluation

Cultural research shows mental distress is universal, but its forms, meanings and responses vary. Recognising this improves care, for example through culturally adapted therapies. However, emphasising cultural difference can also stereotype groups; individuals vary within cultures.

Common mistakeAssuming distress looks the same everywhere. Expression and meaning vary across cultures.

In 2 lines: Culture shapes how distress is expressed and explained. Explanatory models affect help-seeking; exporting Western models is debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A culturally shared way of expressing suffering is an…
    • Idiom of distress
    • Imposed etic
    • Display rule
    • Acculturation strategy
    Show the answer

    Answer: Idiom of distress. Idioms of distress vary between cultures.

  2. A man believes his low mood was caused by a spiritual imbalance and visits a religious healer first. This reflects his…
    • Display rules
    • Explanatory model
    • Big Five profile
    • Sociocultural adaptation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Explanatory model. Beliefs about cause shape where help is sought.

  3. Why do critics question exporting Western mental health models?
    • Western models are culture-free
    • Local meanings never matter
    • They may displace local understandings of distress
    • Distress is identical everywhere
    Show the answer

    Answer: They may displace local understandings of distress. Interventions may fit poorly without local adaptation.

Key terms

Somatisation
Expressing psychological distress through bodily symptoms
Idiom of distress
A culturally shared way of expressing suffering
Explanatory model
A person's beliefs about an illness's cause, meaning and treatment
