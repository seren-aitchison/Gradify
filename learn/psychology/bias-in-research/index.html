Bias in Research

Bias is systematic error; it can enter at sampling, measurement, analysis and publication, and good design, blinding and transparency are the safeguards.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Identify sources of bias at each stage of research
  • Explain publication bias and its effect on the literature
  • Evaluate safeguards against bias, including blinding and registered reports

Bias at every stage

  • Sampling bias: the sample systematically differs from the population.
  • Measurement bias: leading questions, social desirability, unreliable or culturally biased instruments.
  • Researcher bias: expectations affecting how participants are treated or results recorded.
  • Participant bias: demand characteristics and social desirability.
  • Analysis bias: trying many analyses and reporting the ones that "work" (p-hacking).
  • Publication bias: significant results are more likely to be published than null results.

Publication bias

If journals mainly publish significant findings, the published literature overstates how strong and how common effects are; null results sit in "file drawers". Meta-analyses can partly detect this using funnel plots, but can't fully correct it.

Worked example

How publication bias distorts a literature

  1. Twenty small labs test an ineffective intervention; by chance one finds p < .05.
  2. That one study is published; nineteen null results aren't.
  3. Readers see "evidence" for the intervention that is entirely a product of selective publication.

Investigate with the lab. Find the key flaw in each invented study and name the type of bias.

Safeguards

Random sampling and allocation, standardised and validated measures, single- and double-blind procedures, preregistration of hypotheses and analyses, and registered reports, in which journals accept studies before the results are known, all reduce bias.

Common mistakeEquating bias with random error. Random error averages out with bigger samples; bias doesn't.

In 2 lines: Bias can enter at every stage of research. Publication bias distorts the literature; blinding, preregistration and registered reports reduce bias.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Reporting only the analyses that produced significant results is…
    • p-hacking
    • Counterbalancing
    • Debriefing
    • Triangulation
    Show the answer

    Answer: p-hacking. Selective analysis inflates false positives.

  2. A meta-analysis includes only published studies of a therapy, all positive. What is the main concern?
    • The therapy must definitely work
    • Unpublished null results may be missing, inflating the effect
    • Meta-analyses can't include therapy studies
    • The studies were too large
    Show the answer

    Answer: Unpublished null results may be missing, inflating the effect. Publication bias can make an effect look stronger or more reliable than it is.

  3. Which safeguard directly addresses publication bias?
    • Larger font on questionnaires
    • Opportunity sampling
    • Registered reports
    • Testing participants in groups
    Show the answer

    Answer: Registered reports. Accepting studies on design means null results are published too.

  4. What are demand characteristics?
    • Requirements for ethical approval
    • The demands of the experimenter's schedule
    • Features of the statistical test used
    • Cues that lead participants to guess the aim and change their behaviour
    Show the answer

    Answer: Cues that lead participants to guess the aim and change their behaviour. Participants who work out the aim may behave unnaturally.

Key terms

Publication bias
The tendency for significant results to be published more often than null results
p-hacking
Trying multiple analyses and reporting those that give significant results
Registered report
A study format in which a journal accepts the design before results are known
