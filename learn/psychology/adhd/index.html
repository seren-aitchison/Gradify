ADHD

ADHD involves inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity beginning in childhood; it is highly heritable with environmental contributions; screening data differ from diagnosis; diagnosis rates are debated.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the features of ADHD
  • Explain genetic and environmental factors in ADHD
  • Evaluate debates about ADHD diagnosis and screening data
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. ADHD assessment should be carried out by qualified professionals.

Features

ADHD involves persistent patterns of inattention (difficulty sustaining attention, disorganisation, forgetfulness) and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (restlessness, difficulty waiting, interrupting) that begin in childhood and affect functioning in more than one setting. Many people continue to experience ADHD in adulthood, though presentation can change.

Causes

The World Federation of ADHD consensus statement (Faraone et al., 2021) summarised evidence that ADHD is highly heritable, with twin studies suggesting around 70–80%. Environmental factors associated with higher risk include preterm birth, low birth weight and prenatal exposure to some substances. No single cause explains ADHD; many genes and environmental factors contribute.

Worked example

Screening is not diagnosis

  1. The Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey found 13.9% of adults in England screened positive on an ADHD screening questionnaire in 2023/4, up from 8.2% in 2007.
  2. A positive screen indicates symptoms worth assessing; it is not a diagnosis.
  3. Rising screening rates may reflect awareness, changing self-report, or real change, and need careful interpretation.

Evaluation

ADHD is a well-researched condition with strong evidence for heritability and effective treatments, including medication and psychological support recommended in NICE guidance. Debates concern possible over- and under-diagnosis, the role of school and work environments, and long waiting lists for assessment. Recognition in girls and adults has historically been poor.

Common mistakeTreating a positive screening questionnaire as a diagnosis. Screening only shows symptoms worth assessing.

In 2 lines: ADHD involves inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity from childhood. Highly heritable; screening differs from diagnosis; diagnosis rates are debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A quick check to identify who may need a full assessment is…
    • Diagnosis
    • Formulation
    • Screening
    • Psychoeducation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Screening. Screening flags people whose symptoms may need a full specialist assessment.

  2. A student often loses things, struggles to finish tasks and is easily distracted, but isn't hyperactive. This mainly reflects…
    • Hyperactivity-impulsivity
    • Anhedonia
    • Compulsions
    • Inattention
    Show the answer

    Answer: Inattention. Losing things, not finishing tasks and distractibility are features of inattention.

  3. Why must rising ADHD screening rates be interpreted carefully?
    • They may reflect awareness and self-report changes
    • They prove ADHD is rising very fast
    • Screening is the same as diagnosis
    • Screening rates never change at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: They may reflect awareness and self-report changes. Several explanations could account for the rise.

Key terms

Hyperactivity-impulsivity
Restlessness and acting without waiting or thinking
Screening
