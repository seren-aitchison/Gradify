A History of Psychopathology

Explanations moved from supernatural and humoral ideas to biological and psychological models; moral treatment and later deinstitutionalisation reformed care; history shows the influence of culture and power.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe major historical explanations of mental distress
  • Explain how asylums and reform movements developed
  • Evaluate what history teaches about current approaches
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Early explanations

In many ancient and medieval societies, distress was explained by supernatural causes such as spirits or divine punishment. Hippocrates proposed a biological account based on imbalances of bodily "humours". In early modern Europe, some people were accused of witchcraft. Explanations have always reflected the knowledge and values of their time.

Asylums and reform

From the 17th and 18th centuries, people deemed "mad" were increasingly confined in institutions. Bethlem Hospital in London became notorious for poor conditions. Moral treatment, pioneered by Pinel in France and the Tukes at the York Retreat in England from 1796, emphasised humane care, routine and respect. The 19th century saw large public asylums, which often became overcrowded.

Worked example

Deinstitutionalisation

  1. From the 1950s, new medications and critiques of institutions contributed to deinstitutionalisation: closing large psychiatric hospitals.
  2. In England, many long-stay hospitals closed in the 1980s and 1990s under care in the community policies.
  3. Critics argued that community services were often underfunded, leaving some people without support.

Evaluation

History shows that ideas about mental distress are shaped by science, culture and power. Reforms often began with good intentions but produced new problems. Rosenhan's (1973) "On being sane in insane places" was influential in criticising psychiatric diagnosis and institutions, though later investigation has raised serious doubts about how the study was conducted.

Common mistakeAssuming current approaches are free of cultural influence. Every era's ideas reflect its context.

In 2 lines: From supernatural and humoral ideas to biological and psychological models. Moral treatment, asylums and deinstitutionalisation; ideas reflect their time.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The ancient idea that health depends on balanced bodily fluids is…
    • Moral treatment
    • Deinstitutionalisation
    • Diathesis–stress
    • Humoral theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Humoral theory. Hippocrates proposed that illness came from an imbalance of the bodily humours.

  2. A 19th-century institution replaces chains with gardening, routine and kindness. This reflects…
    • Moral treatment
    • Supernatural explanation
    • Deinstitutionalisation
    • Humoral theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Moral treatment. Humane care with routine, activity and respect defined moral treatment.

  3. What does the history of psychopathology suggest about current approaches?
    • They are completely free of bias
    • They are shaped by culture, science and power
    • History has no lessons for today
    • All old ideas were always wrong
    Show the answer

    Answer: They are shaped by culture, science and power. Every era's ideas about mental distress reflect its science, culture and power.

Key terms
