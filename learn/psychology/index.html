Foundations of Psychology

  • What Is Psychology? Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour.
  • The History of Psychology Psychology grew out of philosophy and physiology in the 19th century.
  • Psychology as a Science What makes psychology a science is its method: testable hypotheses, systematic observation and openness to being wrong.
  • Psychology and Its Neighbouring Disciplines Psychology sits between the biological and the social sciences.
  • The Aims of Psychological Research Psychological research has four aims: to describe, explain, predict and control or change behaviour.
  • Structuralism and Functionalism The first two schools of psychology disagreed about what to study: structuralism asked what consciousness is made of, functionalism what it is for.
  • The Cognitive Approach The cognitive approach studies the mental processes between stimulus and response: attention, perception, memory, language and thinking.
  • The Biological Approach The biological approach explains behaviour and experience through the brain, nervous system, hormones and genes.
  • The Evolutionary Approach The evolutionary approach asks how mental abilities and behaviours may have been shaped by natural and sexual selection.
  • Contemporary Psychology Today's psychology is less a set of rival schools than a network of specialisms that share methods and borrow from one another.
  • Levels of Explanation The same behaviour can be explained at different levels: molecules, brain systems, mental processes, individuals and societies.
  • Reductionism and Holism Reductionism explains complex behaviour by breaking it into simpler parts; holism argues some properties only make sense at the level of the whole.
  • The Nature–nurture Debate The nature–nurture debate asks how far behaviour is shaped by inherited biology or by experience.
  • Psychology in Everyday Life Psychological research shapes daily life in ways people rarely notice, from road signs and school timetables to health campaigns and online design.

Biological Psychology and Neuroscience

  • The Central Nervous System The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord.
  • Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Divisions The autonomic nervous system has two branches that usually work in opposition: the sympathetic prepares the body for action and the parasympathetic restores calm.
  • Neurone Structure and Types Neurones are the cells that carry information in the nervous system.
  • The Resting Potential A neurone at rest holds an electrical charge across its membrane.
  • Action Potentials An action potential is the brief electrical impulse that travels along an axon.
  • Synaptic Transmission Synaptic transmission is how one neurone passes a signal to another, usually by releasing a chemical across a tiny gap.
  • Neurotransmitters and Receptors Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers of the nervous system.
  • Brain Anatomy: an Overview The brain is organised into regions with broadly different roles, working together as networks.
  • The Cerebral Cortex and the Four Lobes The cerebral cortex is divided into four lobes, each linked to broad functions.
  • The Limbic System: Hippocampus and Amygdala The limbic system is a set of structures involved in emotion, motivation and memory.
  • Hypothalamus and Thalamus The hypothalamus and thalamus are small structures in the middle of the brain with large roles.
  • Basal Ganglia and Cerebellum The basal ganglia and cerebellum both help control movement, in different ways.
  • Lateralisation of Function Some functions are handled more by one cerebral hemisphere than the other.
  • Neuroplasticity Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function through experience and after injury.
  • Neurogenesis Neurogenesis is the creation of new neurones.
  • Hormones and the Endocrine System The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood to send slower, longer-lasting signals than the nervous system.
  • Circadian Rhythms Circadian rhythms are cycles of about 24 hours in body and behaviour, such as the sleep–wake cycle.
  • Sleep Sleep is a regular state of reduced awareness with distinct stages.
  • Genetics and Behaviour Genes influence behaviour, but always in interaction with the environment.
  • Epigenetics Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence.
  • Evolution and the Brain Evolutionary psychology explains brain and behaviour in terms of natural selection.
  • Brain Imaging: EEG, MRI, FMRI and PET Brain imaging lets researchers study the living brain's structure and activity.
  • Lesion Studies Lesion studies examine what happens when part of the brain is damaged or temporarily disrupted.
  • Psychopharmacology Psychopharmacology studies how drugs affect mind and behaviour.
  • Biological Explanations of Mental Disorders Biological explanations link mental disorders to genes, brain structure and neurochemistry.

Cognitive Psychology

  • The Information-processing Approach The information-processing approach describes the mind as a system that takes in, transforms, stores and retrieves information in stages.
  • Selective Attention Selective attention is the ability to focus on some information while ignoring the rest.
  • Divided Attention Divided attention is attending to more than one task at once.
  • Sustained Attention Sustained attention (vigilance) is maintaining focus over long periods, especially when watching for rare signals.
  • Sensation and Perception Sensation is detecting physical energy with sense organs; perception is organising and interpreting it into meaningful experience.
  • Visual Perception Vision turns patterns of light on the retina into a stable, three-dimensional world of objects.
  • Auditory Perception Auditory perception turns pressure waves into the experience of pitch, loudness, location and speech.
  • Gestalt Principles Gestalt psychologists showed that we perceive organised wholes, not collections of separate parts.
  • Pattern Recognition Pattern recognition is identifying objects, letters, words and faces from sensory input.
  • Sensory Memory Sensory memory holds a brief, detailed copy of what the senses have just registered.
  • Short-term Memory Short-term memory holds a small amount of information for a short time unless it is rehearsed.
  • Working Memory Working memory is the system that holds and manipulates information while we think.
  • Long-term Memory: Episodic, Semantic and Procedural Long-term memory isn't one store but several.
  • Encoding, Storage and Retrieval Remembering involves encoding information, storing it and retrieving it.
  • Forgetting Forgetting is the loss of the ability to recall or recognise information.
  • Interference Interference theory holds that we forget because other memories get in the way.
  • Retrieval Failure Retrieval failure means information is stored but can't be accessed without the right cues.
  • Reconstructive Memory and False Memories Remembering is an active reconstruction, not a replay.
  • Eyewitness Testimony Eyewitness testimony is a witness's account of a crime or event.
  • Language Acquisition Children learn their first language rapidly and in a predictable sequence.
  • Language Processing Understanding language means recognising words, building sentence structures and constructing meaning, in a fraction of a second.
  • Speech Production Speaking involves turning an idea into words, grammar and sounds in real time.
  • Problem Solving Problem solving is finding a way from a current state to a goal when the route isn't obvious.
  • Reasoning Reasoning is drawing conclusions from information.
  • Decision-making Decision-making is choosing between options under uncertainty.
  • Biases and Heuristics Heuristics are mental shortcuts that usually work but can produce systematic errors called biases.
  • Metacognition Metacognition is thinking about thinking: knowing what you know and controlling how you learn.
  • Cognitive Development Cognitive development is how thinking changes from infancy to adulthood.
  • Theories of Intelligence Intelligence is the general ability to reason, learn and solve problems.
  • Intelligence Testing Intelligence tests measure cognitive abilities and compare people with others of the same age.
  • Creativity Creativity is producing ideas or products that are both novel and useful.
  • Cognitive Ageing Cognitive ageing is how thinking changes in later adulthood.

Learning and Behaviour

  • Classical Conditioning Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response after being paired with a stimulus that triggers it naturally.
  • Acquisition and Extinction Acquisition is the phase in which a conditioned response is learned; extinction is its gradual weakening when the conditioned stimulus is no longer followed by the unconditioned stimulus.
  • Spontaneous Recovery Spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period.
  • Generalisation and Discrimination Generalisation is responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus; discrimination is learning to respond only to the conditioned stimulus.
  • Operant Conditioning Operant conditioning is learning from consequences: behaviours followed by good outcomes become more frequent, and those followed by bad outcomes less frequent.
  • Punishment Punishment is a consequence that decreases behaviour.
  • Schedules of Reinforcement Schedules of reinforcement are rules about which responses are reinforced.
  • Shaping Shaping is training a new behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it.
  • Behaviour Modification Behaviour modification applies learning principles to change behaviour, in schools, care settings, health and everyday life.
  • Observational Learning Observational learning is learning by watching others, without needing to perform the behaviour or be reinforced directly.
  • Social Learning Theory Bandura's social learning theory explains how people learn through observation, combining behaviourist ideas with cognitive processes.
  • Vicarious Reinforcement Vicarious reinforcement is learning about the consequences of behaviour by seeing what happens to others.
  • Latent Learning Latent learning is learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it.
  • Insight Learning Insight learning is the sudden solution of a problem through restructuring it mentally, rather than gradual trial and error.
  • Biological Constraints on Learning Not everything is equally easy to learn.
  • Habituation and Sensitisation Habituation and sensitisation are the simplest forms of learning: responding less to a repeated harmless stimulus, or more to stimuli after something intense.
  • Habit Formation Habits are behaviours triggered automatically by context, after being repeated in that context.
  • Applications of Learning Theory Learning theory has shaped therapy, education, parenting, health and the design of technology.

Developmental Psychology

  • Prenatal Development Prenatal development runs from conception to birth.
  • Infancy Infancy is the first two years of life, a time of rapid physical, perceptual and social development.
  • Childhood Childhood, from about 2 to 11 years, brings major growth in thinking, self-control and social understanding.
  • Adolescence Adolescence is the transition from childhood to adulthood, marked by puberty, brain development and social change.
  • Adulthood and Ageing Adulthood brings continued development in relationships, work, personality and well-being.
  • Lifespan Perspectives Lifespan perspectives treat development as continuing from conception to death and shaped by many interacting influences.
  • Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development Piaget's theory proposes that children actively construct understanding through four stages.
  • Vygotsky and Sociocultural Development Vygotsky argued that children's thinking develops through social interaction and cultural tools.
  • Information-processing Approaches to Development Information-processing approaches explain cognitive development as gradual improvements in speed, capacity, strategies and knowledge.
  • Language Development Language development runs from early speech perception to complex grammar and conversation.
  • Emotional Development Emotional development is how children come to express, understand and regulate emotions.
  • Bowlby's Attachment Theory Attachment is a deep, lasting emotional bond to a caregiver.
  • Ainsworth and the Strange Situation Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation to assess the quality of infant attachment.
  • Measuring Attachment Attachment can be measured at different ages using observation, interviews and questionnaires.
  • Caregivers and Parenting Styles Caregivers shape development through warmth, responsiveness and control.
  • Peers and Friendship Peers become increasingly important through childhood and adolescence.
  • Family Influences Families shape development through relationships, conflict, structure and economic circumstances.
  • Identity Development Identity is a coherent sense of who you are, what you value and where you are going.
  • Erikson's Psychosocial Stages Erikson proposed eight stages of psychosocial development across the lifespan, each centred on a conflict.
  • Moral Development: Kohlberg Kohlberg proposed that moral reasoning develops through six stages grouped into three levels.
  • Risk and Resilience Resilience is positive adaptation despite significant adversity.
  • Neurodevelopment Neurodevelopment is how the brain develops from before birth to adulthood.
  • Neurodevelopmental Conditions Neurodevelopmental conditions begin in development and affect how the brain processes information.
  • Developmental Psychopathology Developmental psychopathology studies how mental health difficulties emerge and change across development.
  • Developmental Research Methods Developmental researchers study change over time, which needs special designs.

Social Psychology

  • Social Cognition Social cognition is how people think about themselves, other people and social situations.
  • Social Perception and Impressions People form impressions of others quickly from small amounts of information.
  • Attribution Attribution is how people explain the causes of behaviour.
  • The Fundamental Attribution Error and Self-serving Bias Attributions are often biased in predictable ways.
  • Attitude Formation An attitude is an evaluation of a person, object or idea.
  • Attitude Change and Persuasion Persuasion is deliberately changing attitudes through communication.
  • Cognitive Dissonance Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort felt when beliefs and actions conflict.
  • Conformity Conformity is changing behaviour or beliefs to match a group.
  • Obedience Obedience is following orders from an authority figure.
  • Compliance Compliance is going along with a direct request.
  • Social Norms Social norms are shared expectations about how people behave.
  • Group Dynamics Group dynamics are the processes that operate within groups.
  • Group Decision-making Groups often make decisions together, sometimes better and sometimes worse than individuals.
  • Leadership Leadership is influencing a group towards shared goals.
  • Power Power is the capacity to influence others and control resources.
  • Social Identity Theory Social identity theory explains how group memberships become part of who we are and shape intergroup behaviour.
  • Self-categorisation Self-categorisation theory explains when people see themselves as individuals and when as group members.
  • In-groups and Out-groups An in-group is a group we belong to; an out-group is one we don't.
  • Prejudice, Stereotyping and Discrimination Stereotypes are beliefs about groups, prejudice is an attitude towards them, and discrimination is unfair behaviour.
  • Intergroup Relations and Conflict Intergroup conflict arises between groups competing for resources, status or identity.
  • Bystander Behaviour Bystander behaviour is how people respond when they witness an emergency.
  • Prosocial Behaviour and Altruism Prosocial behaviour is any action that benefits others; altruism is helping without expectation of reward.
  • Aggression Aggression is behaviour intended to harm someone who wants to avoid it.
  • Attraction Interpersonal attraction is the liking that draws people together.
  • Close Relationships Close relationships include friendships, romantic partnerships and family bonds.
  • Culture and Social Behaviour Culture shapes how people think, feel and behave.

Personality and Individual Differences

  • Trait Theories of Personality Trait theories describe personality as a set of stable characteristics that vary between people.
  • The Big Five The Big Five model describes personality using five broad traits.
  • Eysenck's Theory Eysenck proposed a biologically based theory of personality with three dimensions.
  • Biological Approaches to Personality Biological approaches explain personality through genes, brain systems and evolution.
  • Freud's Psychodynamic Theory of Personality Freud's psychodynamic theory explains personality through unconscious processes, internal conflict and early childhood experience.
  • Humanistic Approaches to Personality Humanistic psychology sees people as striving towards growth and self-fulfilment.
  • Self-concept The self-concept is the set of beliefs a person holds about who they are.
  • Self-esteem Self-esteem is a person's overall evaluation of their own worth.
  • Self-efficacy Self-efficacy is belief in one's ability to succeed at a particular task.
  • Temperament Temperament is the early-appearing, biologically based style of emotional reactivity and self-regulation.
  • Personality Development Personality develops from childhood temperament into adult traits through biology, experience and social roles.
  • Stability and Change in Personality Personality is both stable and changing.
  • Personality Assessment: Psychometric Tests Psychometric tests measure personality using standardised questionnaires.
  • Projective Tests Projective tests ask people to respond to ambiguous stimuli, on the idea that responses reveal unconscious aspects of personality.
  • General Intelligence (g) General intelligence, or g, is the common factor underlying performance on diverse cognitive tests.
  • Fluid and Crystallised Intelligence Fluid intelligence is reasoning with new problems; crystallised intelligence is accumulated knowledge.
  • Multiple Intelligences Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences proposes several relatively independent intelligences.
  • Emotional Intelligence Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to perceive, understand and manage emotions.
  • Cognitive Differences People differ in cognitive abilities, and psychologists study both individual and group differences.
  • Motivation and Individual Differences People differ in what motivates them and how strongly.
  • Achievement Motivation Achievement motivation is the drive to succeed and meet standards of excellence.
  • Personality, Behaviour and Mental Health Personality traits are linked to health behaviours and to the risk of mental health difficulties.
  • Personality Across Cultures Cross-cultural research asks whether personality structure and average traits are the same everywhere.

Psychopathology

  • Defining Abnormality Psychologists have proposed several ways to define abnormality, each with strengths and limits.
  • A History of Psychopathology Ideas about mental distress have changed greatly over time.
  • The Biological Model The biological model explains psychological difficulties in terms of genes, brain structure and neurochemistry.
  • The Cognitive Model The cognitive model explains psychological difficulties in terms of thoughts, beliefs and information processing.
  • The Behavioural Model The behavioural model explains psychological difficulties as learned through conditioning.
  • The Psychodynamic Model The psychodynamic model explains psychological difficulties in terms of unconscious conflicts and early experiences.
  • The Humanistic Model The humanistic model sees psychological difficulties as arising when people's needs for growth and self-acceptance are blocked.
  • The Diathesis–stress Model The diathesis–stress model proposes that psychological difficulties arise when an underlying vulnerability meets stressful circumstances.
  • The Biopsychosocial Model The biopsychosocial model explains psychological difficulties through the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors.
  • Classification: DSM and ICD Diagnostic classification systems group symptoms into named conditions.
  • Reliability and Validity of Diagnosis A good diagnosis should be reliable (consistent between clinicians) and valid (meaningful and useful).
  • Generalised Anxiety Disorder Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) involves persistent, hard-to-control worry about many areas of life.
  • Panic Disorder Panic disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks and worry about further attacks.
  • Specific Phobias A specific phobia is an intense, persistent fear of a particular object or situation.
  • Social Anxiety Disorder Social anxiety disorder involves intense fear of being judged or embarrassed in social situations.
  • Obsessive-compulsive Disorder Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) involves intrusive, distressing thoughts and repetitive behaviours performed to reduce distress.
  • Trauma and Post-traumatic Stress Disorder Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can develop after exposure to a traumatic event.
  • Depression Depression involves persistent low mood or loss of interest, with effects on thinking, energy and daily life.
  • Bipolar Disorder Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania, usually alongside episodes of depression.
  • Schizophrenia and Psychosis Psychosis involves losing touch with shared reality, through hallucinations or delusions.
  • Personality Disorders Personality disorders describe long-standing patterns of thinking, feeling and relating that cause significant difficulties.
  • Eating Disorders Eating disorders involve serious disturbances in eating and in thoughts about weight and shape.
  • Substance Use Disorders Substance use disorders involve continued use of alcohol or drugs despite significant harm.
  • Autism Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication and how people experience the world.
  • ADHD Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition involving inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity.
  • Sleep Disorders Sleep disorders affect the quality, timing or amount of sleep and have wide effects on health.
  • Dissociative Disorders Dissociation involves disruptions in the normal integration of memory, identity, perception and awareness.
  • Somatic Symptom Disorders Somatic symptom disorders involve distressing physical symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours about them.
  • Risk and Protective Factors Risk factors increase the likelihood of psychological difficulties, while protective factors reduce it.
  • Comorbidity Comorbidity is the occurrence of two or more conditions in the same person.
  • Stigma Mental health stigma involves negative stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination towards people with mental health difficulties.
  • Culture and Psychopathology Culture shapes how psychological difficulties are expressed, understood and responded to.

Clinical Psychology and Interventions

  • Clinical Assessment Clinical assessment gathers information to understand a person's difficulties and decide how to help.
  • Clinical Interviews The clinical interview is the main way clinicians learn about a person's difficulties.
  • Mental State Assessment A mental state examination (MSE) is a structured description of a person's presentation at the time of assessment.
  • Psychological Testing in Clinical Practice Psychological tests measure aspects of cognition, personality and symptoms in a standardised way.
  • Formulation A formulation is a shared explanation of how a person's difficulties developed and are maintained.
  • Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is a structured psychological therapy that helps people change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behaviour.
  • Behavioural Therapy Behavioural therapies change behaviour directly using principles of learning.
  • Exposure Therapy Exposure therapy helps people reduce fear by gradually and safely facing what they fear.
  • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) helps people relate differently to difficult thoughts and feelings while acting on their values.
  • Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) was developed for people with intense emotions and self-harm.
  • Psychodynamic Therapy Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious feelings, relationship patterns and the past to help people understand and change current difficulties.
  • Humanistic and Person-centred Therapy Person-centred therapy, developed by Carl Rogers, holds that people can grow when offered a supportive relationship.
  • Family Therapy Family therapy works with families and relationships rather than only with individuals.
  • Group Therapy Group therapy brings together several people with shared difficulties, guided by one or more therapists.
  • Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) Interpersonal therapy (IPT) is a time-limited therapy that focuses on the links between mood and relationships.
  • Trauma-focused Therapies Trauma-focused therapies help people process traumatic memories and change the meanings they attach to trauma.
  • Psychological Treatment of Depression Several psychological therapies help people with depression.
  • Psychological Treatment of Anxiety Disorders Anxiety disorders are among the most treatable mental health conditions.
  • Psychological Treatment of OCD Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) responds well to specific psychological treatments.
  • Psychological Treatment of PTSD Psychological treatment is the first-line approach for PTSD.
  • Psychological Interventions for Psychosis Psychological interventions can help people with psychosis alongside medication and other support.
  • Relapse Prevention Relapse prevention helps people maintain progress after therapy.
  • The Therapeutic Relationship The therapeutic relationship is a key ingredient of effective therapy across approaches.
  • Clinical Supervision Clinical supervision is a formal relationship in which an experienced practitioner supports and oversees another's clinical work.
  • Measuring Therapy Outcomes Measuring outcomes shows whether therapy is helping.
  • Evidence-based Practice Evidence-based practice combines the best research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values and preferences.
  • Ethics in Clinical Practice Clinical psychologists follow ethical principles to protect clients and the public.
  • Professional Boundaries Professional boundaries keep the therapeutic relationship safe and focused on the client.

Research Methods

  • Scientific Reasoning in Psychology Scientific reasoning is the way psychologists move from ideas to evidence and back: making claims testable, collecting data systematically and drawing conclusions that go no further than the data allow.
  • Research Questions and Hypotheses A good study starts with a clear research question and a precise hypothesis.
  • Operationalisation Operationalisation turns an abstract concept, such as stress or aggression, into something that can be measured.
  • Independent and Dependent Variables In an experiment, the independent variable is what the researcher changes and the dependent variable is what they measure.
  • Confounding Variables A confounding variable changes along with the independent variable, so it offers an alternative explanation for the results.
  • Experimental Design Experimental design is how participants are allocated to conditions: independent groups, repeated measures or matched pairs.
  • Laboratory Experiments Laboratory experiments manipulate an IV in a controlled setting, allowing strong causal conclusions at a possible cost to realism.
  • Field Experiments Field experiments manipulate an IV in a natural setting, often without participants knowing they are in a study.
  • Natural Experiments In a natural experiment, the independent variable changes naturally, without the researcher manipulating it, and the researcher studies its effects.
  • Quasi-experiments A quasi-experiment compares groups that differ on a pre-existing characteristic, such as age, sex or diagnosis, which the researcher can't assign.
  • Correlational Research Correlational research measures two or more variables to see whether they are related, without manipulating anything.
  • Cross-sectional and Longitudinal Research To study change over time, researchers either compare different age groups at one point (cross-sectional) or follow the same people over years (longitudinal).
  • Case Studies A case study is an in-depth investigation of one person, group or event, usually using several methods.
  • Naturalistic Observation Naturalistic observation records behaviour as it happens in its everyday setting, without interfering.
  • Surveys and Questionnaires Surveys collect self-report data from many people using questionnaires.
  • Focus Groups A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group about a topic, used to explore views and how people talk about an issue together.
  • Quantitative, Qualitative and Mixed Methods Quantitative research measures and counts; qualitative research explores meanings in words and experiences; mixed methods combine them.
  • Opportunity and Volunteer Sampling Opportunity and volunteer samples are quick and common in psychology, but they are rarely representative.
  • Sample Size How many participants a study needs depends on the size of the effect it is looking for and how sure the researchers want to be of detecting it.
  • Reliability Reliability is the consistency of a measure: whether it gives the same results under the same conditions.
  • Validity Validity is whether a study or measure actually captures what it claims to.
  • Generalisability Generalisability is how far findings extend beyond the specific people, place and time studied.
  • Bias in Research Bias is systematic error that pushes results in a particular direction.
  • Replication Replication means repeating a study to see whether its findings hold.
  • Preregistration Preregistration means publicly recording a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan before collecting or analysing data.
  • Open Science Open science is a set of practices that make research transparent, accessible and checkable: sharing data, materials, code and papers, and making plans public.
  • Informed Consent Informed consent means participants agree to take part knowing what the research involves, its risks and their rights.
  • Confidentiality Confidentiality means protecting participants' personal information and ensuring they can't be identified in reports.
  • Deception and Debriefing Deception means misleading participants about a study, and debriefing means explaining the study fully afterwards.
  • Vulnerable Participants Some participants need extra protection because their capacity to consent is limited or they face greater risk: children, people with some cognitive impairments or mental health conditions, prisoners and others in dependent relationships.
  • Research Governance and Ethics Committees Research governance is the system of rules, review and accountability that oversees research.

Statistics

  • Levels of Measurement Data come at different levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio.
  • Measures of Central Tendency Measures of central tendency summarise the typical value of a data set: the mean, median and mode.
  • Measures of Dispersion: Range, Variance and Standard Deviation Measures of dispersion describe how spread out the data are.
  • Distributions: Normal, Skew and Kurtosis The shape of a distribution matters for which statistics are appropriate.
  • Data Visualisation Good graphs show the shape of the data, not just the averages.
  • Sampling Distributions and Standard Error If you repeated a study many times, the sample means would vary.
  • Confidence Intervals A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for a population parameter, showing the precision of an estimate.
  • Null and Alternative Hypotheses Null hypothesis significance testing compares data against a null hypothesis of no effect.
  • P-values and Statistical Significance The p-value is the most reported and most misunderstood number in psychology.
  • Effect Sizes An effect size measures how big an effect is, independently of sample size.
  • Type I and Type II Errors Every significance test risks two kinds of error: a false positive (Type I) or a false negative (Type II).
  • Correlation: Pearson and Spearman Correlation coefficients measure the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables.
  • Simple Linear Regression Simple linear regression fits a straight line that predicts one variable from another.
  • Multiple Regression Multiple regression predicts an outcome from several predictors at once, estimating each predictor's contribution while holding the others constant.
  • Independent T-tests The independent t-test compares the means of two separate groups.
  • Paired T-tests The paired (related) t-test compares two means from the same participants, or matched pairs, by analysing the differences within each pair.
  • Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) ANOVA compares the means of three or more groups at once, avoiding the inflated error rate of many t-tests.
  • Chi-square Tests Chi-square tests analyse frequencies in categories: whether observed counts differ from those expected, or whether two categorical variables are associated.
  • Non-parametric Tests Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about distributions, working with ranks instead of raw scores.
  • Meta-analysis Meta-analysis statistically combines the results of many studies to estimate an overall effect and explain variation between studies.
  • Reproducible Analysis A reproducible analysis can be rerun by someone else, from the raw data to the final numbers, and give the same result.
  • Statistical Software Psychologists analyse data with software ranging from point-and-click programs to programming languages.
  • Interpreting and Reporting Results Results must be reported fully and interpreted carefully: what was found, how large and how certain, and what it does and doesn't show.

Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology

  • Adaptation An adaptation is a trait shaped by natural selection because it helped ancestors survive or reproduce.
  • Natural Selection Natural selection is the process by which heritable traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common.
  • Sexual Selection Sexual selection is selection for traits that improve mating success.
  • Evolution of Cognition Evolutionary psychology asks how the human mind was shaped by selection.
  • Evolution of Emotion Emotions may have evolved to help organisms respond to recurrent challenges and opportunities.
  • Kin Selection and Inclusive Fitness Kin selection explains why organisms help relatives, even at a cost to themselves.
  • Reciprocal Altruism Reciprocal altruism explains helping non-relatives when help is likely to be returned.
  • Evolution of Cooperation Humans cooperate on a scale unusual among animals.
  • Mating Strategies Mating strategies are patterns of behaviour for finding and keeping mates.
  • Parental Investment Parental investment is the time, energy and resources parents devote to offspring.
  • Attachment in Evolutionary Perspective Bowlby's attachment theory was built on evolutionary ideas.
  • Aggression in Evolutionary Perspective Evolutionary psychology explains some aggression as a strategy that helped ancestors gain or defend resources, status and mates.
  • Social Behaviour in Evolutionary Perspective Humans are an intensely social species.
  • Behavioural Ecology Behavioural ecology studies how behaviour is adapted to current ecological conditions.
  • Cultural Evolution Cultural evolution studies how culture changes through processes of variation, transmission and selection.
  • Evolutionary Developmental Psychology Evolutionary developmental psychology studies how evolved mechanisms unfold during development and why childhood itself might be adaptive.
  • Critiques of Evolutionary Psychology Evolutionary psychology is influential but controversial.

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

  • Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation Motivation is what energises and directs behaviour.
  • Drive Theories Drive theories explain motivation as a push to reduce internal tension caused by unmet needs.
  • Incentive Theories Incentive theories explain motivation by the pull of external goals and rewards.
  • Self-determination Theory Self-determination theory explains how social environments support or undermine motivation and wellbeing through three basic psychological needs.
  • Goal-setting Theory Goal-setting theory explains how specific, challenging goals improve performance.
  • Achievement Motivation in Context Achievement motivation is shaped by context: classrooms, cultures, stereotypes and expectations.
  • Theories of Emotion: James–Lange and Cannon–Bard Early theories of emotion asked whether bodily changes cause emotional feelings or occur alongside them.
  • The Two-factor Theory: Schachter–Singer Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory proposes that emotion depends on physiological arousal plus a cognitive label.
  • Emotion Regulation Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them.
  • Stress Stress arises when demands are perceived to exceed resources.
  • The Physiology of Stress Stress triggers bodily responses that prepare for action.
  • Coping Coping is how people manage stressful demands.
  • Resilience Resilience is the ability to adapt well in the face of adversity.
  • Subjective Wellbeing Subjective wellbeing is how people evaluate and experience their own lives.
  • Happiness What makes people happy?
  • Positive Psychology in Practice Positive psychology studies what helps people thrive and develops interventions to increase wellbeing.
  • Flow Flow is a state of deep absorption in a challenging activity.
  • Gratitude Gratitude is noticing and appreciating the good things in life, including what others do for us.
  • Mindfulness Research Mindfulness means paying attention to present-moment experience without judgement.
  • Flourishing Flourishing means living well: feeling good and functioning well.

Applied Psychology

  • Educational Psychology Educational psychology applies psychology to learning and teaching.
  • Achievement and Motivation in Education Pupils' motivation strongly influences their learning.
  • Behaviour in Schools Classroom behaviour affects learning and wellbeing.
  • Assessment in Education Assessment measures and supports learning.
  • Individual Differences in Learning Pupils differ in prior knowledge, working memory, attention, language and motivation.
  • Inclusion and Special Educational Needs Inclusion aims to ensure all pupils, including those with special educational needs and disabilities, can learn and participate.
  • Occupational Psychology Occupational psychology applies psychology to work and organisations.
  • Work Motivation Work motivation is what drives people to put effort into their jobs.
  • Leadership at Work Leadership strongly shapes employees' motivation, wellbeing and performance.
  • Organisational Behaviour Organisational behaviour studies how people act in organisations: in teams, cultures and during change.
  • Job Satisfaction Job satisfaction is how positively people feel about their jobs.
  • Wellbeing at Work Work can support or harm wellbeing.
  • Burnout Burnout is a response to chronic workplace stress characterised by exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy.
  • Human Factors and Ergonomics Human factors and ergonomics design systems, tools and environments to fit human abilities and limits.
  • Mental Workload Mental workload is the demand a task places on a person's limited mental resources.
  • Human–computer Interaction Human–computer interaction (HCI) studies how people use digital technology and how to design it to be usable, accessible and safe.
  • Consumer and Marketing Psychology Consumer psychology studies how people choose, buy and use products.
  • Health Psychology Health psychology studies how psychological and social factors affect health, illness and healthcare.
  • Behaviour Change Behaviour change interventions apply psychology to help people change habits for health, environment or wellbeing.
  • Psychology and Public Health Psychology contributes to public health by understanding behaviour at population level.
  • Environmental Psychology Environmental psychology studies how people and their physical environments affect each other.
  • Forensic Psychology Forensic psychology applies psychology to crime and the criminal justice system.
  • Psychology and the Law Psychology contributes to the legal system by studying eyewitnesses, interviews, juries and false confessions.
  • Sport and Performance Psychology Sport and performance psychology applies psychology to improve performance and wellbeing in sport, music and other high-pressure activities.

Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology

  • Culture and Cognition Cultural psychology asks whether basic thinking processes are the same everywhere.
  • Culture and Perception Perception seems automatic, but experience can shape how we interpret what we see.
  • Culture and Emotion Are emotions universal, or do they vary across cultures?
  • Individualism and Collectivism Individualism and collectivism describe how cultures balance personal goals and group obligations.
  • Cultural Identity Cultural identity is a person's sense of belonging to cultural groups.
  • Acculturation Acculturation is the process of cultural and psychological change when people from different cultures come into contact.
  • Cultural Adaptation People who move to a new culture must adapt psychologically and socially.
  • Cross-cultural Research Methods Comparing cultures raises special methodological problems.
  • Ethnocentrism and Cultural Relativism Ethnocentrism means judging other cultures by the standards of one's own.
  • Culture and Personality Do personality traits have the same structure in every culture?
  • Culture and Development Children develop within cultural contexts that shape childrearing, learning and milestones.
  • Culture and Mental Health Culture shapes how distress is experienced, expressed and explained, and how people seek help.
  • Culture and Diagnosis Psychiatric diagnosis relies on categories that may not fit all cultures equally well.
  • Language and Thought Does the language we speak shape how we think?
  • Indigenous Psychologies Indigenous psychologies develop psychological knowledge from within particular cultures rather than importing Western theories.
  • Globalisation and Psychology Globalisation brings people into contact with many cultures through media, migration and technology.

Contemporary Psychology

  • Cognitive Neuroscience Today Cognitive neuroscience links mental processes to brain activity.
  • Computational Psychology Computational psychology uses mathematical and computer models to explain how the mind works.
  • Computational Psychiatry Computational psychiatry uses models of brain and behaviour to understand mental health conditions.
  • Artificial Intelligence and Psychology Psychology and artificial intelligence (AI) influence each other: psychology inspired AI, and AI is now a tool and a subject for psychological research.
  • Digital Mental Health Digital mental health uses apps, online therapy and other technologies to support mental health.
  • Social Media and Psychology Social media are part of daily life for most young people.
  • The Psychology of Technology Technology changes how we remember, pay attention and relate to others.
  • Human–computer Interaction Today Human–computer interaction (HCI) applies psychology to the design of technology.
  • Neuroeconomics Neuroeconomics combines neuroscience, psychology and economics to study how the brain makes decisions.
  • Behavioural Economics Behavioural economics uses psychology to explain why people's economic decisions depart from traditional models of rational choice.
  • Decision Science Decision science studies how people and organisations make decisions and how decisions can be improved.
  • Predictive Processing Predictive processing proposes that the brain constantly predicts incoming sensory information and updates its predictions when errors occur.
  • Embodied Cognition Embodied cognition proposes that thinking depends on the body and its interaction with the environment, not just on abstract processing in the brain.
  • Bayesian Cognition Bayesian models describe the mind as combining prior knowledge with new evidence according to probability theory.
  • Network Approaches to Mental Health Network approaches see mental health conditions as systems of symptoms that influence each other, rather than as hidden underlying diseases.
  • Psychoneuroimmunology Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) studies how psychological processes, the nervous system and the immune system interact.
  • Behavioural Genetics Behavioural genetics studies how genetic and environmental differences contribute to differences in behaviour.
  • Epigenetics and Behaviour Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence.
  • Open Science and the Replication Crisis In the 2010s, psychology faced a replication crisis when many well-known findings failed to replicate.
  • Current Debates in Psychology Psychology is a lively, contested science.
  • Future Directions Psychology is changing through large-scale collaboration, new technology and broader participation.
