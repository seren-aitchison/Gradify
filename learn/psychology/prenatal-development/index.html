Prenatal Development

Prenatal development moves through germinal, embryonic and fetal stages; timing, dose and susceptibility shape teratogen effects.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes · sources checked 2026-11

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the germinal, embryonic and fetal stages
  • Explain how teratogens can affect prenatal development
  • Evaluate evidence on the long-term effects of the prenatal environment

Three stages

  • Germinal stage (conception to about 2 weeks): the fertilised egg (zygote) divides and implants in the wall of the uterus.
  • Embryonic stage (about weeks 3–8): major organs and structures form, including the neural tube, which becomes the brain and spinal cord.
  • Fetal stage (week 9 to birth): growth and refinement. The brain grows rapidly and neurones form, migrate and connect. From late pregnancy the fetus can hear and learn sounds.
Worked example

Learning before birth

  1. DeCasper and Spence (1986) asked pregnant women to read a particular children's story aloud daily in the last weeks of pregnancy.
  2. After birth, babies sucked on a dummy in a pattern that let them hear the familiar story rather than a new one.
  3. Newborns had learned features of speech heard in the womb.

Teratogens

Teratogens are environmental agents that can harm prenatal development: alcohol, tobacco smoke, some medicines, infections such as rubella and Zika, and pollutants. Effects depend on timing (the embryonic stage is most sensitive for structural defects), dose and genetic susceptibility. Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) can involve learning, attention and behavioural difficulties; UK health guidance is that the safest approach in pregnancy is not to drink alcohol at all.

Long-term effects

The developmental origins of health and disease idea proposes that prenatal conditions affect later health. People conceived during the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944–45 showed higher rates of some health problems in adulthood (Roseboom et al., 2006). Prenatal stress has also been linked to later child outcomes, though genes and postnatal environments shared with mothers make causes hard to separate.

Common mistakeAssuming a fetus is unaffected until late pregnancy. The embryonic weeks, when organs form, are the most sensitive to many teratogens.

In 2 lines: Prenatal development has germinal, embryonic and fetal stages; the fetus learns sounds before birth. Teratogen effects depend on timing, dose and susceptibility; prenatal conditions may affect lifelong health.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. During which stage do major organs first form?
    • Germinal
    • Embryonic
    • Fetal
    • Neonatal
    Show the answer

    Answer: Embryonic. Organs form in weeks 3 to 8, the embryonic stage.

  2. Newborns prefer a story their mother read aloud during pregnancy. What does this show?
    • Teratogen effects
    • Genetic memory
    • Learning before birth
    • Object permanence
    Show the answer

    Answer: Learning before birth. Prenatal exposure to speech shaped newborn preferences.

  3. Why is it hard to show that prenatal stress causes later child problems?
    • Stress can never be measured
    • Children are never studied
    • Stress has no biological effects
    • Shared genes and settings
    Show the answer

    Answer: Shared genes and settings. Shared genes and postnatal settings confound the link.

Key terms

Embryonic stage
Weeks 3–8 of pregnancy, when major organs form
Teratogen
An environmental agent that can harm prenatal development
Neural tube
The embryonic structure that becomes the brain and spinal cord
