Neuroplasticity

The brain changes with experience through synaptic strengthening and reorganisation, which supports learning and recovery.

Psychology · Biological Psychology and Neuroscience · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define neuroplasticity and describe synaptic plasticity
  • Explain evidence for experience-dependent changes in the brain
  • Explain functional recovery after brain injury and the factors that affect it

Changing connections

Hebb (1949) proposed that when one neurone repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens, often summarised as "neurones that fire together wire together". Long-term potentiation (LTP), discovered by Bliss and Lømo (1973), is a lasting increase in synaptic strength after repeated stimulation and is thought to be a mechanism of learning. Synapses can also weaken (long-term depression) or be pruned when unused.

Experience changes the brain

Worked example

Learning to juggle

  1. Draganski et al. (2004) taught adults to juggle over three months.
  2. MRI showed grey matter increased in visual-motion areas.
  3. After three months without practice, the increase had partly reversed.

Musicians show larger cortical representations of the fingers they use most, and enriched environments increase dendritic branching in rats. Plasticity is greatest in childhood, especially during sensitive periods, but continues throughout life.

Recovery after injury

After a stroke or injury, function can partly recover as nearby or opposite-hemisphere areas take over (functional reorganisation), unused pathways are unmasked and new connections form. Recovery is usually fastest in the first weeks and months. Age, the size of the damage, rehabilitation and cognitive reserve (from education and an active life) affect outcomes. Constraint-induced movement therapy, which restricts the unaffected arm to force use of the affected one, improves arm function in some stroke patients (Wolf et al., 2006).

Common mistakeBelieving plasticity means anything can be fully restored. Recovery is often partial and depends on many factors.

In 2 lines: Synaptic plasticity, including LTP, underlies learning. Experience reshapes the brain; recovery after injury depends on age, damage, rehabilitation and reserve.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. "Neurones that fire together wire together" summarises the idea of…
    • Lateralisation
    • Hebbian learning
    • Saltatory conduction
    • The all-or-none law
    Show the answer

    Answer: Hebbian learning. Hebb proposed that co-active connections strengthen.

  2. After a stroke, a patient's arm function improves when their good arm is restrained during practice. This is…
    • Long-term depression
    • Split-brain surgery
    • Constraint-induced therapy
    • Pharmacotherapy
    Show the answer

    Answer: Constraint-induced therapy. Forcing use of the affected arm promotes reorganisation.

  3. Why are younger brains often thought to recover better after injury?
    • Young brains have no damage
    • Older brains have no neurones
    • Age has no effect
    • Plasticity is greater early in life
    Show the answer

    Answer: Plasticity is greater early in life. Greater plasticity allows more reorganisation, though not always.

Key terms

Neuroplasticity
The brain's capacity to change with experience or injury
Long-term potentiation
A lasting strengthening of synapses after repeated stimulation
Functional recovery
Regaining abilities after brain injury through reorganisation
