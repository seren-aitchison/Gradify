Social Behaviour in Evolutionary Perspective

Group living has benefits and costs; status via prestige, the need to belong and social learning may have evolved, but group boundaries and behaviour are flexible and cultural.

Psychology · Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain evolutionary benefits and costs of group living
  • Explain evolutionary accounts of status, in-group favouritism and social learning
  • Evaluate evolutionary explanations of social behaviour

Living in groups

Group living offers protection from predators, shared knowledge, cooperative hunting and childcare, but also costs: competition for food and mates and disease transmission. Dunbar's social brain hypothesis links larger neocortex size to larger group sizes in primates, and suggests humans maintain stable relationships with about 150 people ("Dunbar's number").

Status and belonging

Henrich and Gil-White (2001) distinguished two routes to status: dominance (through fear and force) and prestige (through skill and knowledge, freely granted by others who want to learn). Baumeister and Leary (1995) argued humans have a fundamental need to belong, which may have evolved because exclusion from the group was dangerous.

Worked example

Learning from others

  1. Humans learn heavily from others, copying successful or prestigious individuals and the majority.
  2. Children often over-imitate, copying even unnecessary actions shown by adults (Horner and Whiten, 2005), while chimpanzees skip irrelevant steps.
  3. Such faithful copying helps transmit cultural knowledge across generations.

Evaluation

Evolutionary explanations of in-group bias suggest humans readily favour their group, as minimal group studies show. But who counts as "in-group" is flexible and culturally defined, and cooperation across groups is common. Critics warn against using evolution to naturalise prejudice. Dunbar's number has been questioned: estimates vary widely depending on method (Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind, 2021).

Common mistakeTreating in-group bias as fixed and inevitable. Group boundaries are flexible and can expand.

In 2 lines: Group living brings benefits and costs; the social brain hypothesis links brain size and group size. Prestige, belonging and social learning may be evolved; group boundaries are flexible.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Dunbar proposed humans maintain stable relationships with about…
    • 150 people
    • 15 people
    • 1,500 people
    • 5 people
    Show the answer

    Answer: 150 people. Dunbar's number suggests stable relationships with about 150 people.

  2. Students listen closely to a respected expert and copy her methods. This illustrates…
    • Dominance
    • Prestige-based learning
    • Paternity uncertainty
    • Kin selection
    Show the answer

    Answer: Prestige-based learning. Freely granted deference to skill is prestige.

  3. Why have estimates of Dunbar's number been questioned?
    • It has been confirmed exactly
    • Brains don't vary
    • Estimates vary widely with method
    • Groups are always 150
    Show the answer

    Answer: Estimates vary widely with method. Lindenfors et al. found wide uncertainty.

Key terms

Social brain hypothesis
The idea that large brains evolved to manage complex social groups
Prestige
Status granted freely to skilled and knowledgeable individuals
Over-imitation
Copying unnecessary actions shown by others
