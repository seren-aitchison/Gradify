Clinical Interviews

Clinical interviews may be unstructured, semi-structured or structured; structured interviews such as the SCID improve reliability; skills include active listening, open and closed questions, reflecting and summarising.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Compare unstructured, semi-structured and structured clinical interviews
  • Describe core interviewing skills
  • Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of each type of interview
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or therapy. Clinical interviews should only be carried out by qualified, registered professionals.

Types of interview

Unstructured interviews follow the person's account flexibly, allowing rapport and depth but risking inconsistency. Structured interviews, such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM (SCID), ask set questions in a fixed order to assess diagnostic criteria, improving reliability. Semi-structured interviews combine a core set of topics with flexibility to follow up.

Interviewing skills

Core skills include active listening, open and closed questions, reflecting and summarising, showing empathy and checking understanding. Interviews usually cover the presenting problem, its history and impact, background and relationships, physical health, substance use, strengths and goals, and risk.

Worked example

Open and closed questions

  1. Open question: "Can you tell me what's been happening for you recently?" invites a full account.
  2. Closed question: "Have you been sleeping less than usual?" checks a specific detail.
  3. Skilled interviewers combine both, starting broadly and narrowing as needed. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Structured interviews increase reliability and are valuable in research, but can feel impersonal and may miss what matters most to the person. Unstructured interviews allow rapport but are vulnerable to bias. Most clinical practice uses semi-structured approaches, balancing consistency and flexibility.

Common mistakeAssuming structured interviews are always best. They increase reliability but can miss the person's priorities.

In 2 lines: Unstructured, semi-structured and structured interviews differ. Structured interviews increase reliability; skills include listening, questioning and summarising.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. An interview with set questions asked in a fixed order is…
    • Structured
    • Unstructured
    • Semi-structured
    • Projective
    Show the answer

    Answer: Structured. Structured interviews standardise questions.

  2. A clinician asks "What has been most difficult for you?" and then "Have you lost weight?" This shows…
    • Using only closed questions
    • Combining open and closed questions
    • Reflecting only
    • Diagnostic overshadowing
    Show the answer

    Answer: Combining open and closed questions. Skilled interviewers mix open questions for depth with closed questions for detail.

  3. What is a weakness of fully structured interviews in practice?
    • They always reduce diagnostic reliability
    • They allow far too much flexibility
    • They can feel impersonal and miss the person's priorities
    • They need no training at all to use
    Show the answer

    Answer: They can feel impersonal and miss the person's priorities. Fixed questions may overlook what matters most.

Key terms

Structured interview
An interview with set questions in a fixed order
Active listening
Paying full attention and showing understanding
Reflecting
Repeating back the essence of what someone has said
