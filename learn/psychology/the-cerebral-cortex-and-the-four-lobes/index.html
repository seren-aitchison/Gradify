The Cerebral Cortex and the Four Lobes

The frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes have broad specialisations; motor and sensory cortex map the body contralaterally.

Psychology · Biological Psychology and Neuroscience · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the location and main functions of the four lobes
  • Explain the organisation of the motor and somatosensory cortex
  • Evaluate evidence for localisation of function, including Phineas Gage

Four lobes

  • Frontal lobe: planning, decision-making, self-control and voluntary movement (the motor cortex); in most people the left frontal lobe contains Broca's area for speech production.
  • Parietal lobe: touch and body position (the somatosensory cortex), spatial attention and integrating senses.
  • Temporal lobe: hearing (auditory cortex), language comprehension (Wernicke's area, usually left), and memory with the medial temporal lobe.
  • Occipital lobe: vision (visual cortex).

Maps of the body

The motor cortex (in front of the central sulcus) and somatosensory cortex (behind it) are organised as maps of the body. Penfield and Boldrey (1937) stimulated the cortex of patients during surgery and found that stimulating different points produced movement or sensation in different body parts. The maps are contralateral (the left hemisphere serves the right side of the body) and distorted: areas with fine control or sensitivity, such as hands and lips, have more cortex. This is often drawn as a homunculus.

Worked example

Phineas Gage

  1. In 1848 an iron rod passed through the frontal lobe of railway worker Phineas Gage.
  2. He survived, but reports described him as more impulsive and less reliable afterwards (Harlow, 1868).
  3. His case is often cited as evidence linking the frontal lobe to personality and self-control, though reports of his change vary and he later held jobs, suggesting some recovery (Macmillan, 2000).

The cortex also contains association areas that integrate information across senses and support complex thought. The prefrontal cortex, at the front of the frontal lobe, is among the last areas to mature, continuing into the mid-20s, and supports planning, working memory and impulse control.

Investigate with the lab. Select each of the four lobes and note one function and one piece of classic evidence for it.

Common mistakeTreating lobe functions as exclusive. Each lobe has many roles and they work together.

In 2 lines: Each lobe has broad functions: frontal for planning and movement, parietal for touch and space, temporal for hearing and memory, occipital for vision. Motor and sensory maps are contralateral; Gage's case is classic but contested evidence.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which lobe contains the primary visual cortex?
    • Frontal
    • Temporal
    • Occipital
    • Parietal
    Show the answer

    Answer: Occipital. Vision is processed at the back of the brain.

  2. After a stroke, a patient can't feel touch on their left hand. Where is damage most likely?
    • Left parietal lobe
    • Right occipital lobe
    • Left frontal lobe
    • Right parietal lobe
    Show the answer

    Answer: Right parietal lobe. Somatosensory cortex is in the parietal lobe and contralateral.

  3. Why is Phineas Gage's case limited evidence for frontal lobe function?
    • Accounts are sparse and the damage uncertain
    • He had no brain injury
    • It was a large controlled experiment
    • He showed no change at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: Accounts are sparse and the damage uncertain. Historical reports vary and the exact damage is reconstructed.

  4. Which lobe contains the primary motor cortex?
    • The occipital lobe
    • The frontal lobe
    • The temporal lobe
    • The parietal lobe
    Show the answer

    Answer: The frontal lobe. The primary motor cortex lies at the back of the frontal lobe, in front of the central sulcus.

Key terms

Motor cortex
The frontal lobe strip controlling voluntary movement
Somatosensory cortex
The parietal lobe strip processing touch and body position
Contralateral
Serving the opposite side of the body
