Measuring Therapy Outcomes

Outcomes are measured with questionnaires, goals and functioning; reliable and clinically significant change show real, meaningful improvement; routine outcome monitoring with feedback improves outcomes, especially for clients not on track.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes · sources checked 2025-09

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe common ways of measuring therapy outcomes
  • Explain reliable and clinically significant change
  • Evaluate routine outcome monitoring
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or therapy. Psychological therapies should only be delivered by qualified, registered professionals.

Outcome measures

Therapy outcomes are often measured with standardised questionnaires, such as the PHQ-9 for depressive symptoms and the GAD-7 for anxiety, completed before, during and after therapy. Other outcomes include functioning, quality of life, the person's own goals and client satisfaction. NHS Talking Therapies uses such measures to report recovery: in 2024–25, 50.5% of referrals finishing treatment moved to recovery.

Reliable and clinically significant change

Scores can change by chance. Jacobson and Truax (1991) proposed reliable change: a change larger than would be expected from measurement error alone. Clinically significant change means a person moves from the range typical of a clinical population into the range typical of the general population. Together these show whether change is both real and meaningful.

Worked example

Routine outcome monitoring

  1. Routine outcome monitoring (ROM) means measuring progress at every session and sharing feedback with the therapist.
  2. Lambert, Whipple and Kleinstäuber (2018) meta-analysed studies and found feedback improved outcomes, especially for clients who were not progressing as expected.
  3. Feedback helps therapists notice clients at risk of deterioration and adjust their approach.

Evaluation

Measuring outcomes supports accountability, learning and better care. But questionnaires may miss what matters most to clients, can be gamed when targets are attached, and may feel burdensome. Combining standard measures with personal goals and the client's own account gives a fuller picture.

Common mistakeAssuming any drop in score means real improvement. Change must exceed measurement error.

In 2 lines: Questionnaires, goals and functioning measure outcomes. Reliable and clinically significant change; ROM feedback improves outcomes.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Measuring progress at each session and feeding it back to the therapist is…
    • Clinical supervision
    • Formulation
    • Free association
    • Routine outcome monitoring
    Show the answer

    Answer: Routine outcome monitoring. Routine outcome monitoring tracks progress session by session and feeds it back to the therapist.

  2. A client's score falls by two points, but the measure's error is larger than that. The change is…
    • Not reliable change
    • Clinically significant change
    • Proof of recovery
    • Proof of deterioration
    Show the answer

    Answer: Not reliable change. Change smaller than measurement error isn't reliable.

  3. Why can attaching targets to outcome measures be a problem?
    • Targets always improve accuracy
    • Measures can be gamed or distorted
    • Measures become more valid
    • Clients stop changing
    Show the answer

    Answer: Measures can be gamed or distorted. Pressure to hit targets can distort recording.
