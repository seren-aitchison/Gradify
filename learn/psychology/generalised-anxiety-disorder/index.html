Generalised Anxiety Disorder

GAD involves persistent, uncontrollable worry with physical symptoms; avoidance theory, intolerance of uncertainty and metacognitive models explain worry; NICE recommends stepped care including CBT.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main features of generalised anxiety disorder
  • Explain cognitive explanations of worry
  • Evaluate explanations and recommended approaches
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about anxiety should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Features

GAD involves excessive, persistent worry about everyday matters such as health, work and family, which the person finds hard to control. It is often accompanied by restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and sleep problems, and causes distress or impairment. It frequently co-occurs with depression and other anxiety conditions.

Explaining worry

Borkovec's avoidance theory proposed that worry, being mostly verbal thought, helps people avoid distressing images and bodily arousal in the short term, so it is negatively reinforced. Dugas and colleagues emphasised intolerance of uncertainty: people who find uncertainty very hard to bear worry to try to prepare for every possibility. Wells proposed a metacognitive model in which beliefs about worry ("worrying keeps me safe", "my worry is uncontrollable") maintain it.

Worked example

Approaches in guidance

  1. NICE guidance for GAD in England recommends a stepped care approach, starting with education and monitoring and moving to low-intensity psychological interventions, such as guided self-help.
  2. More intensive options include individual cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or applied relaxation.
  3. Medication may be considered in some cases after discussion with a clinician.

Evaluation

Cognitive models of GAD are supported by research on intolerance of uncertainty and metacognitive beliefs, and have informed effective therapies. But causal direction is hard to establish, and the boundary between normal worry and GAD involves judgement. Social stressors, such as financial insecurity, also contribute.

Common mistakeAssuming all worry is a disorder. GAD involves persistent, excessive worry causing distress or impairment.

In 2 lines: GAD: persistent, uncontrollable worry with physical symptoms. Avoidance, intolerance of uncertainty and metacognition; stepped care in guidance.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Beliefs about one's own thinking, such as "worrying keeps me safe", are…
    • Metacognitive beliefs
    • Conditions of worth
    • Defence mechanisms
    • Diagnostic criteria
    Show the answer

    Answer: Metacognitive beliefs. Beliefs about thinking are metacognitive.

  2. A person worries constantly about every possible outcome because not knowing feels unbearable. This fits…
    • Learned helplessness
    • Intolerance of uncertainty
    • Displacement
    • Humoral theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: Intolerance of uncertainty. Worry used to cope with uncertainty fits this model.

  3. Why is it hard to know whether cognitive processes cause GAD?
    • The processes always come first
    • GAD has no cognitive features at all
    • Worry and these processes may influence each other
    • Worry is never measured in research
    Show the answer

    Answer: Worry and these processes may influence each other. Causal direction is difficult to establish.

Key terms

Metacognitive beliefs

Stepped care
