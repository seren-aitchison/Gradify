Mating Strategies

Sexual strategies theory proposes sex-differentiated short- and long-term strategies; evidence shows average differences that vary with conditions, culture and safety.

Psychology · Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish short-term and long-term mating strategies
  • Explain sexual strategies theory and its predictions
  • Evaluate evidence on mating strategies, including conditional and cultural influences

Short and long term

Buss and Schmitt's (1993) sexual strategies theory proposes that both women and men have evolved short-term and long-term mating strategies, but that the costs and benefits differ by sex because of differences in minimum parental investment. Men may gain more reproductively from short-term mating; women may gain more from careful choice of long-term partners who provide resources and care, though women also pursue short-term mating in some conditions.

Evidence

Clark and Hatfield (1989) had confederates approach students on a US campus and ask "Would you go to bed with me tonight?" About 75% of men agreed but no women did. Critics note women's refusals may reflect safety concerns and stigma rather than evolved preference (Conley, 2011). Schmitt (2005) found men reported more desire for sexual variety across 48 nations, with differences that varied by culture.

Worked example

Conditional strategies

  1. Strategies may shift with conditions.
  2. In environments with higher mortality and resource uncertainty, people may start families earlier and adopt faster life strategies (life history theory).
  3. Sex ratios also matter: when men are scarce, women may face more competition and men may pursue more short-term mating.

Evaluation

Sexual strategies theory explains some average sex differences and their cross-cultural presence. Limitations include reliance on self-report, heterosexual and Western samples, the influence of gender norms and safety, and large individual variation. Many researchers see mating behaviour as flexible, shaped by evolved tendencies, individual differences and culture.

Common mistakeTreating average sex differences as fixed rules for individuals. Variation within each sex is large.

In 2 lines: Both sexes have short- and long-term strategies, with different costs and benefits. Evidence (Clark and Hatfield, Schmitt) shows average differences; conditions, culture and safety shape behaviour.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. In Clark and Hatfield's study, about what proportion of men agreed to the request?
    • About 5%
    • About 75%
    • About 25%
    • All of them
    Show the answer

    Answer: About 75%. Around three-quarters of men agreed, while no women did.

  2. Conley found women's acceptance depended on perceived proposer safety and skill. This challenges…
    • Life history theory
    • Parental investment theory entirely
    • A purely evolved explanation
    • Kin selection theory
    Show the answer

    Answer: A purely evolved explanation. Safety and stigma may explain women's refusals.

  3. How does life history theory explain earlier family formation?
    • Wealth always delays families
    • Culture has no role at all
    • Strategies are always fixed
    • Harsh conditions favour speed
    Show the answer

    Answer: Harsh conditions favour speed. Harsh conditions shift effort towards earlier reproduction.

Key terms

Sexual strategies theory
Buss and Schmitt's theory of sex-differentiated short- and long-term mating strategies
Short-term mating
Brief sexual relationships without long-term commitment
Life history theory
The study of how organisms allocate effort to growth, reproduction and parenting under different conditions
