Defining Abnormality

Abnormality can be defined by statistical rarity, norm violation, failure to function or deviation from ideal mental health; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction combines biology and values; no single definition is sufficient.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe four definitions of abnormality
  • Apply the definitions to examples of behaviour
  • Evaluate the strengths and limitations of each definition
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Four definitions

  • Statistical infrequency: behaviour is abnormal if it is rare, for example very low or very high scores on a test.
  • Deviation from social norms: behaviour breaks a society's unwritten rules.
  • Failure to function adequately: a person can't cope with daily life, such as work, self-care and relationships; Rosenhan and Seligman (1989) listed signs such as suffering, irrationality and unpredictability.
  • Deviation from ideal mental health: Jahoda (1958) set out criteria for good mental health, such as self-acceptance, autonomy and accurate perception of reality; falling short indicates abnormality.

Harmful dysfunction

Wakefield (1992) proposed that a mental disorder is a harmful dysfunction: a failure of a mental mechanism to perform its natural function (a factual element) that causes harm as judged by social values (a value element). This combines biology and social judgement and explains why some rare traits, such as high intelligence, aren't disorders.

Worked example

Applying the definitions

  1. Very high IQ is statistically rare but not considered a disorder, showing the limits of statistical infrequency.
  2. Behaviour that broke social norms in the past, such as same-sex relationships, was once wrongly classed as disorder.
  3. Someone may function well at work while experiencing severe internal distress, which failure to function might miss.

Evaluation

Each definition captures something important but none is sufficient alone. Statistical definitions ignore desirability; social norm definitions vary across cultures and eras and can be used to control people; failure to function involves subjective judgements; ideal mental health is so demanding that most people would fall short. Clinicians in practice combine distress, impairment and risk.

Common mistakeEquating "rare" with "disordered". Many rare traits are positive or neutral.

In 2 lines: Statistical, social norm, functional and ideal mental health definitions. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction combines facts and values; none is sufficient alone.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Defining abnormality as behaviour that is rare is…
    • Statistical infrequency
    • Deviation from social norms
    • Failure to function
    • Ideal mental health
    Show the answer

    Answer: Statistical infrequency. Defining abnormality by how rare a behaviour or score is, is statistical infrequency.

  2. A person stops washing, misses work and can't keep relationships going. Which definition best applies?
    • Statistical infrequency
    • Failure to function adequately
    • Deviation from ideal mental health
    • Harmful dysfunction only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Failure to function adequately. Being unable to cope with daily life fits this definition.

  3. Why is deviation from social norms a risky definition?
    • Social norms never change over time
    • It applies equally in every culture
    • Norms vary and have been used to control people
    • It ignores behaviour completely
    Show the answer

    Answer: Norms vary and have been used to control people. Same-sex relationships were once wrongly labelled disorder.
