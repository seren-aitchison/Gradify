Environmental Psychology

Natural environments can restore attention and support wellbeing; crowding depends on perception and control; norms and defaults encourage pro-environmental behaviour.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how natural environments can affect wellbeing
  • Describe research on crowding and urban environments
  • Apply psychology to encouraging pro-environmental behaviour

Restorative environments

Kaplan and Kaplan's attention restoration theory (1989) proposes that natural environments restore directed attention, which is depleted by demanding tasks. Ulrich (1984) found surgical patients with a window view of trees had shorter post-operative stays and needed fewer strong painkillers than similar patients facing a brick wall. White et al. (2019) found people who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature reported better health and wellbeing.

Crowding and cities

Density is a physical measure of people per space; crowding is the subjective feeling of too many people. Calhoun's (1962) rat studies of high density reported social breakdown, but human crowding effects depend on control, culture and design. Cities can increase stress through noise and pollution but also offer social contact and opportunities.

Worked example

Pro-environmental behaviour

  1. People often say they care about the environment but don't act accordingly (the attitude–behaviour gap).
  2. Interventions using social norms (telling households how their energy use compares with neighbours') reduced energy use in large-scale studies (Allcott, 2011).
  3. Defaults (such as green energy as the default tariff) and making sustainable options easier increase uptake.

Evaluation

Environmental psychology shows environments shape wellbeing and behaviour, informing urban design, healthcare buildings and climate policy. Much nature–health evidence is correlational, so other factors such as income may explain links. Individual behaviour change matters, but large emissions reductions also require policy and infrastructure.

Common mistakeAssuming awareness of environmental problems leads to action. The attitude–behaviour gap is large.

In 2 lines: Nature can restore attention and support wellbeing; 120 minutes a week was linked to better health. Crowding depends on perception; norms and defaults encourage pro-environmental behaviour.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Patients with a view of trees recovered faster in a study by…
    • John Calhoun
    • Roger Ulrich
    • Hunt Allcott
    • Stephen Kaplan
    Show the answer

    Answer: Roger Ulrich. Ulrich's 1984 study compared window views of trees and a brick wall.

  2. Households receive letters comparing their energy use with neighbours' and use less energy. This uses…
    • Attention restoration
    • Crowding
    • Social norms
    • Density
    Show the answer

    Answer: Social norms. Comparison with neighbours uses descriptive social norms.

  3. Why is nature–health evidence often hard to interpret?
    • It always uses experiments
    • Nature has no effect
    • Income never varies
    • Much of it is correlational
    Show the answer

    Answer: Much of it is correlational. Wealthier people may both live near green space and be healthier.

Key terms

Attention restoration theory
The theory that nature restores depleted directed attention
Crowding
The subjective feeling of too many people in a space
Attitude–behaviour gap
The gap between pro-environmental attitudes and actions
