Quantitative, Qualitative and Mixed Methods

Quantitative and qualitative methods answer different questions; mixing them can combine generality with depth.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish quantitative and qualitative data and research
  • Explain how thematic analysis is carried out
  • Evaluate mixed-methods designs and triangulation

Two kinds of data

  • Quantitative data are numerical: scores, counts, reaction times. Analysed statistically; good for testing hypotheses, comparing groups and generalising.
  • Qualitative data are non-numerical: words, images, observations. Analysed for meaning; good for exploring experiences, generating theory and understanding context.

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) is a widely used qualitative method: become familiar with the data; generate initial codes; search for themes; review themes; define and name them; and write up with illustrative quotes. Researchers reflect on how their own position shapes interpretation (reflexivity).

Worked example

A mixed-methods study of exam stress

  1. Quantitative: a survey of 500 students measures stress scores and links them to exam load.
  2. Qualitative: interviews with 20 students explore what makes exams stressful and what helps.
  3. Triangulation: both point to fear of letting family down, giving more confidence in the finding.

Evaluation

Quantitative research is reliable and generalisable but may miss meaning; qualitative research is rich and valid for experience but harder to generalise and replicate. Mixed methods can offer both, though they require more time and skills, and integrating different kinds of data can be difficult.

Common mistakeTreating qualitative research as unscientific. It follows systematic procedures and has its own quality criteria, such as transparency and reflexivity.

In 2 lines: Quantitative research measures; qualitative research explores meaning; each has strengths. Thematic analysis is a systematic qualitative method; mixed methods combine approaches.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Reaction times in milliseconds are…
    • Quantitative data
    • Qualitative data
    • Thematic data
    • Unstructured data
    Show the answer

    Answer: Quantitative data. Numerical measures such as reaction times are quantitative data.

  2. A researcher wants to understand how people make sense of a new diagnosis. Which approach fits best?
    • A laboratory reaction-time experiment
    • Qualitative interviews analysed thematically
    • A correlational study of two scores
    • A national closed questionnaire only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Qualitative interviews analysed thematically. Exploring meaning and experience suits qualitative methods.

  3. What is the main challenge of mixed-methods research?
    • It can't produce quantitative data
    • It always contradicts itself
    • Integrating different kinds of data
    • It forbids the use of interviews
    Show the answer

    Answer: Integrating different kinds of data. Mixed methods require integration and resources, though they can give fuller answers.

Key terms

Quantitative data
Numerical data that can be analysed statistically
Qualitative data
Non-numerical data such as words or images, analysed for meaning
Triangulation
Using several methods or sources to check whether findings converge
