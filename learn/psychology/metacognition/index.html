Metacognition

Metacognition involves monitoring and controlling learning; feelings of fluency mislead, while self-testing and delayed judgements improve accuracy.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define metacognition and distinguish monitoring from control
  • Explain illusions of competence, including the Dunning–Kruger pattern
  • Apply metacognitive strategies to learning

Monitoring and control

Flavell (1979) introduced metacognition: knowledge and regulation of one's own cognition. Monitoring is judging your own learning or memory: "Do I know this well enough?" (judgements of learning, feeling-of-knowing). Control is acting on those judgements: deciding what to study next, when to stop or which strategy to use (Nelson and Narens, 1990).

Illusions of knowing

Monitoring is often inaccurate. Rereading makes material feel familiar, so learners overestimate how well they'll recall it later (an illusion of competence). Learners also prefer massed practice, which feels easier, over spaced practice, which feels harder but works better; Bjork calls effective but effortful methods desirable difficulties.

Worked example

Overconfidence among low performers

  1. Kruger and Dunning (1999) found students scoring in the bottom quarter on tests of logic and grammar estimated they had scored above average.
  2. Top performers slightly underestimated their relative standing.
  3. Part of the pattern may be a statistical artefact (regression to the mean), and its size is debated, but poor calibration among low performers is often found.

Improving metacognition

Accurate monitoring is improved by delayed judgements (judging learning some time after study, not straight away), self-testing (which shows what you can actually recall) and feedback. Teaching students to plan, monitor and evaluate their learning has been found to improve attainment (Education Endowment Foundation, 2018).

Common mistakeTrusting how easy something feels. Ease during study often predicts learning poorly.

In 2 lines: Metacognition combines monitoring and control of one's own learning. Fluency creates illusions of competence; self-testing and delayed judgements improve calibration.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Judging "I'll remember this tomorrow" is an example of…
    • Metacognitive control
    • Proactive interference
    • Encoding specificity
    • Metacognitive monitoring
    Show the answer

    Answer: Metacognitive monitoring. Predicting later recall is a judgement of learning, a form of monitoring.

  2. A student feels confident after rereading but fails a quiz. What happened?
    • Fluency created an illusion of competence
    • They used spaced practice
    • Their monitoring was accurate
    • They were self-testing
    Show the answer

    Answer: Fluency created an illusion of competence. Familiarity from rereading misled their judgement.

  3. Why is the Dunning–Kruger effect debated?
    • It has never been studied
    • Part may be regression to the mean
    • It shows everyone is accurate
    • It only applies to experts
    Show the answer

    Answer: Part may be regression to the mean. Statistical artefacts can produce a similar pattern.

Key terms

Metacognition
Knowledge and regulation of one's own thinking
Judgement of learning
A prediction of how well something will be remembered later
Desirable difficulty
A learning condition that feels harder but improves long-term retention
