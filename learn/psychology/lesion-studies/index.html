Lesion Studies

Lesion and TMS studies reveal which areas are necessary for functions; double dissociations are strong evidence for separate systems.

Psychology · Biological Psychology and Neuroscience · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe case studies of brain damage and experimental lesion methods
  • Explain single and double dissociations
  • Evaluate the strengths and limits of lesion evidence

Learning from damage

Much early neuroscience came from patients with brain damage from strokes, injuries, tumours or surgery. Broca's patient "Tan" (Leborgne), who could say little but "tan", had damage in the left frontal lobe (1861). HM's amnesia after medial temporal lobe surgery showed the hippocampus's role in memory. Lesion studies show whether an area is necessary for a function, which imaging alone can't.

Dissociations

A single dissociation is when damage impairs one function but not another. A double dissociation is stronger: patient A has impaired function X but not Y, while patient B has impaired Y but not X. Broca's and Wernicke's aphasias form a classic double dissociation between speech production and comprehension, suggesting the functions depend on different systems.

Experimental methods

In animals, researchers can make precise lesions in specific areas. In humans, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) uses a magnetic pulse over the scalp to briefly disrupt activity in a cortical area, creating a temporary "virtual lesion". Pascual-Leone and colleagues used TMS over the visual cortex of blind Braille readers and found it disrupted Braille reading, suggesting the visual cortex had been recruited for touch.

Worked example

Lesion mapping

  1. Modern studies combine many patients' scans and test scores (voxel-based lesion–symptom mapping).
  2. Each brain location is tested for whether damage there predicts poorer performance.
  3. This overcomes some limits of single cases by using larger samples.
Common mistakeAssuming a function "lives" where damage disrupts it. Damage can disrupt connections or other areas too.

In 2 lines: Natural and experimental lesions show which areas are necessary for functions. Double dissociations are strong evidence; TMS creates temporary lesions; damage is rarely tidy.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which method creates a temporary "virtual lesion"?
    • fMRI
    • EEG
    • PET
    • TMS
    Show the answer

    Answer: TMS. TMS briefly disrupts activity in the cortical area under the coil.

  2. Patient A can't recognise faces but recognises objects; patient B recognises faces but not objects. This is…
    • A double dissociation
    • A single dissociation
    • Reverse inference
    • Neurogenesis
    Show the answer

    Answer: A double dissociation. Opposite patterns in two patients form a double dissociation.

  3. Which is the main limitation of natural lesion studies?
    • They use healthy volunteers
    • Damage is rarely confined to one area
    • They are always ethical problems
    • They measure blood flow
    Show the answer

    Answer: Damage is rarely confined to one area. Strokes and injuries damage irregular regions and connections.

Key terms

Double dissociation
Two patients with opposite patterns of impaired and spared functions
Transcranial magnetic stimulation
A method of briefly disrupting cortical activity with magnetic pulses
Virtual lesion
A temporary disruption of a brain area produced by TMS
