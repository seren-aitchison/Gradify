Risk and Protective Factors

Risk factors span individual, family and social levels; ACEs increase population risk; protective factors and resilience (Masten's "ordinary magic") support positive adaptation; ACE scores shouldn't label individuals.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish risk and protective factors
  • Explain research on adverse childhood experiences and resilience
  • Evaluate how risk and protection are used in prevention
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Risk factors

Risk factors operate at individual (genetic vulnerability, physical illness), family (conflict, parental mental illness), and social levels (poverty, discrimination, unemployment). Felitti et al. (1998) studied adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), such as abuse, neglect and household dysfunction, and found that the more ACEs people reported, the higher their risk of later health problems, including depression. The Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey found higher rates of common mental health conditions among people in deprived areas and with problem debt in 2023/4.

Protective factors and resilience

Protective factors include supportive relationships, problem-solving skills, a sense of control, community connection and access to services. Masten (2001) described resilience as "ordinary magic": positive adaptation despite adversity arises from common human resources, such as attachment, thinking skills and supportive communities, rather than rare qualities.

Worked example

Using ACE scores carefully

  1. ACE scores predict risk at the population level, but they can't predict what will happen to an individual.
  2. Many people with several ACEs do well, especially with protective relationships.
  3. Experts caution against using ACE scores to label or screen individual children.

Evaluation

Risk and protective factor research supports prevention, such as parenting support and reducing poverty. But factors are often correlated with each other, making causes hard to separate, and focusing on individual risk can neglect structural causes. Resilience research highlights strengths rather than only deficits.

Common mistakeAssuming adverse experiences determine outcomes. Many people do well with protective factors.

In 2 lines: Risk factors operate at individual, family and social levels; ACEs raise risk. Protective factors and resilience; use ACE scores cautiously.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Positive adaptation despite adversity is called…
    • Vulnerability
    • Comorbidity
    • Resilience
    • Diathesis
    Show the answer

    Answer: Resilience. Resilience describes doing well despite adversity.

  2. A young person who grew up in a difficult home does well at school thanks to a supportive grandparent and teacher. This shows…
    • The effect of risk factors only
    • Learned helplessness in school
    • Inflated responsibility for others
    • Protective factors supporting resilience
    Show the answer

    Answer: Protective factors supporting resilience. Supportive relationships with a grandparent and teacher are protective factors.

  3. Why can focusing on individual risk factors be limited?
    • It may neglect structural causes such as poverty
    • Individual factors never matter
    • Structure has no influence
    • Risk factors are always independent
    Show the answer

    Answer: It may neglect structural causes such as poverty. Social conditions such as poverty and discrimination shape many individual risks.

Key terms

Adverse childhood experiences
Stressful or traumatic events in childhood, such as abuse or neglect
Resilience
Positive adaptation despite adversity
Protective factor
Something that reduces the risk of difficulties
