Attitude Formation

Attitudes have affective, behavioural and cognitive parts, form through exposure, conditioning and learning, and predict behaviour under specific conditions.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the ABC model of attitudes
  • Explain how attitudes form through exposure, conditioning and social learning
  • Evaluate the link between attitudes and behaviour

What is an attitude?

The ABC model describes three components: affective (feelings: "I love dogs"), behavioural (actions or intentions: "I walk dogs at the shelter") and cognitive (beliefs: "dogs are loyal"). Attitudes can be explicit (consciously reported) or implicit (automatic associations). The Implicit Association Test (IAT) measures implicit attitudes, though its reliability for individuals and its ability to predict behaviour are debated.

How attitudes form

  • Mere exposure: repeated exposure increases liking. Zajonc (1968) found people rated nonsense words and unfamiliar symbols more positively the more often they had seen them.
  • Classical conditioning: pairing an object with pleasant or unpleasant experiences, as in advertising that links products with attractive images.
  • Operant conditioning: expressing attitudes that are rewarded.
  • Social learning: copying attitudes of parents, peers and media.
  • Direct experience: attitudes formed through experience tend to be stronger.

Do attitudes predict behaviour?

Worked example

LaPiere's travels

  1. In the early 1930s LaPiere travelled around the US with a Chinese couple, at a time of strong anti-Chinese prejudice. They were refused service at only 1 of over 250 establishments.
  2. When LaPiere later wrote asking whether these establishments would serve Chinese guests, over 90% of those replying said no.
  3. Stated attitudes and behaviour didn't match (LaPiere, 1934), though the people replying may not have been the people who served them.

Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour (1991) proposes that behaviour is predicted by intentions, which depend on attitudes, subjective norms (what others think) and perceived behavioural control. Attitudes predict behaviour better when they are strong, specific to the behaviour and formed by direct experience.

Common mistakeAssuming attitudes always predict behaviour. Norms, control and the situation also matter.

In 2 lines: Attitudes have affective, behavioural and cognitive components and can be explicit or implicit. They form through exposure, conditioning and social learning; they predict behaviour best when strong and specific.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Liking a song more after hearing it many times is the…
    • Halo effect
    • Self-serving bias
    • Mere exposure effect
    • Primacy effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Mere exposure effect. Repeated exposure tends to increase liking: the mere exposure effect.

  2. An advert shows a drink alongside happy friends on a beach. Which process is it using?
    • Operant conditioning
    • Mere exposure only
    • Cognitive dissonance
    • Classical conditioning
    Show the answer

    Answer: Classical conditioning. Pairing the product with pleasant feelings conditions an attitude.

  3. Which is a key limitation of LaPiere's study?
    • Letter writers may not have served the couple
    • It used too many establishments
    • It measured behaviour only
    • It found attitudes matched behaviour
    Show the answer

    Answer: Letter writers may not have served the couple. Different staff may have replied and served.
