Psychological Testing in Clinical Practice

Clinical tests include cognitive tests, symptom measures and personality measures; good tests are reliable, valid and standardised with appropriate norms; scores support but don't replace clinical judgement.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe types of psychological tests used clinically
  • Explain reliability, validity and norms in testing
  • Evaluate the use and limitations of tests in practice
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or therapy. Clinical psychological testing should only be carried out by qualified, registered professionals.

Types of tests

  • Cognitive tests: intelligence and neuropsychological tests of memory, attention and executive function, used for example in assessing brain injury or dementia.
  • Symptom measures: questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, used to screen and track change.
  • Personality measures: structured inventories of traits.

Many tests are restricted to qualified users because of the skill needed to administer and interpret them.

Principles of measurement

Good tests are reliable (consistent across time and raters), valid (measuring what they claim to) and standardised (administered and scored the same way). Scores are interpreted against norms: results from a large reference group, which must be appropriate for the person's age, language and culture. The PHQ-9 was validated against clinical interviews (Kroenke et al., 2001).

Worked example

Interpreting scores carefully

  1. A PHQ-9 score suggests the severity of depressive symptoms over the last two weeks.
  2. It doesn't by itself diagnose depression; a clinician considers it alongside an interview.
  3. Scores can change with circumstances, so repeated measurement shows change over time.

Evaluation

Tests add objectivity and allow progress to be tracked. But tests developed in one population may be biased for others, scores can be over-interpreted, and testing can feel reductive. Results should be one part of a broader assessment and explained clearly to the person.

Common mistakeTreating a questionnaire score as a diagnosis. Scores support clinical judgement.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Administering and scoring a test the same way every time is…
    • Validity
    • Formulation
    • Standardisation
    • Insight
    Show the answer

    Answer: Standardisation. Standardisation means every person takes the test under the same conditions and scoring.

  2. A memory test normed on young adults is used to assess an 85-year-old. The main concern is…
    • Too much standardisation
    • High reliability
    • Excessive validity
    • Inappropriate norms
    Show the answer

    Answer: Inappropriate norms. Norms must match the person's age group, or the comparison will mislead.

  3. Why can psychological tests be biased?
    • Tests made in one population may not suit others
    • Tests are completely culture-free
    • All norms fit everyone equally well
    • Test scores never vary at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: Tests made in one population may not suit others. Development samples shape what tests measure.

Key terms

Norms
Scores from a reference group used to interpret results
Standardisation
Administering and scoring a test in the same way every time
Neuropsychological test
A test of specific cognitive functions such as memory
