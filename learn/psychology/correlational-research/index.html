Correlational Research

Correlations show whether and how strongly variables are related, but not why: direction and third variables block causal conclusions.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe positive, negative and zero correlations and the correlation coefficient
  • Explain why correlation doesn't establish causation, including the third-variable and direction problems
  • Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of correlational research

Measuring relationships

A correlation describes the relationship between two co-variables. In a positive correlation, both increase together (height and weight). In a negative correlation, one increases as the other decreases (absences and exam scores). A zero correlation means no linear relationship. The correlation coefficient (r) ranges from −1 to +1; its size shows strength and its sign shows direction. Scatterplots display the pattern.

Correlation is not causation

  • Direction problem: does A cause B, or B cause A? (Does stress cause poor sleep, or poor sleep cause stress?)
  • Third-variable problem: a third variable C may cause both. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer because of hot weather.
Worked example

Screen time and wellbeing

  1. Large surveys find a small negative correlation between teenagers' screen time and wellbeing.
  2. Direction: unhappy teens may use screens more.
  3. Third variables: family circumstances or loneliness could affect both.
  4. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to test causal claims.

Evaluation

Strengths: can study variables that can't be manipulated (smoking, trauma); large samples; reveals relationships worth testing experimentally. Weaknesses: no causal conclusions; only detects linear relationships; can be distorted by outliers (Anscombe's quartet).

Common mistakeThinking a strong correlation means causation, or that a weak one means no relationship. Strength, direction and cause are separate questions.

In 2 lines: Correlations can be positive, negative or zero; r gives strength and direction. Direction and third-variable problems mean correlation doesn't show causation.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. As hours of revision increase, exam scores increase. This is a…
    • Positive correlation
    • Negative correlation
    • Zero correlation
    • Causal experiment
    Show the answer

    Answer: Positive correlation. Both variables increase together, so the relationship is positive.

  2. Cities with more police officers have more crime. What is the most likely explanation?
    • Police cause crime
    • Larger or higher-crime cities employ more police
    • Crime and police are unrelated
    • Crime causes police to be less effective
    Show the answer

    Answer: Larger or higher-crime cities employ more police. The direction problem and third variables (city size) explain the correlation.

  3. Which design would best test whether screen time causes lower wellbeing?
    • A larger correlational survey of screen time
    • A single case study of one teenager
    • Randomly assigning people to reduce screen time and measuring wellbeing
    • Asking teenagers whether screens make them unhappy
    Show the answer

    Answer: Randomly assigning people to reduce screen time and measuring wellbeing. Manipulating screen time with random allocation allows causal inference.

Key terms

Correlation coefficient
A number from −1 to +1 showing the strength and direction of a linear relationship
Third-variable problem
When a third variable causes both correlated variables
Co-variables
The two variables measured in a correlational study
