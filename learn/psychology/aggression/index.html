Aggression

Aggression has biological, social learning and situational causes; media effects on aggression exist but are debated in size and meaning.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish types of aggression
  • Explain biological and social learning explanations of aggression
  • Evaluate the frustration–aggression hypothesis and media violence research

Types

Hostile (reactive) aggression is driven by anger and aims to cause harm. Instrumental (proactive) aggression uses harm as a means to another goal, such as robbery. Aggression can be physical, verbal or relational (damaging relationships, such as spreading rumours).

Biological factors

Brain regions involved include the amygdala (threat responses) and the prefrontal cortex (impulse control). Low serotonin activity has been linked to impulsive aggression. Testosterone is associated with dominance and aggression, though links in humans are modest and depend on context. Twin studies suggest aggressive behaviour is moderately heritable.

Social learning

Worked example

The Bobo doll

  1. Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961) had children aged 3–6 watch an adult model either attack an inflatable Bobo doll or play quietly.
  2. Children who saw the aggressive model were more likely to imitate specific aggressive acts, including novel ones such as hitting the doll with a mallet.
  3. Later studies found imitation was more likely when the model was rewarded (Bandura, 1965).

Situations and media

The frustration–aggression hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939) proposed that frustration always leads to aggression. Berkowitz (1989) revised it: frustration produces negative feelings that make aggression more likely, especially with aggressive cues present. Heat, crowding, alcohol and provocation increase aggression. On violent video games, an APA task force (2015) found an association with increased aggressive behaviour, but other researchers argue effects are very small and don't support links to serious violence (Ferguson, 2015).

Common mistakeEquating aggression in lab tasks with real violence. Lab measures, such as noise blasts, are mild proxies.

In 2 lines: Aggression may be hostile or instrumental, physical, verbal or relational. Biology, social learning and situations contribute; media effects are debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Spreading rumours to damage someone's friendships is…
    • Instrumental aggression only
    • Prosocial behaviour
    • Relational aggression
    • Displacement
    Show the answer

    Answer: Relational aggression. Damaging relationships is relational aggression.

  2. A driver stuck in traffic on a hot day shouts at another driver. Which explanation fits best?
    • Kin selection in traffic
    • Reciprocal altruism
    • Social loafing on the road
    • Frustration and heat
    Show the answer

    Answer: Frustration and heat. Frustration and heat increase the likelihood of aggression.

  3. Why are lab measures of aggression criticised?
    • They are mild proxies
    • They are far too violent
    • They only ever use adults
    • They are never replicated
    Show the answer

    Answer: They are mild proxies. Noise blasts or doll hitting differ from real-world violence.

Key terms

Hostile aggression
Aggression driven by anger and aimed at harm
Instrumental aggression
Aggression used to achieve another goal
Frustration–aggression hypothesis
The proposal that frustration increases the likelihood of aggression
