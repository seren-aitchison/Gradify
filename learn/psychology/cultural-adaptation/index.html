Cultural Adaptation

Culture shock involves disorientation in a new culture; the U-curve doesn't fit everyone; Ward distinguished psychological and sociocultural adaptation; support and skills aid adjustment.

Psychology · Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe culture shock and the U-curve model
  • Distinguish psychological and sociocultural adaptation
  • Evaluate factors that support cultural adaptation

Culture shock

Oberg (1960) described culture shock: the anxiety and disorientation people experience when familiar cues and routines no longer work in a new culture. Lysgaard (1955) proposed a U-curve of adjustment: an initial "honeymoon" phase, a dip as difficulties mount, then recovery and adjustment. Later research found the U-curve doesn't fit many people: difficulties are often greatest at the start and then decline.

Two kinds of adaptation

Ward and colleagues distinguished psychological adaptation (well-being and mental health) from sociocultural adaptation (the ability to "fit in" and manage daily life, such as using public transport, making friends and understanding norms). Ward and Kennedy (1999) developed a scale to measure sociocultural adaptation. Sociocultural adaptation tends to improve steadily with time and contact, while psychological adaptation depends more on stress, coping and support.

Worked example

International students

  1. An international student may feel lonely and anxious in the first months while learning new academic and social norms.
  2. Joining societies, finding friends from both home and host cultures and using university support can improve adaptation.
  3. Language skills and previous experience abroad are linked to easier sociocultural adaptation. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Research shows adaptation is not a fixed sequence but depends on personal resources, social support, language skills, cultural distance and the receiving society's welcome. Much research uses student samples, which may not represent refugees or labour migrants facing very different pressures.

Common mistakeAssuming everyone follows the same adjustment curve. Experiences vary widely.

In 2 lines: Culture shock and the U-curve; many people don't follow the curve. Psychological and sociocultural adaptation differ; support helps.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The idea that adjustment goes from honeymoon to dip to recovery is the…
    • Acculturation gap
    • In-group advantage
    • U-curve model
    • Carpentered world
    Show the answer

    Answer: U-curve model. Lysgaard proposed the U-curve of honeymoon, dip and recovery in 1955.

  2. A new arrival quickly learns to use public transport and shops confidently, but still feels lonely and low. This shows…
    • Better psychological than sociocultural adaptation
    • Complete assimilation
    • Marginalisation
    • Better sociocultural than psychological adaptation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Better sociocultural than psychological adaptation. Practical skills can improve before well-being does.

  3. Why may student-based adaptation research not generalise?
    • Refugees and labour migrants face different pressures
    • Students represent all migrants equally
    • Adaptation never varies between people
    • Students never move abroad to study
    Show the answer

    Answer: Refugees and labour migrants face different pressures. Sample characteristics limit generalisation.

Key terms

Culture shock
Anxiety and disorientation in an unfamiliar culture
Sociocultural adaptation
The ability to fit in and manage daily life in a new culture
Psychological adaptation
Well-being and mental health during cultural transition
