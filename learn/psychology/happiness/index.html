Happiness

Income, relationships and genes predict happiness; people adapt to many changes but not all; claims about the share of happiness under control are overstated.

Psychology · Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe evidence on the links between income, relationships and happiness
  • Explain hedonic adaptation and set-point theory
  • Evaluate claims about how much happiness is under personal control

Money and happiness

Easterlin (1974) found that while richer people within a country were happier, average happiness didn't rise much as countries got richer over time (the Easterlin paradox). Kahneman and Deaton (2010) found life evaluation rose with income, while daily emotional wellbeing levelled off above about $75,000 a year in the US. Killingsworth (2021) later found experienced wellbeing kept rising with income in a larger sample, and a joint study (Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers, 2023) found the plateau applied mainly to the least happy people.

Relationships

Strong relationships are among the most consistent predictors of happiness. Diener and Seligman (2002) found the happiest 10% of students all had strong social relationships. Loneliness is associated with poorer wellbeing and health.

Worked example

Lottery winners and adaptation

  1. Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman (1978) compared lottery winners, people who had become paralysed in accidents and controls.
  2. Lottery winners weren't significantly happier than controls; the accident victims were less happy, but less unhappy than expected.
  3. This was taken as evidence of hedonic adaptation: people return towards a baseline after life changes.

How changeable is happiness?

Set-point theory proposes each person has a baseline level of happiness, partly genetic: twin studies suggest heritability of wellbeing of around 30–40%. Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade (2005) popularised a "pie chart" claiming 40% of happiness is under intentional control; Brown and Rohrer (2020) showed this figure isn't supported by the underlying data. Long-term studies show some life events, such as disability and unemployment, can change happiness lastingly (Lucas, 2007).

Common mistakeRepeating that "40% of happiness is under your control". The figure doesn't follow from the evidence.

In 2 lines: Income, relationships and genes predict happiness; the income plateau is debated. Hedonic adaptation is common but incomplete; the 40% claim is unsupported.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which factor is most consistently linked to happiness?
    • Owning a new car
    • Living in a big city
    • Having a university degree
    • Strong social relationships
    Show the answer

    Answer: Strong social relationships. Relationships are a consistent predictor.

  2. Lottery winners weren't much happier than controls after a while. This supports…
    • Hedonic adaptation
    • The Easterlin paradox only
    • Flow
    • Eudaimonic wellbeing
    Show the answer

    Answer: Hedonic adaptation. Winners adapted back towards their baseline happiness: hedonic adaptation.

  3. What did Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers conclude in 2023?
    • Money never matters
    • The plateau was in the least happy
    • Happiness falls with income
    • Everyone plateaus at $75,000
    Show the answer

    Answer: The plateau was in the least happy. Their joint study resolved the earlier conflict.

Key terms

Easterlin paradox
Average happiness not rising much as countries grow richer over time
Hedonic adaptation
Returning towards a baseline level of happiness after life changes
Set-point theory
The idea that people have a stable baseline level of happiness
