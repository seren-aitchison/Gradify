Psychopharmacology

Drugs alter neurotransmission in specific ways; tolerance and dependence follow repeated use; RCTs test effectiveness.

Psychology · Biological Psychology and Neuroscience · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how psychoactive drugs act on neurotransmission
  • Describe tolerance, withdrawal and dependence
  • Evaluate evidence from randomised controlled trials of psychiatric drugs

How drugs act

Psychoactive drugs change synaptic transmission. They may increase transmitter release (amphetamine releases dopamine), block reuptake (cocaine, SSRIs), mimic transmitters (opioids at opioid receptors), block receptors (antipsychotics at D2 receptors) or enhance receptor function (alcohol and benzodiazepines at GABA-A receptors). Broad classes include stimulants, depressants, opioids and hallucinogens.

Tolerance and dependence

With repeated use, tolerance develops: a larger dose is needed for the same effect, often because receptors become less sensitive or fewer. Stopping can then produce withdrawal symptoms, often the opposite of the drug's effects. Dependence involves compulsive use despite harm. Many drugs of misuse increase dopamine in the brain's reward pathway, from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens.

Worked example

Testing a drug

  1. In a randomised controlled trial (RCT), participants are randomly assigned to the drug or a placebo (an inactive pill).
  2. In a double-blind design, neither participants nor researchers know who receives which, reducing expectation effects.
  3. A meta-analysis of 522 trials found all 21 antidepressants studied were more effective than placebo for adults with depression, with mostly modest average effects (Cipriani et al., 2018).

Chemical imbalance?

A popular idea is that depression is caused by low serotonin. A 2022 umbrella review found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity (Moncrieff et al., 2022). This doesn't mean antidepressants don't work, but how they work is more complex than "correcting an imbalance". Decisions about medication belong with qualified prescribers and patients.

Investigate with the lab. Release a vesicle at the cholinergic synapse, then add an agonist and an antagonist and compare the effect on the receptor.

Common mistakeConcluding that a drug works because it fixes a single chemical imbalance. Mechanisms are usually more complex.

In 2 lines: Drugs act by altering release, reuptake or receptor activity. Tolerance, withdrawal and dependence follow repeated use; RCTs test drugs; simple imbalance theories are weak.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Cocaine increases dopamine at synapses mainly by…
    • Blocking receptors
    • Destroying vesicles
    • Blocking reuptake
    • Producing melatonin
    Show the answer

    Answer: Blocking reuptake. Cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake, so dopamine stays in the cleft longer.

  2. A trial gives half the participants a sugar pill. What is the purpose?
    • To test sugar
    • To increase side effects
    • To reduce sample size
    • To control for placebo effects
    Show the answer

    Answer: To control for placebo effects. Comparing with placebo separates drug effects from expectation.

  3. What did Moncrieff et al.'s 2022 review conclude?
    • No consistent evidence low serotonin causes depression
    • Antidepressants never work
    • Depression has no biology
    • Serotonin is not a neurotransmitter
    Show the answer

    Answer: No consistent evidence low serotonin causes depression. The review questioned the serotonin deficiency theory, not drug effectiveness.

  4. What does an antagonist drug do at a receptor?
    • Activates the receptor like the neurotransmitter
    • Blocks the receptor without activating it
    • Speeds up neurotransmitter release
    • Breaks down the neurotransmitter
    Show the answer

    Answer: Blocks the receptor without activating it. An antagonist binds but doesn't activate, blocking the natural transmitter.

Key terms

Tolerance
Needing more of a drug for the same effect after repeated use
Placebo
An inactive treatment used as a comparison
Double-blind
A design where neither participants nor researchers know who receives which treatment
