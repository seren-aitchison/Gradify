Vicarious Reinforcement

Seeing others rewarded or punished changes whether we perform a behaviour, but not whether we learn it.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define vicarious reinforcement and vicarious punishment
  • Explain Bandura's (1965) study and the learning–performance distinction
  • Evaluate the role of vicarious consequences in real-world behaviour

Learning from others' consequences

Vicarious reinforcement occurs when an observer sees a model rewarded for a behaviour and becomes more likely to perform it. Vicarious punishment occurs when seeing a model punished makes the observer less likely to perform it.

Bandura (1965)

Children watched a film of an adult behaving aggressively towards a Bobo doll. In one condition the model was rewarded, in another punished, and in a third there were no consequences. Children who saw the model punished imitated less aggression. But when all children were then offered rewards for copying the model, the groups' imitation was similar. The children in the punished condition had learned the behaviour just as well; they had simply chosen not to perform it.

Vicarious consequences help explain why social norms spread: people watch what others are rewarded or criticised for, at school, at work and online.

Worked example

The learning–performance distinction at work

  1. Pupils see a classmate punished for copying homework.
  2. Most stop copying (vicarious punishment), but they have still learned how to copy.
  3. If they expect not to be caught, some may copy later.
Common mistakeThinking vicarious punishment prevents learning. It reduces performance; the behaviour can still be learned.

In 2 lines: Seeing a model rewarded or punished changes the observer's likelihood of performing a behaviour. Bandura (1965) showed punishment reduces performance, not learning.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Seeing a classmate praised for helping, a pupil starts helping too. This is…
    • Vicarious punishment
    • Negative reinforcement
    • Vicarious reinforcement
    • Habituation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Vicarious reinforcement. The pupil's behaviour increased after seeing a model rewarded.

  2. In Bandura (1965), what happened when children in all groups were offered rewards to copy the model?
    • Only the rewarded group could imitate
    • Nobody imitated
    • The punished group imitated most
    • Imitation was similar across groups
    Show the answer

    Answer: Imitation was similar across groups. Rewards revealed that all groups had learned the behaviour; consequences had affected performance.

  3. What does Bandura's (1965) study imply about deterring crime by publicising punishment?
    • It may reduce offending but not learning
    • It will stop people learning about crime
    • It has no effect on behaviour
    • It makes crime more rewarding
    Show the answer

    Answer: It may reduce offending but not learning. Vicarious punishment affects performance, not learning, so effects depend on expected consequences.

Key terms

Vicarious reinforcement
Increased likelihood of a behaviour after seeing a model rewarded for it
Vicarious punishment
Decreased likelihood of a behaviour after seeing a model punished for it
Learning–performance distinction
The difference between acquiring a behaviour and choosing to perform it
