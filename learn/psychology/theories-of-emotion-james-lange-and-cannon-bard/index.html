Theories of Emotion: James–Lange and Cannon–Bard

James–Lange says perceived bodily changes are the emotion; Cannon–Bard says feeling and arousal occur simultaneously; evidence suggests bodily feedback contributes but isn't the whole story.

Psychology · Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the James–Lange theory of emotion
  • Describe the Cannon–Bard theory and Cannon's criticisms of James–Lange
  • Evaluate both theories using later evidence

James–Lange

James (1884) and Lange (1885) proposed that an emotional stimulus triggers bodily changes (heart racing, trembling), and the perception of these changes is the emotion. In James's example, we don't run from a bear because we are afraid; we are afraid because we run. Different emotions should therefore involve different patterns of bodily response.

Cannon–Bard

Cannon (1927) criticised James–Lange: bodily changes are too slow to explain rapid emotions; similar arousal occurs in different emotions (fear and anger both raise heart rate); and artificially inducing arousal (with adrenaline injections) doesn't reliably produce emotion. The Cannon–Bard theory proposed that the thalamus sends signals simultaneously to the cortex (producing the feeling) and to the body (producing arousal), so feeling and arousal occur together but independently.

Worked example

Facial feedback

  1. Strack, Martin and Stepper (1988) had participants hold a pen in their teeth (forcing a smile-like expression) or lips (preventing smiling) while rating cartoons.
  2. The "smiling" group rated cartoons as funnier, supporting the idea that bodily states can influence emotion.
  3. A large multi-lab replication found no significant effect (Wagenmakers et al., 2016), though later studies using different methods found small effects (Coles et al., 2022).

Evaluation

Later research found some distinct physiological patterns for different emotions (Levenson, 1992), partly supporting James–Lange, but much overlap. People with spinal cord injuries, who receive less bodily feedback, still feel emotions, though some report reduced intensity. Modern views see bodily feedback as contributing to emotion rather than being sufficient or irrelevant.

Common mistakeThinking either theory has been fully confirmed. Each captures part of a more complex process.

In 2 lines: James–Lange: bodily changes come first and are felt as emotion. Cannon–Bard: feeling and arousal occur together; facial feedback evidence is mixed.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. "We are afraid because we run" summarises which theory?
    • James–Lange
    • Cannon–Bard
    • Two-factor
    • Appraisal
    Show the answer

    Answer: James–Lange. Bodily response comes first in James–Lange.

  2. Fear and anger both raise heart rate similarly. This supports which criticism?
    • Bodily changes are too fast
    • Arousal is too similar across emotions
    • The thalamus is irrelevant
    • Emotions have no physiology
    Show the answer

    Answer: Arousal is too similar across emotions. Similar arousal can't distinguish emotions alone.

  3. What does emotion in people with spinal cord injury suggest?
    • Emotion needs full bodily feedback
    • Emotions disappear after injury
    • Bodily feedback isn't necessary for emotion
    • The cortex is irrelevant
    Show the answer

    Answer: Bodily feedback isn't necessary for emotion. Emotions persist, though some report reduced intensity.

Key terms

James–Lange theory
The theory that emotion is the perception of bodily changes
Cannon–Bard theory
The theory that emotional feeling and arousal occur simultaneously and independently
Facial feedback hypothesis
The idea that facial expressions influence emotional experience
