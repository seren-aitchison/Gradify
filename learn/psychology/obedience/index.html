Obedience

Many people obey authority to harmful extremes in some situations; proximity, legitimacy and identification shape obedience.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe Milgram's procedure, findings and variations
  • Explain situational explanations of obedience such as the agentic state and legitimacy of authority
  • Evaluate Milgram's research, including ethics and later reinterpretations

Milgram's study

Worked example

The "teacher" and the "learner"

  1. Milgram (1963) recruited 40 men by newspaper advert for a "study of memory" at Yale University.
  2. Each participant was the "teacher" and gave what he believed were electric shocks to a "learner" (a confederate) for wrong answers, rising in 15-volt steps to 450 volts.
  3. All 40 went to at least 300 volts, and 26 (65%) went to the maximum 450 volts, despite protests from the learner and visible distress. The shocks weren't real.

Variations

Milgram (1974) ran many variations. Obedience fell when the experiment moved to a run-down office building (48%), when the learner was in the same room (40%), when the teacher had to place the learner's hand on a shock plate (30%), and when orders came by telephone (about 21%). When two other "teachers" refused, obedience fell to 10%.

Explanations

Milgram proposed the agentic state: people see themselves as acting on behalf of an authority and so shift responsibility to it, as opposed to an autonomous state. Legitimacy of authority (the prestige of Yale and the experimenter's lab coat) and gradual commitment (small steps of 15 volts) also mattered. An alternative is engaged followership: Haslam and Reicher (2012) argue participants continued because they identified with the scientific goals, not simply because they obeyed; direct orders ("you have no other choice, you must go on") tended to produce refusal.

Ethics and replications

Baumrind (1964) criticised the deception and distress caused. Milgram argued debriefing was thorough and most participants were glad to have taken part. Burger (2009) ran a partial replication, stopping at 150 volts for ethical reasons, and found obedience rates only slightly lower than Milgram's at that point.

Common mistakeConcluding that people are simply "evil" or "sheep". Variations show obedience depends heavily on the situation.

In 2 lines: Milgram found 65% obeyed to 450 V; variations show strong situational effects. Explanations include the agentic state, legitimacy and engaged followership; ethics remain debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. In Milgram's original study, how many of 40 participants went to 450 V?
    • 4
    • 14
    • 26
    • 40
    Show the answer

    Answer: 26. 26 of 40, or 65%, gave the maximum shock.

  2. Obedience fell when orders were given by telephone. Which factor explains this?
    • Increased legitimacy
    • A larger group
    • More money
    • Reduced presence of authority
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reduced presence of authority. Authority at a distance has less influence.

  3. How do Haslam and Reicher reinterpret Milgram's findings?
    • Participants identified with the science
    • Participants were sadistic
    • Orders always worked
    • Authority played no role
    Show the answer

    Answer: Participants identified with the science. Engaged followership stresses identification with the goals.

Key terms

Agentic state
Seeing oneself as acting for an authority and not responsible
Legitimacy of authority
The perceived right of an authority figure to give orders
Engaged followership
Continuing because one identifies with the authority's goals
