Auditory Perception

Hearing transduces vibrations in the cochlea; pitch depends on place and timing, location on differences between the ears, and speech perception integrates vision.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe how the ear transduces sound
  • Explain how pitch and sound location are perceived
  • Evaluate evidence that hearing interacts with vision, such as the McGurk effect

From sound waves to signals

Sound waves are funnelled by the outer ear to the eardrum, amplified by the bones of the middle ear and transmitted to the fluid-filled cochlea. There, hair cells on the basilar membrane bend and transduce vibrations into neural signals sent via the auditory nerve to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Frequency is heard as pitch; amplitude as loudness.

Pitch and location

Place theory holds that different frequencies stimulate different places along the basilar membrane (high near the base, low near the tip). Temporal theory holds that neurones fire in time with low-frequency sounds. Both contribute. Sounds are located using differences in timing between the two ears (interaural time differences) and differences in intensity (interaural level differences), plus filtering by the shape of the outer ear.

Worked example

The McGurk effect

  1. Participants watch a video of a face saying "ga" while hearing "ba".
  2. Many report hearing "da", a fusion of what they see and hear (McGurk and MacDonald, 1976).
  3. Speech perception integrates vision and hearing automatically.

Hearing loss is common. Conductive loss involves the outer or middle ear and can often be treated; sensorineural loss involves hair cells or the auditory nerve, often from age or noise exposure. Cochlear implants stimulate the auditory nerve directly and can restore useful hearing to some deaf people.

Common mistakeThinking the ear "hears". The cochlea transduces sound; the brain constructs auditory experience.

In 2 lines: The cochlea transduces sound; pitch and loudness follow frequency and amplitude. Location uses differences between the ears; speech perception integrates sight and sound.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The perceived pitch of a sound mainly corresponds to its…
    • Amplitude
    • Frequency
    • Duration
    • Location
    Show the answer

    Answer: Frequency. Frequency is heard as pitch; amplitude as loudness.

  2. A person hears "da" when watching lips say "ga" while the audio says "ba". This shows…
    • Hearing loss
    • Place theory failure
    • Audiovisual integration
    • Sensory adaptation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Audiovisual integration. The McGurk effect shows vision changes what is heard.

  3. Why is it harder to tell whether a sound is in front or behind than left or right?
    • The ears can't detect timing
    • Only vision locates sounds
    • Sounds behind are always quieter
    • Interaural cues are similar
    Show the answer

    Answer: Interaural cues are similar. Similar interaural cues create front–back confusion, resolved partly by outer-ear filtering and head movement.

Key terms

Cochlea
The fluid-filled inner-ear structure where sound is transduced
Place theory
The theory that pitch depends on where the basilar membrane vibrates most
Interaural time difference
The difference in arrival time of a sound at the two ears, used to locate it
