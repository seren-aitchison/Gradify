Open Science and the Replication Crisis

The Open Science Collaboration found 36% of 100 studies replicated significantly; questionable research practices and publication bias contributed; preregistration, Registered Reports and data sharing are key reforms.

Psychology · Contemporary Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe evidence of the replication crisis
  • Explain questionable research practices that contributed to it
  • Evaluate open science reforms

The crisis

The Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 studies from three psychology journals. While 97% of the original studies reported significant results, only 36% of the replications did, and replication effect sizes were on average about half the original size. Camerer et al. (2018) replicated 21 social science experiments published in Nature and Science and found significant effects in 13.

Causes

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed how questionable research practices, such as collecting more data until results are significant, trying many analyses, or reporting only some measures, can make false positives very likely. Publication bias favours significant results, and small samples produce unstable, inflated effects. Incentives rewarded novel, positive findings over careful replication.

Worked example

Open science reforms

  1. Preregistration records hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection.
  2. Registered Reports, introduced at the journal Cortex in 2013, are peer reviewed before results are known, and accepted regardless of outcome.
  3. Data and materials sharing and large multi-site studies improve transparency and power (Munafò et al., 2017).

Evaluation

The crisis prompted major reforms that are improving reliability. Some argue failures to replicate partly reflect differences in context or methods. Reforms can add workload and don't suit every kind of research, such as exploratory or qualitative work. Psychology is now a leader in developing open science practices.

Common mistakeConcluding that psychology is unreliable as a whole. Many findings replicate, and reforms are improving the field.

In 2 lines: 36% of 100 studies replicated significantly; effect sizes halved. QRPs and publication bias contributed; preregistration and Registered Reports respond.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Recording hypotheses and analysis plans before collecting data is…
    • Publication bias
    • p-hacking
    • Preregistration
    • Meta-analysis
    Show the answer

    Answer: Preregistration. Preregistration limits flexible analysis.

  2. A researcher keeps adding participants and testing until p falls below .05, then stops. This is…
    • Preregistration of analyses
    • A Registered Report
    • A direct replication
    • A questionable research practice
    Show the answer

    Answer: A questionable research practice. Optional stopping inflates false positives.

  3. Why might some replication failures not mean the original was false?
    • Differences in context or methods may matter
    • Replications are always flawed
    • Originals are always right
    • Context never matters
    Show the answer

    Answer: Differences in context or methods may matter. Hidden moderators, such as differences in context or samples, can change results.

Key terms

Questionable research practices
Analytic choices that inflate false-positive rates
Preregistration
Recording hypotheses and analyses before collecting data
Registered Report
A study peer reviewed and accepted before results are known
