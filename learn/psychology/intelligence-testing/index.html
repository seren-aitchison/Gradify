Intelligence Testing

IQ tests are reliable and predictive within limits, but scores reflect environment as well as ability, and tests have a history of harmful misuse.

Psychology · Cognitive Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe how IQ tests are constructed, standardised and scored
  • Explain the reliability, validity and predictive power of IQ tests
  • Evaluate controversies in intelligence testing, including bias and misuse

Building a test

Binet and Simon (1905) created the first practical intelligence test to identify French schoolchildren needing extra support. Modern tests such as the Wechsler scales (WAIS for adults, WISC for children) include subtests of verbal comprehension, perceptual reasoning, working memory and processing speed.

Tests are standardised on large representative samples. Scores are converted to a scale with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, so about 68% of people score between 85 and 115 and about 95% between 70 and 130.

What tests predict

IQ tests are highly reliable (test–retest correlations often above 0.9). They correlate moderately with school achievement and job performance, and with later health and lifespan (Deary, 2012). They don't measure everything valued as "intelligent", such as creativity or wisdom, and motivation, schooling and test familiarity affect scores.

Worked example

The Flynn effect

  1. Average raw scores on IQ tests rose by about 3 points per decade across the 20th century in many countries (Flynn, 1987).
  2. The rise is too fast to be genetic; better nutrition, health, schooling and more abstract thinking are suggested causes.
  3. In some countries the rise has recently slowed or reversed.

Misuse and bias

Early 20th-century tests were used to support the eugenics movement, restrict immigration to the United States and justify forced sterilisation. Tests developed in one culture may disadvantage people from others. Modern test standards (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014) require evidence of fairness for the groups tested.

Common mistakeTreating an IQ score as a fixed, complete measure of a person's mind. Scores change with environment and measure particular abilities.

In 2 lines: IQ tests are standardised to a mean of 100 and SD of 15, are reliable and predict some outcomes. The Flynn effect shows environmental influence; tests have been misused and need fairness evidence.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. The first practical intelligence test was developed by…
    • David Wechsler
    • Charles Spearman
    • Howard Gardner
    • Binet and Simon
    Show the answer

    Answer: Binet and Simon. It was designed to identify pupils needing support in France.

  2. Why can't the Flynn effect be explained by genetic change?
    • The rise was too rapid
    • Genes don't affect intelligence
    • Tests weren't standardised
    • Scores fell over time
    Show the answer

    Answer: The rise was too rapid. Genetic change across a few decades is far too slow.

  3. Which is the strongest reason to interpret an individual's IQ score cautiously?
    • IQ tests are very unreliable
    • Scores reflect the environment
    • IQ scores predict nothing at all
    • Scores can never change
    Show the answer

    Answer: Scores reflect the environment. Environmental factors influence performance on the day and over life.

Key terms

Standardisation
Giving a test to a large representative sample to establish norms
IQ
A standardised score with a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15
Flynn effect
The rise in average IQ test scores over the 20th century
