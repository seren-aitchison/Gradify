Compliance

Compliance techniques exploit consistency, reciprocity and commitment; they can be used ethically or manipulatively.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the main sequential compliance techniques
  • Explain the psychological principles behind them
  • Evaluate the ethics of using compliance techniques

Sequential techniques

  • Foot-in-the-door: a small request makes agreement to a larger one more likely. Freedman and Fraser (1966) found homeowners who first agreed to put a small "Be a safe driver" sign in their window were much more likely to later accept a large, ugly sign on their lawn.
  • Door-in-the-face: a large request that is refused makes a smaller one more likely. Cialdini et al. (1975) found students who first refused to mentor juvenile offenders for two years were more likely to agree to take them on a two-hour zoo trip.
  • Low-balling: getting agreement, then raising the cost. People often stay committed (Cialdini et al., 1978).

Why they work

Foot-in-the-door may work through self-perception ("I'm the kind of person who helps") and consistency. Door-in-the-face may work through reciprocity: the requester has "conceded", so we concede too. Low-balling relies on commitment.

Worked example

Cialdini's principles

  1. Cialdini (1984) identified six principles of influence from studying salespeople, fundraisers and advertisers.
  2. Reciprocity (returning favours), commitment and consistency, social proof (doing what others do), liking, authority and scarcity ("only three left").
  3. Each is a useful shortcut normally, but can be exploited.

Ethics

Compliance techniques are used in charity fundraising and health promotion, but also in high-pressure sales and scams. The line between persuasion and manipulation depends on honesty, the target's interests and whether they can freely refuse. Regulators, such as the UK's Competition and Markets Authority, have acted against misleading "scarcity" claims in online selling.

Common mistakeThinking only gullible people are influenced. These principles affect almost everyone because they are normally useful shortcuts.

In 2 lines: Foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face and low-balling increase compliance. They rely on consistency, reciprocity and commitment; ethics depend on honesty and freedom to refuse.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Refusing a big request and then agreeing to a smaller one shows the…
    • Door-in-the-face technique
    • Foot-in-the-door technique
    • Low-ball technique
    • Mere exposure effect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Door-in-the-face technique. The smaller request feels like a concession to reciprocate.

  2. A car dealer agrees a price, then adds fees, and the buyer still goes ahead. This is…
    • Door-in-the-face
    • Low-balling
    • Social proof
    • Reciprocity only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Low-balling. Raising costs after commitment is low-balling.

  3. Which principle does "Only 2 rooms left at this price!" exploit?
    • Reciprocity
    • Liking
    • Scarcity
    • Authority
    Show the answer

    Answer: Scarcity. Limited availability makes options seem more valuable.

Key terms

Foot-in-the-door
A small request increases compliance with a larger later request
Door-in-the-face
A refused large request increases compliance with a smaller one
Low-balling
Raising the cost after initial agreement
