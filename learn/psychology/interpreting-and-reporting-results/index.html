Interpreting and Reporting Results

Good reporting gives descriptives, test statistics, exact p, effect sizes and intervals; good interpretation matches claims to the design and evidence.

Psychology · Statistics · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Report statistical results in APA style
  • Interpret results in terms of effect size, uncertainty and design
  • Evaluate conclusions for over-interpretation

Reporting

APA style (7th edition) reports descriptive statistics (M, SD), the test statistic with degrees of freedom, the exact p-value (to two or three decimals, or p < .001), an effect size and, ideally, a confidence interval. For example: "Participants who slept recalled more words (M = 14.2, SD = 3.1) than those who stayed awake (M = 11.8, SD = 3.4), t(58) = 2.86, p = .006, d = 0.74, 95% CI for the difference [0.7, 4.1]."

Interpreting

  • Size: is the effect large enough to matter?
  • Uncertainty: how wide is the interval?
  • Design: does the design support causal claims?
  • Generality: who were the participants, and how far might results extend?
  • Consistency: does it fit or contradict previous evidence?
Worked example

Over-interpreting a correlation

  1. A survey finds r = .20 between daily gratitude journalling and wellbeing, p = .01.
  2. Headline: "Gratitude makes you happier."
  3. Better: "People who journal gratitude report slightly higher wellbeing; the association is small and doesn't show causation. Randomised trials are needed."

Common errors

Treating non-significance as proof of no effect; ignoring effect size; inferring causation from correlation; generalising beyond the sample; and selectively reporting significant outcomes. Reporting all planned analyses, including null results, helps the field build an accurate picture.

Common mistakeWriting "the results prove…". Data support or fail to support hypotheses; they don't prove them.

In 2 lines: Report descriptives, test statistics, exact p, effect sizes and confidence intervals. Interpret size, uncertainty, design and generality; avoid causal and universal over-claims.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which is a correctly formatted APA result?
    • t = 2.45 (significant)
    • p < 0.05!!!
    • t(28) = 2.45, p = .021, d = 0.89
    • The t-test worked
    Show the answer

    Answer: t(28) = 2.45, p = .021, d = 0.89. APA style gives the statistic, df, exact p and an effect size.

  2. A study finds no significant difference between two teaching methods, p = .30, with 15 per group. Which conclusion is best?
    • The methods are equally effective
    • Method A is better
    • The study proves teaching doesn't matter
    • The evidence is inconclusive given the small sample
    Show the answer

    Answer: The evidence is inconclusive given the small sample. Low power means the result can't establish equivalence.

  3. A headline says "Video games cause aggression" based on r = .08 in a large survey. What is the strongest correction?
    • It is tiny and only correlational
    • The result is not statistically significant
    • Video games reduce aggression
    • The sample is too large to trust
    Show the answer

    Answer: It is tiny and only correlational. Small effect size and a correlational design both limit the claim.

Key terms

APA style
The American Psychological Association's standard format for reporting research
Over-interpretation
Drawing conclusions stronger than the design and data support
