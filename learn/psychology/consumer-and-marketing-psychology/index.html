Consumer and Marketing Psychology

Consumers rely on heuristics such as anchoring, scarcity and social proof; advertising uses persuasion routes and conditioning; ethics concern vulnerable groups and manipulation.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how heuristics and biases affect consumer decisions
  • Describe how advertising uses persuasion and conditioning
  • Evaluate ethical issues in marketing to consumers

Decision shortcuts

Consumers often use heuristics rather than careful comparison. Anchoring affects how prices are judged ("was £50, now £30"); scarcity ("only 3 left") increases desire; social proof (reviews, "bestseller") reassures; and defaults shape choices. Choice overload can reduce buying: Iyengar and Lepper (2000) found shoppers offered 24 jam flavours were less likely to buy than those offered 6, though later meta-analysis found the effect depends on conditions (Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, 2015).

Advertising and persuasion

Advertising uses both the central route (information and arguments) and the peripheral route (attractive celebrities, music, emotion) described in the elaboration likelihood model. Classical conditioning pairs products with positive feelings; mere exposure increases liking through repetition. Brand loyalty can develop through these processes.

Worked example

Pricing tricks

  1. Charm pricing (prices ending in 99) can make products seem cheaper because people focus on the left digit.
  2. Decoy pricing adds an unattractive option to make another seem better value (the "decoy effect").
  3. These techniques exploit predictable features of judgement.

Ethics

Concerns include targeting children, who are less able to recognise persuasive intent; promoting unhealthy products; personalised online advertising using data; and manipulative design. UK rules restrict advertising of foods high in fat, sugar or salt to children, and the Advertising Standards Authority regulates misleading ads.

Common mistakeAssuming consumers always make rational comparisons. Shortcuts and framing strongly influence choices.

In 2 lines: Heuristics such as anchoring, scarcity and social proof shape consumer choices. Advertising uses central and peripheral routes and conditioning; ethics concern children and manipulation.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. "Only 2 left in stock!" uses which principle?
    • Anchoring
    • Charm pricing
    • Scarcity
    • Mere exposure
    Show the answer

    Answer: Scarcity. Signalling limited availability increases desire through the scarcity principle.

  2. A coat labelled "was £120, now £60" seems a bargain. This uses…
    • Choice overload
    • Social proof
    • Decoy pricing
    • Anchoring
    Show the answer

    Answer: Anchoring. The original price acts as an anchor that makes the sale price seem cheap.

  3. Why are children seen as vulnerable to advertising?
    • They miss persuasive intent
    • They never watch adverts
    • They make fully rational choices
    • Adverts are never aimed at them
    Show the answer

    Answer: They miss persuasive intent. Young children may not understand that ads aim to persuade.

Key terms

Choice overload
Reduced decision-making or satisfaction when faced with too many options
Decoy effect
Adding an inferior option to make another seem better value
Social proof
Using others' behaviour as evidence of what to do
