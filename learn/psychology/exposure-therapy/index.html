Exposure Therapy

Exposure includes graded exposure, systematic desensitisation and flooding; habituation and inhibitory learning explain its effects; it is highly effective but can be distressing.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe graded exposure and systematic desensitisation
  • Explain habituation and inhibitory learning accounts of exposure
  • Evaluate exposure therapy
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to therapy. Exposure therapy should only be delivered by qualified, registered professionals.

Forms of exposure

In graded exposure, the person builds a fear hierarchy from least to most feared situations and works through it step by step. Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe) pairs gradual exposure with relaxation. Flooding involves prolonged exposure to a highly feared situation without escape. Exposure can be in vivo (real life), imaginal, or through virtual reality.

How does exposure work?

Early accounts emphasised habituation: fear reduces with repeated, prolonged contact. Foa and Kozak's emotional processing theory proposed that exposure activates fear memories so they can be corrected. Craske et al. (2014) proposed an inhibitory learning account: exposure creates new learning ("the dog didn't bite me") that competes with the old fear memory, so the aim is to violate expectations rather than simply wait for fear to drop.

Worked example

A fear hierarchy

  1. For a fear of dogs, steps might be: looking at photos of dogs; watching videos; seeing a dog behind a fence; standing near a calm dog on a lead; stroking a calm dog.
  2. The person moves up only when ready, staying in each situation long enough to learn.
  3. Safety behaviours are reduced so new learning can occur. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Exposure is one of the most effective treatments for anxiety disorders, phobias, OCD and PTSD. But it can be distressing, some people drop out, and therapists sometimes avoid using it. Return of fear can occur in new contexts, which inhibitory learning research aims to reduce by varying contexts.

Common mistakeAssuming exposure means forcing people to face fears suddenly. Graded exposure is collaborative and gradual.

In 2 lines: Graded exposure, systematic desensitisation and flooding. Habituation versus inhibitory learning; effective but can be distressing.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Gradual exposure paired with relaxation is…
    • Flooding
    • Behavioural activation
    • Systematic desensitisation
    • Free association
    Show the answer

    Answer: Systematic desensitisation. Wolpe paired relaxation with gradual exposure.

  2. A therapist designs exposure so a person's prediction ("I will faint") is clearly disproved. This reflects…
    • Pure habituation only
    • Free association
    • Unconditional positive regard
    • An inhibitory learning approach
    Show the answer

    Answer: An inhibitory learning approach. Violating expectations creates new learning.

  3. Why might fear return after successful exposure?
    • The old fear memory remains and can resurface elsewhere
    • Exposure completely erases old memories
    • Fear never returns once it has gone
    • The context of exposure never matters
    Show the answer

    Answer: The old fear memory remains and can resurface elsewhere. Inhibitory learning competes with, rather than erases, fear.

Key terms

Fear hierarchy

Inhibitory learning
New learning that competes with an old fear memory
Systematic desensitisation
Gradual exposure paired with relaxation
