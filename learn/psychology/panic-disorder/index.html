Panic Disorder

Panic attacks are sudden surges of fear with physical symptoms; Clark's model explains panic as catastrophic misinterpretation of sensations in a vicious cycle; safety behaviours maintain it.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the features of panic attacks and panic disorder
  • Explain Clark's cognitive model of panic
  • Evaluate cognitive explanations of panic
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about panic attacks should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Panic attacks

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear with physical symptoms such as a racing heart, breathlessness, dizziness, sweating and chest discomfort, peaking within minutes. Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected attacks plus persistent worry about further attacks or changes in behaviour to avoid them. Some people develop agoraphobia, avoiding places where escape might be difficult.

Clark's cognitive model

Clark (1986) proposed that panic results from the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations. A trigger produces anxiety and normal sensations, such as a faster heartbeat; the person interprets them as a sign of immediate danger ("I'm having a heart attack"), which increases anxiety and sensations further, creating a vicious cycle. Safety behaviours, such as sitting down or holding on to something, prevent people learning that the feared catastrophe doesn't happen.

Worked example

The vicious cycle

  1. Trigger: climbing stairs makes the heart beat faster.
  2. Interpretation: "Something is wrong with my heart."
  3. More anxiety, faster heartbeat, more alarm, leading to a panic attack. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Clark's model is supported by research showing people with panic disorder interpret bodily sensations more catastrophically, and cognitive therapy based on it is effective. But some panic attacks occur without obvious catastrophic thoughts, and biological factors such as sensitivity to carbon dioxide also play a role. Integrated models combine cognitive and biological factors.

Common mistakeAssuming panic attacks are physically dangerous. They are frightening, but the feared catastrophe usually doesn't occur.

In 2 lines: Panic attacks involve sudden fear and physical symptoms. Clark: catastrophic misinterpretation and a vicious cycle; safety behaviours maintain it.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Avoiding places where escape might be hard is called…
    • Social anxiety
    • Specific phobia
    • Panic attack only
    • Agoraphobia
    Show the answer

    Answer: Agoraphobia. Agoraphobia can develop alongside panic disorder.

  2. During a panic attack, a woman always sits down, believing this stops her fainting. Clark would call this…
    • A safety behaviour
    • Catastrophic misinterpretation
    • Conditions of worth
    • Displacement
    Show the answer

    Answer: A safety behaviour. Safety behaviours prevent disconfirming learning.

  3. Why do some researchers favour integrated models of panic?
    • Cognition explains every single attack
    • Some attacks occur without catastrophic thoughts
    • Biology plays no role in panic at all
    • Panic never involves the body
    Show the answer

    Answer: Some attacks occur without catastrophic thoughts. Biological sensitivity may contribute alongside cognition.

Key terms

Panic attack
A sudden surge of intense fear with physical symptoms
Catastrophic misinterpretation
Interpreting bodily sensations as signs of immediate danger
Safety behaviour
An action taken to prevent a feared outcome that maintains anxiety
