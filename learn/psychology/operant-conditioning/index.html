Operant Conditioning

Operant conditioning changes voluntary behaviour through its consequences: reinforcement strengthens it and punishment weakens it.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the law of effect and the principles of operant conditioning
  • Explain how operant conditioning differs from classical conditioning
  • Evaluate Skinner's approach and its use of animal research

The law of effect

Edward Thorndike (1898) placed hungry cats in puzzle boxes they could escape by pressing a lever or pulling a loop to reach food. Over trials, escape times fell gradually. He proposed the law of effect: responses followed by satisfying consequences become more likely; those followed by discomfort become less likely.

Skinner's analysis

B. F. Skinner (1938) studied operant behaviour, behaviour that operates on the environment, in an operant chamber (the "Skinner box") where rats pressed levers or pigeons pecked keys for food. He described consequences as reinforcement (increases behaviour) or punishment (decreases it), and measured learning by response rate.

Operant versus classical

  • Classical conditioning links two stimuli; the learner's behaviour doesn't change what happens.
  • Operant conditioning links a behaviour to its consequence; what the learner does matters.
  • Classical responses are mostly involuntary (salivation, fear); operant behaviours are mostly voluntary (pressing, speaking, studying).

Investigate with the lab. Compare cumulative records for fixed and variable ratio schedules, then switch to extinction.

Worked example

A student and phone notifications

  1. Checking the phone (behaviour) is sometimes followed by an interesting message (reinforcement).
  2. Checking becomes more frequent: operant learning.
  3. The notification sound also becomes a conditioned stimulus for anticipation: classical and operant learning often work together.

Evaluation

Operant principles are reliable and widely applied, from classrooms to animal training. Criticisms: a strict focus on observable behaviour neglects cognition (Tolman's latent learning; Bandura's social learning); biological constraints limit what can be learned; and generalising from animals in boxes to complex human behaviour is limited.

Common mistakeThinking operant conditioning only applies to animals. Much human behaviour, from studying to social media use, is shaped by its consequences.

In 2 lines: Thorndike's law of effect and Skinner's research show behaviour is shaped by consequences. Operant conditioning differs from classical in linking behaviour to consequence; it neglects cognition.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who studied cats escaping from puzzle boxes?
    • Edward Thorndike
    • Ivan Pavlov
    • B. F. Skinner
    • John Watson
    Show the answer

    Answer: Edward Thorndike. Thorndike's puzzle boxes led to the law of effect.

  2. A pupil answers questions more often after the teacher praises answers. Which kind of learning is this?
    • Classical conditioning
    • Operant conditioning
    • Habituation
    • Imprinting
    Show the answer

    Answer: Operant conditioning. The behaviour (answering) increases because of its consequence (praise).

  3. Which finding most challenges a purely operant account of learning?
    • Reinforced behaviours increase in frequency
    • Punished behaviours decrease
    • Rats learned a maze without reward, showing it once rewarded
    • Pigeons peck keys for food
    Show the answer

    Answer: Rats learned a maze without reward, showing it once rewarded. Tolman's latent learning shows learning can occur without reinforcement, implying cognition.

  4. Which schedule of reinforcement produces behaviour most resistant to extinction?
    • Continuous reinforcement
    • Fixed interval
    • Fixed ratio
    • Variable ratio
    Show the answer

    Answer: Variable ratio. Unpredictable reinforcement keeps responding going long after rewards stop.

Key terms

Law of effect
Thorndike's principle that behaviours followed by satisfying consequences are more likely to recur
Operant conditioning
Learning in which behaviour is shaped by its consequences
Reinforcement
A consequence that increases the likelihood of a behaviour
