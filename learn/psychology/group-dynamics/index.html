Group Dynamics

Others' presence facilitates simple tasks and impairs complex ones; groups can lead to loafing unless contributions are identifiable and valued.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe what makes a collection of people a group and how groups develop
  • Explain social facilitation and social loafing with evidence
  • Apply group dynamics research to teams at work or school

What is a group?

A group is two or more people who interact, share goals or identity and influence one another. Groups have norms (shared expectations), roles (expected behaviour for positions) and cohesion (how strongly members are attached). Tuckman (1965) proposed that small groups develop through forming, storming, norming and performing, later adding adjourning; real groups don't always follow this sequence neatly.

Social facilitation

Triplett (1898) observed that cyclists rode faster when racing others than alone. Zajonc (1965) explained social facilitation: the presence of others increases arousal, which improves performance on simple or well-learned tasks but impairs performance on complex or new ones. Cottrell suggested arousal comes from evaluation apprehension: concern about being judged.

Worked example

Pool players

  1. Michaels et al. (1982) watched pool players in a student union, then had observers stand near the table.
  2. Good players improved from about 71% to 80% of shots; weaker players dropped from about 36% to 25%.
  3. An audience helped well-practised players and hurt less skilled ones.

Social loafing

Social loafing is putting in less effort when working in a group where individual contributions aren't identifiable. Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979) found people clapped and shouted less loudly per person in larger groups. Loafing is reduced when contributions are identifiable, the task is meaningful, the group is cohesive and members believe their effort matters. A meta-analysis found it was smaller in collectivist cultures and among women (Karau and Williams, 1993).

Investigate with the lab. Repeat the line judgements and think about how group size and unanimity might change your answers.

Common mistakeAssuming an audience always helps performance. It helps only for simple or well-learned tasks.

In 2 lines: Groups have norms, roles and cohesion and may develop through stages. Social facilitation and social loafing show how groups change individual performance.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who first studied cyclists racing alone versus with others?
    • Robert Zajonc
    • Bibb Latané
    • Norman Triplett
    • Bruce Tuckman
    Show the answer

    Answer: Norman Triplett. Triplett's 1898 study is often called the first social psychology experiment.

  2. A beginner pianist makes more mistakes playing for an audience. This is explained by…
    • Social loafing in a group
    • Groupthink in the audience
    • Obedience to the teacher
    • Arousal impairing a hard task
    Show the answer

    Answer: Arousal impairing a hard task. Arousal hurts performance on new or difficult tasks.

  3. Why might social loafing be smaller in collectivist cultures?
    • Group goals are valued more
    • Groups are always smaller
    • People work alone
    • Effort is never measured
    Show the answer

    Answer: Group goals are valued more. Commitment to group outcomes reduces loafing.

  4. What happened in Asch's studies when one confederate gave the right answer?
    • Conformity rose sharply
    • Conformity fell sharply
    • It made no difference
    • Participants refused to answer
    Show the answer

    Answer: Conformity fell sharply. Breaking the majority's unanimity gave participants support to dissent.

Key terms

Social facilitation
Improved performance on simple tasks in the presence of others
Social loafing
Reduced individual effort in groups when contributions aren't identifiable
Cohesion
The strength of members' attachment to a group
