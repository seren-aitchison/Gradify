Habituation and Sensitisation

Habituation reduces responses to repeated, harmless stimuli; sensitisation increases responses after intense ones; both change synaptic strength.

Psychology · Learning and Behaviour · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define habituation and sensitisation
  • Explain the neural basis of habituation, with reference to research on Aplysia
  • Apply habituation and sensitisation to everyday experiences and research methods

Two simple forms of learning

Habituation is a decrease in response to a stimulus that is repeated and has no consequence: you stop noticing a ticking clock. It is stimulus-specific (a different sound still gets a response) and recovers with time. Sensitisation is an increase in response, often to a range of stimuli, after an intense or harmful stimulus: after a loud bang, you jump at small noises. Both are non-associative learning, because no link between two stimuli is formed.

The neural basis

Eric Kandel studied the sea slug Aplysia, whose gill-withdrawal reflex habituates when its siphon is touched repeatedly. He showed that habituation involves reduced neurotransmitter release at specific synapses, and that sensitisation involves increased release. This showed learning changes the strength of synapses, work recognised by the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Worked example

Habituation as a research tool

  1. Infants look less at a picture shown repeatedly (habituation).
  2. If a new picture brings renewed looking (dishabituation), the infant can tell the two apart.
  3. Researchers use this to study what babies perceive and know before they can speak.

Why it matters

Habituation saves attention for new information. Sensitisation prepares the organism for danger, and may contribute to chronic pain and anxiety, where nervous systems become oversensitive. Habituation is also one reason exposure to feared stimuli reduces fear.

Common mistakeConfusing habituation with sensory adaptation. Adaptation happens in sense organs (receptors stop responding); habituation is learning in the nervous system and can be reversed by a change in the stimulus.

In 2 lines: Habituation decreases and sensitisation increases responding, without associations between stimuli. Kandel showed both involve synaptic change; habituation is also used to study infant perception.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which animal did Kandel use to study habituation?
    • The pigeon
    • The chimpanzee
    • The sea slug Aplysia
    • The rat
    Show the answer

    Answer: The sea slug Aplysia. Aplysia's simple nervous system allowed synaptic changes to be studied directly.

  2. After a near-miss in a car, a driver startles at every horn for days. This is…
    • Habituation
    • Extinction
    • Latent learning
    • Sensitisation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Sensitisation. An intense experience increased responsiveness to other stimuli.

  3. How does habituation differ from sensory adaptation?
    • Habituation is central learning and can dishabituate
    • They are the same process with different names
    • Sensory adaptation lasts for years
    • Habituation happens only in sense organs
    Show the answer

    Answer: Habituation is central learning and can dishabituate. Adaptation is receptor fatigue; habituation is central learning that dishabituates.

Key terms

Habituation
A decrease in response to a repeated stimulus that has no consequence
Sensitisation
An increase in response following an intense or harmful stimulus
Dishabituation
Recovery of a habituated response when a new stimulus appears
