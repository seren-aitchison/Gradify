Social Anxiety Disorder

Social anxiety disorder involves fear of scrutiny and avoidance; Clark and Wells highlight self-focused attention, observer images and safety behaviours; post-event processing maintains anxiety.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the features of social anxiety disorder
  • Explain cognitive models of social anxiety
  • Evaluate explanations and approaches in guidance
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about social anxiety should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Features

Social anxiety disorder involves marked fear of social situations where a person might be scrutinised, such as meeting new people, speaking in groups or eating in public. People fear acting in a way that will be humiliating or lead to rejection. They avoid these situations or endure them with intense distress, and the fear interferes with work, study or relationships. It often begins in adolescence.

Cognitive models

Clark and Wells (1995) proposed that socially anxious people shift attention inward, building an observer perspective image of how they appear, often more negative than reality. They use safety behaviours, such as avoiding eye contact or rehearsing sentences, which can make them seem less friendly and prevent learning that feared outcomes don't happen. Rapee and Heimberg (1997) emphasised biased predictions about how others evaluate them. Post-event processing, going over social interactions afterwards, maintains anxiety.

Worked example

Safety behaviours backfiring

  1. A student avoids eye contact and speaks quietly in seminars to avoid being noticed.
  2. Others may read this as unfriendliness, so they respond less warmly.
  3. The student takes this as proof of being disliked, maintaining the fear. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Cognitive models are well supported and underpin effective cognitive therapy for social anxiety, which NICE guidance recommends as a first-line psychological approach. The boundary between shyness and disorder involves judgement, and cultural norms about social behaviour vary. Biological temperament, such as behavioural inhibition in childhood, also contributes.

Common mistakeEquating social anxiety disorder with ordinary shyness. It involves intense fear and impairment.

In 2 lines: Fear of scrutiny, avoidance and impairment. Self-focus, safety behaviours and post-event processing maintain anxiety.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Seeing oneself as if from the outside, often negatively, is the…
    • Field perspective
    • Observer perspective
    • Reverse inference
    • Moral treatment
    Show the answer

    Answer: Observer perspective. Clark and Wells described the observer perspective.

  2. After a party, a man replays every conversation, focusing on what he got wrong. This is…
    • Self-actualisation
    • Displacement
    • Post-event processing
    • Learned helplessness
    Show the answer

    Answer: Post-event processing. Negative reviewing afterwards is post-event processing.

  3. Why is it hard to draw the line between shyness and social anxiety disorder?
    • Shyness and the disorder are identical
    • There is a simple blood test for it
    • Everyone in society has the disorder
    • It rests on judgements of distress, and norms vary
    Show the answer

    Answer: It rests on judgements of distress, and norms vary. Thresholds involve judgement and context.
