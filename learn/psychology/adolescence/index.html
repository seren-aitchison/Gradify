Adolescence

Adolescence brings puberty, synaptic pruning, increasing white matter and a prefrontal cortex still maturing, with heightened sensitivity to peers and rewards.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the physical changes of puberty and their timing
  • Explain how the adolescent brain develops
  • Evaluate explanations of adolescent risk-taking, including peer influence

Puberty

Puberty involves hormonal changes that produce growth spurts, sexual maturation and changes in body shape, usually between about 8 and 14 in girls and 9 and 15 in boys. The average age of puberty has fallen over the past century, linked to better nutrition and health. Early maturation, especially for girls, is associated with higher risk of some emotional and behavioural difficulties.

The developing brain

Brain imaging shows the adolescent brain continues to change. Grey matter volume in the cortex peaks and then declines as synapses are pruned, while white matter increases as connections become more efficient (Giedd et al., 1999). The prefrontal cortex, involved in planning and impulse control, matures into the mid-20s, while reward-related areas such as the ventral striatum become highly responsive earlier.

Risk and peers

Worked example

Driving with friends

  1. Gardner and Steinberg (2005) had adolescents, young adults and adults play a driving game alone or with two peers watching.
  2. Peers' presence doubled risky driving among adolescents, increased it by about half in young adults and had no effect on adults.
  3. Adolescents' decisions appear especially sensitive to peers and rewards.

The dual-systems model proposes that risk-taking peaks when reward sensitivity is high but cognitive control is still developing. Critics argue adolescents aren't simply impulsive: many risks reflect exploration, social learning and seeking independence, and much adolescent behaviour is thoughtful. Blakemore (2018) stresses that adolescence is also a period of opportunity for learning and social development.

Common mistakeDescribing adolescents as having "broken" brains. Their brains are developing, adapted to learning and becoming independent.

In 2 lines: Puberty brings physical changes with variable timing. The adolescent brain is pruned and reorganised; peers and rewards strongly influence risk-taking.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which brain region continues maturing into the mid-20s?
    • Cerebellum
    • Brainstem
    • Prefrontal cortex
    • Occipital lobe
    Show the answer

    Answer: Prefrontal cortex. Planning and impulse-control areas in the prefrontal cortex mature last.

  2. A 15-year-old drives more riskily when friends are watching. Which finding does this fit?
    • Adults take more risks with peers
    • Peers reduce risk
    • Risk is fixed
    • Peer presence increases adolescent risk-taking
    Show the answer

    Answer: Peer presence increases adolescent risk-taking. Gardner and Steinberg found peers doubled adolescent risk-taking.

  3. Which is a fair criticism of the dual-systems model?
    • Risk can be adaptive
    • Adolescents never take risks
    • The brain doesn't change
    • Peers have no effect at all
    Show the answer

    Answer: Risk can be adaptive. Risk-taking can be adaptive, not just poor control.

Key terms

Puberty
Biological changes leading to sexual maturity
Synaptic pruning
Removal of unused synapses, refining brain networks
Dual-systems model
The idea that risk-taking peaks when reward sensitivity outpaces cognitive control
