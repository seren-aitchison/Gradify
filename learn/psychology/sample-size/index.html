Sample Size

Sample size should be set by power analysis; underpowered studies often miss real effects and exaggerate the ones they find.

Psychology · Research Methods · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why sample size affects the precision of estimates
  • Describe how power analysis is used to choose a sample size
  • Evaluate the consequences of underpowered research

Bigger samples, more precise estimates

Every sample differs from the population by chance (sampling error). Larger samples give more precise estimates: the standard error of a mean shrinks in proportion to the square root of the sample size, so quadrupling the sample halves the error. Small samples produce estimates that bounce around and may badly mislead.

Power analysis

Statistical power is the probability that a study will detect an effect if it really exists. Researchers use a power analysis before collecting data: given the expected effect size, the significance level (usually .05) and the desired power (often 80%), it calculates the sample size needed. Cohen (1992) gave simple tables: to detect a medium effect (d = 0.5) in a two-group comparison with 80% power at α = .05 needs about 64 participants per group.

Worked example

Why small studies mislead

  1. A study with 15 per group has only about a one-in-four chance of detecting a medium effect.
  2. When such a study does find a significant result, the estimated effect is likely to be exaggerated (the winner's curse).
  3. Many small studies with exaggerated effects contributed to the replication crisis (Button et al., 2013).

Effect sizes for the power analysis should come from previous research or from the smallest effect that would matter in practice, rather than optimistic guesses.

Investigate with the lab. Draw hundreds of samples of different sizes and watch the standard error shrink.

Common mistakeThinking a significant result from a small sample is "even more impressive". Small significant studies are more likely to overestimate effects or be false positives.

In 2 lines: Larger samples reduce sampling error; power analysis sets the size needed. Underpowered studies miss real effects and exaggerate significant ones.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which three values are needed for a power analysis?
    • Effect size, alpha and power
    • Mean, mode and median
    • IV, DV and confounds
    • Sample, population and frame
    Show the answer

    Answer: Effect size, alpha and power. These determine the sample size required.

  2. Why did low power contribute to the replication crisis?
    • Large studies always find false effects
    • Small studies inflated or invented effects
    • Power analysis was used too often
    • Replications used smaller samples
    Show the answer

    Answer: Small studies inflated or invented effects. Underpowered studies inflated published effect sizes, so replications found smaller or no effects.

  3. What happens to the standard error as sample size increases?
    • It increases
    • It stays the same
    • It decreases
    • It becomes negative
    Show the answer

    Answer: It decreases. Standard error is proportional to 1 over the square root of the sample size.

Key terms

Statistical power
The probability that a study detects an effect that really exists
Power analysis
A calculation of the sample size needed to achieve a given power
Sampling error
Chance differences between a sample and its population
