Comorbidity

Comorbidity is very common; shared risks, causal links, overlapping criteria and imperfect categories explain it; the p factor suggests a general liability; dimensional models respond to comorbidity.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define comorbidity and describe how common it is
  • Explain possible reasons for comorbidity
  • Evaluate the p factor and dimensional accounts
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

How common is comorbidity?

Kessler et al. (1994) found in a large US survey that many people with one mental health condition also met criteria for others, and a minority of people accounted for most lifetime conditions. Anxiety and depression very often co-occur, as do ADHD and autism, and substance use with many conditions.

Why does comorbidity occur?

Explanations include shared risk factors (such as genes and adversity), one condition causing another (for example, anxiety leading to avoidance and then depression), overlapping criteria in diagnostic systems, and categories that don't reflect natural boundaries. Network approaches suggest bridge symptoms, such as sleep problems, link conditions.

Worked example

The p factor

  1. Caspi et al. (2014) analysed symptoms across many conditions and found evidence for a general psychopathology factor, called p, alongside more specific factors.
  2. A higher p score was associated with worse functioning and more conditions over time.
  3. This suggests some shared liability underlies many diagnoses.

Evaluation

High comorbidity challenges the idea that mental health conditions are separate categories and supports dimensional and hierarchical models such as HiTOP. Critics argue the meaning of p is unclear: it may reflect severity, impairment or measurement artefacts. Comorbidity also matters practically, as people with several conditions often have greater needs.

Common mistakeAssuming each diagnosis is an entirely separate condition. Many share risk factors and symptoms.

In 2 lines: Comorbidity is very common across conditions. Shared risks, causal links and overlapping criteria; the p factor and dimensional models.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. A factor such as childhood adversity that increases risk for several conditions is a…
    • Shared risk factor
    • Bridge diagnosis
    • Protective factor
    • Safety behaviour
    Show the answer

    Answer: Shared risk factor. Factors that raise risk for several conditions help explain why comorbidity is common.

  2. A woman with long-standing anxiety stops going out and later develops depression. This illustrates comorbidity arising from…
    • Overlapping criteria only
    • One condition leading to another
    • Pure coincidence
    • A single gene
    Show the answer

    Answer: One condition leading to another. Avoidance due to anxiety contributed to depression.

  3. Why is the meaning of the p factor debated?
    • It has one meaning agreed by everyone
    • It applies to only one condition
    • It may reflect severity, impairment or measurement
    • It has never actually been measured
    Show the answer

    Answer: It may reflect severity, impairment or measurement. Researchers disagree about whether p captures a real liability, severity or measurement artefacts.

Key terms

Comorbidity
Two or more conditions occurring in the same person
p factor
A general psychopathology factor underlying many conditions
Shared risk factor
A factor that increases risk for several conditions
