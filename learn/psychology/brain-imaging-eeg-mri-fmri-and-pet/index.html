Brain Imaging: EEG, MRI, FMRI and PET

EEG has fine timing; fMRI and MRI have fine spatial detail; PET measures chemistry; imaging shows correlations that must be interpreted carefully.

Psychology · Biological Psychology and Neuroscience · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe how EEG, ERPs, MRI, fMRI and PET work
  • Compare the spatial and temporal resolution of imaging methods
  • Evaluate the interpretation of brain imaging findings

Methods

  • EEG (electroencephalography): electrodes on the scalp record electrical activity from large groups of neurones. Event-related potentials (ERPs) are averaged EEG responses to a repeated stimulus. Excellent temporal resolution (milliseconds) but poor spatial resolution.
  • MRI (magnetic resonance imaging): strong magnetic fields and radio waves produce detailed images of brain structure.
  • fMRI (functional MRI): measures the BOLD signal, changes in blood oxygenation that follow neural activity. Good spatial resolution (millimetres) but slower temporal resolution (seconds), because blood flow lags neural activity.
  • PET (positron emission tomography): a radioactive tracer shows metabolism or neurotransmitter receptors. Useful for chemistry, but involves radiation and has poorer resolution.
Worked example

Choosing a method

  1. To find exactly when the brain distinguishes faces from objects, EEG and ERPs are best: a face-sensitive response appears about 170 ms after the image.
  2. To find where face processing happens, fMRI is best: it highlights the fusiform face area.
  3. To measure dopamine receptors, PET is needed.

Interpreting images

Brain images show correlations between activity and tasks, not necessarily causes. An active area may be involved, but not essential. Reverse inference (concluding a person feels fear because the amygdala is active) is weak, because most areas serve many functions (Poldrack, 2006). Small samples and many statistical tests can produce false positives: a famous demonstration found "activity" in a dead salmon when corrections for multiple comparisons weren't applied (Bennett et al., 2010).

Other methods include MEG (magnetoencephalography), which records magnetic fields from neural activity with good timing and better localisation than EEG, and DTI (diffusion tensor imaging), an MRI method that maps white-matter tracts connecting brain regions.

Common mistakeTreating colourful brain images as proof of cause. They show statistical associations between activity and tasks.

In 2 lines: EEG and ERPs give precise timing; MRI and fMRI give precise location; PET measures chemistry. Images show correlations; reverse inference and multiple comparisons need caution.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which method uses a radioactive tracer?
    • PET
    • EEG
    • MRI
    • fMRI
    Show the answer

    Answer: PET. PET detects positrons emitted by a radioactive tracer in the blood.

  2. A researcher wants to know which brain area is active when people read words. Which method is best?
    • EEG
    • fMRI
    • A questionnaire
    • A lesion study
    Show the answer

    Answer: fMRI. fMRI has good spatial resolution for locating activity.

  3. What did the "dead salmon" study demonstrate?
    • Fish can think
    • fMRI can't detect anything
    • Uncorrected tests mislead
    • The method is perfect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Uncorrected tests mislead. Without multiple comparisons correction, noise looks like activity.

Key terms

Temporal resolution
How precisely a method can measure when activity occurs
Spatial resolution
How precisely a method can locate where activity occurs
BOLD signal
The blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal measured by fMRI
