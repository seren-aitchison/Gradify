Substance Use Disorders

Substance use disorders involve impaired control, harm, cravings, tolerance and withdrawal; disease, learning and social models explain addiction; context strongly affects recovery.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe features of substance use disorders
  • Compare disease, learning and social models of addiction
  • Evaluate evidence on recovery from addiction
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about alcohol or drug use should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Features

Substance use disorders involve impaired control over use, continued use despite harm, cravings and, for some substances, tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and withdrawal. The Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey found the share of 16 to 24 year olds drinking at hazardous levels or above halved from 40.7% in 2000 to 18.1% in 2023/4 (NHS England, 2025).

Models of addiction

The disease model sees addiction as a chronic brain condition involving changes to reward and control systems. Learning models explain use through reinforcement: substances produce pleasure (positive reinforcement) or relieve withdrawal and distress (negative reinforcement), and cues trigger cravings through classical conditioning. Social models stress peers, availability, poverty, trauma and culture.

Worked example

Context and recovery

  1. Robins, Helzer and Davis (1975) studied US soldiers who had used heroin in Vietnam.
  2. Most stopped using after returning home, where the drug and its cues were less available and their lives changed.
  3. This suggested environment and context strongly influence addiction and recovery.

Evaluation

Each model captures part of addiction. The disease model reduces blame and supports treatment, but may underplay choice and context. Learning models explain cravings and relapse. Social models explain why addiction clusters in deprived communities. Many people recover, sometimes without formal treatment, which challenges the view that addiction is always lifelong.

Common mistakeAssuming addiction is purely a matter of willpower. Biological, learning and social factors all play a part.

In 2 lines: Impaired control, harm, cravings, tolerance and withdrawal. Disease, learning and social models; context strongly affects recovery.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Unpleasant symptoms when a substance is stopped are called…
    • Withdrawal
    • Tolerance
    • Craving
    • Relapse
    Show the answer

    Answer: Withdrawal. Withdrawal is the set of unpleasant symptoms that follows stopping a substance.

  2. A person drinks to relieve anxiety, and the relief makes drinking more likely next time. This is…
    • Positive reinforcement
    • Negative reinforcement
    • Tolerance
    • Withdrawal
    Show the answer

    Answer: Negative reinforcement. Drinking removes anxiety, and removing an unpleasant state negatively reinforces use.

  3. Why does recovery without treatment challenge the disease model?
    • It proves addiction is a brain disease
    • Nobody ever recovers from addiction
    • It suggests addiction isn't always lifelong
    • Treatment for addiction always fails
    Show the answer

    Answer: It suggests addiction isn't always lifelong. Natural recovery points to context and choice.
