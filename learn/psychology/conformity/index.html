Conformity

People conform for acceptance (normative) and accuracy (informational); conformity depends on group size, unanimity, task difficulty and culture.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish normative and informational social influence
  • Describe Asch's procedure, findings and variations
  • Evaluate conformity research, including cultural and historical differences

Why people conform

Deutsch and Gerard (1955) distinguished normative social influence (conforming to be liked or accepted) and informational social influence (conforming because others may know better, especially in ambiguous situations). Kelman (1958) described levels: compliance (public agreement only), identification (adopting group views while part of the group) and internalisation (genuine private acceptance).

Sherif (1935) used the autokinetic effect, in which a still point of light in a dark room appears to move. When estimating movement in groups, participants' estimates converged into a group norm that persisted when they were later tested alone, an example of informational influence.

Asch's line studies

Worked example

Which line matches?

  1. Asch (1951, 1956) asked participants to judge which of three lines matched a standard line. The answer was obvious.
  2. Each real participant sat with confederates who, on 12 of 18 trials, gave the same wrong answer.
  3. On those critical trials, participants conformed about 37% of the time; about 75% conformed at least once, and about 25% never conformed. Control participants alone erred under 1% of the time.

Factors

Conformity rose with group size up to about three or four confederates and then levelled off. A single dissenting ally reduced conformity to about 5–10% of trials. Writing answers privately reduced it. Harder tasks (lines more similar) increased it. A meta-analysis found conformity was higher in collectivist cultures and had declined in the US since the 1950s (Bond and Smith, 1996).

Common mistakeConcluding that most people conform most of the time. In Asch's studies, about two-thirds of responses on critical trials were independent.

In 2 lines: Normative and informational influence produce compliance, identification or internalisation. Asch found about 37% conformity on critical trials; size, unanimity, difficulty and culture matter.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Conforming publicly but not privately is called…
    • Internalisation
    • Identification
    • Obedience
    • Compliance
    Show the answer

    Answer: Compliance. Compliance is public agreement without private change.

  2. A new student copies classmates' answers on an unfamiliar topic because they seem to know. This is…
    • Informational social influence
    • Normative social influence
    • Obedience
    • Minority influence
    Show the answer

    Answer: Informational social influence. Conforming because others seem correct is informational.

  3. Why might Asch's findings be described as a "child of its time"?
    • They used children
    • They reflect 1950s US norms
    • They were run in the 2000s
    • They studied obedience
    Show the answer

    Answer: They reflect 1950s US norms. McCarthy-era culture may have encouraged conformity.

Key terms

Normative social influence
Conforming to be accepted by a group
Informational social influence
Conforming because others may be correct
Internalisation
Genuine private acceptance of a group's views
