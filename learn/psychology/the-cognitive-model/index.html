The Cognitive Model

Beck's cognitive triad and Ellis's ABC model link distress to thoughts and beliefs; attentional and interpretive biases maintain difficulties; cognitive therapies are effective, but causal direction is debated.

Psychology · Psychopathology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe Beck's cognitive model and Ellis's ABC model
  • Explain cognitive biases associated with distress
  • Evaluate the cognitive model
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to diagnosis or treatment. Anyone concerned about their own or someone else's mental health should speak to a GP or another qualified professional.

Beck and Ellis

Beck (1967) proposed that people prone to depression hold negative schemas and a cognitive triad: negative views of the self, the world and the future. Cognitive distortions, such as overgeneralising or catastrophising, maintain low mood. Ellis (1962) proposed the ABC model: an Activating event triggers Beliefs, which lead to emotional and behavioural Consequences. Irrational beliefs, such as "I must always succeed", produce distress.

Cognitive biases

Research has found attentional biases towards threat in anxiety, interpretive biases (seeing ambiguous situations as threatening) and rumination in depression. These processes may maintain difficulties, which forms the basis of cognitive therapies.

Worked example

Applying the ABC model

  1. A: A friend doesn't reply to a message.
  2. B: "They must be angry with me; nobody likes me."
  3. C: Feeling low and avoiding contact. Changing the belief, for example "they may just be busy", can change the consequence. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

The cognitive model has strong practical support, as cognitive therapies are effective for many conditions. But it is unclear whether negative thinking causes distress or results from it; longitudinal studies suggest both. Critics argue it can underplay real adversity and social circumstances by focusing on how people interpret events.

Investigate with the lab. Use the thought record to trace how a situation leads to a thought, an emotion and a behaviour.

Common mistakeAssuming negative thoughts are always irrational. Some reflect genuinely difficult circumstances.

In 2 lines: Beck's triad and distortions; Ellis's ABC model. Attentional biases and rumination; causal direction and social context debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Thinking that one mistake means everything will go wrong is a…
    • Schema therapy
    • Cognitive distortion
    • Neurotransmitter effect
    • Moral treatment
    Show the answer

    Answer: Cognitive distortion. Expecting the worst from one mistake is catastrophising, a cognitive distortion.

  2. After failing a test, a student thinks "I'm stupid and will never succeed". In the ABC model, this thought is the…
    • Activating event
    • Consequence
    • Belief
    • Schema only
    Show the answer

    Answer: Belief. In the ABC model, the person's interpretation of the event is the belief.

  3. Why do critics say the cognitive model may underplay social circumstances?
    • It focuses only on wider society
    • It ignores thoughts completely
    • It rejects therapy of every kind
    • It focuses on interpretation, not real adversity
    Show the answer

    Answer: It focuses on interpretation, not real adversity. Some distress reflects real difficulties.

  4. In Beck's cognitive model, what drives emotional reactions?
    • How we interpret events
    • The events themselves alone
    • Unconscious drives
    • Reinforcement history only
    Show the answer

    Answer: How we interpret events. Beck argued that interpretations, not events, shape emotions.

Key terms

Cognitive triad
Negative views of the self, the world and the future
Cognitive distortion
A biased way of thinking, such as catastrophising
ABC model
Ellis's model of activating event, belief and consequence
