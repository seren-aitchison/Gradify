Childhood

Childhood brings theory of mind around age 4, steadily improving executive function and an increasingly psychological self-concept.

Psychology · Developmental Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the development of theory of mind and the false-belief task
  • Explain the development of executive function and self-control
  • Explain how self-concept and social understanding change through childhood

Theory of mind

Theory of mind is understanding that other people have beliefs, desires and intentions that can differ from one's own. It is often tested with false-belief tasks.

Worked example

The Sally–Anne task

  1. Sally puts her marble in a basket and leaves. Anne moves it to a box. Where will Sally look for her marble?
  2. Most 4-year-olds say the basket, understanding Sally's false belief; most 3-year-olds say the box.
  3. Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith (1985) found 85% of typically developing children and 86% of children with Down syndrome passed, but only 20% of autistic children, though later research shows great variation among autistic people.

Executive function

Executive functions include working memory, inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility. They develop steadily through childhood and adolescence as the prefrontal cortex matures. In Mischel's "marshmallow" studies, children who waited longer for a second treat had better later outcomes, but a larger replication found the link was much weaker once family background was taken into account (Watts, Duncan and Quan, 2018).

Self and others

Young children describe themselves in concrete terms ("I have a red bike"); by middle childhood they use traits and social comparisons ("I'm good at maths"). Understanding of emotions, fairness and friendship grows. Gender identity develops early: most children label their own gender by about age 2–3.

Common mistakeOverstating the marshmallow test's predictive power. Replications with larger, more diverse samples found much smaller links.

In 2 lines: Theory of mind typically emerges around age 4, tested by false-belief tasks. Executive function develops gradually; self-concept becomes trait-based and comparative.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. At about what age do most children pass the Sally–Anne task?
    • 1 year
    • 8 years
    • 12 years
    • 4 years
    Show the answer

    Answer: 4 years. Most typically developing children pass around age 4.

  2. A child says Sally will look in the box, where the marble now is. What does this suggest?
    • They don't yet understand false belief
    • They have strong theory of mind
    • They have poor eyesight
    • They are older than 6
    Show the answer

    Answer: They don't yet understand false belief. Answering with reality, not Sally's belief, shows no false-belief understanding yet.

  3. Why should the Sally–Anne autism findings be interpreted cautiously?
    • All autistic children failed
    • Autistic people vary greatly
    • The task is perfect
    • Theory of mind is fixed
    Show the answer

    Answer: Autistic people vary greatly. Later research shows wide variation and task limitations.

Key terms

Theory of mind
Understanding that others have beliefs and desires different from one's own
False-belief task
A test of whether a child understands that someone can hold a mistaken belief
Executive function
Cognitive control processes including working memory, inhibition and flexibility
