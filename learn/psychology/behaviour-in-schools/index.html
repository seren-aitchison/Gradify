Behaviour in Schools

Behaviour can be shaped by clear routines and reinforcement and supported by relationships and restorative approaches; evidence favours combining structure with support.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe behaviourist approaches to classroom behaviour
  • Explain relational and whole-school approaches
  • Evaluate evidence on behaviour management strategies

Behaviourist approaches

Behaviourist approaches use reinforcement (praise, rewards) and consequences (sanctions) to shape behaviour. Clear rules, routines and consistent responses help pupils know what is expected. Token economies and behaviour charts give points for desired behaviour. Evidence shows specific praise and consistent routines can improve classroom behaviour, though rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation if overused.

Relational and whole-school approaches

Relational approaches emphasise trusting teacher–pupil relationships, understanding causes of behaviour and teaching social and emotional skills. Restorative practice brings together those involved in an incident to repair harm. Trauma-informed approaches recognise that adverse experiences can affect behaviour. The EEF's behaviour guidance (2019) recommends knowing pupils, teaching learning behaviours, using classroom management strategies and consistency across the school.

Worked example

The Good Behaviour Game

  1. In the Good Behaviour Game, classes are divided into teams that earn rewards when all members follow agreed rules.
  2. US trials found reductions in disruptive behaviour and some long-term benefits (Kellam et al., 2008).
  3. An EEF-funded trial in England found no overall impact on reading attainment, though there were some behaviour benefits for boys at risk of conduct problems.

Evaluation

Strict "zero tolerance" approaches are debated: supporters argue they create calm, consistent environments; critics argue they can increase exclusions, especially for pupils with special educational needs. Behaviour is shaped by needs, relationships and context, so effective approaches combine clear structure with support.

Investigate with the lab. Read an ABAB graph of classroom behaviour and see how noise changes what you can conclude.

Common mistakeAssuming one behaviour strategy suits all pupils. Needs and context differ.

In 2 lines: Behaviourist approaches use routines, reinforcement and consequences. Relational, restorative and trauma-informed approaches add support; evidence favours structure plus support.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Giving pupils points for following rules that can be exchanged for rewards is a…
    • Restorative circle
    • Token economy
    • Trauma-informed approach
    • Detention
    Show the answer

    Answer: Token economy. Points exchanged for rewards are tokens that reinforce desired behaviour.

  2. After a fight, both pupils meet with a teacher to discuss the harm and agree how to make amends. This is…
    • A token economy
    • Zero tolerance
    • Restorative practice
    • Exclusion
    Show the answer

    Answer: Restorative practice. Bringing those involved together to repair harm is restorative practice.

  3. Why are zero-tolerance policies debated?
    • They never create calm
    • They always reduce exclusions
    • They ignore rules
    • They may increase exclusions for some groups
    Show the answer

    Answer: They may increase exclusions for some groups. Strictness may disproportionately affect pupils with SEN.

  4. In an ABAB design, what does the first A phase measure?
    • The baseline before any intervention
    • The effect of the intervention
    • Behaviour after the study ends
    • The teacher's behaviour only
    Show the answer

    Answer: The baseline before any intervention. A is the baseline; B is the intervention phase.

Key terms

Token economy
A system where tokens earned for desired behaviour can be exchanged for rewards
Restorative practice
Bringing people together to repair harm after an incident
Trauma-informed approach
Practice that recognises the effects of adverse experiences on behaviour
