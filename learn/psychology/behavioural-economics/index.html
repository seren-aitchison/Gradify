Behavioural Economics

Prospect theory explains loss aversion and reference points; framing and choice architecture shape decisions; nudge effects are debated, but defaults such as pension auto-enrolment are powerful.

Psychology · Contemporary Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain prospect theory and loss aversion
  • Describe framing effects and choice architecture
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of nudges

Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) proposed prospect theory: people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, not as final states of wealth. Loss aversion means losses feel larger than equivalent gains. People tend to be risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses.

Framing and choice architecture

Tversky and Kahneman (1981) showed framing effects: people chose differently between identical options described as lives saved or lives lost. Thaler and Sunstein (2008) described choice architecture: how options are presented influences choices. A nudge changes choice architecture without forbidding options or significantly changing incentives, such as making pension saving the default.

Worked example

Do nudges work?

  1. Mertens et al. (2022) meta-analysed choice architecture studies and reported a small to medium average effect.
  2. Maier et al. (2022) re-analysed the same studies and argued that after adjusting for publication bias, there was no evidence of an overall effect, though some types of nudge may still work.
  3. Default effects, such as automatic enrolment in workplace pensions in the UK from 2012, have shown large real-world effects.

Evaluation

Behavioural economics has changed policy and shown that context shapes choice. But effects of many nudges may be smaller than early studies suggested, and critics raise ethical concerns about manipulation. Defaults appear among the most robust interventions.

Common mistakeAssuming every nudge works. Many effects shrink after correcting for publication bias.

In 2 lines: Prospect theory: reference points and loss aversion. Framing and nudges; defaults are robust, average nudge effects debated.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Changing how options are presented without restricting them is a…
    • Tax
    • Ban
    • Subsidy
    • Nudge
    Show the answer

    Answer: Nudge. Nudges alter choice architecture without banning options or changing incentives much.

  2. More people choose a surgery described as having a "90% survival rate" than one with a "10% mortality rate". This is…
    • A framing effect
    • Loss aversion only
    • A default effect
    • Anchoring
    Show the answer

    Answer: A framing effect. Identical options described differently change choices.

  3. Why do critics raise ethical concerns about nudges?
    • They ban many of people's options
    • They may steer choices without people noticing
    • They always fail to work at all
    • They raise taxes on all consumers
    Show the answer

    Answer: They may steer choices without people noticing. Influencing choices covertly raises autonomy concerns.

Key terms

Framing effect
