The Cognitive Approach

The cognitive approach treats the mind as an information processor whose workings are inferred from careful measures of behaviour, now often combined with brain imaging.

Psychology · Foundations of Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the assumptions of the cognitive approach, including schemas and the information-processing model
  • Explain how cognitive psychologists infer mental processes from behaviour
  • Evaluate the cognitive approach, including the computer analogy

Assumptions

  • Mental processes can and should be studied scientifically, by inference from behaviour.
  • The mind processes information: input, storage, processing and output (the information-processing model).
  • People use schemas, mental frameworks built from experience, to interpret new information quickly, sometimes at the cost of accuracy.
  • Mental processes can be modelled and tested, for example as box-and-arrow models or computer simulations.

Methods

Cognitive psychologists use experiments that measure accuracy and reaction time. For example, if people take longer to respond when two tasks compete for the same resource, the tasks are inferred to share a processing system. Models such as the multi-store model of memory (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) are tested against such data. Since the 1990s, cognitive neuroscience has added brain imaging to link processes to brain systems.

Worked example

Schemas in action

  1. Bartlett (1932) asked English participants to retell an unfamiliar Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts".
  2. Over retellings the story became shorter and more conventional; unfamiliar details were changed to fit participants' own expectations.
  3. He concluded memory is reconstructive, shaped by schemas. (His procedure was informal by modern standards, but later controlled studies support the idea.)

Evaluation

Strengths: scientific methods; wide applications, including eyewitness testimony, cognitive behavioural therapy and interface design; integration with neuroscience. Limitations: the computer analogy overlooks emotion, motivation and the body (embodied cognition); laboratory tasks can lack ecological validity; and inferred processes risk becoming abstract boxes that explain little.

Common mistakeTreating the computer analogy as a claim that the brain works like a laptop. It is a metaphor for describing information flow, not a claim about hardware.

In 2 lines: The cognitive approach studies mental processes scientifically by inference, using the information-processing model and schemas. It is rigorous and widely applied but can neglect emotion, context and the body.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Which analogy is central to the cognitive approach?
    • The mind as a blank slate
    • The mind as a hydraulic system of drives
    • The mind as an information processor, like a computer
    • The mind as a reflex machine
    Show the answer

    Answer: The mind as an information processor, like a computer. The information-processing analogy describes input, storage, processing and output.

  2. People told a story about a "restaurant" recall details that weren't mentioned, such as paying the bill. What does this show?
    • Memory works like a perfect recording of events
    • Stories are learned by classical conditioning
    • Participants were deliberately lying to please the researcher
    • Schemas fill gaps in memory with expected information
    Show the answer

    Answer: Schemas fill gaps in memory with expected information. Restaurant schemas supply typical events, so recall includes details consistent with the schema.

  3. Which is the strongest criticism of the computer analogy?
    • Human cognition is shaped by emotion, motivation and the body in ways computers aren't
    • Computers process information much faster than any human brain can
    • Psychologists can't use computers to run their cognitive experiments
    • Computers have a memory store but human minds have no equivalent
    Show the answer

    Answer: Human cognition is shaped by emotion, motivation and the body in ways computers aren't. Critics argue that emotion, bodily states and context shape cognition, which a purely computational metaphor can miss (embodied cognition).

Key terms

Schema
A mental framework of knowledge and expectations built from experience
Information-processing model
Describes the mind as receiving, storing, transforming and outputting information in stages
Cognitive neuroscience
The study of how brain systems carry out mental processes
