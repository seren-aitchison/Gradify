Cognitive Dissonance

Inconsistency between cognitions creates dissonance, which people reduce by changing attitudes, behaviour or beliefs.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Define cognitive dissonance and the ways people reduce it
  • Explain the findings of Festinger and Carlsmith's induced compliance study
  • Evaluate dissonance theory against self-perception theory

Dissonance and its reduction

Festinger (1957) proposed that holding inconsistent cognitions (for example, "smoking is harmful" and "I smoke") creates unpleasant tension called cognitive dissonance. People reduce it by changing behaviour (quitting), changing a belief ("the risks are exaggerated"), adding consonant beliefs ("smoking keeps my weight down") or trivialising the conflict.

Worked example

One dollar or twenty?

  1. Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) had participants do a boring task, then paid them $1 or $20 to tell the next participant it was interesting.
  2. Afterwards, those paid $1 rated the task as more enjoyable than those paid $20.
  3. With $20, the lie was justified by the payment; with $1 it wasn't, so participants reduced dissonance by changing their attitude.

Other paradigms

Effort justification: Aronson and Mills (1959) found women who went through a severe (embarrassing) initiation to join a discussion group rated it as more interesting than those with a mild initiation. Post-decision dissonance: after a difficult choice, people rate the chosen option more highly and the rejected one less (Brehm, 1956).

Alternative explanation

Bem's self-perception theory (1967) suggests people infer their attitudes from their behaviour, as an observer would, without any tension: "I said it was fun for only $1, so I must have enjoyed it." Studies showing physiological arousal during dissonance, and that dissonance effects disappear when people can attribute arousal to a pill, support Festinger's view (Zanna and Cooper, 1974). Both processes may operate in different conditions.

Common mistakeAssuming bigger rewards produce bigger attitude change. In induced compliance, smaller rewards produce more change.

In 2 lines: Dissonance arises from inconsistency and is reduced by changing attitudes, behaviour or beliefs. Classic studies support it; self-perception theory is an alternative for some findings.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Who proposed cognitive dissonance theory?
    • Leon Festinger
    • Daryl Bem
    • Solomon Asch
    • Fritz Heider
    Show the answer

    Answer: Leon Festinger. Leon Festinger introduced cognitive dissonance theory in 1957.

  2. After buying an expensive phone, a person reads only positive reviews of it. This reduces…
    • Effort justification only
    • Post-decision dissonance
    • The halo effect
    • Self-perception
    Show the answer

    Answer: Post-decision dissonance. Seeking supportive information reduces post-decision dissonance.

  3. What did Zanna and Cooper's "pill" study suggest?
    • Self-perception explains everything
    • Pills cause attitudes
    • Dissonance involves arousal
    • Arousal never matters
    Show the answer

    Answer: Dissonance involves arousal. Effects vanished when arousal could be blamed on a pill.

Key terms

Cognitive dissonance
Discomfort from holding inconsistent cognitions
Effort justification
Valuing something more because of effort spent obtaining it
Self-perception theory
Bem's theory that people infer attitudes from their own behaviour
