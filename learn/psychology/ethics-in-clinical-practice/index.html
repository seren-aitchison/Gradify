Ethics in Clinical Practice

The BPS principles are respect, competence, responsibility and integrity; confidentiality has limits for serious risk, safeguarding and law; informed consent includes the limits of confidentiality; dilemmas need consultation.

Psychology · Clinical Psychology and Interventions · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe the BPS ethical principles
  • Explain confidentiality, its limits and informed consent
  • Evaluate how practitioners handle ethical dilemmas
Competence and safetyThis lesson is educational and is not a guide to therapy or legal advice. Ethical decisions in practice are made by qualified, registered professionals.

Ethical principles

The British Psychological Society's Code of Ethics and Conduct is based on four principles: respect (for dignity, privacy and self-determination), competence (working within one's skills), responsibility (avoiding harm and acting in clients' interests) and integrity (honesty and fairness). Practitioner psychologists in the UK must also meet the Health and Care Professions Council's standards of conduct, performance and ethics.

Confidentiality and consent

Confidentiality is central to trust, but it is not absolute. Information may need to be shared without consent where there is a serious risk of harm to the client or others, a safeguarding concern about a child or vulnerable adult, or a legal requirement. Informed consent means clients understand what therapy involves, its likely benefits and risks, alternatives and the limits of confidentiality before agreeing.

Worked example

An ethical dilemma

  1. A client discloses that a child in their household is being harmed.
  2. The psychologist must balance confidentiality against the duty to protect the child.
  3. Safeguarding procedures require sharing information with appropriate services, ideally explaining this to the client. This example is illustrative.

Evaluation

Ethical codes provide shared standards and protect the public. But principles can conflict, as when respect for autonomy clashes with preventing harm, so practitioners use structured decision-making, consultation and supervision. Cultural differences in values, such as about family involvement, add complexity.

Common mistakeAssuming everything said in therapy stays confidential. Serious risk and safeguarding can require sharing.

In 2 lines: Respect, competence, responsibility and integrity guide practice. Confidentiality has limits; informed consent; dilemmas need consultation.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Agreeing to therapy with a clear understanding of what it involves is…
    • Confidentiality
    • Informed consent
    • Safeguarding
    • Supervision
    Show the answer

    Answer: Informed consent. Informed consent requires understanding before agreeing.

  2. A psychologist realises a client's needs are beyond their training and refers them to a specialist. This reflects the principle of…
    • Integrity only
    • Confidentiality
    • Competence
    • Formulation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Competence. Working within one's skills is competence.

  3. Why do ethical principles sometimes conflict?
    • The principles always agree with each other
    • Ethics has only a single principle
    • Ethical codes forbid any consultation
    • Respect for autonomy can clash with preventing harm
    Show the answer

    Answer: Respect for autonomy can clash with preventing harm. Competing duties require careful judgement.

Key terms
