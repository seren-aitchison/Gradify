Psychology and Public Health

Psychology informs population health through nudges, campaigns and policy; structural measures such as the sugar levy can change environments; interventions must consider inequalities.

Psychology · Applied Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Explain how psychology informs population-level health interventions
  • Evaluate nudges and mass media campaigns
  • Explain why psychological interventions must address health inequalities

Population approaches

Rose (1985) argued that small changes across a whole population can prevent more illness than targeting only high-risk individuals (the prevention paradox). Public health interventions include legislation (smoking bans, sugar taxes), environmental change, mass media campaigns and nudges.

Nudges and campaigns

Nudges (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008) change the choice environment without restricting options, such as making healthier foods more visible or setting opt-out defaults. Organ donation in England moved to an opt-out system in 2020. Mass media campaigns can raise awareness, but effects on behaviour are often small unless combined with other measures (Wakefield, Loken and Hornik, 2010).

Worked example

The soft drinks industry levy

  1. The UK introduced a tax on sugary soft drinks in April 2018, with higher rates for drinks with more sugar.
  2. Many manufacturers reduced sugar in their drinks before the levy started (reformulation).
  3. Scarborough et al. (2020) found the sugar content of soft drinks in the UK fell substantially between the levy's announcement in 2016 and 2019, showing how policy can change environments as well as individual choices.

Inequalities and COVID-19

Interventions relying on individual effort can widen health inequalities if better-off groups respond more (intervention-generated inequality). During COVID-19, behavioural science informed messaging on handwashing, distancing and vaccination; adherence was affected by trust, practical barriers (such as being unable to work from home) and financial support for isolation. Population-level structural measures tend to reduce inequalities more than information campaigns.

Common mistakeAssuming information campaigns alone change behaviour. Structural and environmental measures are often more effective.

In 2 lines: Population approaches include legislation, environments, campaigns and nudges. The sugar levy shows structural change; interventions must avoid widening inequalities.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Making healthier foods more visible in a canteen without banning others is a…
    • Tax
    • Ban
    • Nudge
    • Mass campaign
    Show the answer

    Answer: Nudge. Changing choice architecture without restricting options is a nudge.

  2. Manufacturers reduce sugar in drinks before a sugar tax starts. This is called…
    • Self-monitoring
    • Social proof
    • A dark pattern
    • Reformulation
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reformulation. Changing products to avoid the levy is reformulation.

  3. Why can individual-focused interventions widen inequalities?
    • Better-off groups often respond more
    • Everyone responds equally
    • They target only the poor
    • Structural measures always widen gaps
    Show the answer

    Answer: Better-off groups often respond more. Better-off groups often have more resources to respond, so benefits are unequal.

Key terms

Prevention paradox
Rose's idea that small population-wide changes prevent more illness than targeting high-risk groups
Nudge
A change to the choice environment that guides behaviour without restricting options
Intervention-generated inequality
When interventions benefit better-off groups more, widening inequality
