Personality Across Cultures

Big Five structure replicates in many cultures, but national stereotypes are inaccurate, emic research finds additional dimensions and cross-cultural comparisons need care.

Psychology · Personality and Individual Differences · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Describe evidence on the universality of personality structure
  • Explain research comparing average traits across cultures and national stereotypes
  • Evaluate etic and emic approaches to personality across cultures

Same structure everywhere?

McCrae and Terracciano (2005) analysed observer ratings in 50 cultures and found the five-factor structure replicated in most. Schmitt et al. (2007) found broadly similar results with self-reports in 56 nations. Replication was weaker in some lower-literacy samples, such as the Tsimane of Bolivia, where traits clustered into fewer dimensions (Gurven et al., 2013).

Worked example

National character

  1. Terracciano et al. (2005) asked people in 49 cultures to describe the "typical" member of their own culture.
  2. These national stereotypes were fairly consistent within each culture.
  3. But they didn't match the average personality scores of people in those cultures, suggesting national character stereotypes are largely unfounded.

Etic and emic

An etic approach applies a framework developed in one culture (such as the Big Five) to others. An emic approach develops concepts from within a culture. The Chinese Personality Assessment Inventory (Cheung et al., 1996) found an interpersonal relatedness factor, including harmony and "face", not well captured by the Big Five. Combining both approaches gives a fuller picture.

Interpreting comparisons

Comparing average trait scores across cultures is difficult: people may use rating scales differently, compare themselves with different reference groups (the reference group effect) and items may not mean the same thing after translation. Differences within cultures are much larger than average differences between them.

Common mistakeAssuming national stereotypes reflect real average personality. Evidence suggests they often don't.

In 2 lines: The Big Five replicate widely but not universally; national stereotypes don't match average scores. Emic research adds dimensions such as interpersonal relatedness; comparisons face measurement problems.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. Applying the Big Five to a new culture is an example of…
    • An emic approach
    • A projective test
    • A case study
    • An etic approach
    Show the answer

    Answer: An etic approach. Applying a framework developed in another culture is an etic approach.

  2. Researchers in Hong Kong develop a test including "harmony" and "face". This approach is…
    • Emic
    • Etic
    • Projective
    • Behaviourist
    Show the answer

    Answer: Emic. Concepts from within the culture make it emic.

  3. Why are average trait comparisons between cultures difficult?
    • Cultures have no traits
    • Reference group effects distort ratings
    • All scores are identical
    • Translation is always perfect
    Show the answer

    Answer: Reference group effects distort ratings. People rate themselves relative to their own culture.

Key terms

Etic approach
Applying a framework from one culture to others
Emic approach
Developing concepts from within a culture
Reference group effect
Rating oneself relative to one's own cultural group, distorting comparisons
