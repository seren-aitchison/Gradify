Social Norms

Descriptive norms say what people do and injunctive norms what they should do; combining them can change behaviour without boomerang effects.

Psychology · Social Psychology · about 2 minutes

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish descriptive and injunctive norms
  • Explain how norms form and are maintained
  • Apply social norms research to behaviour change, including the boomerang effect

Two kinds of norm

Descriptive norms describe what most people do ("most people recycle"). Injunctive norms describe what people approve of or should do ("people ought to recycle"). Cialdini, Reno and Kallgren (1990) found people littered less in a clean environment (a descriptive norm against littering) and least when they saw a single person pick up litter, which made the injunctive norm salient.

Forming and keeping norms

Sherif's autokinetic studies showed groups converge on shared norms that persist. Norms are maintained by approval and disapproval, and violations draw sanctions ranging from a frown to exclusion. People may also follow norms they privately reject, a pattern called pluralistic ignorance: Prentice and Miller (1993) found many students privately felt less comfortable with campus drinking than they believed their peers did.

Worked example

Hotel towels

  1. Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius (2008) placed different cards in hotel rooms encouraging guests to reuse towels.
  2. A standard environmental message led to about 35% reuse; a message saying most guests reuse towels led to about 44%.
  3. The most effective message said most previous guests in that same room reused towels (49%).

The boomerang effect

Norm messages can backfire. Schultz et al. (2007) told households how their energy use compared with neighbours. Below-average users increased their use towards the average (a boomerang effect) unless the message also included an approving smiley face, an injunctive norm. Messages that stress how common a bad behaviour is ("many people drink-drive") can also make it seem normal.

Common mistakePublicising how common a problem is. Emphasising widespread bad behaviour can increase it.

In 2 lines: Descriptive and injunctive norms both influence behaviour. Norm messages can change behaviour, but may boomerang without injunctive cues.

Practice questions

Answer each one in your head before you open it: recalling beats rereading.

  1. "Most students at this school vote in elections" is a…
    • Injunctive norm
    • Script
    • Self-schema
    • Descriptive norm
    Show the answer

    Answer: Descriptive norm. It describes what most people actually do, so it is a descriptive norm.

  2. A household using less energy than neighbours increases its use after a comparison letter. What has happened?
    • A boomerang effect
    • Pluralistic ignorance
    • Low-balling
    • Reactance only
    Show the answer

    Answer: A boomerang effect. Below-average users moved towards the norm.

  3. Why should an anti-drink-driving campaign avoid saying "thousands of people drink-drive"?
    • It is always untrue
    • It may make drink-driving seem normal
    • It reduces drink-driving too much
    • It uses an injunctive norm
    Show the answer

    Answer: It may make drink-driving seem normal. Emphasising prevalence creates a descriptive norm for the bad behaviour.

  4. In Asch's study, roughly how often did participants conform to a clearly wrong majority?
    • Almost never
    • About 90% of the time
    • About a third of the time
    • Every time
    Show the answer

    Answer: About a third of the time. Participants conformed on about 37% of critical trials.
