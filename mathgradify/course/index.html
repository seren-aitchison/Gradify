Schools and lessons

School 1: Number and Arithmetic

29 lessons

Number and Arithmetic: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Arithmetic: Arithmetic is calculating with numbers: adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing.
  2. Order of Operations: When a calculation has several operations, the order you do them in changes the answer.
  3. Number Systems: A number system is a way of representing numbers with symbols.
  4. Natural Numbers: The natural numbers are the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, … (and, in many university courses, 0 as well).
  5. Integers: The integers extend the natural numbers to include zero and the negatives: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ….
  6. Rational Numbers: Rational numbers are numbers that can be written as a fraction of two integers, p/q with q not zero.
  7. Irrational Numbers: An irrational number can't be written as a fraction of two integers.
  8. Real Numbers: The real numbers are all the rational and irrational numbers together: every point on the number line.
  9. Factors and Multiples: A factor of a number divides it exactly; a multiple is the result of multiplying it by a whole number.
  10. Divisibility: Divisibility tests tell you whether one number divides another without doing the division.
  11. Prime Numbers: A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.
  12. Composite Numbers: A composite number is a natural number greater than 1 that isn't prime: it has at least one factor other than 1 and itself.
  13. Prime Factorisation: Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from order.
  14. Greatest Common Divisor (GCD): The greatest common divisor (GCD), called the highest common factor (HCF) at GCSE, is the largest number that divides two numbers exactly.
  15. Least Common Multiple (LCM): The least common multiple (LCM), called the lowest common multiple at GCSE, is the smallest number that is a multiple of two numbers.
  16. Fractions: A fraction represents part of a whole, or a division: 3/4 means 3 ÷ 4.
  17. Decimals: Decimals extend place value to the right of the units: tenths, hundredths, thousandths.
  18. Percentages: A percentage is a fraction out of 100.
  19. Percentage Change and Reverse Percentages: Percentage change measures an increase or decrease relative to the original amount.
  20. Ratios and Proportions: A ratio compares the sizes of quantities; a proportion says two ratios are equal.
  21. Exponents and Powers: A power (exponent, index) tells you how many times to multiply a number by itself.
  22. Roots and Radicals: A root undoes a power: the square root of 49 is 7 because 7² = 49.
  23. Scientific Notation: Scientific notation (standard form) writes numbers as A × 10ⁿ, with 1 ≤ A < 10.
  24. Rounding and Estimation: Rounding simplifies numbers to a sensible accuracy; estimation uses rounded numbers to predict an answer.
  25. Significant Figures: Significant figures count the digits that carry meaning about a number's size and precision.
  26. Sequences of Numbers: A sequence is an ordered list of numbers following a rule.
  27. Number Patterns: Number patterns (square, triangular, cube and Fibonacci numbers, and patterns in tables) reveal structure in mathematics.
  28. Number Bases: A number base is the number of digits a positional system uses.
  29. Binary Arithmetic: Binary (base 2) uses only the digits 0 and 1, matching the off and on states of electronic circuits.

School 2: Number Theory and Cryptography

36 lessons

Number Theory and Cryptography: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Elementary Number Theory: Number theory studies the integers: divisibility, primes and the patterns they hide.
  2. Modular Arithmetic: Modular arithmetic is "clock arithmetic": numbers wrap around after reaching a modulus.
  3. Congruences: A congruence equation such as 3x ≡ 4 (mod 7) asks which integers satisfy a relationship modulo n.
  4. Euclidean Algorithm: The Euclidean algorithm finds the greatest common divisor of two integers by repeated division.
  5. Extended Euclidean Algorithm: The extended Euclidean algorithm finds integers x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b).
  6. Diophantine Equations: Diophantine equations are equations whose solutions must be integers, named after Diophantus of Alexandria.
  7. Divisor Functions: Divisor functions count or add the divisors of a number.
  8. Perfect Numbers: A perfect number equals the sum of its proper divisors, like 6 = 1 + 2 + 3.
  9. Fibonacci Numbers: The Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … each add the two before.
  10. Continued Fractions: A continued fraction writes a number as an integer plus 1 over (an integer plus 1 over …).
  11. Prime Number Theory: Prime number theory studies how primes are distributed.
  12. Fermat's Little Theorem: Fermat's little theorem says that for a prime p and any a not divisible by p, a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p).
  13. Euler's Totient Function: Euler's totient function φ(n) counts the numbers from 1 to n that are coprime to n.
  14. Euler's Theorem: Euler's theorem generalises Fermat's little theorem to any modulus: if gcd(a, n) = 1, then a^(φ(n)) ≡ 1 (mod n).
  15. Chinese Remainder Theorem: The Chinese remainder theorem says that a system of congruences with pairwise coprime moduli has exactly one solution modulo their product.
  16. Quadratic Residues: A quadratic residue modulo p is a number that's a perfect square mod p.
  17. Primitive Roots: A primitive root modulo p is a number whose powers run through every non-zero residue.
  18. Pell's Equation: Pell's equation x² − Dy² = 1, for a positive non-square integer D, always has infinitely many integer solutions: every positive solution is a power of the smallest (fundamental) one, and sign changes give the rest.
  19. Diophantine Approximation: Diophantine approximation asks how closely real numbers can be approximated by fractions with small denominators.
  20. Analytic Number Theory: Analytic number theory uses calculus and complex analysis to study integers, especially primes.
  21. Algebraic Number Theory: Algebraic number theory studies number systems such as the Gaussian integers and ℤ[√−5].
  22. Computational Number Theory: Computational number theory designs algorithms for number-theoretic problems: testing primality, factorising, computing gcds and discrete logarithms.
  23. Elliptic Curves: An elliptic curve is a curve y² = x³ + ax + b whose points can be added together, forming a group.
  24. Arithmetic Geometry: Arithmetic geometry studies the integer and rational solutions of polynomial equations using geometry.
  25. Cryptography: Cryptography protects information: keeping it secret, proving it hasn't been altered and confirming who sent it.
  26. Public-Key Cryptography: Public-key cryptography lets strangers communicate securely without sharing a secret in advance.
  27. Modular Exponentiation: Modular exponentiation computes a^e mod n.
  28. RSA Cryptography: RSA, published by Rivest, Shamir and Adleman in 1977, was the first practical public-key system for encryption and signatures.
  29. Discrete Logarithm Problem: The discrete logarithm problem asks, given g and h = gˣ mod p, to find x.
  30. Elliptic Curve Cryptography: Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) uses the group of points on an elliptic curve over a finite field.
  31. Cryptographic Hash Functions: A cryptographic hash function turns any input into a short fixed-length digest that acts like a fingerprint.
  32. Primality Testing: Primality tests decide whether a number is prime without factorising it.
  33. Integer Factorisation: Integer factorisation is splitting a number into primes.
  34. Prime Factorisation Algorithms: Beyond trial division, mathematicians have built clever factorisation algorithms that exploit hidden structure: Pollard's rho and p − 1 methods, Lenstra's elliptic curve method, and the quadratic and number field sieves.
  35. Lattice-Based Cryptography: Lattice-based cryptography builds security on hard geometric problems in high-dimensional lattices, such as finding short vectors.
  36. Post-Quantum Cryptography: Post-quantum cryptography is public-key cryptography designed to resist attacks by large quantum computers.

School 3: Algebra

45 lessons

Algebra: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Basic Algebra: Algebra uses letters to stand for numbers, so that we can describe patterns, rules and relationships that hold for many values at once.
  2. Elementary Algebra: Elementary algebra is the toolkit of school algebra: the rules for manipulating expressions and equations, built on the properties of numbers.
  3. Algebraic Expressions: An algebraic expression is built from terms joined by + and −.
  4. Simplifying Expressions: Simplifying an expression means writing it in an equivalent, shorter form by collecting like terms and using index laws.
  5. Expanding Brackets: Expanding brackets means multiplying out so that an expression is written as a sum of terms.
  6. Factorisation: Factorising writes an expression as a product of factors.
  7. Algebraic Manipulation: Algebraic manipulation is rearranging, substituting and transforming expressions and formulae while keeping them equal.
  8. Algebraic Fractions: Algebraic fractions have expressions on the top or bottom.
  9. Linear Equations: A linear equation has the unknown only to the first power, like 3x + 5 = 20.
  10. Simultaneous Equations: Simultaneous equations are two or more equations that must be true at the same time.
  11. Quadratic Equations: A quadratic equation has the unknown squared, like x² − 5x + 6 = 0.
  12. Completing the Square: Completing the square rewrites x² + bx + c as (x + p)² + q.
  13. The Quadratic Formula: The quadratic formula gives the solutions of any quadratic equation directly from its coefficients.
  14. Cubic Equations: A cubic equation has the unknown cubed, like x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0.
  15. Polynomial Equations: Polynomial equations of any degree arise in modelling and science.
  16. Inequalities: An inequality compares two quantities using <, >, ≤ or ≥.
  17. Simultaneous Inequalities: Simultaneous inequalities are several conditions that must all hold at once.
  18. Absolute Value Equations: The absolute value (modulus) |x| is the distance of x from zero, so it's never negative.
  19. Rational Equations: A rational equation contains algebraic fractions, like 2/x + 3/(x + 1) = 1.
  20. Surds: A surd is a root that can't be simplified to a rational number, such as √2 or √12.
  21. Logarithms: A logarithm answers the question "what power?": log₂ 8 = 3 because 2³ = 8.
  22. Exponential Equations: In an exponential equation the unknown is in a power, like 3ˣ = 20.
  23. Logarithmic Equations: Logarithmic equations have the unknown inside a logarithm.
  24. Polynomials: A polynomial is a sum of terms each made of a number times a whole-number power of the variable, like 3x⁴ − 2x + 7.
  25. Polynomial Algebra: Polynomial algebra is adding, subtracting and multiplying polynomials, and comparing their coefficients.
  26. Polynomial Division: Polynomials can be divided like numbers, giving a quotient and a remainder.
  27. Remainder Theorem: The remainder theorem gives the remainder of dividing a polynomial by (x − a) without doing the division: it's simply f(a).
  28. Factor Theorem: The factor theorem is the remainder theorem with remainder zero: (x − a) is a factor of f(x) exactly when f(a) = 0.
  29. Roots of Polynomials: The roots of a polynomial are the values that make it zero.
  30. Vieta's Formulas: Vieta's formulas link the roots of a polynomial to its coefficients: for example, the roots of x² + bx + c add to −b and multiply to c. They let you work with roots without finding them.
  31. Symmetric Polynomials: A symmetric polynomial is unchanged when its variables are swapped, like x + y or x² + y².
  32. Partial Fractions: Partial fractions split a complicated algebraic fraction into a sum of simpler ones.
  33. Functions: A function is a rule that takes each input and gives exactly one output.
  34. Domain and Range: The domain of a function is the set of inputs it accepts; the range is the set of outputs it actually produces.
  35. Composite Functions: A composite function applies one function and then another: fg(x) means "do g, then f".
  36. Inverse Functions: An inverse function undoes a function: if f takes 3 to 11, then f⁻¹ takes 11 back to 3.
  37. Transformations of Functions: Transformations move, stretch and reflect graphs.
  38. Piecewise Functions: A piecewise function uses different rules on different parts of its domain, like a tax system with bands or a phone tariff.
  39. Parametric Equations: Parametric equations describe a curve using a third variable, the parameter, for both coordinates: x = t², y = 2t.
  40. Implicit Equations: An implicit equation links x and y without making y the subject, like x² + y² = 25 or x³ + y³ = 6xy.
  41. Recurrence Relations: A recurrence relation defines each term of a sequence from earlier terms, like uₙ₊₁ = 2uₙ + 3.
  42. Intermediate Algebra: Intermediate algebra bridges GCSE and A level: confident manipulation of quadratics, functions, inequalities, surds and indices together, in multi-step problems.
  43. Advanced Algebra: Advanced algebra extends school methods with proof by induction, the binomial theorem for any power, series and complex roots.
  44. Complex Numbers: Complex numbers extend the reals with i, where i² = −1.
  45. Boolean Algebra: Boolean algebra is the algebra of true and false (1 and 0), with operations AND, OR and NOT.

School 4: Abstract Algebra

22 lessons

Abstract Algebra: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Abstract Algebra: Abstract algebra studies structures defined by rules (axioms) rather than by particular numbers: groups, rings, fields, modules.
  2. Algebraic Structures: An algebraic structure is a set with one or more operations satisfying axioms.
  3. Group Theory: Group theory studies symmetry.
  4. Homomorphisms: A homomorphism is a map between algebraic structures that respects their operations.
  5. Isomorphisms: An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism: it shows two structures are "the same" apart from the names of their elements.
  6. Automorphisms: An automorphism is an isomorphism from a structure to itself: a symmetry of the structure.
  7. Quotient Structures: A quotient structure "divides out" a substructure, treating elements as equal when they differ by something in it.
  8. Direct Products: The direct product combines two structures into one by working component by component.
  9. Free Groups: A free group on a set of generators has no relations except those forced by the group axioms.
  10. Ring Theory: A ring has addition and multiplication, linked by the distributive law.
  11. Field Theory: Field theory studies fields and their extensions: bigger fields containing smaller ones, like ℚ ⊂ ℚ(√2) ⊂ ℝ.
  12. Modules: A module is like a vector space but with scalars from a ring instead of a field.
  13. Algebras: An algebra over a field is a vector space with a compatible multiplication.
  14. Commutative Algebra: Commutative algebra studies commutative rings and their ideals.
  15. Galois Theory: Galois theory links field extensions to groups: the symmetries of the roots of a polynomial form its Galois group.
  16. Homological Algebra: Homological algebra studies sequences of algebraic objects and maps, measuring how far they fail to be exact.
  17. Universal Algebra: Universal algebra studies algebraic structures in general: sets with operations satisfying identities.
  18. Representation Theory: Representation theory studies abstract algebraic objects by making them act as linear maps on vector spaces.
  19. Group Representations: The representations of a finite group are captured by characters: the traces of the representing matrices.
  20. Ring Representations: A representation of a ring is the same thing as a module: the ring acts on an abelian group by linear maps.
  21. Lie Groups: A Lie group is a group that's also a smooth space, with smooth multiplication and inversion.
  22. Lie Algebras: A Lie algebra captures the infinitesimal structure of a Lie group: its tangent space at the identity with the bracket [X, Y] = XY − YX.

School 5: Geometry

50 lessons

Geometry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Euclidean Geometry: Euclidean geometry is the geometry of flat space, built by Euclid around 300 BCE from a handful of definitions and axioms.
  2. Plane Geometry: Plane geometry studies two-dimensional figures (points, lines, angles, polygons and circles) and their measurements.
  3. Points, Lines and Planes: Points, lines and planes are the basic undefined objects of geometry.
  4. Angles: An angle measures the amount of turn between two lines meeting at a point.
  5. Parallel and Perpendicular Lines: Parallel lines never meet; perpendicular lines meet at right angles.
  6. Triangles: Triangles are the simplest polygons and the building blocks of all others.
  7. Congruence and Similarity: Congruent shapes are identical in size and shape; similar shapes have the same shape but may differ in size.
  8. Quadrilaterals: Quadrilaterals are four-sided polygons: squares, rectangles, parallelograms, rhombuses, trapeziums and kites.
  9. Polygons: A polygon is a closed shape with straight sides.
  10. Pythagorean Theorem: Pythagoras' theorem says that in a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides.
  11. Circles: A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from a centre.
  12. Chords and Tangents: Chords and tangents connect lines and circles.
  13. Circle Theorems: The circle theorems describe the angles formed by chords, tangents and radii in a circle.
  14. Constructions: Geometric constructions use only a straight edge and compasses to draw accurate figures: perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, perpendiculars and triangles.
  15. Loci: A locus is the set of all points satisfying a rule, such as "always 3 m from a tree".
  16. Bearings: A bearing gives a direction as a three-figure angle measured clockwise from north.
  17. Perimeter: The perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a shape.
  18. Area: Area measures the amount of surface a 2D shape covers, in square units.
  19. Arc Length: An arc is part of a circle's circumference.
  20. Solid Geometry: Solid geometry studies three-dimensional shapes: prisms, pyramids, cylinders, cones and spheres.
  21. Polyhedra: A polyhedron is a solid with flat polygonal faces.
  22. Surface Area: The surface area of a solid is the total area of all its faces or curved surfaces.
  23. Volume: Volume measures the space a 3D object occupies, in cubic units.
  24. Geometric Transformations: Transformations move or change shapes: translations, reflections, rotations and enlargements.
  25. Transformations and Symmetry: A shape has symmetry if a transformation maps it onto itself.
  26. Tessellations: A tessellation is a pattern of shapes covering a plane with no gaps or overlaps.
  27. Coordinate Geometry: Coordinate geometry describes points, lines and shapes with numbers, so geometric problems can be solved with algebra.
  28. Analytic Geometry: Analytic geometry studies curves through their equations: lines, circles and the conics, in two and three dimensions.
  29. Vectors in Geometry: Vectors have size and direction, and they make geometric proofs remarkably clean.
  30. 3D Coordinate Geometry: 3D coordinate geometry extends coordinates to (x, y, z).
  31. Conic Sections: Conic sections are the curves formed by slicing a double cone with a plane: circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas.
  32. Parabolas: A parabola is the set of points equidistant from a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix).
  33. Ellipses: An ellipse is a stretched circle: the set of points whose distances to two foci add to a constant.
  34. Hyperbolas: A hyperbola is the set of points whose distances to two foci differ by a constant.
  35. Non-Euclidean Geometry: Non-Euclidean geometries keep Euclid's first four postulates but replace the parallel postulate.
  36. Spherical Geometry: Spherical geometry is geometry on the surface of a sphere, where the "straight lines" are great circles.
  37. Hyperbolic Geometry: Hyperbolic geometry has constant negative curvature: through a point not on a line there are infinitely many parallels, and triangles are "thin", with angle sums less than 180°.
  38. Affine Geometry: Affine geometry studies the properties preserved by affine transformations: those that keep straight lines straight and parallel lines parallel, though not lengths or angles.
  39. Projective Geometry: Projective geometry is the geometry of perspective: what stays the same when a scene is projected onto a canvas or a camera.
  40. Fractal Geometry: Fractals are shapes with detail at every scale, often self-similar: zoom in and you see copies of the whole.
  41. Discrete Geometry: Discrete geometry studies finite or countable sets of geometric objects: points, lines, polygons and lattices, and how they're arranged.
  42. Convex Geometry: A convex set contains the whole segment between any two of its points: discs, triangles and filled polygons are convex; crescents aren't.
  43. Computational Geometry: Computational geometry designs efficient algorithms for geometric problems: convex hulls, intersections, nearest neighbours, triangulations and Voronoi diagrams.
  44. Integral Geometry: Integral geometry measures sets of geometric objects (lines, needles, planes) using integrals, often with probability.
  45. Differential Geometry: Differential geometry uses calculus to study curves and surfaces: how they bend and twist.
  46. Riemannian Geometry: Riemannian geometry studies curved spaces of any dimension using a metric that measures lengths and angles at every point.
  47. Symplectic Geometry: Symplectic geometry is the geometry of classical mechanics' phase space, where positions and momenta live together.
  48. Algebraic Geometry: Algebraic geometry studies the shapes defined by polynomial equations, called varieties.
  49. Topological Geometry: Topological geometry studies the properties of shapes that survive stretching and bending without tearing or gluing.
  50. Knot Theory: A mathematical knot is a closed loop in 3D space that can't pass through itself.

School 6: Trigonometry

30 lessons

Trigonometry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Basic Trigonometry: Trigonometry studies the relationships between the angles and sides of triangles.
  2. Trigonometric Ratios: The trigonometric ratios sine, cosine and tangent compare pairs of sides in a right-angled triangle.
  3. Sine: The sine of an angle starts as a ratio in a right-angled triangle and becomes a function defined for every angle, using the unit circle.
  4. Cosine: The cosine is the x-coordinate of the point at angle θ on the unit circle.
  5. Tangent: The tangent of an angle is sin θ/cos θ, the gradient of the line at angle θ to the horizontal.
  6. Radians and Degrees: Radians measure angles by arc length: one radian is the angle subtended by an arc equal to the radius.
  7. Unit Circle: The unit circle (radius 1, centred at the origin) defines sine and cosine for every angle: the point at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ).
  8. Trigonometric Geometry: Trigonometry solves geometric problems: heights, distances, angles of elevation and depression, and 3D problems such as the angle between a line and a plane.
  9. Sine Rule: The sine rule relates the sides of any triangle to the sines of their opposite angles.
  10. Cosine Rule: The cosine rule generalises Pythagoras' theorem to any triangle.
  11. Area of a Triangle: The area of any triangle can be found from two sides and the included angle: ½ab sin C. It extends ½ × base × height to triangles whose height you don't know.
  12. Spherical Trigonometry: Spherical trigonometry solves triangles drawn on a sphere, whose sides are arcs of great circles.
  13. Trigonometric Graphs: The graphs of sine, cosine and tangent show their periodic behaviour.
  14. Periodic Functions: A periodic function repeats its values at regular intervals.
  15. Cosecant: Cosecant is the reciprocal of sine: cosec θ = 1/sin θ.
  16. Secant: Secant is the reciprocal of cosine: sec θ = 1/cos θ.
  17. Cotangent: Cotangent is the reciprocal of tangent: cot θ = cos θ/sin θ.
  18. Inverse Trigonometric Functions: The inverse trigonometric functions arcsin, arccos and arctan give an angle from a ratio.
  19. Sinusoidal Modelling: Sinusoidal models y = A sin(B(t − C)) + D describe repeating real-world patterns: tides, daylight hours, temperatures, sound and populations.
  20. Harmonic Functions: Harmonic motion is motion described by sines and cosines: springs, pendulums, vibrating strings and AC circuits.
  21. Trigonometric Identities: Trigonometric identities are equations true for every angle.
  22. Compound Angle Formulas: The compound angle formulas give the sine, cosine and tangent of a sum or difference of angles.
  23. Double Angle Formulas: The double angle formulas express sin 2A, cos 2A and tan 2A in terms of A. They come straight from the addition formulas, and they're vital for solving equations and integrating sin² x and cos² x.
  24. Half Angle Formulas: The half angle formulas give the trig functions of A/2 in terms of A. They follow from the double angle formulas and provide exact values such as sin 22.5°, plus the useful "t-substitution".
  25. Trigonometric Equations: Trigonometric equations usually have many solutions, because trig functions repeat.
  26. Trigonometric Inequalities: Trigonometric inequalities ask where a trig function is above or below a value.
  27. Hyperbolic Functions: The hyperbolic functions sinh, cosh and tanh are built from exponentials.
  28. Inverse Hyperbolic Functions: The inverse hyperbolic functions arsinh, arcosh and artanh undo sinh, cosh and tanh.
  29. Fourier Series: A Fourier series writes a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines of increasing frequencies.
  30. Fourier Analysis: Fourier analysis decomposes signals into their frequencies.

School 7: Calculus

44 lessons

Calculus: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Precalculus: Precalculus is the toolkit you need before calculus: functions and their graphs, algebraic manipulation, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometry.
  2. Infinitesimal Calculus: Calculus began with the idea of infinitely small changes, the "infinitesimals" of Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century.
  3. Limits: A limit describes the value a function approaches as its input approaches some value.
  4. Continuity: A function is continuous at a point if its graph has no break there: you could draw it without lifting your pen.
  5. Differentiability: A function is differentiable at a point if it has a well-defined tangent there: a single slope.
  6. Differential Calculus: Differential calculus studies how quantities change.
  7. Derivatives: A derivative is defined as the limit of the gradient of a chord, and computed in practice with rules.
  8. First Derivatives: The first derivative f′(x) tells you the gradient of a curve everywhere: where it rises, falls and levels off.
  9. Higher-Order Derivatives: Differentiating a derivative gives the second derivative, and so on.
  10. Chain Rule: The chain rule differentiates composite functions, functions of functions like (3x + 1)⁵ or e^(sin x).
  11. Product Rule: The product rule differentiates a product of two functions.
  12. Quotient Rule: The quotient rule differentiates one function divided by another.
  13. Implicit Differentiation: Implicit differentiation finds dy/dx when y isn't the subject, as on the circle x² + y² = 25.
  14. Logarithmic Differentiation: Logarithmic differentiation takes the log of both sides before differentiating.
  15. Parametric Differentiation: When a curve is given parametrically, x = f(t) and y = g(t), its gradient comes from the chain rule: dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt).
  16. Rates of Change: Derivatives measure how fast one quantity changes with another.
  17. Related Rates: Related rates problems connect the rates of change of several linked quantities.
  18. L'Hôpital's Rule: L'Hôpital's rule evaluates limits of the indeterminate forms 0/0 and ∞/∞ by differentiating the top and bottom separately.
  19. Stationary Points: Stationary points are where a curve's gradient is zero: maxima, minima and stationary points of inflection.
  20. Critical Points: Critical points are where a function's derivative is zero or doesn't exist.
  21. Local Maxima and Minima: A local maximum is higher than all nearby points; a local minimum is lower.
  22. Global Optimisation: Global optimisation finds the absolute largest or smallest value of a function, often under constraints.
  23. Concavity and Convexity: Concavity describes how a graph bends: concave up (convex), like a cup, or concave down (concave), like a cap.
  24. Points of Inflection: A point of inflection is where a curve changes concavity, from bending one way to bending the other.
  25. Curve Sketching: Curve sketching uses calculus and algebra together to draw an accurate picture of a function: its intercepts, asymptotes, stationary points, inflections and end behaviour.
  26. Integral Calculus: Integral calculus is the mathematics of accumulation: adding up infinitely many small pieces to find areas, volumes, distances and totals.
  27. Indefinite Integrals: An indefinite integral is the family of all antiderivatives of a function.
  28. Riemann Sums: A Riemann sum approximates the area under a curve by adding up the areas of thin rectangles.
  29. Definite Integrals: A definite integral ∫ₐ^b f(x) dx gives a number: the net signed area between the curve and the x-axis from a to b.
  30. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: The fundamental theorem of calculus links the two halves of calculus: differentiation and integration are inverse processes.
  31. Integration by Substitution: Integration by substitution reverses the chain rule.
  32. Integration by Parts: Integration by parts reverses the product rule.
  33. Partial Fraction Integration: Rational functions are integrated by splitting them into partial fractions (School 3), each of which integrates to a logarithm or a power.
  34. Trigonometric Integration: Integrating trigonometric functions uses standard results, identities to rewrite powers and products, and substitutions.
  35. Improper Integrals: An improper integral has an infinite limit or an integrand that blows up somewhere in the interval.
  36. Numerical Integration: Many integrals can't be found exactly, and data often comes as a table rather than a formula.
  37. Arc Length and Surface Area: Integration measures more than areas: the length of a curve and the surface area of a solid of revolution come from adding up tiny straight pieces.
  38. Volumes of Revolution: Rotating a region around an axis creates a solid of revolution.
  39. Sequences and Series: A sequence is a list of numbers; a series is their sum.
  40. Infinite Series: An infinite series adds infinitely many terms.
  41. Convergence and Divergence: Deciding whether an infinite series converges is a central question in analysis.
  42. Power Series: A power series is an infinite polynomial, Σ aₙ(x − c)ⁿ.
  43. Taylor Series: A Taylor series represents a function as a power series built from its derivatives at one point.
  44. Maclaurin Series: A Maclaurin series is a Taylor series about x = 0.

School 8: Multivariable and Vector Calculus

22 lessons

Multivariable and Vector Calculus: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Multivariable Calculus: Multivariable calculus extends derivatives and integrals to functions of several variables, like temperature across a room or profit depending on price and advertising.
  2. Directional Derivatives: A directional derivative measures how fast a function changes as you move in any chosen direction, not just along the axes.
  3. Gradient: The gradient ∇f collects all the partial derivatives of a function into a vector.
  4. Multiple Integrals: Multiple integrals add up quantities over areas and volumes: mass from density, probability from a joint density, average temperature over a region.
  5. Double Integrals: Double integrals over general regions need careful limits, and some become easy only after swapping the order of integration or switching to polar coordinates.
  6. Triple Integrals: Triple integrals add up quantities over solid regions: volume, mass and centre of mass.
  7. Jacobians: The Jacobian matrix collects all first partial derivatives of a transformation, and its determinant measures how the transformation stretches area or volume.
  8. Change of Variables: Changing variables in a multiple integral replaces awkward regions or integrands with simple ones, at the cost of a Jacobian factor.
  9. Vector Calculus: Vector calculus studies vector fields, such as wind velocity, electric fields and fluid flow, using three differential operators (gradient, divergence and curl) and integral theorems that link them.
  10. Divergence: The divergence of a vector field measures how much it spreads out from a point: positive at sources, negative at sinks, zero for an incompressible flow.
  11. Curl: The curl of a vector field measures its local rotation: the axis and strength of the swirl a tiny paddle wheel would feel.
  12. Laplacian Operator: The Laplacian ∇²f is the divergence of the gradient: it compares a function's value at a point with its average nearby.
  13. Line Integrals: A line integral adds up a quantity along a curve: the mass of a wire, or the work done by a force along a path.
  14. Surface Integrals: Surface integrals add up quantities over curved surfaces: the area of a dome, the mass of a shell, or the flux of a fluid or field through a membrane.
  15. Volume Integrals: Volume integrals add a scalar or vector quantity over a three-dimensional region: total mass, charge, energy or the volume itself.
  16. Green's Theorem: Green's theorem links a line integral around a closed plane curve with a double integral over the region inside.
  17. Stokes' Theorem: Stokes' theorem says the circulation of a field around a closed curve equals the flux of its curl through any surface bounded by that curve.
  18. Divergence Theorem: The divergence theorem says that the total outward flux through a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence over the volume inside.
  19. Matrix Calculus: Matrix calculus differentiates functions of vectors and matrices with respect to those vectors and matrices.
  20. Tensor Calculus: Tensor calculus extends vector calculus to curved spaces and to quantities with several indices, like stress or curvature.
  21. Calculus of Variations: The calculus of variations finds functions that make an integral as small or large as possible: the shortest path, the fastest slide, the shape of a hanging chain.
  22. Fractional Calculus: Fractional calculus defines derivatives and integrals of non-integer order, such as a half-derivative.

School 9: Analysis

14 lessons

Analysis: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Real Analysis: Real analysis puts calculus on rigorous foundations: precise definitions of limits, continuity and convergence, built on the completeness of the real numbers.
  2. Complex Analysis: Complex analysis studies differentiable functions of a complex variable.
  3. Harmonic Analysis: Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into basic waves and studies how well the pieces reconstruct the whole.
  4. Measure Theory: Measure theory generalises length, area and probability to assign sizes to very complicated sets.
  5. Lebesgue Integration: Lebesgue integration slices the range of a function rather than its domain.
  6. Distribution Theory: Distributions, or generalised functions, give rigorous meaning to objects like the Dirac delta, which physicists used long before mathematicians could justify them.
  7. Functional Analysis: Functional analysis studies infinite-dimensional vector spaces of functions, equipped with norms or inner products, and the linear maps between them.
  8. Banach Spaces: A Banach space is a complete normed vector space: Cauchy sequences always converge.
  9. Hilbert Spaces: A Hilbert space is a complete space with an inner product, so it has lengths, angles and orthogonal projections, even in infinite dimensions.
  10. Operator Theory: Operator theory studies linear maps between infinite-dimensional spaces: their norms, adjoints, inverses and special classes such as compact, self-adjoint and unitary operators.
  11. Spectral Theory: Spectral theory generalises eigenvalues to operators on infinite-dimensional spaces.
  12. Banach Algebras: A Banach algebra is a Banach space with a multiplication compatible with the norm.
  13. C*-Algebras: A C-algebra is a Banach algebra with an adjoint operation whose norm obeys ‖xx‖ = ‖x‖².
  14. Operator Algebras: Operator algebras are algebras of operators on Hilbert space closed under adjoints and suitable limits: C*-algebras and von Neumann algebras.

School 10: Differential Equations

46 lessons

Differential Equations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs): An ordinary differential equation relates an unknown function of one variable to its derivatives.
  2. First-Order Differential Equations: First-order ODEs y′ = f(x, y) model rates of change: cooling, decay, growth, mixing tanks and simple circuits.
  3. Separable Differential Equations: A separable equation can be rearranged so that all the y terms are on one side and all the x terms on the other, then each side is integrated.
  4. Linear Differential Equations: A first-order linear ODE y′ + P(x)y = Q(x) can always be solved with an integrating factor, which turns the left side into the derivative of a product.
  5. Exact Differential Equations: An exact equation M dx + N dy = 0 is the total differential of some function F(x, y), so its solutions are the level curves F = C. Testing for exactness and finding F are quick once you see the pattern.
  6. Homogeneous Differential Equations: A first-order equation is homogeneous (in this sense) if it can be written y′ = F(y/x).
  7. Bernoulli Equations: A Bernoulli equation y′ + P(x)y = Q(x)yⁿ is nonlinear when n ≠ 0, 1, but the substitution v = y¹⁻ⁿ turns it into a linear equation.
  8. Second-Order Differential Equations: Second-order linear ODEs ay″ + by′ + cy = f(x) describe vibrating springs, swinging pendulums (approximately) and electrical oscillators.
  9. Higher-Order Differential Equations: Linear ODEs of order three and above work like second-order ones: a characteristic polynomial gives the homogeneous solutions, and particular integrals handle the forcing.
  10. Nonlinear Differential Equations: Nonlinear ODEs, where y or its derivatives appear non-linearly, describe real pendulums, populations, chemical reactions and much more.
  11. Autonomous Differential Equations: An autonomous equation y′ = f(y) has no explicit dependence on the independent variable.
  12. Systems of Differential Equations: Systems of ODEs track several interacting quantities: predators and prey, coupled springs, chemical species.
  13. Initial Value Problems: An initial value problem gives an ODE together with the value of the solution (and enough derivatives) at a starting point.
  14. Boundary Value Problems: A boundary value problem specifies conditions at two or more points, such as both ends of a beam or a string.
  15. Laplace Transforms: The Laplace transform turns a function of time into a function of a complex variable s, converting derivatives into multiplication by s.
  16. Inverse Laplace Transforms: The inverse Laplace transform recovers a time function from its transform.
  17. Fourier Transform Methods: The Fourier transform turns derivatives in space into multiplication by frequency, so linear PDEs on infinite domains become ODEs that are easy to solve.
  18. Green's Functions: A Green's function is the response of a linear system to a point source.
  19. Sturm–Liouville Theory: Sturm–Liouville problems are second-order eigenvalue problems whose eigenfunctions form orthogonal families: sines and cosines, Legendre polynomials, Bessel functions.
  20. Partial Differential Equations (PDEs): Partial differential equations involve functions of several variables and their partial derivatives.
  21. Heat Equation: The heat equation uₜ = kuₓₓ describes how temperature spreads through a material, and more generally any diffusion process.
  22. Wave Equation: The wave equation uₜₜ = c²uₓₓ describes vibrating strings, sound, light and water waves.
  23. Laplace Equation: Laplace's equation ∇²u = 0 describes steady states: equilibrium temperature, electrostatic potential in empty space, ideal fluid flow.
  24. Poisson Equation: Poisson's equation ∇²u = f adds a source term to Laplace's equation.
  25. Diffusion Equations: Diffusion equations describe how substances spread from high to low concentration: perfume in a room, oxygen in tissue, pollutants in groundwater.
  26. Reaction-Diffusion Equations: Reaction–diffusion equations combine local reactions (growth, decay, chemistry) with diffusion.
  27. Transport Equations: Transport equations describe how a quantity is carried and conserved as it moves: traffic along a road, pollutants in a river, particles in a reactor.
  28. Advection Equations: The advection equation uₜ + cuₓ = 0 moves a profile along at constant speed c without changing its shape.
  29. Fluid Dynamics Equations: The equations of fluid dynamics express conservation of mass, momentum and energy for liquids and gases.
  30. Navier–Stokes Equations: The Navier–Stokes equations describe the motion of viscous fluids: water, air, blood.
  31. Maxwell's Equations: Maxwell's four equations unify electricity, magnetism and light.
  32. Schrödinger Equation: The Schrödinger equation governs how quantum states evolve.
  33. Numerical Solutions of ODEs: Most differential equations can't be solved exactly, so computers step forward in small increments.
  34. Numerical Solutions of PDEs: Numerical methods for PDEs replace continuous space and time with grids, meshes or basis functions, turning the equation into a large system of algebraic equations.
  35. Finite Difference Methods: Finite difference methods approximate derivatives by differences of function values at nearby grid points.
  36. Finite Element Methods: The finite element method splits a domain into small elements and approximates the solution by simple functions on each.
  37. Finite Volume Methods: Finite volume methods divide a domain into cells and track the average of a conserved quantity in each, updating it by the fluxes through cell faces.
  38. Spectral Methods: Spectral methods represent the solution with global smooth functions such as Fourier modes or Chebyshev polynomials.
  39. Dynamical Systems: Dynamical systems theory studies how states evolve under fixed rules, whether continuous flows from ODEs or discrete maps iterated step by step.
  40. Stability Analysis: Stability analysis asks whether a system returns to equilibrium after a small disturbance.
  41. Bifurcation Theory: Bifurcation theory studies how the qualitative behaviour of a system changes as a parameter varies: equilibria appearing, disappearing or swapping stability, and oscillations being born.
  42. Chaos Theory: Chaos is deterministic behaviour that's unpredictable in practice because tiny differences in starting conditions grow exponentially.
  43. Solitons: A soliton is a solitary wave that keeps its shape while travelling and survives collisions with other solitons.
  44. Delay Differential Equations: A delay differential equation makes the rate of change depend on past states as well as the present: populations responding to resources a generation ago, control systems with lag, physiological feedback.
  45. Stochastic Differential Equations: Stochastic differential equations add random noise to differential equations, modelling share prices, particles jostled by molecules and populations with random environments.
  46. Fractional Differential Equations: Fractional differential equations use derivatives of non-integer order to model systems with memory: anomalous diffusion in crowded cells, viscoelastic materials and long-tailed relaxation.

School 11: Linear Algebra and Matrices

50 lessons

Linear Algebra and Matrices: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Vectors: A vector has both size and direction: a displacement, velocity or force.
  2. Vector Algebra: Vector algebra multiplies vectors in two useful ways.
  3. Vector Spaces: A vector space is any collection of objects that can be added and scaled following the familiar rules.
  4. Subspaces: A subspace is a vector space sitting inside a larger one: a line or plane through the origin, or the solutions of a homogeneous linear system.
  5. Linear Combinations: A linear combination adds scalar multiples of vectors: c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ + … + cₖvₖ.
  6. Span: The span of a set of vectors is everything you can build from them by linear combinations.
  7. Linear Independence: Vectors are linearly independent when none of them can be built from the others.
  8. Basis and Dimension: A basis is an independent set that spans the space: the smallest complete set of building blocks.
  9. Matrices: A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers.
  10. Matrix Algebra: Matrix algebra has its own rules.
  11. Matrix Operations: The basic matrix operations are addition, scalar multiplication, the trace and elementary row operations.
  12. Matrix Multiplication: Matrix multiplication combines rows of the first matrix with columns of the second.
  13. Matrix Transposition: The transpose of a matrix swaps its rows and columns.
  14. Matrix Inversion: The inverse of a square matrix undoes it: A⁻¹A = I. Inverses exist exactly when the determinant is non-zero, and they express solutions of linear systems, though in practice systems are solved…
  15. Determinants: The determinant of a square matrix is a single number that tells you whether the matrix is invertible and how it scales area or volume.
  16. Rank of a Matrix: The rank of a matrix is the number of independent rows (or columns), equal to the number of pivots after row reduction.
  17. Column Space: The column space of a matrix is the span of its columns: every vector Ax can produce.
  18. Row Space: The row space of a matrix is the span of its rows.
  19. Null Space: The null space (kernel) of a matrix is the set of solutions of Ax = 0.
  20. Systems of Linear Equations: Systems of linear equations appear everywhere: balancing chemical reactions, network flows, economics, circuits and fitting curves.
  21. Linear Transformations: A linear transformation is a function between vector spaces that respects addition and scaling.
  22. Change of Basis: The same vector has different coordinates in different bases, and the same linear map has different matrices.
  23. Similarity Transformations: Two matrices are similar when they represent the same linear map in different bases: B = P⁻¹AP.
  24. Eigenvalues: An eigenvalue λ of a square matrix A is a number for which Av = λv has a non-zero solution v.
  25. Eigenvectors: An eigenvector is a direction a linear map doesn't turn: it's only stretched by its eigenvalue.
  26. Eigenspaces: The eigenspace for an eigenvalue λ is the set of all its eigenvectors together with zero.
  27. Characteristic Polynomials: The characteristic polynomial det(A − λI) packages a matrix's eigenvalues as its roots, along with the trace and determinant as coefficients.
  28. Diagonalisation: Diagonalising a matrix means writing it as A = PDP⁻¹ with D diagonal.
  29. Jordan Normal Form: Not every matrix can be diagonalised, but every complex matrix is similar to a Jordan normal form: nearly diagonal, with some 1s just above the diagonal.
  30. Symmetric Matrices: Real symmetric matrices (A = Aᵀ) are the best-behaved matrices: their eigenvalues are real and their eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal.
  31. Hermitian Matrices: A Hermitian matrix equals its own conjugate transpose: the complex counterpart of a real symmetric matrix.
  32. Positive Definite Matrices: A symmetric matrix is positive definite when xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero x.
  33. Inner Product Spaces: An inner product generalises the dot product, giving lengths and angles in any vector space, including spaces of functions.
  34. Norms: A norm measures the size of a vector.
  35. Orthogonality: Orthogonal vectors are perpendicular: their inner product is zero.
  36. Orthogonal Projections: An orthogonal projection drops a vector onto a subspace at right angles, giving the closest point in that subspace.
  37. Gram–Schmidt Process: The Gram–Schmidt process turns any linearly independent set into an orthonormal one spanning the same space.
  38. Matrix Decomposition: A matrix decomposition writes a matrix as a product of simpler matrices: triangular, orthogonal or diagonal.
  39. LU Decomposition: LU decomposition writes a square matrix as a lower triangular L times an upper triangular U. It's Gaussian elimination recorded as matrices, and with row pivoting it's the standard way to solve general linear systems.
  40. QR Decomposition: QR decomposition writes a matrix as an orthogonal (or orthonormal-column) Q times an upper triangular R. It's the stable way to solve least squares problems and the engine of the QR algorithm that computes eigenvalues.
  41. Cholesky Decomposition: The Cholesky decomposition writes a symmetric positive definite matrix as LLᵀ, a lower triangular matrix times its transpose.
  42. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD): The singular value decomposition writes any matrix as A = UΣVᵀ: a rotation, a stretch and another rotation.
  43. Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Principal component analysis finds the directions in which data vary most and re-expresses the data along them.
  44. Sparse Matrices: A sparse matrix is mostly zeros.
  45. Block Matrices: Partitioning a matrix into blocks lets you treat submatrices as single entries.
  46. Numerical Linear Algebra: Numerical linear algebra designs fast, accurate algorithms for matrix problems on real computers with finite precision.
  47. Multilinear Algebra: Multilinear algebra studies functions that are linear in each of several vector arguments, such as the dot product, the determinant and bilinear forms.
  48. Tensor Products: The tensor product V ⊗ W combines two vector spaces into one whose dimension is the product of theirs.
  49. Tensor Algebra: The tensor algebra collects all tensor powers of a vector space into one algebra, and tensors of each type describe multilinear quantities such as stress, curvature and multiway data.
  50. Exterior Algebra: The exterior algebra is built from the wedge product, an antisymmetric product of vectors that measures oriented areas, volumes and their higher-dimensional analogues.

School 12: Probability

50 lessons

Probability: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Probability Theory: Probability theory is the mathematics of uncertainty.
  2. Foundations of Probability: Modern probability rests on three axioms set out by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933.
  3. Independent Events: Two events are independent when knowing that one has happened doesn't change the probability of the other.
  4. Conditional Probability: Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another has happened.
  5. Joint Probability: A joint probability is the probability that two or more events, or random variables, take particular values together.
  6. Bayes' Theorem: Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probabilities: from P(evidence ∣ cause) it gives P(cause ∣ evidence).
  7. Random Variables: A random variable assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment: the number of heads, the waiting time, the profit.
  8. Discrete Random Variables: A discrete random variable takes a countable set of values, each with a positive probability.
  9. Continuous Random Variables: A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval: heights, times, temperatures.
  10. Probability Distributions: A probability distribution describes how probability is spread over the values of a random variable.
  11. Probability Mass Functions: A probability mass function (pmf) gives the probability of each value of a discrete random variable.
  12. Probability Density Functions: A probability density function (pdf) describes a continuous random variable: probabilities are areas under it.
  13. Cumulative Distribution Functions: The cumulative distribution function (CDF) F(x) = P(X ≤ x) works for every random variable, discrete or continuous.
  14. Expected Value: The expected value of a random variable is its long-run average: the probability-weighted mean of its values.
  15. Variance: Variance measures how far a random variable typically strays from its mean: the expected squared deviation.
  16. Standard Deviation: The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, which puts spread back into the original units.
  17. Covariance: Covariance measures how two random variables vary together: positive when they rise together, negative when one rises as the other falls.
  18. Moments and Moment-Generating Functions: Moments summarise a distribution's shape: the first gives the mean, the second the spread, the third the skewness, the fourth the tail weight.
  19. Law of Large Numbers: The law of large numbers says that the average of many independent repetitions settles down to the expected value.
  20. Central Limit Theorem: The central limit theorem says that sums and averages of many independent random variables are approximately normally distributed, whatever the original distribution.
  21. Bernoulli Distribution: The Bernoulli distribution describes a single trial with two outcomes: success with probability p, failure with probability 1 − p.
  22. Binomial Distribution: The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials with the same probability of success.
  23. Geometric Distribution: The geometric distribution models the number of trials until the first success.
  24. Negative Binomial Distribution: The negative binomial distribution counts the number of trials needed to get r successes.
  25. Hypergeometric Distribution: The hypergeometric distribution counts successes when sampling without replacement from a finite population.
  26. Poisson Distribution: The Poisson distribution counts events that happen randomly and independently at a constant average rate: calls per minute, decays per second, accidents per year.
  27. Multinomial Distribution: The multinomial distribution generalises the binomial to more than two outcomes.
  28. Beta-Binomial Distribution: The beta-binomial distribution is a binomial whose success probability is itself random, drawn from a beta distribution.
  29. Uniform Distribution: The uniform distribution spreads probability evenly: every value in an interval is equally likely.
  30. Normal Distribution: The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell-shaped curve defined by its mean and standard deviation.
  31. Standard Normal Distribution: The standard normal distribution Z ~ N(0, 1) is the normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  32. Exponential Distribution: The exponential distribution models the waiting time until the next event in a Poisson process: the next customer, decay or failure.
  33. Gamma Distribution: The gamma distribution models the waiting time until the kth event in a Poisson process, and more generally any positive, right-skewed quantity.
  34. Beta Distribution: The beta distribution lives on [0, 1], so it naturally models proportions and probabilities.
  35. Chi-Squared Distribution: The chi-squared distribution is the distribution of a sum of squared independent standard normal variables.
  36. Student's T-Distribution: Student's t-distribution is like the standard normal but with heavier tails.
  37. F-Distribution: The F-distribution is the ratio of two independent chi-squared variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom.
  38. Weibull Distribution: The Weibull distribution models lifetimes and strengths.
  39. Log-Normal Distribution: A variable is log-normal when its logarithm is normally distributed.
  40. Pareto Distribution: The Pareto distribution is a power law: the probability of exceeding x falls like x^(−α).
  41. Cauchy Distribution: The Cauchy distribution looks like a bell curve but has such heavy tails that it has no mean or variance.
  42. Multivariate Normal Distribution: The multivariate normal extends the bell curve to several correlated variables, described by a mean vector and a covariance matrix.
  43. Student's T Multivariate Distribution: The multivariate t-distribution is a heavy-tailed version of the multivariate normal.
  44. Dirichlet Distribution: The Dirichlet distribution is a distribution over probability vectors: sets of non-negative numbers adding to 1.
  45. Wishart Distribution: The Wishart distribution is the distribution of a sample covariance (scatter) matrix from multivariate normal data.
  46. Mixture Distributions: A mixture distribution combines several distributions, each chosen with some probability.
  47. Empirical Distributions: The empirical distribution puts equal probability on each observed data point.
  48. Heavy-Tailed Distributions: Heavy-tailed distributions give extreme values far more probability than the normal does.
  49. Stable Distributions: Stable distributions keep their shape when independent copies are added: the sum is the same type, just shifted and rescaled.
  50. Extreme Value Distributions: Extreme value distributions describe the largest (or smallest) values in a sample: the highest flood, the strongest gust, the biggest daily loss.

School 13: Statistics

75 lessons

Statistics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Descriptive Statistics: Descriptive statistics summarise data with a few numbers and pictures: where the centre is, how spread out the values are, and what shape they make.
  2. Correlation: Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.
  3. Data Interpretation: Data interpretation is reading charts, tables and statistics critically: what they show, what they hide and how they might mislead.
  4. Statistics in Everyday Life: Statistics shape everyday decisions: inflation figures set pay rises and benefits, polls guide elections, weather forecasts plan days out, and medical risks inform treatment.
  5. Inferential Statistics: Inferential statistics draws conclusions about a population from a sample, with a measure of how uncertain those conclusions are.
  6. Statistical Inference: Statistical inference has several frameworks (frequentist, Bayesian and likelihood-based) and a common toolkit for judging estimators: bias, variance, consistency and efficiency.
  7. Sampling Theory: Sampling theory designs ways to choose samples so that they represent the population and give precise estimates for a given cost.
  8. Sampling Distributions: A sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic over all possible samples.
  9. Sampling Bias: Sampling bias happens when the way a sample is chosen makes it unrepresentative.
  10. Confidence Intervals: A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for a parameter, with a stated confidence level.
  11. Maximum Likelihood Estimation: Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the parameter values that make the observed data most probable.
  12. Method of Moments: The method of moments estimates parameters by matching the sample's moments (mean, variance, …) to the model's theoretical moments.
  13. Hypothesis Testing: A hypothesis test asks whether data are consistent with a claim about a population.
  14. Null Hypothesis Significance Testing: Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is the standard ritual of declaring results "significant" when p < 0.05.
  15. P-Values: A p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting data at least as extreme as those observed.
  16. Type I and Type II Errors: Every test can go wrong in two ways: rejecting a true null hypothesis (a Type I error, a false alarm) or failing to reject a false one (a Type II error, a missed effect).
  17. Statistical Power: Statistical power is the probability that a test detects an effect that's really there.
  18. Effect Sizes: An effect size measures how big a difference or relationship is, independently of sample size.
  19. Multiple Testing Correction: When many hypotheses are tested, some will be "significant" by chance alone.
  20. False Discovery Rate: The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected proportion of false positives among the results declared significant.
  21. Parametric Statistics: Parametric methods assume the data come from a family of distributions described by a few parameters, usually the normal.
  22. Nonparametric Statistics: Nonparametric methods make few assumptions about the shape of the population, often working with ranks or signs.
  23. Z-Tests: A z-test compares a statistic with its hypothesised value using the standard normal distribution.
  24. T-Tests: The t-test compares means when the population standard deviation is unknown and estimated from the data.
  25. Chi-Squared Tests: Chi-squared tests compare observed counts with the counts expected under a hypothesis.
  26. Fisher's Exact Test: Fisher's exact test analyses 2 × 2 tables using the exact hypergeometric probabilities, rather than a chi-squared approximation.
  27. Mann–Whitney U Test: The Mann–Whitney U test (also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) compares two independent groups using ranks.
  28. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test: The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares paired data, or one sample with a hypothesised median, using the ranks of the absolute differences.
  29. Kruskal–Wallis Test: The Kruskal–Wallis test compares three or more independent groups using ranks.
  30. Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test: The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares whole distributions: a sample with a hypothesised distribution, or two samples with each other.
  31. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA): Analysis of variance tests whether several group means are equal by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within them.
  32. Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA): Analysis of covariance combines ANOVA with regression: it compares group means after adjusting for a continuous covariate, such as a baseline score.
  33. Categorical Data Analysis: Categorical data analysis deals with outcomes that fall into categories: yes or no, blood group, disease stage.
  34. Contingency Tables: A contingency table cross-tabulates counts for two or more categorical variables.
  35. Regression Analysis: Regression analysis models how a response variable depends on one or more predictors.
  36. Linear Regression: Simple linear regression fits the straight line ŷ = a + bx that minimises the sum of squared residuals.
  37. Multiple Linear Regression: Multiple linear regression models a response using several predictors at once.
  38. Logistic Regression: Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome, such as disease or no disease, pass or fail, using predictors.
  39. Poisson Regression: Poisson regression models counts, such as hospital admissions, accidents or species sightings, using a log link so predicted counts stay positive.
  40. Nonlinear Regression: Nonlinear regression fits models whose parameters enter non-linearly, such as exponential growth, saturation curves and enzyme kinetics.
  41. Robust Regression: Robust regression fits lines that aren't thrown off by outliers or heavy-tailed errors.
  42. Quantile Regression: Quantile regression models how a chosen quantile of the response, such as the median or the 90th percentile, depends on predictors.
  43. Ridge Regression: Ridge regression adds a penalty on the size of the coefficients to least squares.
  44. Lasso Regression: The lasso penalises the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients.
  45. Generalised Linear Models (GLMs): Generalised linear models unify linear, logistic, Poisson and other regressions in one framework: a distribution for the response, a linear predictor and a link function joining them.
  46. Mixed-Effects Models: Mixed-effects (multilevel) models handle data with groups or repeated measurements: pupils in schools, patients in hospitals, measurements over time on the same person.
  47. Generalised Additive Models: Generalised additive models (GAMs) replace straight-line terms in a GLM with smooth curves learned from the data.
  48. Statistical Modelling: Statistical modelling is the craft of building, fitting, checking and revising models that describe data and the process generating them.
  49. Multivariate Statistics: Multivariate statistics studies several variables measured together, keeping track of how they relate.
  50. Multivariate Analysis: Multivariate analysis covers techniques that find structure in many variables at once: principal components, canonical correlations, correspondence analysis and multidimensional scaling.
  51. Factor Analysis: Factor analysis explains correlations among many observed variables by a few unobserved (latent) factors, such as general intelligence or anxiety.
  52. Discriminant Analysis: Discriminant analysis classifies observations into known groups using their measurements, and finds the combinations of variables that best separate the groups.
  53. Cluster Analysis: Cluster analysis groups observations so that those in a cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters, without any known labels.
  54. Frequentist Statistics: Frequentist statistics defines probability as long-run frequency and judges methods by how they perform over repeated samples: how often intervals cover the truth, how often tests err.
  55. Bayesian Statistics: Bayesian statistics treats unknown quantities as having probability distributions that express uncertainty.
  56. Bayesian Inference: Bayesian inference is the practical process of fitting a Bayesian model: choosing a likelihood and prior, computing the posterior, summarising it and checking the model.
  57. Prior and Posterior Distributions: The prior expresses what is believed about a parameter before the data; the posterior expresses what is believed after.
  58. Bootstrap Methods: The bootstrap estimates the uncertainty of almost any statistic by resampling the data with replacement many times and recalculating the statistic.
  59. Permutation Tests: A permutation test finds a p-value by shuffling group labels many times and seeing how often the shuffled data look as extreme as the real data.
  60. Monte Carlo Methods: Monte Carlo methods use random simulation to compute quantities that are hard to find exactly: probabilities, integrals, the behaviour of complex systems.
  61. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): Markov chain Monte Carlo draws samples from complicated distributions, especially Bayesian posteriors, by running a random walk designed to spend time in each region in proportion to its probability.
  62. Statistical Computing: Statistical computing is the software, algorithms and numerical care that turn statistical ideas into reliable results.
  63. Experimental Design: Experimental design plans how to collect data so that the comparisons of interest are fair and precise.

Graph Theory and Networks: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Graph Theory: Graph theory studies networks of points (vertices) joined by lines (edges).

  7. Graph Connectivity: Connectivity measures how well a graph holds together: whether every vertex can reach every other, and how many vertices or edges must fail before it falls apart.
  8. Trees: A tree is a connected graph with no cycles: the simplest connected structure.
  9. Trees and Forests: A forest is a graph with no cycles: a collection of trees.
  10. Spanning Trees: A spanning tree is a tree that includes every vertex of a graph, using some of its edges.
  11. Directed Acyclic Graphs: A directed acyclic graph (DAG) has directed edges and no directed cycles.
  12. Bipartite Graphs: A bipartite graph splits its vertices into two groups with edges only between groups: workers and jobs, students and courses, users and products.

  14. Planarity: Planarity testing decides whether a graph can be drawn without crossings, and if not, why not.
  15. Eulerian Paths: An Eulerian path uses every edge of a graph exactly once.
  16. Hamiltonian Paths: A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once.
  17. Graph Colouring: Graph colouring assigns colours to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ.
  18. Chromatic Polynomials: The chromatic polynomial P(G, k) counts the proper colourings of a graph using k colours.
  19. Matching Theory: A matching pairs up vertices along edges with no vertex used twice: workers with jobs, students with places, donors with recipients.
  20. Matching and Vertex Covers: A vertex cover is a set of vertices touching every edge, such as security cameras covering every corridor.
  21. Shortest Path Algorithms: Shortest path algorithms find the cheapest routes through weighted graphs.
  22. Shortest Path Problems: Shortest path problems come in many forms: one source or all pairs, fastest or cheapest, with constraints or alternatives.
  23. Minimum Spanning Trees: A minimum spanning tree connects all vertices of a weighted graph at the least total cost.
  24. Network Flows: A flow network moves something (water, traffic, data, goods) from a source to a sink through edges with capacities.
  25. Maximum Flow: The maximum flow problem asks for the largest possible flow from source to sink.
  26. Minimum Cut: A cut splits a network into a side containing the source and a side containing the sink; its capacity is the total capacity of edges crossing from source side to sink side.
  27. Weighted Networks: Real networks have ties of different strengths: how often people call, how many passengers fly a route, how much two countries trade.
  28. Network Centrality: Centrality measures identify the most important nodes in a network, but "important" depends on the question: the most connected person, the best-placed broker, or the one closest to everyone.
  29. Betweenness Centrality: Betweenness centrality measures how often a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes.
  30. Closeness Centrality: Closeness centrality measures how near a node is to all others: the inverse of its average distance.
  31. Eigenvector Centrality: Eigenvector centrality scores a node highly if it's linked to other high-scoring nodes: influence by association.
  32. PageRank Mathematics: PageRank ranks web pages by imagining a random surfer who follows links and occasionally jumps to a random page.
  33. Community Detection: Community detection finds groups of nodes more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network: friend circles, research fields, functional modules in cells.
  34. Network Clustering: Clustering in networks measures how often a node's neighbours are also connected to each other: the tendency of friends to be friends.
  35. Spectral Graph Theory: Spectral graph theory studies graphs through the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of their matrices, especially the Laplacian.
  36. Random Graphs: Random graph models generate networks by chance, to compare with real ones and to prove that graphs with certain properties exist.
  37. Small-World Networks: In small-world networks, any two nodes are linked by a short chain, even though most links are local.
  38. Scale-Free Networks: In a scale-free network, the degree distribution follows a power law: most nodes have few links but a few hubs have enormous numbers.
  39. Network Science: Network science studies complex networks across physics, biology, sociology and computing using shared mathematical tools.
  40. Percolation Theory: Percolation theory studies what happens when parts of a network are randomly removed or kept: at a critical threshold, a spanning cluster suddenly appears or disappears.
  41. Social Network Analysis: Social network analysis studies relationships among people and organisations: who knows whom, who advises whom, who trades with whom.
  42. Biological Network Analysis: Biological networks map interactions among genes, proteins, metabolites, neurons and species.
  43. Network Epidemiology: Network epidemiology models how infections spread along contacts rather than through a perfectly mixed population.
  44. Graph Neural Network Mathematics: Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn from data on graphs by repeatedly passing messages between neighbouring nodes.

School 16: Logic, Sets and Foundations

52 lessons

Logic, Sets and Foundations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Elementary Set Theory: A set is a collection of distinct objects, called its elements.
  2. Set Theory: Set theory studies sets in general: how they're built, compared and measured.
  3. Set Operations: Set operations combine sets into new ones: union, intersection, difference, complement and symmetric difference.
  4. Union and Intersection: Union collects everything in either set; intersection keeps only what's common.
  5. Complements: The complement of a set is everything not in it, relative to a chosen universal set.
  6. Cartesian Products: The Cartesian product A × B is the set of all ordered pairs with first entry from A and second from B. It builds the coordinate plane ℝ × ℝ, and relations and functions are defined as subsets of products.
  7. Power Sets: The power set of A is the set of all subsets of A. A set with n elements has 2ⁿ subsets, and Cantor's theorem shows the power set is always strictly larger than the set, even for infinite sets.
  8. Countability: A set is countable if its elements can be listed as a first, second, third and so on.
  9. Countable and Uncountable Sets: Some infinite sets are too big to list.
  10. Relations: A relation from A to B is any set of ordered pairs, recording which elements are related.
  11. Relations and Functions: A function is a relation in which every input is related to exactly one output.
  12. Functions and Mappings: Functions, also called mappings, can be one-to-one, onto or both.
  13. Equivalence Relations: An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive: it says when two things count as "the same" for some purpose.
  14. Equivalence Classes: An equivalence relation splits a set into disjoint equivalence classes, each containing elements that are equivalent to each other.
  15. Partial Orders: A partial order is a reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive relation, capturing "comes before" when not every pair needs to be comparable.
  16. Partially Ordered Sets: A partially ordered set, or poset, is a set together with a partial order.
  17. Totally Ordered Sets: A total order is a partial order where every two elements are comparable.
  18. Lattices: A lattice is a poset in which every pair of elements has a least upper bound (join) and a greatest lower bound (meet).
  19. Boolean Lattices: A Boolean lattice is a distributive lattice with a least and greatest element in which every element has a complement.
  20. Mathematical Logic: Mathematical logic studies reasoning itself with mathematical precision.
  21. Propositional Logic: Propositional logic studies statements that are true or false, combined with connectives such as "and", "or", "not" and "implies".
  22. Predicate Logic: Predicate logic adds variables, predicates and the quantifiers "for all" (∀) and "there exists" (∃) to propositional logic.
  23. First-Order Logic: First-order logic is predicate logic in which quantifiers range over individual objects, with a precise syntax, proof system and semantics.
  24. Higher-Order Logic: Higher-order logic lets quantifiers range over properties, sets and functions, not just individual objects.
  25. Modal Logic: Modal logic adds operators for "necessarily" (□) and "possibly" (◇).
  26. Intuitionistic Logic: Intuitionistic logic treats a statement as true only when it has a proof or construction.
  27. Fuzzy Logic: Fuzzy logic allows truth values anywhere between 0 and 1, modelling vague ideas like "tall" or "warm".
  28. Temporal Logic: Temporal logic adds operators about time: "always", "eventually", "next" and "until".
  29. Proof Techniques: A proof is a convincing logical argument that a statement follows from accepted facts.
  30. Direct Proof: A direct proof starts from the hypotheses and moves step by step to the conclusion using definitions and known results.
  31. Proof by Contraposition: To prove "if P then Q", you can instead prove the equivalent "if not Q then not P".
  32. Proof by Contradiction: Proof by contradiction assumes the statement is false and derives something impossible.
  33. Mathematical Induction: Mathematical induction proves a statement for every natural number by proving a base case and showing each case implies the next.
  34. Formal Proofs: A formal proof is a sequence of formulas, each an axiom or following from earlier ones by an explicit rule.
  35. Axiomatic Systems: An axiomatic system starts from a few basic statements, the axioms, and derives everything else by logic.
  36. Axiomatic Set Theory: Axiomatic set theory replaces naive set formation with explicit axioms saying which sets exist.
  37. Zermelo–Fraenkel Set Theory: Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with choice, ZFC, is the standard foundation of mathematics.
  38. Axiom of Choice: The axiom of choice says that from any collection of non-empty sets you can choose one element from each, even when there's no rule for choosing.
  39. Set-Theoretic Mathematics: In set-theoretic foundations every mathematical object is a set: numbers, pairs, functions and spaces.
  40. Ordinal Arithmetic: Ordinals extend counting beyond the finite: after 0, 1, 2, … comes ω, then ω + 1, ω + 2, and on.
  41. Cardinal Arithmetic: Cardinals measure the size of sets.
  42. Large Cardinal Theory: Large cardinals are infinite sizes so big that ZFC can't prove they exist.
  43. Proof Theory: Proof theory treats proofs as mathematical objects.
  44. Model Theory: Model theory studies the relationship between formal theories and the structures that satisfy them.
  45. Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems: Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) show that any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory strong enough for basic arithmetic leaves some true statements unprovable, and can't prove its own consistency.
  46. Computability Theory: Computability theory asks which problems can be solved by an algorithm at all.
  47. Recursive Functions: The recursive functions are built from simple starting functions by composition, primitive recursion and minimisation.
  48. Lambda Calculus: The lambda calculus, created by Alonzo Church in the 1930s, describes computation using only function definition and application.
  49. Type Theory: Type theory classifies every term by a type, preventing nonsense like adding a number to a function.
  50. Constructive Mathematics: Constructive mathematics requires that existence proofs show how to find the object.
  51. Category-Theoretic Foundations: Category-theoretic foundations describe mathematics through structures and maps rather than membership.
  52. Philosophy of Mathematics: The philosophy of mathematics asks what mathematical objects are and how we know mathematical truths.

Topology and Advanced Pure Mathematics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  19. Complex Manifolds: A complex manifold looks locally like ℂⁿ, with holomorphic changes of coordinates.
  20. Riemann Surfaces: A Riemann surface is a one-dimensional complex manifold: a surface on which complex analysis works.
  21. Scheme Theory: Scheme theory, developed by Grothendieck in the 1960s, rebuilds algebraic geometry on commutative rings.
  22. Category Theory: Category theory studies mathematical structures through the maps between them.

Optimisation and Operations Research: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Mathematical Optimisation: Optimisation is choosing the best option from a set of possibilities: the cheapest route, the strongest design or the most accurate model.

  5. Karush–Kuhn–Tucker Conditions: The KKT conditions extend Lagrange multipliers to inequality constraints.
  6. Convex Optimisation: In convex optimisation the objective is convex and the feasible set is convex.
  7. Nonconvex Optimisation: Nonconvex problems can have many local minima, saddle points and flat regions, so finding the global optimum is generally hard.
  8. Nonlinear Programming: Nonlinear programming (NLP) covers problems where the objective or constraints are nonlinear functions.
  9. Quadratic Programming: Quadratic programming minimises a quadratic objective subject to linear constraints.

  12. Robust Optimisation: Robust optimisation protects against the worst case within a set of possible data values, rather than averaging.
  13. Optimal Control: Optimal control chooses inputs to a dynamical system over time to minimise a cost, such as steering a rocket with least fuel.
  14. Linear Programming: Linear programming optimises a linear objective subject to linear inequalities.
  15. Simplex Algorithm: The simplex algorithm, invented by George Dantzig in 1947, solves linear programs by moving from vertex to neighbouring vertex, improving the objective each step.
  16. Interior-Point Methods: Interior-point methods solve linear and convex programs by travelling through the interior of the feasible region rather than along its edges.
  17. Integer Programming: Integer programming requires some or all variables to take whole-number values.
  18. Mixed-Integer Programming: Mixed-integer programs combine continuous and integer variables, modelling problems such as unit commitment in power systems, where plants are on or off but output levels vary continuously.
  19. Branch and Bound: Branch and bound solves integer and combinatorial problems by splitting them into smaller subproblems and discarding any whose bound shows they can't beat the best solution found so far.
  20. Combinatorial Optimisation: Combinatorial optimisation seeks the best object from a finite but huge set, such as a tour, schedule, matching or tree.
  21. Dynamic Programming: Dynamic programming solves problems by breaking them into overlapping subproblems and reusing their solutions.
  22. Gradient Descent: Gradient descent minimises a function by repeatedly stepping in the direction of steepest decrease, the negative gradient.
  23. Newton's Method: Newton's method for optimisation uses both gradient and curvature, stepping to the minimum of a local quadratic model.
  24. Quasi-Newton Methods: Quasi-Newton methods build an approximation to the Hessian from the gradients seen so far, getting fast convergence without computing second derivatives.
  25. Heuristic Optimisation: Heuristics are practical rules that find good solutions quickly without guaranteeing optimality.
  26. Metaheuristic Algorithms: Metaheuristics are general strategies that guide heuristics to explore beyond local optima.
  27. Genetic Algorithms: Genetic algorithms evolve a population of candidate solutions using selection, crossover and mutation, inspired by natural evolution.
  28. Simulated Annealing: Simulated annealing explores solutions like a cooling metal: it accepts worse moves with a probability that falls as the "temperature" decreases.
  29. Particle Swarm Optimisation: Particle swarm optimisation moves a swarm of candidate points through the search space, each pulled towards its own best position and the swarm's best.
  30. Bayesian Optimisation: Bayesian optimisation finds the best settings of expensive black-box functions, such as machine learning hyperparameters or experiments, using few evaluations.
  31. Operations Research: Operations research (OR) applies mathematical models and analysis to decisions in organisations: how many staff to roster, where to put depots, how to route deliveries.
  32. Decision Analysis: Decision analysis structures choices under uncertainty using decision trees, probabilities and utilities.
  33. Queueing Theory: Queueing theory models waiting lines at checkouts, call centres, hospitals and computer servers.
  34. Inventory Theory: Inventory theory decides how much stock to order and when, balancing ordering costs against holding costs and the risk of running out.
  35. Scheduling Theory: Scheduling theory decides when and where to process jobs on machines, staff or processors to meet deadlines or minimise completion times.
  36. Supply Chain Optimisation: Supply chain optimisation coordinates suppliers, factories, warehouses and retailers to deliver products at low cost and high service.

  39. Transportation Problems: The transportation problem ships goods from supply points to demand points at least total cost.
  40. Assignment Problems: The assignment problem matches n workers to n tasks, one each, at least total cost.
  41. Resource Allocation: Resource allocation distributes limited budget, staff, equipment or time among competing activities to achieve the most benefit.
  42. Markov Decision Processes: A Markov decision process models sequential decisions under uncertainty: in each state you choose an action, receive a reward and move randomly to a new state.

School 22: Time Series and Forecasting

34 lessons

Time Series and Forecasting: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Time Series Analysis: A time series is a sequence of observations ordered in time, such as monthly sales, daily temperatures or heartbeats.
  2. Time Series Decomposition: Decomposition splits a series into trend–cycle, seasonal and remainder components.
  3. Trend Analysis: Trend analysis estimates the long-run direction of a series and asks whether an apparent trend is real.
  4. Seasonality: Seasonality is regular variation with a fixed period: higher energy use in winter, retail spikes at Christmas, rush-hour traffic.
  5. Stationarity: A stationary series has statistical properties that don't change over time: constant mean, variance and autocorrelation structure.
  6. Differencing: Differencing replaces a series by the changes between successive values.
  7. Autocorrelation: Autocorrelation measures how strongly a series is correlated with its own past values.
  8. Partial Autocorrelation: The partial autocorrelation at lag k measures the correlation between yₜ and yₜ₋ₖ after removing the effects of the lags in between.
  9. Moving Averages: Moving averages smooth a series by averaging neighbouring values, revealing trends and removing noise or seasonality.
  10. Exponential Smoothing: Exponential smoothing forecasts with weighted averages of past observations, with weights falling off exponentially for older data.
  11. AR Models: An autoregressive model predicts a value from a linear combination of its own past values plus noise.
  12. MA Models: A moving-average model expresses each value as a combination of current and past random shocks.
  13. ARMA Models: ARMA models combine autoregressive and moving-average parts, describing stationary series parsimoniously.
  14. ARIMA Models: ARIMA models extend ARMA to non-stationary series by differencing first: ARIMA(p, d, q) applies an ARMA(p, q) to the d-times differenced data.
  15. SARIMA Models: Seasonal ARIMA models add seasonal AR, MA and differencing terms at multiples of the seasonal period.
  16. Multivariate Time Series: Multivariate time series track several related variables over time, such as GDP, inflation and interest rates.
  17. Vector Autoregression (VAR): A vector autoregression models several series together, each regressed on lags of all of them.
  18. Vector Error Correction Models: When non-stationary series share a long-run equilibrium, they're cointegrated, and a vector error correction model describes both their short-run changes and their pull back towards equilibrium.
  19. State-Space Models: State-space models describe an observed series through an unobserved state that evolves over time.
  20. Kalman Filters: The Kalman filter estimates the hidden state of a linear system from noisy measurements, updating its estimate each time a new observation arrives.
  21. Hidden Markov Models: A hidden Markov model assumes the system switches between unobserved discrete states, each producing observations with its own distribution.
  22. Functional Time Series: Functional time series are sequences of curves rather than numbers: a daily electricity demand curve, a yearly mortality-by-age curve, or the yield curve each day.
  23. ARCH Models: ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity) models let a series' variance depend on recent squared shocks, capturing the clustering of volatility.
  24. GARCH Models: GARCH models generalise ARCH by letting the conditional variance depend on its own past values too.
  25. Volatility Modelling: Volatility, the size of typical price fluctuations, is central to risk management and option pricing.
  26. Change-Point Detection: Change-point detection finds times when a series' statistical properties shift: its mean, variance or trend.
  27. Structural Break Analysis: Structural breaks are lasting changes in the relationships a model describes, such as a policy change altering how inflation responds to interest rates.
  28. Spectral Analysis: Spectral analysis decomposes a time series into oscillations of different frequencies, showing how much variance each frequency contributes.
  29. Frequency-Domain Analysis: Frequency-domain methods analyse and filter series through their frequency components using the Fourier transform.
  30. Forecast Evaluation: Forecast evaluation measures how good forecasts are and compares methods fairly.
  31. Prediction Intervals: A prediction interval gives a range in which a future observation is expected to fall with stated probability.
  32. Probabilistic Forecasting: Probabilistic forecasting provides a full predictive distribution for future values, not just a point or interval.
  33. Intermittent Demand Forecasting: Intermittent demand has many periods of zero demand and occasional non-zero values, typical of spare parts and slow-moving stock.
  34. Causal Forecasting: Causal (explanatory) forecasting predicts a variable from other variables believed to drive it, such as sales from price, advertising and weather.

Control Theory and Systems: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Control Theory: Control theory designs inputs that make a dynamical system behave as we want: a thermostat holding temperature, cruise control holding speed, an autopilot holding course.
  2. Linear Systems: Linear systems obey superposition: the response to a sum of inputs is the sum of the responses.
  3. Nonlinear Systems: Real systems are often non-linear: saturating actuators, friction, aerodynamics, chemical kinetics.
  4. State-Space Representation: State-space models describe a system by a vector of state variables and first-order differential equations, ẋ = Ax + Bu and y = Cx + Du.
  5. Transfer Functions: A transfer function is the Laplace transform of a system's output divided by that of its input, assuming zero initial conditions.
  6. Hybrid Systems: Hybrid systems combine continuous dynamics with discrete events and modes, like a bouncing ball, a thermostat switching a heater, or a car changing gears.
  7. Switched Systems: A switched system changes between several dynamical modes according to a switching signal, as in power converters or gear changes.
  8. Open-Loop Control: Open-loop control applies a pre-planned input without measuring the result, like a toaster timer or a washing machine cycle.
  9. Closed-Loop Control: Closed-loop control measures the output and uses the error to adjust the input continuously.
  10. Feedback Control: Feedback is the principle behind all closed-loop control: measure, compare, correct.
  11. PID Control: PID controllers combine proportional, integral and derivative actions on the error.
  12. Stability Theory: Stability theory asks whether a system returns to equilibrium after a disturbance, or whether its responses stay bounded.
  13. Lyapunov Stability: Lyapunov's direct method proves stability without solving the equations, by finding an energy-like function that always decreases along trajectories.
  14. Controllability: A system is controllable if inputs can steer it from any state to any other in finite time.
  15. Observability: A system is observable if its internal state can be worked out from its outputs and inputs over time.
  16. Frequency Response: The frequency response describes how a stable linear system scales and shifts sinusoids of each frequency.
  17. Bode Plots: Bode plots show a system's gain in decibels and its phase against frequency on a logarithmic scale.
  18. Nyquist Stability Criterion: The Nyquist criterion decides closed-loop stability from the open-loop frequency response by counting how the Nyquist plot encircles the point −1.
  19. Root Locus: The root locus shows how the closed-loop poles move in the complex plane as a gain varies from zero to infinity.
  20. State Estimation: State estimation reconstructs a system's internal state from noisy, incomplete measurements.
  21. Kalman Filtering: In control, the Kalman filter is the optimal state observer for linear systems with Gaussian process and measurement noise.
  22. System Identification: System identification builds mathematical models of dynamical systems from measured input–output data.
  23. Linear Quadratic Regulation: The linear quadratic regulator chooses state feedback that minimises a weighted sum of state deviations and control effort.
  24. Model Predictive Control: Model predictive control repeatedly solves an optimisation over a future horizon using a model, applies the first input, then re-plans with new measurements.
  25. Robust Control: Robust control designs controllers that keep stability and performance despite uncertainty in the plant model.
  26. Adaptive Control: Adaptive controllers adjust their own parameters online as they learn about an uncertain or changing plant.
  27. Stochastic Control: Stochastic control chooses actions for systems driven by random disturbances, optimising expected performance.
  28. Nonlinear Control: Nonlinear control designs controllers that work across large operating ranges where linear approximations fail.
  29. Distributed Control: Distributed control coordinates many agents, such as drones, robots, power generators or sensors, each acting on local information and communicating with neighbours.
  30. Networked Control Systems: Networked control systems close feedback loops over communication networks, which add delays, packet losses and limited data rates.

  40. PARI/GP: PARI/GP is a free computer algebra system specialising in number theory, started by Henri Cohen and colleagues at Bordeaux in 1985.
  41. FEniCS: FEniCS is an open-source platform for solving partial differential equations with the finite element method.
  42. COMSOL Multiphysics: COMSOL Multiphysics is a commercial simulation package, originally released in 1998 as FEMLAB, for finite element modelling of coupled physical phenomena.
  43. ANSYS: ANSYS is a widely used commercial suite for engineering simulation, founded by John Swanson in 1970.
  44. OpenFOAM: OpenFOAM is a free, open-source C++ toolbox for computational fluid dynamics, released as open source in 2004.
  45. MapleSim: MapleSim is a commercial physical modelling tool from Maplesoft, released in 2008 and built on the Modelica language and Maple's symbolic engine.
  46. Simulink: Simulink, from MathWorks, is a graphical environment for modelling, simulating and analysing dynamic systems with block diagrams.
  47. Gurobi: Gurobi is a commercial optimisation solver for linear, quadratic and mixed-integer programming, founded in 2008 by Zonghao Gu, Edward Rothberg and Robert Bixby.
  48. IBM ILOG CPLEX: CPLEX is a commercial optimisation solver for linear, quadratic and mixed-integer programming, originally developed by Robert Bixby from 1988 and now owned by IBM.
  49. OR-Tools: Google OR-Tools is a free, open-source software suite for combinatorial optimisation.
  50. CVXPY: CVXPY is an open-source Python library for convex optimisation using disciplined convex programming.

School 28: School and Examination Mathematics

25 lessons

School and Examination Mathematics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Primary Mathematics: Primary mathematics builds number sense, calculation, shape, measure and early data handling from age 5 to 11.
  2. Functional Skills Mathematics: Functional Skills Mathematics is an English qualification in practical maths for work and life, available at Entry Levels 1 to 3, Level 1 and Level 2.
  3. Foundation Mathematics: "Foundation mathematics" covers the core skills needed before more advanced study: the GCSE Foundation tier, and foundation-year maths that prepares students for university courses in science, engineering and economics.
  4. GCSE Mathematics: GCSE Mathematics is the main maths qualification in England for students aged 14 to 16, graded 9 (highest) to 1.
  5. Core Mathematics: Core Maths is a Level 3 qualification in England for students aged 16 to 18 who passed GCSE maths but aren't taking A level maths.
  6. A-Level Mathematics: A level Mathematics is the main advanced maths qualification for students aged 16 to 18 in England, essential for degrees in mathematics, physics, engineering, economics and computing.
  7. Pure Mathematics: Pure mathematics at A level is the core of algebra, functions, calculus, trigonometry and proof that every applied topic relies on.
  8. Mechanics: Mechanics applies mathematics to motion and forces: how objects move, why they move and what holds them still.
  9. Statistics (A level): The statistics in A level Mathematics teaches sampling, data presentation, probability, the binomial and normal distributions and hypothesis testing, using a real large data set.
  10. Further Mathematics: A level Further Mathematics extends A level Mathematics with complex numbers, matrices, further calculus, proof by induction and optional applied or pure modules.
  11. International Baccalaureate Mathematics: The International Baccalaureate (IB) Diploma offers two mathematics courses, Analysis and Approaches and Applications and Interpretation, each at Standard Level or Higher Level.
  12. University Entrance Mathematics: Some universities set extra mathematics admissions tests for competitive courses, such as the MAT (Oxford) and STEP (Cambridge), and the TMUA used by several universities.
  13. SAT Mathematics: The SAT is a US college admissions test with a Reading and Writing section and a Math section, now taken digitally.
  14. ACT Mathematics: The ACT is a US college admissions test whose Mathematics section covers pre-algebra through elementary trigonometry, testing speed and accuracy.
  15. GRE Quantitative Reasoning: The GRE General Test is used for many graduate programmes, and its Quantitative Reasoning measure tests arithmetic, algebra, geometry and data analysis at roughly high school level, with an emphasis on reasoning.
  16. Word Problems: Word problems describe a situation in words and ask you to turn it into mathematics.
  17. Graph Interpretation: Graph interpretation means reading information from charts and graphs, describing trends and drawing conclusions, while spotting misleading presentations.
  18. Percentages and Interest: Percentage questions appear in every maths exam: percentage of an amount, increases and decreases, reverse percentages, and simple and compound interest.
  19. Speed, Distance and Time: Speed, distance and time problems link three quantities with one formula.
  20. Probability Problems: Exam probability problems test listing outcomes, tree diagrams, Venn diagrams, two-way tables and conditional probability.
  21. Mathematical Problem Solving: Problem solving is what you do when you don't immediately know a method.
  22. Exam Techniques: Exam technique turns knowledge into marks: planning time, reading questions precisely, showing working and checking.
  23. Mental Arithmetic: Mental arithmetic is calculating in your head using number facts and flexible strategies.
  24. Calculator Skills: A calculator is a powerful tool only if you use it well: entering expressions correctly, using memory and special functions, and recognising when an answer is wrong.
  25. Estimation and Checking: Estimation gives a quick approximate answer by rounding, and checking confirms an answer is right.

School 29: Everyday and Practical Mathematics

28 lessons

Everyday and Practical Mathematics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Financial Literacy: Financial literacy is the knowledge and confidence to manage money well: budgeting, saving, borrowing, comparing products and avoiding scams.
  2. Personal Budgeting: A personal budget plans how income is spent and saved, so that money lasts the month and goals are reached.
  3. Household Budgeting: Household budgeting manages shared costs: housing, energy, council tax, food and bills split between members.
  4. Tax Calculations: Income tax and National Insurance are taken from earnings using bands: different rates apply to different slices of income.
  5. VAT Calculations: Value Added Tax (VAT) is a tax on most goods and services in the UK.
  6. Discounts: Discounts reduce prices by a percentage, a fixed amount or a deal such as "3 for 2".
  7. Profit and Loss: Profit is what's left from sales after costs.
  8. Currency Conversion: Currency conversion turns an amount in one currency into another using an exchange rate.
  9. Currency and Exchange Rates: Exchange rates move with trade, interest rates, inflation and confidence.
  10. Percent Change: Percentage change measures how much a quantity has grown or shrunk relative to where it started.
  11. Compound Growth: Compound growth happens when something grows by a fixed percentage each period, so the growth itself grows.
  12. Inflation Calculations: Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, measured in the UK mainly by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI).
  13. Loan Repayments: Most loans are repaid in equal monthly instalments that cover both interest and part of the balance.
  14. Mortgage Calculations: A mortgage is a long-term loan to buy property, secured on the home.
  15. Investment Returns: Investment returns combine income (dividends or interest) and changes in value.
  16. Measurement: Measurement assigns numbers to quantities like length, mass, time and temperature using standard units.
  17. Unit Conversion: Unit conversion changes a quantity from one unit to another without changing its size: metres to centimetres, square metres to square centimetres, miles to kilometres.
  18. Time Calculations: Time calculations involve the 24-hour clock, durations, timetables, time zones and converting between hours, minutes and decimals.
  19. Speed and Distance: Everyday speed and distance problems include planning journeys, fuel use, running pace and stopping distances.
  20. Ratios in Recipes: Recipes are ratios: the proportions of ingredients matter more than the amounts.
  21. Scaling and Proportions: Proportion problems appear everywhere: map scales, model sizes, best-buy comparisons, staffing and dosages.
  22. Area and Volume Calculations: Practical area and volume calculations decide how much paint, turf, flooring, concrete or water a job needs.
  23. Construction Mathematics: Construction mathematics turns plans into materials and measurements: setting out square corners, calculating concrete and bricks, working out roof pitches and designing stairs within building regulations.
  24. Surveying Mathematics: Surveying measures land: distances, angles, heights and levels, for maps, building sites and roads.
  25. Reading Graphs: Everyday graphs show energy use, prices, tariffs and conversions.
  26. Reading Tables: Tables organise information for timetables, nutrition labels, distance charts and tax bands.
  27. Risk and Probability: Everyday decisions involve risk: insurance, health choices, travel, investments and weather.
  28. Estimation and Mental Maths: In everyday life, quick estimates check bills, budgets, tips and quantities on the spot.
