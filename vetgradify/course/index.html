Veterinary Science: a free course on VetGradify

VetGradify's free Veterinary Science course runs from Comparative Anatomy to Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice: 209 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for veterinary students.

Education, not veterinary advice. VetGradify teaches veterinary science for study. It isn't a veterinary qualification, doesn't replace your degree's teaching or extra-mural studies, and doesn't authorise anyone to treat animals. Only a veterinary surgeon can diagnose, prescribe and treat (Veterinary Surgeons Act 1966). Doses and figures in worked examples are illustrative, never for a real animal. If you are worried about an animal, contact your vet; out of hours, contact an emergency vet. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a veterinary surgeon, so they may contain errors: check important points against your course materials, the BSAVA formulary and official RCVS, VMD and APHA guidance.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Comparative Anatomy

18 lessons

Comparative Anatomy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Anatomical terms of position and direction: Veterinary anatomy uses a standard language, set out in the Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria, so that a radiograph report, a surgical note or a referral letter means the same thing to every reader.
  2. The skeleton and joints compared: The skeletons of domestic mammals share one plan, modified for how each species moves and feeds.
  3. Muscles, tendons and fascia: Skeletal muscles move the skeleton through tendons, while ligaments, fascia, bursae and tendon sheaths shape and protect that movement.
  4. The thoracic limb of the dog and cat: The carnivore forelimb carries about 60% of the body's weight and is attached to the trunk by muscle.
  5. The equine distal limb and hoof: The horse stands on a single digit, and most equine lameness arises below the carpus and tarsus.
  6. The pelvic limb and the equine stay apparatus: The pelvic limb is the engine of propulsion, joined to the spine at the sacroiliac joint.
  7. The bovine foot and claw: Cattle walk on two claws per foot, and lameness from claw lesions is one of the biggest welfare and economic problems in dairy herds.
  8. The vertebral column and spinal cord: The vertebral column supports the body and protects the spinal cord, and it is the site of some of the commonest neurological problems in dogs.
  9. The skull, teeth and dental formulae: The skull houses the brain and sense organs and carries the teeth, whose number, shape and growth reflect each species' diet.
  10. The brain and cranial nerves: The brain's regions and the twelve pairs of cranial nerves are tested every time a vet examines a head.
  11. The thorax, heart and lungs: The thorax holds the heart and lungs within the ribs and diaphragm.
  12. The carnivore digestive tract: Dogs and cats have a simple stomach and a short intestine suited to a diet rich in protein and fat.
  13. The equine digestive tract: The horse is a hindgut fermenter: a small stomach and a long small intestine feed a huge caecum and colon where microbes ferment fibre.
  14. The ruminant stomach: Ruminants digest grass with the help of microbes in a four-chambered stomach.
  15. The urinary system: The kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra are built on one plan, but kidney shape and the male urethra differ markedly between species.
  16. Male and female reproductive anatomy: Reproductive anatomy varies more between domestic species than almost any other system, and the differences matter for breeding, neutering and obstetrics.
  17. Avian anatomy: Birds are built for flight and egg-laying: a light, fused skeleton, a gut without teeth, a cloaca and, in the hen, a single functional ovary.
  18. Skin, hoof, horn and the mammary gland: The skin and its derivatives, hair, glands, claws, horn and the mammary gland, protect the body, regulate temperature and in mammals feed the young.

School 2: Veterinary Physiology

18 lessons

Veterinary Physiology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Homeostasis and body fluids: Physiology is the study of how an animal keeps its internal environment stable while the world around it changes.
  2. Membrane potentials and nerve conduction: Nerves and muscles signal with electrical changes across their membranes.
  3. The neuromuscular junction and muscle contraction: Every voluntary movement depends on acetylcholine crossing the neuromuscular junction and calcium switching on actin and myosin.
  4. The autonomic nervous system: The autonomic nervous system controls the heart, blood vessels, glands and gut without conscious effort.
  5. The cardiac cycle: The heart is two pumps in series whose valves open and close as pressures change.
  6. Blood pressure and its control: Blood pressure drives blood through the tissues and must be held within limits from moment to moment.
  7. Blood: cells, clotting and blood groups in animals: Blood carries oxygen, fights infection and seals its own leaks, and its cells and blood groups differ between species.
  8. Respiration and gas exchange: Breathing brings oxygen to the blood and removes carbon dioxide, and the haemoglobin–oxygen curve explains how oxygen is loaded in the lung and unloaded in the tissues.
  9. Avian respiration: Birds breathe with rigid lungs ventilated by air sacs, a system more efficient than the mammalian lung.
  10. Rumen fermentation and volatile fatty acids: A ruminant feeds its microbes, and its microbes feed it.
  11. Monogastric and hindgut digestion: Dogs, cats and pigs digest mainly with their own enzymes, horses and rabbits rely on microbes in the hindgut, and the cat has metabolic needs that make it a true carnivore.
  12. Thermoregulation: Mammals and birds hold their core temperature within narrow limits by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  13. Renal function and urine concentration: The kidneys filter the plasma, reclaim what the body needs and excrete the rest, adjusting water and electrolyte balance minute by minute.
  14. Acid–base balance: Enzymes work only within a narrow pH range, so the body buffers acid, breathes off carbon dioxide and excretes acid in urine.
  15. The hypothalamus and pituitary: The hypothalamus links the nervous system to the endocrine system through the pituitary gland.
  16. Thyroid, adrenal and endocrine pancreas: The thyroid sets metabolic rate, the adrenal glands manage stress, salt and water, and the endocrine pancreas controls blood glucose.
  17. Reproductive cycles in domestic species: Each domestic species has its own breeding pattern, from the cow's year-round 21-day cycle to the bitch's single long cycle and the cat's induced ovulation.
  18. Pregnancy, parturition and lactation: Pregnancy, birth and milk production are controlled by the placenta, the fetus and the mother's hormones, and they differ between species in ways that matter for every vet.

School 3: Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics

16 lessons

Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The animal cell and its organelles: Every tissue in the body is built from cells that share the same basic parts.
  2. Proteins and enzymes: Proteins do most of the work of the body, and enzymes are the proteins that speed its chemical reactions.
  3. Carbohydrate metabolism: Glucose is the fuel every cell can use, and many cannot do without it.
  4. Lipid metabolism and ketogenesis: Fat is the body's largest energy store, and how it is mobilised and burnt underlies several of the most important metabolic diseases in practice.
  5. Protein and nitrogen metabolism: Animals must dispose of the nitrogen from the protein they break down.
  6. Energy metabolism in the ruminant: A cow lives on volatile fatty acids rather than glucose, yet her udder needs huge amounts of glucose for milk.
  7. Minerals, trace elements and vitamins: Small amounts of minerals and vitamins keep enzymes, bones, blood and nerves working, and deficiencies and toxicities are common causes of disease in farm and companion animals.
  8. DNA, genes and gene expression: Every inherited trait and inherited disease starts in DNA.
  9. Mendelian inheritance in domestic animals: Many traits and diseases of domestic animals are controlled by single genes and follow Mendel's laws.
  10. Sex-linked and polygenic traits: Some traits follow the sex chromosomes and many important ones are controlled by many genes.
  11. Inherited diseases and breed health schemes: Selective breeding has concentrated inherited diseases in many breeds of dogs, cats, horses and cattle, and screening schemes aim to reduce them.
  12. Genetic testing in animals: DNA tests now let owners, breeders and farmers know an animal's genotype for many diseases and traits, verify parentage and predict genetic merit.
  13. Population genetics and inbreeding: Breeds and herds are populations, and their health depends on how genes are shared within them.
  14. Animal breeding and selection: Breeding programmes change populations deliberately, for production, health and temperament, and sometimes create welfare problems by selecting too narrowly.
  15. Embryology and organogenesis: Every organ, and many birth defects, can be traced to events in the first weeks of development.
  16. Congenital abnormalities: Congenital abnormalities are defects present at birth, caused by genes, infections, toxins, drugs or nutrition acting on the developing embryo.

School 4: Animal Husbandry, Nutrition and Welfare

19 lessons

Animal Husbandry, Nutrition and Welfare: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What animal welfare means: Animal welfare is the state of an animal as it experiences it.
  2. The Five Freedoms and the Five Domains: Two frameworks dominate practical welfare assessment.
  3. The Animal Welfare Act 2006: The Animal Welfare Act 2006 is the main welfare law in England and Wales.
  4. Assessing welfare in practice: Welfare must be assessed with evidence, not assumptions.
  5. Behaviour and its role in welfare: Behaviour is both a window on an animal's welfare and part of its welfare.
  6. Pain recognition and scoring: Animals cannot tell us they are in pain, so vets must recognise it from behaviour and physiology.
  7. Husbandry of dogs and cats: Dogs and cats are the most common pets in the UK, and their welfare depends on owners understanding their needs.
  8. Husbandry of horses: Horses evolved as grazing, herd-living animals on open plains.
  9. Dairy and beef cattle systems: Cattle farming in the UK ranges from intensive dairy units to extensive upland suckler herds.
  10. Sheep and pig systems: Sheep and pig farming present very different welfare challenges.
  11. Poultry systems: Billions of chickens are farmed worldwide, so poultry welfare has enormous scale.
  12. Rabbits, small mammals and exotic pets: Rabbits, guinea pigs and exotic pets are often kept in ways that do not meet their needs.
  13. Biosecurity on farm: Biosecurity means keeping disease out of a farm and stopping it spreading within and beyond it.
  14. Principles of animal nutrition: Every animal needs energy, protein, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals and water in the right balance.
  15. Feeding dogs and cats: Dogs and cats have different nutritional needs.
  16. Feeding horses: The horse's digestive system is built for a near-continuous supply of fibre.
  17. Ruminant nutrition and rationing: Ruminants rely on rumen microbes to digest fibre.
  18. Body condition scoring: Body condition scoring assesses an animal's fat reserves by sight and touch.
  19. Welfare at transport and slaughter: Transport and slaughter are critical points for farm animal welfare.

School 5: Veterinary Pathology

18 lessons

Veterinary Pathology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cell injury and cell death: Disease begins when cells are injured.
  2. Acute inflammation: Acute inflammation is the body's rapid response to injury or infection.
  3. Chronic inflammation and repair: When inflammation persists, its character changes, and tissues must repair.
  4. Disturbances of circulation: Many diseases involve problems with blood flow and fluid balance.
  5. Disorders of growth: Cells and tissues can adapt by changing their size, number or type.
  6. Neoplasia: principles: Neoplasia is uncontrolled growth of cells.
  7. Tumour classification and grading: Tumours are named and classified by their tissue of origin, and graded and staged to guide prognosis.
  8. Pathology of the cardiovascular system: Heart disease is common in dogs and cats.
  9. Pathology of the respiratory system: Respiratory diseases are common in all domestic species.
  10. Pathology of the alimentary system: Diseases of the digestive tract range from dental disease to life-threatening obstructions.
  11. Pathology of the liver and pancreas: The liver and pancreas have vital metabolic and digestive roles.
  12. Pathology of the urinary system: Kidney and lower urinary tract disease are common, especially in cats.
  13. Pathology of the skin: Skin disease is one of the most common reasons animals are taken to a veterinary surgeon.
  14. Pathology of the nervous system: Diseases of the brain, spinal cord and nerves cause a wide range of signs.
  15. The post-mortem examination: The post-mortem examination, or necropsy, helps establish the cause of death and supports disease surveillance.
  16. Cytology and histopathology: Examining cells and tissues under the microscope is central to diagnosis.
  17. Clinical pathology: haematology: The complete blood count provides essential information about health and disease.
  18. Clinical pathology: biochemistry: Serum biochemistry helps assess organ function and metabolic disease.

School 6: Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology

18 lessons

Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Bacteria of veterinary importance: Bacteria cause many of the most important diseases of domestic animals.
  2. Viruses of veterinary importance: Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that cause many notifiable and economically important animal diseases.
  3. Fungi and prions: Fungi and prions are less common causes of disease than bacteria and viruses but include important zoonoses and fatal neurological diseases.
  4. Antimicrobial resistance: Antimicrobial resistance threatens both animal and human health.
  5. Disinfection and sterilisation: Killing or removing microorganisms from surfaces, instruments and skin prevents infection in practices and on farms.
  6. Nematodes of domestic animals: Nematodes, or roundworms, are the most important parasites of grazing livestock and common in pets.
  7. Cestodes and trematodes: Tapeworms and flukes are flatworms with complex life cycles that involve intermediate hosts.
  8. Ectoparasites: fleas, lice, mites and ticks: Ectoparasites live on or in the skin and cause itching, skin disease and the spread of infections.
  9. Protozoa: Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic parasites that cause diarrhoea, abortion and blood disease in animals.
  10. Parasite control and anthelmintic resistance: Parasite control keeps animals healthy and productive, but overuse of wormers has bred resistant worms.
  11. Innate immunity: Innate immunity is the body's first, fast and non-specific defence against infection.
  12. Adaptive immunity: Adaptive immunity is specific, slower to start and creates memory.
  13. Antibodies and the humoral response: Antibodies are the effector molecules of humoral immunity.
  14. Hypersensitivity and autoimmunity: The immune system can cause disease when it overreacts or attacks the body's own tissues.
  15. Maternally derived immunity and colostrum: Newborn animals depend on antibodies from their mother for early protection.
  16. Principles of vaccination: Vaccination is one of the most effective tools in veterinary medicine.
  17. Vaccination of companion animals: Vaccination protects dogs, cats and rabbits against serious infectious diseases.
  18. Vaccination of farm animals and horses: Vaccination is central to herd health planning and to equine competition rules.

School 7: Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics

18 lessons

Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Pharmacokinetics: Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug.
  2. Pharmacodynamics: Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body.
  3. Species differences in drug handling: Drugs that are safe in one species can be dangerous in another.
  4. Adverse drug reactions and pharmacovigilance: Every medicine can cause unwanted effects.
  5. The Veterinary Medicines Regulations: The Veterinary Medicines Regulations control how veterinary medicines are made, authorised, supplied and used in the UK.
  6. The prescribing cascade: When no authorised medicine exists for a condition in a species, vets may use the prescribing cascade.
  7. Prescription categories and controlled drugs: Veterinary medicines are classified into legal categories that control who may prescribe and supply them, and some are controlled drugs with extra rules.
  8. Withdrawal periods and food-producing animals: Medicines given to food-producing animals can leave residues in meat, milk and eggs.
  9. Responsible use of antimicrobials: Vets have a legal and professional duty to use antimicrobials responsibly.
  10. Antimicrobials: Antimicrobials kill or inhibit bacteria by acting on targets that human and animal cells lack.
  11. Antiparasitic drugs: Antiparasitic drugs control worms, flukes, protozoa and ectoparasites.
  12. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: NSAIDs are among the most widely used drugs in veterinary practice for pain and inflammation.
  13. Corticosteroids: Corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drugs.
  14. Opioids and analgesia: Opioids are the most effective drugs for moderate to severe pain.
  15. Sedatives and anaesthetic agents: Sedation and general anaesthesia allow safe handling, imaging and surgery.
  16. Cardiovascular drugs: Drugs for heart failure and hypertension are widely used in dogs and cats.
  17. Drugs acting on the gut: Gastrointestinal drugs treat vomiting, ulceration, diarrhoea and constipation.
  18. Drug calculations: principles: Accurate calculations prevent medication errors.

School 8: Clinical Method and Reasoning

17 lessons

Clinical Method and Reasoning: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The medical history: Most diagnoses are made, or strongly suggested, by the history.
  2. Consultation models and patient-centred care: Consultation models turn communication into teachable, observable skills.
  3. Communicating risk and shared decision-making: Patients can only decide well if risks are explained in ways they understand.
  4. Breaking bad news: How bad news is given affects patients for years.
  5. Principles of physical examination: Examination is a skill of observation, technique and interpretation, built on consent and dignity.
  6. Vital signs and the early warning score (NEWS2): Deterioration is usually preceded by hours of abnormal observations.
  7. The cardiovascular examination: The cardiovascular examination looks for the signs of heart failure, valve disease, endocarditis and vascular disease, from the hands to the heart.
  8. The respiratory examination: The respiratory examination combines inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation to locate disease in the airways, lung tissue or pleura.
  9. The abdominal examination: The abdominal examination detects organ enlargement, masses, fluid, tenderness and the signs of chronic liver disease.
  10. The neurological examination: The neurological examination localises a lesion, which narrows the causes dramatically.
  11. Clinical reasoning and diagnostic error: Diagnosis is a reasoning process that can be studied and improved.
  12. Interpreting tests: sensitivity, specificity and predictive values: No test is perfect, and the same result means different things in different patients.
  13. Interpreting a full blood count and electrolytes: The full blood count and urea and electrolytes are the most requested tests in hospital.
  14. Arterial blood gas interpretation: An arterial blood gas tells you about oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base status within minutes.
  15. The 12-lead ECG: a systematic approach: The ECG is cheap, fast and diagnostic for arrhythmias, ischaemia and electrolyte disturbance.
  16. Chest X-ray interpretation: The chest X-ray is the most common imaging test in medicine.
  17. Presenting a patient and documenting care: Clinical information only helps patients if it is communicated clearly.

School 9: Small Animal Medicine and Surgery

21 lessons

Small Animal Medicine and Surgery: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Vomiting and diarrhoea in dogs and cats: Vomiting and diarrhoea are among the commonest reasons pets are brought to a vet.
  2. Cardiac disease in dogs and cats: Heart disease is common in small animals and differs between dogs and cats.
  3. Respiratory disease in dogs and cats: Respiratory disease ranges from mild infections to life-threatening airway obstruction.
  4. Chronic kidney disease: Chronic kidney disease is common in older cats and dogs.
  5. Diabetes mellitus: Diabetes mellitus is a common endocrine disease of dogs and cats.
  6. Hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism: Thyroid disease is common in small animals: hyperthyroidism mostly affects older cats and hypothyroidism mostly affects dogs.
  7. Hyperadrenocorticism and hypoadrenocorticism: The adrenal glands can produce too much or too little cortisol.
  8. Skin and ear disease: Skin and ear problems are among the commonest reasons dogs see a vet.
  9. Infectious diseases of dogs and cats: Infectious diseases remain important in small animal practice despite vaccination.
  10. Seizures and neurological disease: Seizures are a common neurological emergency in dogs and cats.
  11. Common poisonings: Pets often eat things that are toxic to them.
  12. Oncology in practice: Cancer is a leading cause of death in older dogs and cats.
  13. Neutering: Neutering is one of the commonest operations in small animal practice.
  14. Gastric dilatation and volvulus: Gastric dilatation and volvulus is a life-threatening emergency in large, deep-chested dogs.
  15. Intestinal foreign bodies: Dogs and cats often swallow objects that can block the gut.
  16. Fracture management principles: Fractures are common in dogs and cats after road traffic accidents and falls.
  17. Cruciate ligament disease: Cranial cruciate ligament disease is the commonest cause of hind limb lameness in dogs.
  18. Hip and elbow dysplasia: Hip and elbow dysplasia are inherited developmental diseases that cause lameness and arthritis in many dog breeds.
  19. Dental disease: Dental disease is the most common health problem in adult dogs and cats.
  20. Rabbit medicine: Rabbits are the third most popular mammalian pet in the UK and have very different needs from dogs and cats.
  21. Reptiles and birds in practice: Reptiles and birds are increasingly common pets, and most of their illnesses come from poor husbandry.

School 10: Equine Practice

15 lessons

Equine Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Colic: Colic means abdominal pain and is one of the most common equine emergencies.
  2. Equine respiratory disease: Respiratory disease affects horses' welfare and performance.
  3. Equine infectious and notifiable diseases: Some equine diseases spread rapidly or threaten trade, so they are notifiable.
  4. Laminitis and equine metabolic syndrome: Laminitis is a painful, often recurring disease of the hoof, and most cases are linked to hormonal disease.
  5. Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction: Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction, often called equine Cushing's disease, is common in older horses and ponies.
  6. Equine skin disease: Skin disease is common in horses and often linked to the environment.
  7. Lameness diagnosis in the horse: Lameness is the most common cause of lost performance in horses.
  8. Diagnostic analgesia: Diagnostic analgesia uses local anaesthetic to find the source of pain in a lame horse.
  9. Common causes of lameness: A small number of conditions cause most equine lameness.
  10. Wounds and synovial sepsis: Horses often injure themselves, and wounds near joints or tendon sheaths can be life-threatening.
  11. Castration in the horse: Castration is one of the most common surgical procedures in horses.
  12. Equine reproduction: Breeding horses successfully needs an understanding of the mare's seasonal cycle and careful veterinary management.
  13. The neonatal foal: The first days of a foal's life are critical, and problems can become emergencies within hours.
  14. Equine dentistry: Dental disease is common in horses and affects eating, comfort and performance.
  15. Equine passports and medicines records: Every horse in the UK must have a passport, which also controls which medicines it may receive.

School 11: Farm Animal Practice and Herd Health

15 lessons

Farm Animal Practice and Herd Health: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Mastitis: Mastitis, inflammation of the udder, is one of the most costly diseases of dairy cattle.
  2. Lameness in cattle: Lameness is a major welfare and productivity problem in dairy cattle.
  3. Metabolic disease of dairy cows: The transition period around calving puts great metabolic stress on dairy cows.
  4. Calf health and the colostrum: Calves are born without protective antibodies and rely on colostrum.
  5. Bovine respiratory disease: Bovine respiratory disease, or calf pneumonia, is a leading cause of illness and death in young cattle.
  6. Bovine tuberculosis: Bovine tuberculosis is one of the most serious and costly animal diseases in Great Britain.
  7. Bovine viral diarrhoea and Johne's disease: Bovine viral diarrhoea and Johne's disease are endemic diseases that cause major losses in cattle.
  8. Fertility and herd health planning: Good fertility and proactive herd health planning keep dairy and beef herds productive and healthy.
  9. Sheep health planning: Flock health planning helps sheep farmers prevent disease and improve productivity.
  10. Lambing and neonatal lamb losses: Lambing is the most important time of the sheep year, and lamb losses are a major welfare and economic issue.
  11. Sheep scab, fly strike and lameness: Sheep scab, blowfly strike and lameness are major welfare problems in UK sheep.
  12. Goat medicine: Goats are not simply small sheep: they have distinct diseases, behaviour and drug responses.
  13. Pig health and production: Pig health depends on biosecurity, housing and careful medicine use.
  14. Poultry health and avian influenza: Poultry health is shaped by housing, biosecurity and the threat of avian influenza.
  15. Notifiable diseases of livestock: Some animal diseases are so serious that suspicion must be reported to the government.

School 12: Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice

16 lessons

Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. One Health: The health of people, animals and the environment are closely linked.
  2. Zoonoses: Zoonoses are diseases that pass between animals and people.
  3. Food safety and meat inspection: Vets protect public health and animal welfare in the food chain.
  4. Notifiable diseases and disease control: Controlling serious animal diseases requires legal powers, planning and rapid action.
  5. Surveillance and the role of APHA: Surveillance detects disease threats early and shows whether control is working.
  6. Epidemiology for veterinarians: Epidemiology studies the patterns and causes of disease in populations.
  7. The RCVS and the regulation of the profession: The Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons regulates vets and veterinary nurses in the UK.
  8. The RCVS Code of Professional Conduct: The RCVS Code of Professional Conduct sets the standards vets and veterinary nurses must meet.
  9. Consent and communication with clients: Clients must give informed consent before their animals are treated.
  10. Clinical records and confidentiality: Good records and confidentiality protect animals, clients and vets.
  11. Certification: Vets sign many certificates, from pet travel to export health certificates.
  12. Animal welfare and the law: Animal welfare is protected by law and assessed using scientific frameworks.
  13. Working with veterinary nurses and the wider team: Veterinary practice depends on effective teamwork.
  14. Clinical governance and evidence-based veterinary medicine: Clinical governance helps practices improve care, and evidence-based veterinary medicine helps vets choose the best treatments.
  15. Wellbeing and professional life: Veterinary work is rewarding but demanding, and mental health in the profession is a serious concern.
  16. The Day One Competences: The RCVS sets out the competences new vets need on their first day in practice.
