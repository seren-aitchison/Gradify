History: a free course on HistoryGradify

HistoryGradify's free History course runs from Historical Thinking and Methods to Historical Examination and Revision: 301 lessons in 18 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for history students, GCSE to A level and beyond.

Study material, not a qualification. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials and official sources.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Historical Thinking and Methods

16 lessons

Historical Thinking and Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is history?: History is the study of the human past through the evidence it left behind.
  2. Primary and secondary sources: Primary sources were produced at or close to the time being studied; secondary sources are later accounts that interpret the past.
  3. Historical evidence: Sources become evidence when they are used to answer a question.
  4. Source provenance: Provenance is the origin of a source: who made it, when, where, why and for whom.
  5. Reliability and usefulness: Reliability asks whether a source's account can be trusted; usefulness asks what it can show for a particular enquiry.
  6. Bias and perspective: Every source is written from a perspective.
  7. Historical interpretations: An interpretation is a historian's (or film-maker's, or museum's) considered view of the past.
  8. Cause and consequence: Explaining why things happened is central to history.
  9. Continuity and change: Historians ask not only what changed but what stayed the same, how fast, how far and for whom.
  10. Significance: Not everything in the past is equally significant.
  11. Chronology and periodisation: Chronology is the ordering of events in time; periodisation divides the past into named periods.
  12. Historical argument: A historical argument makes a claim and supports it with evidence and reasoning, while dealing with counter-arguments.
  13. Essay construction: A history essay answers a question with a sustained argument.
  14. Evidence selection: Good history uses the right evidence, not the most evidence.
  15. Evaluating historical claims: Popular history is full of claims that sound plausible but don't survive examination.
  16. Comparing interpretations: Comparing interpretations means identifying how two accounts differ, explaining why, and judging which is more convincing.

School 2: Medieval Britain

18 lessons

Medieval Britain: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Anglo-Saxon England: Between the end of Roman rule in the early fifth century and 1066, England was formed out of several Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.
  2. The Viking invasions: From the late eighth century Scandinavian raiders attacked Britain; from 865 a large army set out to conquer.
  3. Alfred the Great: Alfred, king of Wessex from 871 to 899, survived near-defeat by the Vikings and laid foundations for a united England.
  4. The Norman Conquest: In 1066 England had three kings in one year.
  5. The Battle of Hastings: The battle fought on 14 October 1066 decided the English throne.
  6. William I and Norman control: Winning at Hastings was not the same as controlling England.
  7. Feudalism: Textbooks often describe Norman England as a "feudal pyramid".
  8. The Domesday Book: In 1086 William's commissioners surveyed most of England in a few months.
  9. Medieval monarchy: Medieval kings ruled personally, but not without limits.
  10. Henry II and legal reform: Henry II (1154–1189) restored order after civil war and made royal justice available across England.
  11. Thomas Becket: Thomas Becket rose from royal chancellor to Archbishop of Canterbury, quarrelled with Henry II over the rights of the Church, and was murdered in his cathedral in 1170.
  12. King John and Magna Carta: King John (1199–1216) lost Normandy, quarrelled with the Pope and taxed heavily, until rebel barons forced him to agree Magna Carta in 1215.
  13. The development of Parliament: Parliament grew out of the king's great councils in the thirteenth century.
  14. Medieval society: Medieval people described their society as made up of those who prayed, those who fought and those who worked.
  15. The Black Death: The Black Death reached England in 1348 and killed a large share of the population within two years.
  16. The Peasants' Revolt: In June 1381 rebels from Essex and Kent marched on London, burned the property of the king's ministers and met the fourteen-year-old Richard II.
  17. The Wars of the Roses: Between 1455 and 1487 the houses of Lancaster and York fought for the English crown.
  18. The rise of the Tudor dynasty: Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485 with a weak hereditary claim.

School 3: Tudor England

14 lessons

Tudor England: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Henry VII and dynastic security: Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485 with a weak claim and spent his reign making it secure.
  2. Henry VIII's government: Henry VIII ruled from 1509 to 1547 through powerful ministers, above all Thomas Wolsey and Thomas Cromwell.
  3. The English Reformation: In the 1530s Henry VIII broke with the Roman Catholic Church and made himself head of the Church in England.
  4. The dissolution of the monasteries: Between 1536 and 1540 Henry VIII closed every monastery, nunnery and friary in England and Wales and took their wealth.
  5. Edward VI and Protestant reform: Edward VI became king at nine in 1547, and in his six-year reign England's church became clearly Protestant.
  6. Mary I and Catholic restoration: Mary I, queen from 1553 to 1558, restored Catholicism and the Pope's authority, and burned around 280 Protestants.
  7. Elizabeth I and the religious settlement: Elizabeth I's religious settlement of 1559 created a Protestant Church of England that tried to include as many people as possible.
  8. Elizabethan government: Elizabeth I ruled from 1558 to 1603 through a small Privy Council, Parliament and unpaid local officials.
  9. The Spanish Armada: In 1588 Philip II of Spain sent a great fleet, the Armada, to escort an army to invade England.
  10. Elizabethan exploration: Elizabethan sailors crossed the Atlantic, raided Spanish America and sailed around the world.
  11. Poverty and social policy: Poverty grew in the sixteenth century as population and prices rose.
  12. Tudor culture and society: Tudor society was a strict hierarchy, but the sixteenth century also saw a growing gentry, rising literacy and a flowering of drama.
  13. Tudor foreign policy: Tudor monarchs balanced the two great powers of France and Spain, fought Scotland and struggled to control Ireland.
  14. Succession and the end of the Tudor dynasty: The Tudors were repeatedly troubled by the question of who would succeed.

School 4: Stuart Britain and the Civil War

18 lessons

Stuart Britain and the Civil War: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. James I and the Union of the Crowns: In 1603 James VI of Scotland became James I of England, ruling two kingdoms, and Ireland, as one monarch.
  2. The divine right of kings: The early Stuarts believed that kings were appointed by God and answerable only to Him.
  3. Parliament and taxation: The crown could not pay its way without Parliament, and arguments over money ran through the reigns of James I and Charles I. This lesson explains the crown's financial problems, the disputes they…
  4. Religious tensions under the early Stuarts: Religion divided Stuart England: Puritans wanted further reform, Catholics were feared, and Charles I backed a new style of churchmanship under Archbishop Laud.
  5. Charles I: Charles I became king in 1625 and within four years had quarrelled so badly with Parliament that he ruled without it.
  6. The Personal Rule: From 1629 to 1640 Charles I ruled without Parliament, raising money through old royal rights and reforming the Church through Laud.
  7. The Bishops' Wars: Charles I's attempt to impose a new Prayer Book on Scotland in 1637 provoked a national rebellion and two wars.
  8. The Long Parliament: The Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, dismantled the Personal Rule within a year, but by 1642 the country had divided.
  9. Causes of the English Civil War: Why did England go to war with itself in 1642?
  10. Royalists and Parliamentarians: In 1642 people had to choose between king and Parliament, or try to stay neutral.
  11. Key campaigns and battles of the Civil War: The First Civil War lasted from 1642 to 1646 and ended in victory for Parliament.
  12. The trial and execution of Charles I: On 30 January 1649 Charles I was executed outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, after a trial by a court created by a purged House of Commons.
  13. The Commonwealth: From 1649 to 1653 England was a republic, governed by the Rump Parliament and a Council of State.
  14. Oliver Cromwell: Oliver Cromwell rose from a Huntingdon gentleman to Lord Protector of England, Scotland and Ireland.
  15. The Protectorate: From 1653 to 1659 England was governed by a Lord Protector under a written constitution.
  16. The Restoration: In May 1660 Charles II returned to England, and the monarchy, the House of Lords and the Church of England were restored.
  17. The Glorious Revolution: In 1688 the Catholic James II was driven from the throne and replaced by his Protestant daughter Mary and her husband William of Orange.
  18. The Bill of Rights: The Bill of Rights of 1689 set limits on royal power that still form part of the British constitution.

School 5: The Industrial Revolution

18 lessons

The Industrial Revolution: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Agricultural change: Between about 1700 and 1850 English farming produced far more food with a smaller share of the workforce.
  2. Population growth: England's population grew faster in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries than ever before.
  3. The factory system: The factory brought workers, machines and power together under one roof and one discipline.
  4. The textile industry: Cotton was the leading industry of the Industrial Revolution, transformed by a series of inventions and supplied with raw cotton grown by enslaved people in the Americas.
  5. Steam power: The steam engine freed industry from dependence on water and muscle.
  6. Coal and iron: Coal and iron were the foundations of industrial Britain, supplying energy and the material for machines, bridges and rails.
  7. Railways: Railways transformed travel, trade and everyday life in Britain from the 1830s.
  8. Urbanisation: In 1851 Britain became the first country in which more people lived in towns than in the countryside.
  9. Industrial working conditions: Industrial work meant long hours, harsh discipline and danger, but also regular wages.
  10. Child labour: Children worked in mills, mines and workshops throughout the Industrial Revolution, and campaigns to protect them produced Britain's first effective factory laws.
  11. Public health: Cholera, typhus and typhoid killed thousands in Britain's industrial towns.
  12. Housing and sanitation: Working families in industrial towns crowded into back-to-back houses, courts and cellars, with shared privies and little clean water.
  13. Industrial capitalism: The Industrial Revolution was driven by private investment, markets and the search for profit.
  14. Trade unions: Workers in industrial Britain organised trade unions to protect wages and conditions, despite laws against them.
  15. Chartism: Chartism was the largest working-class political movement of nineteenth-century Britain, demanding the vote for all men.
  16. The Great Reform Act: The Reform Act of 1832 changed who could vote and which places sent MPs to Parliament, after a political crisis that some feared would end in revolution.
  17. Early Victorian social reform: In the 1830s and 1840s Parliament passed a wave of reforms on poverty, work, town government, trade and health.
  18. The changing British economy: By 1851 Britain was the "workshop of the world", but by the 1880s rivals were catching up.

School 6: Nineteenth-Century Britain

15 lessons

Nineteenth-Century Britain: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The British Empire in the nineteenth century: In the nineteenth century the British Empire became the largest the world had seen, covering about a quarter of the world's land by 1900.
  2. Victorian monarchy: Queen Victoria reigned from 1837 to 1901, longer than any previous British monarch.
  3. Parliamentary reform, 1867–1884: Between 1867 and 1885 Parliament extended the vote to most working men and changed how elections were run.
  4. The development of democracy: As the electorate grew, politics changed: parties built national organisations, leaders campaigned to mass audiences, and the elected Commons won out over the Lords.
  5. The growth of the Labour movement: Between the 1880s and 1906 unskilled workers formed new unions and the labour movement created its own political party.
  6. Ireland and the Union: The Act of Union of 1800 made Ireland part of the United Kingdom, but Ireland's Catholic majority remained poor and largely excluded from power.
  7. Irish nationalism: Irish nationalists sought self-government, some through Parliament and some through revolution.
  8. Imperial expansion: Britain added vast territories to its empire in the nineteenth century, from Asia to Africa.
  9. Colonial administration: Britain governed hundreds of millions of people with relatively few British officials.
  10. The Scramble for Africa: Between about 1880 and 1914 European powers divided almost the whole of Africa among themselves.
  11. Imperial resistance: Colonised peoples resisted British rule throughout the nineteenth century, in rebellions, wars and, increasingly, political movements.
  12. The Boer Wars: Britain fought two wars against the Boer republics of southern Africa, in 1880 to 1881 and 1899 to 1902.
  13. Women's suffrage: Women campaigned for the parliamentary vote for over half a century before winning it in 1918 and 1928.
  14. The Liberal welfare reforms: Between 1906 and 1914 Liberal governments introduced pensions, school meals, labour exchanges and national insurance.
  15. Britain's changing international position: In the mid-nineteenth century Britain avoided binding alliances, confident in its navy and empire.

School 7: The First World War

21 lessons

The First World War: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. European power politics: In 1914 Europe was dominated by a handful of Great Powers whose rivalries had been building for decades.
  2. Militarism: Before 1914 the Great Powers built ever larger armies and navies and planned for rapid offensive war.
  3. Alliances: By 1914 Europe was divided between the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente.
  4. Imperial rivalry: Competition for colonies and influence produced repeated crises between the Great Powers before 1914.
  5. Nationalism: Nationalism, the belief that each nation should have its own state and that loyalty to it comes first, both strengthened the Great Powers and threatened to tear Austria-Hungary apart.
  6. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand: On 28 June 1914 a young Bosnian Serb shot the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne in Sarajevo.
  7. The July Crisis: In the five weeks after Sarajevo the Great Powers moved from a Balkan crisis to a European war.
  8. The outbreak of war: In August 1914 millions of men marched to war expecting it to be over quickly.
  9. Trench warfare: From late 1914 the war in the west was fought from trenches.
  10. The Western Front: The Western Front saw the war's most famous battles: Verdun, the Somme and Passchendaele.
  11. Gallipoli: In 1915 the Allies tried to knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war by forcing the Dardanelles.
  12. The Eastern Front, 1914–1917: In the east the war was more mobile than in the west, with huge advances and retreats.
  13. The British Home Front, 1914–1918: The First World War changed life in Britain: government took new powers, men were conscripted, food was rationed and civilians were bombed.
  14. Total war: The First World War demanded the mobilisation of entire societies and economies.
  15. Propaganda in the First World War: Governments in the First World War used propaganda on an unprecedented scale to recruit soldiers, sustain morale and win neutral opinion.
  16. Women's wartime roles: The war drew women into new work, from munitions factories to nursing near the front.
  17. Russia leaves the war: Revolution in 1917 took Russia out of the war.
  18. The United States enters the war: The United States stayed neutral until April 1917.
  19. The Armistice: Germany came close to victory in spring 1918 and was defeated by the autumn.
  20. The Treaty of Versailles: The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, set the terms of peace with Germany.
  21. The consequences of the First World War: The First World War killed millions, destroyed four empires and reshaped politics, economies and memory across the world.

School 8: The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union

20 lessons

The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Tsarist Russia: At the start of the twentieth century Russia was a vast empire ruled by an autocrat, with a largely peasant population and a fast-growing industry.
  2. Nicholas II: Nicholas II, the last tsar, reigned from 1894 to 1917.
  3. Russian society and inequality: Most Russians around 1900 were poor peasants, while a small elite held land, wealth and power.
  4. The 1905 Revolution: In 1905 strikes, mutinies and peasant risings swept Russia after troops fired on peaceful protesters.
  5. The First World War and Russia: The First World War placed unbearable strain on Russia's army, economy and government.
  6. The February Revolution: In February 1917 strikes and bread protests in Petrograd turned into a revolution when soldiers joined them, and within days Nicholas II abdicated.
  7. The Provisional Government: The Provisional Government ruled Russia from March to October 1917 but shared power with the Petrograd Soviet and lost support month by month.
  8. Lenin and the Bolsheviks: Vladimir Lenin built the Bolsheviks into a disciplined party and, after returning to Russia in April 1917, set them on course to seize power.
  9. The October Revolution: On 24 and 25 October 1917 the Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd.
  10. The Russian Civil War: From 1918 to 1921 the Bolsheviks fought a brutal civil war against the Whites, nationalist movements and foreign intervention.
  11. War Communism: From 1918 to 1921 the Bolsheviks ran the economy by force to win the Civil War, taking grain from peasants and nationalising industry.
  12. The New Economic Policy: In March 1921 Lenin replaced War Communism with the New Economic Policy, allowing peasants to sell surplus grain and small traders to operate.
  13. Stalin's rise to power: When Lenin died in January 1924, few expected Joseph Stalin to succeed him.
  14. Collectivisation: From 1929 Stalin forced Soviet peasants into collective farms, destroying the peasantry's independence and causing a catastrophic famine.
  15. Soviet industrialisation: In the 1930s Stalin drove the Soviet Union to industrialise at breakneck speed, building new cities, factories and dams.
  16. The Five-Year Plans: Stalin's Five-Year Plans set targets for every part of Soviet industry from 1928.
  17. The Great Purge: In 1936 to 1938 Stalin's secret police arrested well over a million people and shot hundreds of thousands in the Great Purge, or Great Terror.
  18. Soviet propaganda: Stalin's regime used posters, film, newspapers, schools and art to build loyalty and a cult of the leader.
  19. Life under Stalin: Life in Stalin's Soviet Union combined new opportunities in education and work with shortages, crowded housing, surveillance and fear.
  20. The Soviet Union in the Second World War: The Soviet Union suffered the heaviest losses of any country in the Second World War and played the largest part in defeating Nazi Germany.

School 9: The Rise of Fascism and Nazism

20 lessons

The Rise of Fascism and Nazism: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Post-war Italy: Italy was on the winning side in 1918, yet many Italians felt cheated by the peace and frightened by unrest at home.
  2. Mussolini: Benito Mussolini moved from revolutionary socialism to nationalism during the First World War, founded the Fascist movement in 1919 and became prime minister in 1922.
  3. The development of Italian Fascism: Mussolini took office as head of a coalition in 1922 and built a dictatorship by 1926.
  4. The Weimar Republic: Germany became a democratic republic in November 1918, as the war ended in defeat.
  5. The Treaty of Versailles and Germany: For many Germans, the new republic was born with the humiliation of Versailles.
  6. Political instability in Weimar Germany: Between 1919 and 1923 the Weimar Republic faced uprisings from left and right, political murders and an attempted putsch by Hitler.
  7. Hyperinflation: In 1923 the German mark collapsed: by November one US dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks.
  8. The Great Depression in Germany: The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 plunged Germany into an economic and political crisis.
  9. Hitler's rise to power: Adolf Hitler went from a failed putsch in 1923 to chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933.
  10. Nazi ideology: Nazism was built on a set of racial and nationalist ideas that shaped everything the regime did.
  11. Nazi consolidation of power: In January 1933 Hitler led a coalition cabinet; by August 1934 he was Führer of a one-party state.
  12. The police state: The Nazi regime controlled Germans through the SS, the Gestapo, concentration camps and courts.
  13. Propaganda and censorship in Nazi Germany: Joseph Goebbels ran a Ministry of Propaganda that tried to control everything Germans read, heard and saw.
  14. Nazi economic policy: Unemployment in Germany fell from about six million in 1933 to near full employment by 1938.
  15. Youth and education in Nazi Germany: The Nazis saw young people as the future of the national community and set out to shape them through schools and youth organisations.
  16. Women and family policy in Nazi Germany: Nazi ideology assigned women the role of wives and mothers serving the national community.
  17. Persecution of minorities: Nazi racial ideology targeted many groups besides Jews: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, gay men and others judged a threat to the national community.
  18. Antisemitism: Antisemitism was at the centre of Nazi ideology.
  19. The Holocaust: Between 1941 and 1945 the Nazi regime and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews.
  20. Resistance to Nazi rule: Opposition to Nazi rule ranged from grumbling and nonconformity to attempts to kill Hitler.

School 10: The Second World War

19 lessons

The Second World War: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The causes of the Second World War: Europe went to war again in September 1939, only twenty years after Versailles.
  2. Appeasement: In the 1930s Britain and France tried to avoid war by meeting some of Hitler's demands.
  3. The invasion of Poland: Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and the Soviet Union invaded from the east on 17 September.
  4. The fall of France: In six weeks in May and June 1940 Germany defeated France, the Low Countries and the British army in France.
  5. The Battle of Britain: In the summer and autumn of 1940 the Luftwaffe tried to win control of the air over southern England as a step towards invasion.
  6. The Blitz: From September 1940 to May 1941 German bombers attacked British cities, killing over 40,000 civilians.
  7. Operation Barbarossa: On 22 June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union in the largest military operation in history.
  8. The Eastern Front, 1941–1945: The war in the east was the largest and deadliest part of the Second World War.
  9. The Pacific War: From 1941 to 1945 Japan fought the United States, Britain and their allies across Asia and the Pacific.
  10. Pearl Harbor: On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii, bringing the United States into the war.
  11. The Holocaust in wartime: During the war the Nazi regime deported Jews from across occupied Europe to their deaths.
  12. The Home Front, 1939–1945: The Second World War mobilised British society even more than the First.
  13. Wartime economies: The Second World War was won as much in factories as on battlefields.
  14. D-Day: On 6 June 1944 Allied forces landed in Normandy in the largest seaborne invasion in history.
  15. The defeat of Nazi Germany: In the last year of the war Germany was squeezed between the Soviet advance from the east and the Western Allies from the west, while bombers destroyed its cities.
  16. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki: In August 1945 the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing many tens of thousands of people at once and more in the months that followed.
  17. The surrender of Japan: Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945 and signed it on 2 September.
  18. The post-war settlement: The Allied leaders who met at Yalta and Potsdam in 1945 reshaped Europe.
  19. The creation of the United Nations: The United Nations was founded in 1945 to keep the peace where the League of Nations had failed.

School 11: The Cold War

19 lessons

The Cold War: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Origins of the Cold War: Within two years of defeating Germany together, the United States and the Soviet Union had become rivals.
  2. Capitalism and communism: The Cold War was a conflict of ideas as well as of power.
  3. The division of Europe: Between 1945 and 1949 Europe split into a Soviet-controlled east and an American-backed west.
  4. The Iron Curtain: In March 1946 Winston Churchill declared that an "iron curtain" had descended across Europe.
  5. The Berlin Blockade: From June 1948 to May 1949 the Soviet Union cut all land routes into West Berlin, and the Western powers supplied the city by air.
  6. NATO and the Warsaw Pact: The Cold War divided Europe into two military alliances.
  7. The Korean War: In June 1950 communist North Korea invaded South Korea, and the Cold War became a hot war.
  8. The arms race: From 1949 the United States and the Soviet Union competed to build ever more powerful nuclear weapons and the means to deliver them.
  9. Nuclear deterrence: Nuclear weapons were built in the hope that they would never be used.
  10. The Hungarian Uprising: In October 1956 Hungarians rose against Soviet-backed communist rule, and within days Soviet tanks crushed them.
  11. The Berlin Wall: On 13 August 1961 East German forces sealed the border through Berlin, and the Berlin Wall became the most famous symbol of the Cold War.
  12. The Cuban Missile Crisis: For thirteen days in October 1962 the world came closer to nuclear war than at any other time.
  13. Vietnam and containment: The Vietnam War was the longest and most divisive application of containment.
  14. Détente: In the late 1960s and 1970s the superpowers tried to reduce tension through arms control, trade and agreements.
  15. Soviet intervention in Afghanistan: In December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan to save a communist government.
  16. Reagan and Gorbachev: In the 1980s Ronald Reagan's confrontational policies and Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms transformed superpower relations.
  17. The fall of the Berlin Wall: On the night of 9 November 1989 East Berliners poured through the Berlin Wall.
  18. The collapse of the Soviet Union: In December 1991 the Soviet Union ceased to exist.
  19. The end of the Cold War: Between 1989 and 1991 the Cold War ended without a war between the superpowers.

School 12: Britain since 1945

17 lessons

Britain since 1945: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Post-war reconstruction: In July 1945 Labour won a landslide and set out to rebuild a bankrupt, bomb-damaged Britain.
  2. The welfare state: Between 1945 and 1948 Britain built a welfare state designed to protect people "from the cradle to the grave".
  3. The creation of the NHS: On 5 July 1948 the National Health Service began, offering medical care free at the point of use to everyone in Britain.
  4. Housing and education reform: Post-war governments set out to rehouse millions and to give every child a secondary education.
  5. Immigration and multicultural Britain: After 1945 migrants from the Caribbean, South Asia and elsewhere helped rebuild Britain and changed its society.
  6. The end of the British Empire: In 1945 Britain ruled the largest empire in the world; by the late 1960s most of it had gone.
  7. Decolonisation: Decolonisation was often violent and its legacy contested.
  8. The Suez Crisis: In 1956 Britain and France secretly planned with Israel to seize the Suez Canal from Egypt, and were forced to withdraw under American pressure.
  9. The Swinging Sixties: The 1960s are remembered as a decade of pop music, fashion and new freedoms.
  10. Social and cultural change: Between the 1950s and the 1990s British society changed in women's lives, education, religion and popular culture.
  11. The Troubles in Northern Ireland: From the late 1960s to 1998 Northern Ireland suffered a violent conflict known as the Troubles, in which around 3,500 people were killed.
  12. Thatcherism: Margaret Thatcher, prime minister from 1979 to 1990, set out to reverse Britain's economic decline by reducing the role of the state.
  13. Industrial decline: The industries that had made Britain the first industrial nation shrank dramatically after 1945.
  14. New Labour: Under Tony Blair, Labour won three general elections from 1997, combining market economics with investment in public services.
  15. Britain and Europe: Britain stayed out of European integration in the 1950s, joined the European Economic Community in 1973 and argued over its place in Europe for the next four decades.
  16. Brexit: In June 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union, and it left in January 2020.
  17. Contemporary British society: Britain in the 2020s is older, more diverse, less religious and more devolved than in 1945.

School 13: The United States in the Twentieth Century

15 lessons

The United States in the Twentieth Century: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Progressive reform: Between about 1900 and 1920 American reformers tried to curb the power of big business, clean up politics and protect consumers.
  2. The Great Depression: The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 was followed by the worst economic depression in American history.
  3. Franklin D. Roosevelt: Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected president four times and led the United States through the Depression and most of the Second World War.
  4. The New Deal: Roosevelt's New Deal of 1933 to 1938 used federal power to provide relief, recovery and reform.
  5. The Second World War and America: The Second World War ended the Depression and made the United States the world's leading military and economic power.
  6. Post-war prosperity: After 1945 the United States enjoyed a long boom of rising incomes, suburban homes, cars and television.
  7. The Civil Rights Movement: From the 1950s to the late 1960s Black Americans and their allies challenged segregation and won landmark laws.
  8. Martin Luther King Jr.: Martin Luther King Jr. became the best-known leader of the Civil Rights Movement through his philosophy of non-violent protest and his powerful speeches.
  9. Malcolm X: Malcolm X rejected integration and non-violence as the answer to racism, and became the most powerful voice of Black nationalism in the early 1960s.
  10. The Montgomery Bus Boycott: The Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955 to 1956 was one of the first great mass protests of the Civil Rights Movement.
  11. The Vietnam War and American society: The Vietnam War divided American society more deeply than any conflict since the Civil War.
  12. The Watergate scandal: The Watergate scandal began with a burglary in 1972 and ended with the only resignation of a president in American history.
  13. Reaganomics: Ronald Reagan, president from 1981 to 1989, promised to shrink government, cut taxes and revive the economy.
  14. The United States and the end of the Cold War: In the 1980s the Cold War moved from a dangerous new confrontation to its peaceful end in 1991.
  15. The changing role of the United States: After the Cold War the United States was the world's only superpower, but its role changed through globalisation, the attacks of 11 September 2001, long wars and the financial crisis of 2008.

School 14: Global History and Empires

17 lessons

Global History and Empires: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The Ottoman Empire: The Ottoman Empire lasted from around 1300 until 1922 and at its height ruled south-eastern Europe, the Middle East and North Africa.
  2. The Mughal Empire: The Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent from the sixteenth to the early eighteenth century and was one of the richest states in the world.
  3. The Qing dynasty: The Qing dynasty ruled China from 1644 to 1912, presiding over a golden age in the eighteenth century and a century of crisis afterwards.
  4. The rise of modern Japan: In the half-century after 1853 Japan transformed itself from an isolated feudal state into an industrial and imperial power.
  5. European colonialism: From the late fifteenth century European states built empires across the Americas, Africa and Asia.
  6. The Atlantic slave trade: Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas.
  7. Abolition and emancipation: Between the 1780s and 1888 the Atlantic slave trade and then slavery itself were abolished across the Americas.
  8. The Haitian Revolution: Between 1791 and 1804 enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue overthrew slavery and founded Haiti, the first state created by formerly enslaved people.
  9. Latin American independence: Between 1808 and 1826 most of Spain's and Portugal's American colonies became independent.
  10. The Scramble for Africa: African experiences: The partition of Africa looks different when seen from African perspectives rather than European capitals.
  11. Anti-colonial resistance: In the twentieth century colonised peoples built mass movements that brought down European empires.
  12. Indian independence: India won independence from Britain on 15 August 1947 after decades of nationalist campaigning.
  13. Partition: The independence of India and Pakistan in August 1947 was accompanied by partition, mass violence and one of the largest migrations in history.
  14. The Chinese Revolution: Between 1911 and 1949 China moved from the fall of its last dynasty through warlordism, civil war and Japanese invasion to communist victory.
  15. Mao and the People's Republic of China: Mao Zedong ruled China from 1949 until his death in 1976, launching campaigns that transformed the country and caused immense suffering.
  16. Post-colonial states: Dozens of new states emerged from the European empires between 1945 and 1975.
  17. Globalisation and historical change: Globalisation, the growing connection of the world's economies and societies, has transformed life since 1945.

School 15: Revolutions and Political Change

13 lessons

Revolutions and Political Change: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The English Revolution: Historians have long argued about whether the upheavals in England between 1640 and 1660 amounted to a revolution.
  2. The American Revolution: Between 1763 and 1783 thirteen British colonies in North America rebelled and won independence as the United States.
  3. The French Revolution: The French Revolution, beginning in 1789, overthrew the monarchy, executed a king and spread ideas of liberty and equality across Europe.
  4. The Haitian Revolution in comparison: The Haitian Revolution was the most radical of the Atlantic revolutions, the only one in which enslaved people overthrew slavery and created their own state.
  5. The Latin American revolutions: The wars of independence in Spanish America ended three centuries of colonial rule, but they changed society far less than politics.
  6. The European revolutions of 1848: In 1848 revolutions broke out across Europe, from Paris to Vienna, Berlin, Budapest and the Italian states.
  7. The Russian Revolution in comparison: The Russian Revolution of 1917 is often compared with the French Revolution of 1789.
  8. The Chinese Revolution in comparison: China's communist revolution, completed in 1949, differed sharply from Russia's in 1917, though both were led by Marxist parties.
  9. Decolonisation movements: The end of European empires after 1945 transferred power to new states across Asia and Africa.
  10. The Iranian Revolution: The Iranian Revolution of 1979 overthrew the Shah and created an Islamic Republic, surprising observers who expected revolutions to be secular.
  11. Revolutionary ideologies: Revolutionaries have been inspired by very different sets of ideas, from liberal rights to Marxist class struggle and Islamic government.
  12. The causes and consequences of revolution: What makes revolutions happen, and what do they leave behind?
  13. Comparative revolutionary history: Comparing revolutions can reveal patterns that a single case hides, but it also risks forcing different events into one model.

School 16: Religion, Culture and Society

14 lessons

Religion, Culture and Society: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Medieval Christianity: In medieval Western Europe the Catholic Church shaped belief, learning, law and daily life, and the papacy claimed authority over kings.
  2. The European Reformation: In 1517 Martin Luther's protest against indulgences began a movement that split Western Christianity.
  3. Religious conflict in Europe: The divisions of the Reformation led to more than a century of religious wars in Europe, from the French Wars of Religion to the Thirty Years' War.
  4. The Enlightenment: In the eighteenth century the thinkers of the Enlightenment argued that reason, rather than tradition or religious authority, should guide human affairs.
  5. Scientific change: Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries European thinkers transformed understanding of the heavens, the body and nature.
  6. The development of political thought: Ideas about power, authority and liberty developed dramatically between the Renaissance and the nineteenth century.
  7. The growth of secularism: Over the last two centuries religion has lost much of its public role in Europe.
  8. Women's changing roles: Women's legal rights, work and public roles changed profoundly between the early modern period and the late twentieth century.
  9. Education and literacy: Literacy in Britain rose from a skill of the clergy and elite to near-universal by 1900.
  10. The development of mass media: Newspapers, radio, cinema, television and the internet have each transformed how people receive news, entertainment and propaganda.
  11. Art and cultural movements: Art and culture reflect and shape the societies that produce them.
  12. Social class and identity: People have understood their place in society through ideas of rank, class, gender, race, religion and region.
  13. Historical memory: Societies remember the past in ways that differ from what historians write.
  14. Commemoration and contested heritage: Memorials, statues and museums express what a society chooses to honour, and they have become fiercely contested.

School 17: A Level Historical Investigation

13 lessons

A Level Historical Investigation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Constructing historical enquiries: An A level historical investigation begins with an enquiry: a question worth asking, about a debate historians care about, which the available evidence can answer.
  2. Developing research questions: The wording of a research question shapes the whole investigation.
  3. Selecting primary sources: An investigation needs primary sources that are relevant, varied and reliable enough to support an argument.
  4. Evaluating provenance in depth: At A level, evaluating a source means going beyond "it is biased" to ask who made it, when, why, for whom and in what circumstances, and what that means for its value to a particular question.
  5. Comparing interpretations at A level: At A level, comparing interpretations means explaining why historians reach different conclusions, not just stating that they do.
  6. Historiographical debate: Historiography is the history of how historians have written history.
  7. Using academic literature: An A level investigation must engage with what historians have written, not just with primary sources.
  8. Constructing an argument: A historical investigation succeeds when it makes a clear argument, supports it with evidence and deals with objections.
  9. Structuring an extended essay: A 4,000-word investigation needs a structure that carries the argument from introduction to judgement.
  10. Citation and referencing: Historians cite their sources so that readers can check the evidence and see whose ideas are being used.
  11. Avoiding anachronism: Anachronism means placing something in the wrong time: using ideas, words or standards that did not exist then, or assuming people knew what would happen next.
  12. Evaluating historical significance: Deciding what is historically significant is itself a historical judgement, made with criteria.
  13. Writing a sustained historical judgement: The highest-level historical writing reaches a clear, consistent and well-supported judgement, rather than simply listing views.

School 18: Historical Examination and Revision

14 lessons

Historical Examination and Revision: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Source analysis questions: Source questions test whether you can use evidence from the past to answer a historical question.
  2. Interpretation analysis questions: Interpretation questions ask you to explain and evaluate how historians and others have presented the past.
  3. Causation questions: Causation questions ask why something happened.
  4. Consequence questions: Consequence questions ask what happened as a result of an event, and how important those results were.
  5. Change and continuity questions: Change and continuity questions ask how far things changed over a period, and how quickly.
  6. Significance questions: Significance questions ask why an event, person or development mattered.
  7. Comparison questions: Comparison questions ask you to identify and explain similarities and differences between two events, people or periods.
  8. Evidence-based essays: Examiners reward essays that support every point with precise, relevant evidence.
  9. Extended historical arguments: A level essays reward a sustained argument that weighs factors and reaches a substantiated judgement.
  10. Planning examination answers: A few minutes of planning in an exam produces a clearer, better-argued answer.
  11. Time management: Good history answers are wasted if the exam time runs out.
  12. Mark-scheme interpretation: Understanding how examiners mark helps students write answers that meet the criteria.
  13. GCSE practice examinations: Practising full questions under exam conditions is the best preparation for GCSE History.
  14. A level practice examinations: A level examinations test sustained argument, source evaluation and the analysis of interpretations.
