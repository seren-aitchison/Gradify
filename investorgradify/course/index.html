Investing: a free course on InvestorGradify

InvestorGradify's free Investing course runs from Money Foundations to Building Your Plan: 183 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for new and growing investors.

Education, not financial advice. InvestorGradify teaches how investing works. It never tells you what to buy or sell, and nothing here is a personal recommendation. The value of investments can fall as well as rise and you may get back less than you put in: your capital is at risk. Tax rules, allowances and limits change and depend on your circumstances; figures are labelled with their tax year or marked illustrative. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by an expert, so check important points against official sources. For a decision about your own money, use a financial adviser authorised by the FCA (check the FCA Register) or free, impartial guidance from MoneyHelper.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Money Foundations

16 lessons

Money Foundations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Saving and investing: what is the difference?: Saving keeps money safe and available; investing puts money to work in assets whose value can rise or fall, in return for the chance of a higher long-run return.
  2. Budgeting and cash flow: Before anyone can invest, they need money left over each month.
  3. The emergency fund: An emergency fund is easily accessible cash set aside for unexpected costs or loss of income.
  4. Debt before investing: Paying off expensive debt is a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to its interest rate.
  5. Protecting your finances: insurance and resilience: Insurance transfers the cost of rare but serious events to an insurer in return for a premium.
  6. Interest rates: AER, APR and the base rate: Interest is the price of money: what borrowers pay and savers earn.
  7. Simple and compound interest: Simple interest is paid only on the original sum; compound interest is paid on the interest already earned as well.
  8. Doubling time and the rule of 72: The rule of 72 is a mental shortcut: dividing 72 by an annual percentage rate gives the approximate number of years for money to double.
  9. Inflation and real returns: Inflation is the general rise in prices over time.
  10. The time value of money: A pound today is worth more than a pound in the future, because today's pound can be invested, is certain, and is not eroded by inflation.
  11. Present value and discounting: Discounting is compounding in reverse: it converts a future sum into its value today.
  12. Setting financial goals: A plan starts with goals.
  13. Time horizon and liquidity: Time horizon is how long until money is needed; liquidity is how quickly an asset can become cash without loss.
  14. Attitude to risk and capacity for loss: Attitude to risk is how much uncertainty you are willing to accept; capacity for loss is how much you can afford to lose without harming your life.
  15. Cash savings accounts: Cash accounts differ in access, rate and protection.
  16. Investing, speculation and gambling: Investing, speculation and gambling all involve uncertainty, but they differ in what drives the expected return.

School 2: How Financial Markets Work

16 lessons

How Financial Markets Work: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The role of the financial system: The financial system moves money from people who have more than they need now to people and firms who can use it productively, and prices the risk of doing so.
  2. Savers, borrowers and financial intermediaries: Intermediaries such as banks, insurers, pension funds and asset managers stand between savers and borrowers.
  3. Banks and the Bank of England: The Bank of England is the UK's central bank.
  4. Interest rates and monetary policy: Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and the central bank's balance sheet to keep inflation on target.
  5. Primary and secondary markets: In the primary market, companies and governments sell new securities to raise money; in the secondary market, investors trade existing securities with each other.
  6. Stock exchanges: the LSE, AIM and global markets: A stock exchange provides a regulated marketplace where shares and other securities are listed and traded.
  7. How a trade works: orders, bid and offer: Every share has two prices at any moment: the bid, at which you can sell, and the offer (or ask), at which you can buy.
  8. Brokers, market makers and investment platforms: Investors reach the market through firms.
  9. Clearing, settlement and custody: After a trade is agreed, it has to be completed: the buyer's money and the seller's securities must change hands safely.
  10. Liquidity and price discovery: Markets do two jobs at once: they let investors trade quickly and cheaply (liquidity) and they combine the information of many traders into a price (price discovery).
  11. Stock market indices: An index measures the performance of a group of securities, such as the 100 largest UK companies.
  12. The economic cycle and markets: Economies move through expansions and recessions.
  13. Inflation, growth and asset prices: Inflation and economic growth affect each asset class differently.
  14. Currencies and exchange rates: When a UK investor buys overseas assets, their return in pounds depends on the exchange rate as well as on the asset.
  15. Bull and bear markets: A bull market is a sustained rise in prices; a bear market is a fall of 20% or more from a peak.
  16. Bubbles and crashes: A bubble occurs when prices rise far above any reasonable estimate of value, driven by enthusiasm and the expectation of further rises; a crash is a sudden, steep fall.

School 3: Asset Classes

17 lessons

Asset Classes: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cash as an asset class: In a portfolio, "cash" means deposits and very short-term, high-quality instruments such as Treasury bills and money market funds.
  2. What a bond is: A bond is a loan to a government or company that is traded as a security.
  3. Gilts: Gilts are bonds issued by the UK government.
  4. Corporate bonds and credit ratings: Companies borrow by issuing bonds that pay more than gilts to compensate for the risk of default.
  5. Bond prices and yields: Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions.
  6. Duration and interest-rate risk: Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates.
  7. Index-linked bonds: Index-linked bonds adjust their coupons and repayment for inflation, protecting real value.
  8. What a share is: A share is a unit of ownership in a company.
  9. Ordinary and preference shares: Most shares are ordinary shares, with votes and a variable share of profits.
  10. Dividends and capital growth: Shareholders earn returns in two ways: dividends paid from profits and growth in the share price.
  11. Shareholder rights and corporate actions: Shareholders have legal rights to vote, receive information and be offered new shares first.
  12. Property as an investment: Property can be owned directly, as a home or buy-to-let, or indirectly through funds and listed companies.
  13. Real estate investment trusts (REITs): A REIT is a listed company that owns income-producing property and, in return for tax advantages, must pay out most of its rental profits.
  14. Commodities and gold: Commodities are raw materials such as oil, metals and agricultural products.
  15. Alternative investments: private equity and hedge funds: Alternatives are investments outside listed shares, bonds and cash: private equity, hedge funds, infrastructure, private credit and others.
  16. Crypto-assets: Crypto-assets are digital tokens recorded on distributed ledgers (blockchains), such as bitcoin and ether.
  17. Derivatives: an introduction: A derivative is a contract whose value depends on another asset, such as a share, index, currency or commodity.

School 4: Risk and Return

14 lessons

Risk and Return: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Holding period return: The holding period return measures what an investment earned over the whole time it was held, combining price change and income.
  2. Arithmetic and geometric average returns: The arithmetic average of yearly returns is not the rate at which money actually grew.
  3. Total return and reinvested income: Total return combines price changes with income.
  4. Nominal and real returns in practice: Nominal returns are measured in pounds; real returns measure the change in purchasing power.
  5. Volatility and standard deviation: Volatility measures how widely returns vary around their average, and standard deviation is the usual statistic for it.
  6. Drawdowns and recovery: A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a subsequent low.
  7. Sequence of returns risk: When money is being added or withdrawn, the order of returns matters, not just their average.
  8. Types of investment risk: Investment risk is not one thing.
  9. The risk–return trade-off: Investors require higher expected returns to hold riskier assets.
  10. The equity risk premium: The equity risk premium is the extra return shares are expected to earn over a risk-free asset.
  11. The long-run evidence: shares, bonds and cash: More than a century of data on UK and global markets shows shares beating bonds and cash on average, with much larger swings.
  12. Risk-adjusted return: the Sharpe ratio: Comparing returns without considering risk can mislead.
  13. The range of outcomes and probability: Investment results are uncertain, so it is better to think in ranges and probabilities than in single forecasts.
  14. Survivorship bias in performance data: Survivorship bias arises when failed or closed funds and companies drop out of the data, leaving only the survivors.

School 5: Diversification and Portfolio Theory

15 lessons

Diversification and Portfolio Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why diversification works: Diversification means spreading money across many investments that do not all move together.
  2. Correlation between assets: Correlation measures how closely two assets' returns move together, on a scale from −1 to +1.
  3. Specific and systematic risk: Total risk divides into specific risk, unique to a company or sector, and systematic risk, which affects the whole market.
  4. How many holdings are enough?: Adding holdings reduces specific risk quickly at first and then more slowly.
  5. Home bias and global diversification: Investors everywhere tend to hold far more of their own country's shares than its share of world markets would suggest.
  6. Modern portfolio theory: Modern portfolio theory, developed by Harry Markowitz, treats a portfolio as a whole: investors should choose combinations of assets that give the highest expected return for each level of risk,…
  7. The efficient frontier: The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk.
  8. The capital asset pricing model (CAPM): The CAPM says an asset's expected return equals the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market risk premium.
  9. Beta: Beta measures how sensitive an investment is to movements in the overall market.
  10. Factor investing: Factors are characteristics of securities, such as value, size, momentum, quality and low volatility, that have historically been associated with returns beyond the market.
  11. Strategic asset allocation: Strategic asset allocation is the long-term mix of asset classes, such as shares, bonds and cash, chosen to fit an investor's goals and risk tolerance.
  12. Tactical asset allocation: Tactical asset allocation means deliberately moving away from the strategic mix for a period to exploit expected short-term opportunities, for example holding more cash when shares look expensive.
  13. Rebalancing: As markets move, a portfolio drifts away from its target mix.
  14. Lifestyling and glide paths: Lifestyling automatically moves a pension from higher-risk to lower-risk assets as the planned retirement date approaches, following a glide path.
  15. Multi-asset funds and model portfolios: Multi-asset funds hold a mix of shares, bonds and other assets in one fund, rebalancing automatically.

School 6: Valuation

14 lessons

Valuation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Price and value: The price of a share is what you pay; its value is what the business is worth to an owner.
  2. Discounted cash flow valuation: A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation estimates what a business is worth by forecasting its future free cash flows and discounting them to today.
  3. The dividend discount model: The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of the dividends it is expected to pay.
  4. The Gordon growth model: The Gordon growth model values a share whose dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate for ever.
  5. The required return and the cost of equity: Every valuation needs a discount rate, the return investors require for bearing the risk.
  6. Earnings per share: Earnings per share (EPS) is the profit attributable to each ordinary share.
  7. The price-to-earnings ratio: The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio compares a share's price with its earnings per share.
  8. Dividend yield and dividend cover: Dividend yield shows the income a share pays relative to its price, and dividend cover shows how comfortably profits support that dividend.
  9. Price-to-book: The price-to-book ratio compares a company's market value with the net assets shown in its accounts.
  10. Enterprise value and EV/EBITDA: Enterprise value measures the value of the whole business, to shareholders and lenders together.
  11. Valuing a bond: A bond's price is the present value of its coupons and its final repayment, discounted at the market yield.
  12. Valuing the whole market: CAPE: The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, or CAPE, compares a market's price with its average real earnings over ten years.
  13. Growth and value investing: Growth and value are two broad styles of choosing shares: one pays up for fast-growing companies, the other buys shares that look cheap on measures such as price-to-book and P/E.
  14. The limits of valuation: Every valuation method rests on uncertain forecasts and assumptions, and markets can stay away from estimated value for long periods.

School 7: Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

17 lessons

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why analyse financial statements?: Financial statement analysis turns published figures into information users can act on.
  2. Profitability ratios: Profitability ratios measure how well a company turns revenue and capital into profit.
  3. Liquidity ratios: Liquidity ratios show whether a company can meet its short-term obligations.
  4. Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle: Efficiency ratios show how quickly a business turns inventory into sales, collects cash from customers and pays suppliers.
  5. Gearing and investor ratios: Gearing ratios show how far a company is financed by debt, and investor ratios show returns to shareholders.
  6. Interpreting ratios in context: Ratios are only useful when interpreted in context.
  7. Limitations of ratio analysis: Ratio analysis is powerful but has important limitations.
  8. Analysing cash flows: Cash flow analysis shows whether a company generates enough cash to sustain itself and how it funds investment and returns to shareholders.
  9. Performance in not-for-profit organisations: Charities, public bodies and other not-for-profit organisations exist to achieve a mission, not to make profit.
  10. Groups and control: A group is a parent company and the subsidiaries it controls.
  11. Goodwill on acquisition: When a parent buys a subsidiary, it usually pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets: the excess is goodwill.
  12. The consolidated statement of financial position: The consolidated statement of financial position adds together the parent's and subsidiaries' assets and liabilities and shows the equity attributable to the parent and to non-controlling interests.
  13. Intra-group trading and unrealised profit: When group companies trade with each other, the sales, purchases, balances and any profit left in inventory must be eliminated on consolidation.
  14. The consolidated statement of profit or loss: The consolidated statement of profit or loss combines the income and expenses of the parent and subsidiaries for the period.
  15. Associates and joint arrangements: Investments that give significant influence or joint control aren't consolidated line by line.
  16. Creative accounting and earnings management: Creative accounting uses the flexibility in accounting rules, and sometimes breaks them, to present a better picture than reality.
  17. Narrative reporting and the strategic report: Annual reports contain much more than the financial statements.

School 8: Funds, Index Investing and Costs

15 lessons

Funds, Index Investing and Costs: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why pool investments?: A fund pools money from many investors and invests it as one portfolio.
  2. Unit trusts and OEICs: Unit trusts and open-ended investment companies (OEICs) are the main types of open-ended fund in the UK.
  3. Investment trusts: Investment trusts are closed-ended funds structured as listed companies.
  4. Exchange-traded funds: Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are funds whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, usually tracking an index.
  5. Fund factsheets and key information documents: Fund factsheets and key information documents summarise a fund's objective, risks, costs and past performance.
  6. Active fund management: Active fund managers try to beat a benchmark by choosing which assets to hold and when.
  7. Index tracking funds: Index tracking funds, or passive funds, aim to match the return of a market index at low cost.
  8. The efficient market hypothesis: The efficient market hypothesis says that market prices reflect available information, so it is hard to beat the market consistently.
  9. Tracking error and replication methods: Index funds never match their index exactly.
  10. The evidence on active and passive funds: Decades of research compare active funds with index trackers.
  11. Ongoing charges and platform fees: Investors pay a fund's own charges and, usually, a platform's charges for holding it.
  12. How fees compound: Small annual charges compound over time into large differences in final value.
  13. Dealing costs, spreads and stamp duty: Buying and selling investments brings costs beyond annual charges.
  14. Accumulation and income units: Many funds offer income units, which pay out dividends and interest, and accumulation units, which reinvest them automatically.
  15. Choosing a platform: An investment platform is where most UK investors buy and hold funds and shares.

School 9: Behavioural Finance

14 lessons

Behavioural Finance: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Rational investors and their limits: Traditional finance assumes investors are rational, use all available information and maximise expected utility.
  2. Loss aversion and prospect theory: Prospect theory describes how people actually value gains and losses.
  3. Overconfidence: Overconfidence leads people to overrate their knowledge, skill and control.
  4. Anchoring and investment decisions: Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on a first number when making estimates.
  5. Herding: Herding is the tendency to follow what others are doing.
  6. Recency and availability in markets: Recency bias and the availability heuristic lead people to give too much weight to recent and memorable events.
  7. The disposition effect: The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winning investments too soon and hold losing ones too long.
  8. Mental accounting: Mental accounting is the habit of treating money differently depending on where it came from or which mental 'pot' it is in.
  9. Confirmation bias: Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and favour information that supports what we already believe.
  10. Investor psychology in bubbles: Bubbles are periods when prices rise far above fundamental value, driven largely by investor psychology.
  11. Market timing: Market timing means moving money in and out of markets to avoid falls and catch rises.
  12. News, noise and social media: Investors face a constant stream of news, opinions and social media posts.
  13. Pound-cost averaging and automatic investing: Pound-cost averaging means investing a fixed sum at regular intervals.
  14. Rules, checklists and investment journals: Because knowing about biases is not enough to avoid them, investors benefit from systems that structure decisions.

School 10: UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax

16 lessons

UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. How investments are taxed in the UK: Investment returns in the UK can be taxed as income or as capital gains, depending on their form and the account they are held in.
  2. Income tax on savings and dividends: Interest and dividends are taxed under special rules with their own allowances and rates.
  3. Capital gains tax: Capital Gains Tax is charged on the profit from selling investments held outside tax wrappers.
  4. General investment accounts: A general investment account (GIA) is an ordinary, unsheltered account for holding investments.
  5. Individual Savings Accounts: ISAs are tax-free accounts for saving and investing.
  6. Stocks and shares ISAs: A stocks and shares ISA lets you invest in funds, shares and bonds with no UK tax on income or gains.
  7. Lifetime ISAs and Junior ISAs: Lifetime ISAs help adults under 40 save for a first home or later life with a government bonus, and Junior ISAs let families save tax-free for children.
  8. Why save in a pension?: Pensions are long-term savings accounts for retirement with generous tax treatment.
  9. Pension tax relief: Pension tax relief returns income tax on contributions, within limits.
  10. Workplace pensions and auto-enrolment: Auto-enrolment requires employers to put eligible workers into a workplace pension and contribute.
  11. Defined benefit and defined contribution pensions: Pensions are either defined benefit, promising an income based on salary and service, or defined contribution, building a pot from contributions and returns.
  12. Self-invested personal pensions (SIPPs): A self-invested personal pension (SIPP) is a personal pension in which you choose the investments.
  13. The State Pension: The State Pension is a regular payment from the government, based on your National Insurance record.
  14. Accessing your pension: From the normal minimum pension age, defined contribution pensions can be accessed in several ways.
  15. Inheritance tax basics: Inheritance Tax is charged on the value of an estate on death above certain thresholds.
  16. Wills, nominations and estate planning: Estate planning makes sure your money goes where you want after death, and that someone can act for you if you lose capacity.

School 11: Regulation, Protection and Ethics

16 lessons

Regulation, Protection and Ethics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why financial services are regulated: Financial products are complex, and mistakes or misconduct can cost people their savings.
  2. The FCA and the PRA: Two main regulators oversee UK financial services.
  3. The Consumer Duty: The Consumer Duty raised the standard of care that firms must give retail customers.
  4. Advice, guidance and execution-only: Investors can get regulated advice, free guidance or make their own decisions.
  5. Financial promotions and finfluencers: Adverts and social media posts about investments must follow strict rules.
  6. The Financial Services Compensation Scheme: The Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects customers if an authorised firm fails.
  7. The Financial Ombudsman Service: The Financial Ombudsman Service settles disputes between consumers and financial firms.
  8. Client money and asset protection: When you invest through a platform or broker, your money and investments should be kept separate from the firm's own.
  9. Investment scams: Investment scams cost UK victims hundreds of millions of pounds a year.
  10. Red flags and the FCA Warning List: Recognising warning signs can stop a scam before money is lost.
  11. Pension scams: Pensions are a major target for scammers because they can be large and are often hard to monitor.
  12. Ethics in investment: Investment raises ethical questions for firms, professionals and individual investors.
  13. The CFA Institute Code of Ethics: Professional bodies set standards for investment professionals.
  14. ESG and responsible investing: Many investors now consider environmental, social and governance factors.
  15. Stewardship and engagement: Investors who own shares can influence how companies behave.
  16. Greenwashing and sustainability labels: Greenwashing means making misleading claims about environmental benefits.

School 12: Building Your Plan

13 lessons

Building Your Plan: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The personal financial plan: A financial plan brings together your goals, money and investments into one picture.
  2. An investment policy statement: An investment policy statement sets out how you will invest and why.
  3. Choosing an asset allocation: Asset allocation, the mix of shares, bonds and cash, is the most important decision for most investors.
  4. Choosing funds for a portfolio: Once you have an asset allocation, you need funds to put it into practice.
  5. Lump sums and regular investing: Should you invest a large sum all at once or spread it over time?
  6. Investing early in adult life: The years from 18 to 30 are when time is the investor's greatest asset.
  7. Mid-life: competing goals: In mid-life, people often juggle mortgages, children, caring and retirement saving.
  8. Approaching retirement: The ten years before retirement are a critical time for planning.
  9. Drawdown and sustainable withdrawals: Pension drawdown lets retirees keep their pension invested and withdraw income flexibly.
  10. Annuities: An annuity turns a pension pot into a guaranteed income for life or a set period.
  11. Reviewing and monitoring a portfolio: A portfolio needs regular, but not constant, attention.
  12. When to get regulated financial advice: Some decisions are complex enough that paying for advice is worthwhile.
  13. Record keeping and paperwork: Good records make investing, tax returns and later-life planning far easier.
