Design Thinking and Creativity: a free course on CreateGradify

CreateGradify's free Design Thinking and Creativity course runs from Foundations of Design Thinking to Collaboration, Communication and Intellectual Property: 173 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for makers and creative thinkers.

Study material, not a qualification or professional advice. CreateGradify teaches design thinking, creativity and design practice for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by design lecturers or practitioners, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials and the sources cited. Accessibility lessons explain WCAG and UK law for learning, not as a compliance audit, and intellectual property lessons are not legal advice: for a real product, use the UK IPO's guidance and a registered patent or trade mark attorney.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Design Thinking

12 lessons

Foundations of Design Thinking: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is design?: Design is the deliberate shaping of products, places, services and systems to meet human needs.
  2. A short history of design: Design became a separate profession when industry separated planning from making.
  3. Design thinking defined: Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems that borrows the methods designers use: understanding people, framing problems, generating ideas and learning through prototypes.
  4. Design as a process, not a product: Designers do not move in a straight line from problem to answer.
  5. The Double Diamond: The Double Diamond is the Design Council's model of the design process: four phases, Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver, shaped as two diamonds of divergent and convergent thinking.
  6. Divergent and convergent thinking: Design alternates between opening up possibilities and narrowing down to the best.
  7. The d.school and IDEO models: Stanford's d.school teaches design thinking as five modes: empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test.
  8. Iterative design in Design and Technology: GCSE and A level Design and Technology require an iterative design process: explore, create and evaluate in repeated cycles.
  9. Designers and design disciplines: Design is a family of disciplines, from product and graphic design to UX, service design and architecture.
  10. Human-centred design: Human-centred design puts the people who will use a product or service at the centre of every decision, from research to testing.
  11. Wicked problems: Some problems have no clear definition, no right answer and no point at which they are solved.
  12. The value of design: Design creates value for users, organisations and society, but measuring it is hard.

School 2: The Psychology of Creativity

12 lessons

The Psychology of Creativity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is creativity?: Creativity researchers broadly agree that a creative idea must be both original and effective.
  2. The four Ps of creativity: Rhodes (1961) organised creativity research into four strands: the person, the process, the press (environment) and the product.
  3. Divergent thinking and its measurement: Divergent thinking tests ask people to produce many answers to open problems, such as unusual uses for a brick.
  4. Stages of the creative process: Graham Wallas (1926) proposed four stages of creative thought: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification.
  5. Insight and incubation: Insight is the sudden reorganisation of a problem that makes a solution obvious; incubation is the effect of stepping away.
  6. Knowledge, expertise and creativity: Creativity draws on deep knowledge, but expertise can also trap people in familiar solutions.
  7. Motivation and creativity: People tend to be most creative when they are interested in the work itself.
  8. Design fixation and how to break it: Designers often get stuck on the first ideas they see or have, copying features without realising it.
  9. Creative confidence: Many people believe they are "not creative" and stop trying.
  10. Constraints and creativity: A blank page can paralyse, while the right limits can spark invention.
  11. Creative climate in teams: Most design happens in teams, and the climate of a team strongly affects its creativity.
  12. Myths about creativity: Popular beliefs about creativity, such as right-brained creative people, lone geniuses and the mad artist, are widespread but often unsupported.

School 3: Ideation Methods

14 lessons

Ideation Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Brainstorming and its rules: Brainstorming is the best-known idea generation method, devised by Alex Osborn in the 1940s.
  2. Brainwriting and 6-3-5: Brainwriting replaces talking with writing: people generate ideas silently and pass them on for others to build on.
  3. SCAMPER: SCAMPER is a checklist of seven prompts for changing an existing product or idea: substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate and reverse.
  4. Mind mapping: Mind maps radiate ideas outwards from a central theme, making associations and structure visible.
  5. Analogies and biomimicry: Many inventions come from seeing how a problem in one domain was solved in another.
  6. Morphological analysis: Morphological analysis breaks a design into its functions or parameters and lists options for each, then combines them systematically.
  7. How might we questions: "How might we...?" questions turn research insights into prompts for ideas.
  8. Reversal and the worst possible idea: Thinking about how to make a problem worse can be a fast route to better ideas.
  9. Clustering and affinity diagrams: After generating many ideas or research notes, teams need to find patterns.
  10. Dot voting and its limits: Dot voting lets a group quickly show which ideas it prefers by placing sticky dots.
  11. Evaluation matrices: Evaluation matrices compare design concepts against criteria in a table, making selection systematic and transparent.
  12. Concept development: A chosen concept is still only a direction.
  13. TRIZ basics: TRIZ is a systematic method of inventive problem solving developed by Genrich Altshuller from the study of patents.
  14. Sketching to think: For designers, sketching is a way of thinking, not just a way of showing.

School 4: User Research and Empathy

16 lessons

User Research and Empathy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why research users: Designers research users because their own assumptions are unreliable guides to what other people need and do.
  2. Research questions and plans: Good research starts with clear questions and a plan that matches methods to them.
  3. Qualitative and quantitative research: Qualitative research explores why and how; quantitative research measures how many and how much.
  4. Research ethics and consent: Research with people must protect them from harm, respect their choices and handle their data lawfully.
  5. Recruiting participants: Research is only as good as the people in it.
  6. User interviews: Interviews are the most common user research method.
  7. Contextual inquiry and observation: Contextual inquiry combines observation and interviewing in the place where people actually do the work or activity.
  8. Surveys: Surveys collect standardised answers from many people quickly.
  9. Diary studies: Diary studies ask participants to record their experiences over days or weeks, capturing behaviour that unfolds over time or in private settings.
  10. Ethnography in design: Ethnography is the study of people's everyday lives and cultures through immersion.
  11. Desk research and competitor analysis: Before talking to users, designers learn what is already known and what already exists.
  12. Empathy maps: Empathy maps summarise what a team has learned about a user in four quadrants: what they say, think, do and feel.
  13. Personas: A persona is a fictional but research-based profile representing a group of users with similar goals and behaviours.
  14. Customer journey maps: A journey map visualises the steps a person goes through to achieve a goal, with their actions, thoughts and emotions at each step.
  15. Jobs to be done: Jobs to be done is a way of understanding why people "hire" a product: to make progress in a particular situation.
  16. Synthesis and insight: Research only helps if it is turned into insight: a new understanding that changes what the team will do.

School 5: Defining Problems

12 lessons

Defining Problems: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Problem framing and reframing: How a problem is framed decides which solutions anyone will consider.
  2. Point-of-view statements: A point-of-view statement captures a user, a need and an insight in one sentence, giving the team a clear, human-centred problem to solve.
  3. Design briefs: A design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom and why.
  4. Design specifications: A design specification lists the measurable requirements a design must meet.
  5. Requirements and constraints: Requirements say what a design must do; constraints limit how it can do it.
  6. Stakeholder mapping: Every design affects more people than its users.
  7. Root cause analysis and the five whys: Designers often meet symptoms rather than causes.
  8. Prioritisation methods: Design teams always have more problems and ideas than time.
  9. Assumptions and hypotheses: Every design rests on assumptions about users, problems and solutions.
  10. Success criteria and metrics: To know whether a design works, teams need success criteria agreed in advance and metrics that reflect real user value.
  11. Scoping the problem space: A problem can be too big to solve or too small to matter.
  12. The ethics of problem choice: Designers choose, or accept, which problems to work on, and that choice is itself ethical.

School 6: Prototyping, Testing and Iteration

16 lessons

Prototyping, Testing and Iteration: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why prototype: A prototype is any representation of a design that lets people explore, test or communicate an idea before it is finished.
  2. Prototype fidelity: Fidelity is how closely a prototype resembles the finished product.
  3. Paper prototyping: Paper prototyping uses hand-drawn screens and pieces of paper to simulate an interface, with a person playing the computer.
  4. Wireframes: A wireframe is a simplified visual guide to the structure of a screen or page, showing layout, content and functions without visual styling.
  5. Physical models and mock-ups: Product designers use physical models to explore form, scale, ergonomics and function.
  6. Wizard of Oz and experience prototypes: Some ideas are too complex or expensive to build just to test.
  7. Digital prototyping tools: Designers use digital tools to create interactive prototypes, from linked screens to coded components.
  8. Usability testing: Usability testing watches real people try to complete realistic tasks with a design, to find where it causes difficulty.
  9. Thinking aloud: In a think-aloud test, participants say what they are thinking as they use a design.
  10. How many users to test: How many participants does a usability test need?
  11. Heuristic evaluation: Heuristic evaluation is an expert review in which evaluators check an interface against a set of usability principles.
  12. A/B testing: A/B testing compares two versions of a design with real users at random to see which performs better on a chosen measure.
  13. Analysing test results: Testing produces notes, recordings, numbers and quotes.
  14. Iterating on feedback: Iteration means using what testing reveals to improve the design, then testing again.
  15. Minimum viable product: A minimum viable product is the smallest version of a product that lets a team learn whether its idea works with real customers.
  16. Design sprints: A design sprint is a five-day process for answering a critical business question through design, prototyping and testing with users.

School 7: The Visual Language of Art and Design

18 lessons

The Visual Language of Art and Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is art and design?: Art and design are the making of things that are looked at, used and thought about.
  2. How art and design are assessed: the four assessment objectives: GCSE and A level Art and Design are marked against the same four assessment objectives: develop, refine, record and present.
  3. Looking closely: an approach to visual analysis: Visual analysis is the disciplined description and interpretation of what a work looks like.
  4. Line: Line is the most basic visual element: a mark with length and direction, or an edge the eye perceives.
  5. Shape and form: Shape is a flat, two-dimensional area; form is three-dimensional, with height, width and depth.
  6. Tone and value: Tone (or value) is the lightness or darkness of a colour or surface.
  7. Colour as a visual element: Colour is the visual element with the most immediate emotional impact.
  8. Texture: Texture is the surface quality of a work, how it feels or looks as if it would feel.
  9. Space: Space is the area around, between and within forms, real in sculpture and architecture and illusory in pictures.
  10. Pattern: Pattern is the regular repetition of a motif across a surface.
  11. Balance and symmetry: Balance is the distribution of visual weight in a composition.
  12. Contrast and emphasis: Emphasis makes one part of a work dominant, and contrast is the main way to achieve it.
  13. Rhythm, repetition and movement: Rhythm is created by repeating visual elements at intervals, and movement is the path the eye takes through a work or the illusion of motion within it.
  14. Proportion and scale: Proportion is the size relationship of parts to each other and to the whole; scale is size relative to a standard, usually the human body.
  15. Unity and variety: Unity is the sense that all parts of a work belong together; variety is the difference that keeps it interesting.
  16. Composition and the picture plane: Composition is the arrangement of elements within the format of a work.
  17. The rule of thirds and the golden ratio: The rule of thirds and the golden ratio are proportional systems often recommended for composition.
  18. Gestalt principles in art and design: Gestalt psychology studies how we perceive wholes rather than separate parts.

School 8: Accessibility and Inclusive Design

14 lessons

Accessibility and Inclusive Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why accessibility matters: Accessibility means people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate and use a design.
  2. Models of disability: How disability is understood shapes how designers respond to it.
  3. Inclusive design principles: Inclusive design is a way of designing that considers the full range of human diversity from the start.
  4. Universal design: Universal design aims to create products and environments usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without need for adaptation.
  5. WCAG 2.2 and the POUR principles: The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the international standard for digital accessibility.
  6. Conformance levels: WCAG success criteria are grouped into three conformance levels: A, AA and AAA.
  7. Colour contrast: Text and interface elements must stand out enough from their background for people with low vision or colour vision deficiency to see them.
  8. Keyboard access and focus: Many people use a keyboard, or devices that act like one, instead of a mouse.
  9. Text alternatives: People who can't see images or hear audio need equivalent information in another form.
  10. Accessible forms: Forms are where many people get stuck: booking, applying, paying.
  11. Assistive technologies: Assistive technologies help disabled people use computers, phones and other products.
  12. Testing for accessibility: No single method finds all accessibility barriers.
  13. Cognitive accessibility: Cognitive and learning disabilities affect memory, attention, language, reasoning and processing.
  14. Accessibility and UK law: In the UK, accessibility is required by equality law and, for public bodies, by specific regulations for websites and apps.

School 9: Mechanics of Materials

15 lessons

Mechanics of Materials: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Normal stress and strain: Stress is internal force per unit area, and strain is deformation per unit length.
  2. Shear stress and strain: Shear stress acts parallel to a surface.
  3. Hooke's law and elastic constants: In the elastic range, stress is proportional to strain.
  4. Axially loaded members: Bars, rods and columns under axial load stretch or shorten.
  5. Thermal stress: Temperature changes make materials expand or contract.
  6. Torsion of circular shafts: Shafts transmit power by twisting.
  7. Bending stress in beams: Bending creates tension on one side of a beam and compression on the other.
  8. Shear stress in beams: Beams carry shear force as well as bending moment.
  9. Beam deflection: Beams must be stiff enough not to deflect too much in service.
  10. Combined loading: Many members carry more than one type of load at once.
  11. Stress transformation and Mohr's circle: Stresses at a point depend on the orientation of the plane considered.
  12. Failure theories: Failure theories predict when a material will yield or fracture under combined stresses.
  13. Buckling of columns: Slender columns can fail by buckling sideways at stresses well below their crushing strength.
  14. Thin-walled pressure vessels: Pressure vessels such as tanks, pipes and boilers hold fluids under pressure.
  15. Strain energy: When a material deforms elastically, it stores energy.

School 10: Service and Systems Design

12 lessons

Service and Systems Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is service design?: Service design is the design of whole services, from what customers see to the people, processes and systems behind them.
  2. Service blueprints: A service blueprint maps how a service is delivered, linking what users do to what staff and systems do behind the scenes.
  3. Touchpoints and channels: Users experience a service through many touchpoints across several channels.
  4. Co-design and participatory design: Co-design involves the people affected by a service in designing it.
  5. Designing public services: Public services must work for everyone, including people in difficult circumstances.
  6. Systems thinking for designers: Many design challenges sit inside complex systems where changing one part affects others.
  7. Feedback loops and leverage points: Feedback loops drive how systems grow, stabilise or collapse.
  8. Ecosystem maps: Services depend on networks of people and organisations.
  9. Designing for behaviour change: Many services aim to help people change behaviour, such as saving, recycling or attending appointments.
  10. Nudges and choice architecture: The way choices are presented changes what people choose.
  11. Platforms and networks: Many modern services are platforms that connect groups of users, such as buyers and sellers.
  12. Measuring service outcomes: Good services are measured by whether they achieve their purpose, not just by activity.

School 11: Sustainable and Ethical Design

16 lessons

Sustainable and Ethical Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Design and the environment: Design decisions shape how much energy, material and waste a product creates over its whole life.
  2. Life cycle assessment: Life cycle assessment measures the environmental impact of a product from raw materials to disposal.
  3. The circular economy: The circular economy keeps products and materials in use instead of throwing them away.
  4. Designing out waste: The best way to deal with waste is to stop it being created.
  5. Design for disassembly and repair: Products that can be taken apart can be repaired, upgraded and recycled.
  6. Choosing materials sustainably: Material choice shapes a product's footprint, durability and end of life.
  7. Cradle to cradle: Cradle to cradle is a design philosophy in which materials are designed to cycle safely forever.
  8. The waste hierarchy: The waste hierarchy ranks ways of managing waste from most to least preferred.
  9. Design ethics: Design shapes how people live, so designers face ethical choices.
  10. Dark patterns: Dark patterns, also called deceptive patterns, are design tricks that push people into choices they did not intend.
  11. Persuasive technology: Many digital products are designed to change what people do and how often they return.
  12. Privacy by design: Privacy by design builds data protection into products from the start rather than adding it later.
  13. Bias in design and data: Designs and data sets can systematically disadvantage some groups.
  14. Designing with AI: Artificial intelligence is changing both how designers work and what they design.
  15. Social and cultural responsibility: Design affects workers, communities and cultures as well as users.
  16. Professional codes of conduct: Professional bodies set out what is expected of designers.

School 12: Collaboration, Communication and Intellectual Property

16 lessons

Collaboration, Communication and Intellectual Property: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Creative collaboration: Most design work is done in teams, so designers need to collaborate well.
  2. Workshops and facilitation: Workshops bring people together to explore problems, generate ideas and make decisions.
  3. Giving and receiving critique: Critique helps designers see their work through others' eyes and improve it.
  4. Working with stakeholders: Design projects succeed only if stakeholders understand, support and act on the work.
  5. Sketching and visual communication: Designers communicate ideas visually to themselves, teams, clients and makers.
  6. Storytelling in design: Stories help designers understand users and persuade others to act.
  7. Presenting and pitching: Designers regularly present work and pitch ideas to clients, managers and investors.
  8. Design portfolios: A portfolio shows what a designer can do and how they think.
  9. Documentation and developer handoff: A design only becomes real when others can build it accurately.
  10. Design systems: A design system is a shared set of reusable components, patterns, standards and guidance.
  11. Intellectual property basics: Intellectual property rights protect creations of the mind, such as designs, inventions, brands and artworks.
  12. Copyright: Copyright protects original creative works automatically.
  13. Registered designs: Registered designs protect the appearance of products.
  14. Patents: Patents protect new inventions in exchange for publishing how they work.
  15. Trade marks: Trade marks protect the signs that identify a business's goods or services.
  16. Protecting and licensing ideas: Designers need practical strategies to protect their work and earn from it.
