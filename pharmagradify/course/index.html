Pharmacy: a free course on PharmaGradify

PharmaGradify's free Pharmacy course runs from Foundations of Pharmacy Practice to Registration Assessment and Foundation Training: 318 lessons in 19 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Clinical reasoning). It's written for pharmacy students and pharmacists.

Education, not clinical advice. PharmaGradify doesn't register you with the GPhC, replace your MPharm, foundation training or local training, or authorise you to supply, check or prescribe medicines. Doses and examples are illustrative: in practice, use the current BNF or BNFC, the SPC, local guidance and your professional judgement. Law and services differ across the four UK nations.

Course overview

  • 19 schools, 318 lessons
  • Study paths: MPharm: the sciences, MPharm: therapeutics, MPharm: practice and prescribing, Registration assessment preparation, Foundation training year, Pharmacy technicians, Pharmacist prescribers, Practising pharmacists: CPD
  • 4 practice exams to test yourself under time
  • Curriculum: GPhC standards for the initial education and training of pharmacists (aligned). What this means

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Pharmacy Practice

20 lessons

Foundations of Pharmacy Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to pharmacy: Pharmacy is the health profession that makes sure people get the most from their medicines safely.
  2. Pharmacy across sectors: Pharmacists work in community pharmacy, hospitals, general practice, care homes, industry and beyond.
  3. The pharmacy team: Pharmacists work with pharmacy technicians, dispensing assistants and medicines counter assistants, each with defined training and roles.
  4. The GPhC and professional regulation: The General Pharmaceutical Council regulates pharmacists, pharmacy technicians and registered pharmacies in Great Britain.
  5. The standards for pharmacy professionals: The GPhC's nine Standards for pharmacy professionals describe how every pharmacist and pharmacy technician is expected to practise.
  6. Person-centred care: Person-centred care means treating each person as an individual with their own values, preferences and circumstances, and involving them in decisions.
  7. Communication and consultation skills: Good consultations make care safer and more effective.
  8. Health literacy and adapting information: Health literacy is a person's ability to find, understand and use health information.
  9. Consent in pharmacy practice: Before any examination, treatment or service, a person must give valid consent.
  10. Confidentiality and information governance: People share sensitive information with pharmacy teams on the understanding that it will be kept confidential.
  11. Equality, diversity and inclusion: Health outcomes differ between groups, and pharmacy teams can reduce or widen those gaps.
  12. Professional judgement and accountability: Pharmacy is full of decisions that rules alone can't settle: whether to supply, refer, query or intervene.
  13. Raising concerns and speaking up: Pharmacy professionals must speak up when they have concerns about safety, or when things go wrong.
  14. Patient safety and learning from errors: Medication errors are common and many are preventable.
  15. Evidence-based practice: Evidence-based practice combines the best research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values.
  16. Reflective practice and CPD: Reflection turns experience into learning, and continuing professional development (CPD) keeps knowledge and skills current.
  17. The NHS and pharmacy services across the UK: The NHS is organised differently in each UK nation, and pharmacy services and contracts differ too.
  18. Public health and health promotion: Pharmacies are in the heart of communities and see healthy people as well as unwell ones.
  19. Working in multidisciplinary teams: Safe care depends on professionals working together.
  20. Digital pharmacy and technology: Technology is reshaping pharmacy: electronic prescriptions, shared records, automation, online pharmacy and artificial intelligence.

School 2: Pharmacology: How Drugs Act

24 lessons

Pharmacology: How Drugs Act: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What pharmacology is: Pharmacology is the study of how drugs affect the body and how the body handles drugs.
  2. Drug targets: Most drugs work by binding to a protein in the body and changing what it does.
  3. Receptors and receptor families: Receptors are the body's sensors for its own signals, such as hormones and neurotransmitters.
  4. Agonists and antagonists: A drug that binds a receptor either activates it (an agonist) or prevents activation (an antagonist).
  5. Dose-response relationships: As the dose of a drug increases, the response increases until it reaches a maximum.
  6. Affinity, efficacy and potency: Three terms describe how a drug interacts with its receptor: affinity (how well it binds), efficacy (how well it activates) and potency (how much is needed).
  7. Tolerance, desensitisation and dependence: Repeated exposure to a drug can reduce its effect (tolerance) and lead the body to adapt so that stopping causes withdrawal (dependence).
  8. G protein-coupled receptors and second messengers: G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest family of drug targets.
  9. Ion channels as drug targets: Ion channels control the electrical activity of nerves, muscle and the heart.
  10. Enzymes as drug targets: Many medicines work by inhibiting an enzyme, stopping the production of a harmful product or the breakdown of a useful one.
  11. Transporters as drug targets: Transporters carry ions and molecules across membranes, against gradients if they use energy.
  12. Nuclear receptors: Nuclear receptors sit inside the cell and act as transcription factors when a hormone or drug binds.
  13. The autonomic nervous system: The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions: heart rate, blood pressure, airways, gut, bladder and glands.
  14. Cholinergic pharmacology: Drugs that mimic, block or prolong acetylcholine treat conditions from overactive bladder to myasthenia gravis and dementia.
  15. Adrenergic pharmacology: Adrenoceptors (α1, α2, β1, β2, β3) respond to noradrenaline and adrenaline.
  16. Histamine and its antagonists: Histamine is released from mast cells in allergy and from stomach cells to drive acid secretion.
  17. Eicosanoids and inflammation: Prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes are lipid mediators made from arachidonic acid.
  18. Neurotransmitters in the brain: Psychiatric and neurological medicines act on a handful of brain transmitter systems: dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA, glutamate and acetylcholine.
  19. Adverse drug reactions: Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are a major cause of hospital admission.
  20. Mechanisms of drug interactions: Drug interactions happen when one drug changes the effect or level of another.
  21. Drug toxicity and overdose: Toxicity can follow overdose, accumulation or interactions.
  22. Pharmacogenomics: Genetic differences change how people respond to some medicines, by altering drug-metabolising enzymes or targets, or by increasing the risk of serious reactions.
  23. Drug development and clinical trials: A new medicine takes over a decade to move from discovery to licence, through preclinical studies and phased clinical trials, then regulatory review and post-marketing surveillance.
  24. Biological medicines and biosimilars: Biological medicines are large, complex molecules made in living cells, such as monoclonal antibodies, insulins and vaccines.

School 3: Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics

21 lessons

Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to pharmacokinetics: Pharmacokinetics describes how drug concentrations change over time as the body absorbs, distributes, metabolises and excretes a drug.
  2. Routes of administration: The route of administration decides how quickly and how completely a drug reaches the circulation, and sometimes where it acts.
  3. Absorption and bioavailability: Before an oral drug can act, it must dissolve and cross the gut wall, then survive the liver.
  4. First-pass metabolism: Drugs absorbed from the gut pass through the gut wall and liver before reaching the rest of the body, and some are extensively metabolised on the way.
  5. Distribution and volume of distribution: Once in the blood, drugs distribute into tissues to different extents.
  6. Plasma protein binding: Many drugs bind to plasma proteins, mainly albumin.
  7. Drug metabolism: phase I and II: Most drugs are metabolised to more water-soluble compounds that the kidneys can excrete.
  8. Cytochrome P450 enzymes: The cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes carry out most phase I metabolism.
  9. Renal excretion: The kidneys excrete many drugs and metabolites by filtration, tubular secretion and reabsorption.
  10. Biliary excretion and enterohepatic recycling: Some drugs and metabolites are excreted into bile and pass into the gut.
  11. Clearance: Clearance measures the body's ability to eliminate a drug.
  12. Half-life and the elimination rate constant: Half-life is the time for a drug's concentration to halve.
  13. Steady state and accumulation: With regular dosing, a drug accumulates until the amount eliminated each interval equals the amount given.
  14. Loading and maintenance doses: A loading dose gets a drug to its effective concentration quickly; maintenance doses keep it there.
  15. Linear and non-linear kinetics: For most drugs, concentration rises in proportion to dose (linear, first-order kinetics).
  16. Compartment models: Pharmacokinetic models describe the body as one or more compartments.
  17. Therapeutic drug monitoring: For some drugs, measuring blood levels guides dosing because the effective and toxic levels are close or response is hard to judge.
  18. Pharmacokinetics in renal impairment: Kidney disease reduces the elimination of renally cleared drugs and their active metabolites, and can change absorption, distribution and protein binding.
  19. Pharmacokinetics in hepatic impairment: Liver disease can reduce drug metabolism, change protein binding and increase sensitivity to some drugs.
  20. Biopharmaceutics and dissolution: Biopharmaceutics links a formulation's physical properties to how the drug is absorbed.
  21. Bioequivalence: Generic medicines are licensed when they're shown to be bioequivalent to the reference product.

School 4: Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry

20 lessons

Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Functional groups in drugs: A drug's functional groups decide how it binds its target, how it dissolves and how the body handles it.
  2. Acids, bases and pKa: Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases.
  3. Ionisation and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation turns pKa and pH into a ratio of ionised to un-ionised drug.
  4. Lipophilicity and partition coefficients: Lipophilicity describes how much a drug prefers fats to water.
  5. Stereochemistry and chirality: Many drugs exist as mirror-image forms that the body can tell apart.
  6. Structure-activity relationships: Structure-activity relationships link changes in a molecule's structure to changes in its biological effect.
  7. Pharmacophores and lead optimisation: A pharmacophore is the arrangement of features a molecule needs to act at its target.
  8. Prodrugs: A prodrug is inactive or less active until the body converts it into the active drug.
  9. Bioisosteres: Bioisosteres are groups that can replace each other while keeping similar biological effects.
  10. Drug-likeness and Lipinski's rule of five: Drug-likeness describes properties that make a compound likely to be absorbed orally.
  11. Beta-lactam chemistry: Beta-lactam antibiotics share a strained four-membered ring that attacks bacterial cell wall enzymes.
  12. Steroid chemistry: Steroids share a rigid four-ring skeleton.
  13. Opioid chemistry: Morphine's structure is the template for many opioids.
  14. Benzodiazepine chemistry: Benzodiazepines share a fused ring system that binds the GABA-A receptor.
  15. Chemical degradation pathways: Medicines break down by predictable chemical reactions.
  16. Drug stability and shelf life: Shelf life is how long a product stays within its specification under stated storage conditions.
  17. Pharmaceutical analysis: Pharmaceutical analysis checks that medicines contain the right drug, in the right amount, at the right purity.
  18. Spectroscopy in pharmaceutical analysis: Spectroscopy measures how molecules interact with light and other radiation.
  19. Chromatography: Chromatography separates mixtures so their components can be identified and measured.
  20. Quality control and pharmacopoeias: Quality control makes sure every batch of a medicine meets its specification before release.

School 5: Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms

23 lessons

Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to pharmaceutics: Pharmaceutics is the science of turning a drug into a medicine that patients can take safely and effectively.
  2. Solubility and dissolution: A drug must dissolve before it can be absorbed.
  3. Particle size and powders: Most medicines start as powders.
  4. Rheology: Rheology is the study of how materials flow and deform.
  5. Surfactants and interfacial phenomena: Surfactants gather at surfaces and interfaces and lower surface tension.
  6. Solutions: Solutions contain a drug fully dissolved in a liquid.
  7. Suspensions: A suspension contains undissolved drug particles dispersed in a liquid.
  8. Emulsions: An emulsion is a mixture of two immiscible liquids, with one dispersed as droplets in the other.
  9. Creams, ointments and gels: Semisolid preparations deliver drugs to the skin and mucous membranes.
  10. Tablets: Tablets are the most widely used dosage form.
  11. Capsules: Capsules enclose a drug in a shell, usually of gelatin.
  12. Modified-release dosage forms: Modified-release products change when or how fast a drug is released.
  13. Excipients: Excipients are the ingredients other than the active drug.
  14. Parenteral products: Parenteral products are given by injection or infusion, bypassing the gut.
  15. Sterile products and aseptic manufacture: Sterile products must be free from viable microorganisms.
  16. Ophthalmic preparations: Eye preparations must be sterile and comfortable, and they must overcome the eye's rapid tear turnover.
  17. Inhaled dosage forms: Inhaled medicines deliver drugs straight to the lungs.
  18. Transdermal and topical delivery: Transdermal patches deliver drugs through the skin into the bloodstream over hours or days.
  19. Rectal and vaginal dosage forms: Rectal and vaginal dosage forms deliver drugs locally or systemically when oral use is unsuitable.
  20. Extemporaneous preparation: Extemporaneous preparation means making a medicine in the pharmacy for an individual patient.
  21. Packaging and storage: Packaging protects medicines from light, moisture, air, contamination and damage, and helps patients use them correctly.
  22. Pharmaceutical microbiology and preservation: Microorganisms can spoil medicines and harm patients.
  23. Manufacturing and quality assurance: Quality assurance is the whole system that makes sure every medicine is fit for its purpose.

School 6: Pharmaceutical Calculations

20 lessons

Pharmaceutical Calculations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Units and conversions: Almost every pharmaceutical calculation starts with units.
  2. Significant figures and rounding: Rounding decides whether a calculated dose can actually be measured and given.
  3. Ratio and proportion: Most dose and volume calculations are proportions: if a known amount of drug is in a known volume, how much volume holds the dose you need?
  4. Percentage concentrations: Percentage strengths describe how much drug is in a product.
  5. Ratio strengths and parts per million: Some products describe very dilute concentrations as ratios (1 in 1,000) or parts per million.
  6. Molarity, millimoles and milliequivalents: Electrolytes and many infusions are prescribed in millimoles, not milligrams.
  7. Dilutions: Diluting a stock solution is routine in pharmacy, from making up preparations to preparing injections.
  8. Concentrating and mixing: Sometimes you need a strength between two that you have, or you mix products of different strengths.
  9. Dose calculations: A dose calculation turns a prescription into what's measured and given, and what's supplied.
  10. Weight-based dosing: Many medicines are dosed per kilogram of body weight, especially in children, in critical care and for drugs with narrow therapeutic ranges.
  11. Body surface area dosing: Cytotoxic chemotherapy and a few other medicines are dosed by body surface area (BSA), which tracks metabolic rate more closely than weight.
  12. Doses in children: Children's doses come from the BNF for Children (BNFC), by age band, weight or BSA.
  13. Quantities to supply: Working out how much to supply is everyday pharmacy arithmetic: tablets for a course, millilitres of a liquid, doses in an inhaler, or grams of a cream.
  14. Infusion rates: Intravenous fluids and many medicines are given by infusion.
  15. Drug infusions in mg per hour: Medicine infusions are prescribed as a dose rate (mg per hour, or micrograms per kg per minute).
  16. Displacement values: When a powder is dissolved or a drug is incorporated into a suppository base, the drug itself takes up space.
  17. Estimating renal function: Many drugs are cleared by the kidneys, so doses depend on renal function.
  18. Dose adjustment in renal impairment: When kidney function falls, drugs cleared by the kidneys accumulate.
  19. Pharmacokinetic calculations: A few simple pharmacokinetic equations explain loading doses, steady state and dosing intervals, and they appear in exams and in therapeutic drug monitoring.
  20. Calculations in the registration assessment: The GPhC registration assessment has a calculations paper, and calculation errors are a common reason candidates struggle.

School 7: Cardiovascular Therapeutics

18 lessons

Cardiovascular Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Hypertension: High blood pressure is the commonest treatable risk factor for heart attack, stroke, kidney disease and dementia.
  2. Antihypertensive drugs: NICE's step-wise approach chooses the first drug by age, ethnicity and diabetes, then adds drugs from the main classes.
  3. Lipid management: Lowering cholesterol, especially LDL and non-HDL cholesterol, reduces heart attacks and strokes.
  4. Cardiovascular risk assessment: Risk tools estimate a person's chance of a heart attack or stroke over 10 years, helping decide who benefits from treatment.
  5. Stable angina: Stable angina is chest pain on exertion from narrowed coronary arteries.
  6. Acute coronary syndromes: Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) are emergencies: unstable angina and heart attacks (NSTEMI and STEMI).
  7. Secondary prevention after a heart attack: After a heart attack, a set of medicines and lifestyle changes greatly reduces the risk of another event.
  8. Chronic heart failure: In heart failure the heart can't pump enough blood for the body's needs.
  9. Atrial fibrillation: Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the commonest heart rhythm disorder and a major cause of stroke.
  10. Antiarrhythmic drugs: Antiarrhythmic drugs restore or maintain a normal rhythm, but many can also cause arrhythmias and have serious adverse effects.
  11. Venous thromboembolism: Venous thromboembolism (VTE), meaning deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, is common and can be fatal.
  12. Anticoagulants: Anticoagulants prevent and treat clots by acting on the clotting cascade.
  13. Warfarin management: Warfarin has a narrow therapeutic range, many interactions and is affected by diet and illness, so it needs regular INR monitoring and careful counselling.
  14. Direct oral anticoagulants: DOACs have largely replaced warfarin for atrial fibrillation and VTE.
  15. Antiplatelet drugs: Antiplatelet drugs reduce arterial clots, which cause heart attacks and strokes.
  16. Stroke and transient ischaemic attack: Stroke is a medical emergency: rapid recognition and treatment limit brain damage.
  17. Peripheral arterial disease: Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) narrows the arteries to the legs, causing pain on walking and, in severe cases, threatening the limb.
  18. Cardiovascular adverse drug reactions: Many drugs affect the heart and circulation: slowing the heart, prolonging the QT interval, causing fluid retention or raising potassium.

School 8: Respiratory Therapeutics

11 lessons

Respiratory Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Asthma in adults: Asthma is a common inflammatory airways disease causing variable breathlessness, wheeze, cough and chest tightness.
  2. Asthma in children: Diagnosing and treating asthma in children needs care: many young children wheeze with viral infections without having asthma.
  3. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: COPD causes persistent breathlessness and cough from airflow obstruction, usually due to smoking.
  4. Inhaler devices and technique: An inhaler only works if the drug reaches the lungs.
  5. Bronchodilators: Bronchodilators relax airway smooth muscle.
  6. Inhaled corticosteroids: Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) control airway inflammation and prevent asthma attacks.
  7. Acute exacerbations: Asthma attacks and COPD exacerbations can be life-threatening.
  8. Allergic rhinitis and allergy: Allergic rhinitis (hay fever) is very common and is mostly managed in pharmacies.
  9. Anaphylaxis: Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction.
  10. Cough and respiratory infections: Most acute coughs and respiratory infections are viral and self-limiting.
  11. Smoking cessation: Stopping smoking is the most effective way for smokers to improve their health.

School 9: Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics

13 lessons

Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Type 1 diabetes: Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing beta cells, so insulin is lifelong and essential.
  2. Insulins: Insulin is a high-risk medicine: errors with type, strength, device or dose can cause severe hypoglycaemia or ketoacidosis.
  3. Type 2 diabetes: Type 2 diabetes results from insulin resistance and reduced insulin secretion.
  4. Glucose-lowering drugs: Each class of glucose-lowering drug has its own benefits, risks and monitoring.
  5. Hypoglycaemia: Hypoglycaemia is the commonest acute complication of diabetes treatment and can be dangerous.
  6. Diabetes in special situations: Illness, steroids, pregnancy, older age, fasting and surgery all change glucose control and medicine needs.
  7. Diabetic emergencies: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) are life-threatening emergencies.
  8. Thyroid disorders: Underactive and overactive thyroid disease are common and treatable.
  9. Adrenal insufficiency and corticosteroids: Adrenal insufficiency, whether from Addison's disease or long-term steroid use, can lead to a life-threatening adrenal crisis.
  10. Osteoporosis: Osteoporosis makes bones fragile, so fractures happen after minor falls.
  11. Contraception: Pharmacists supply, advise on and increasingly provide contraception, including emergency contraception.
  12. Menopause and hormone replacement therapy: Menopause symptoms affect many people's health and work.
  13. Obesity and weight management: Obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, some cancers and joint problems.

School 10: Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics

20 lessons

Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Depression: Depression is common, disabling and treatable.
  2. Antidepressants: Antidepressant classes differ in side effects, interactions and safety in overdose.
  3. Anxiety disorders: Generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder and related conditions are common.
  4. Bipolar disorder and lithium: Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania and depression.
  5. Schizophrenia and psychosis: Psychosis involves losing touch with reality through hallucinations, delusions or disordered thinking.
  6. Antipsychotics: Antipsychotics block dopamine D2 receptors and other receptors.
  7. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: ADHD affects attention, impulsivity and activity from childhood, often into adulthood.
  8. Dementia: Dementia causes progressive decline in memory and thinking.
  9. Insomnia: Insomnia is common and often linked to stress, health problems or habits.
  10. Substance misuse and harm reduction: Pharmacies are central to treatment and harm reduction for people who use drugs or alcohol: supervised consumption of opioid substitution therapy, needle and syringe programmes, naloxone and alcohol support.
  11. Epilepsy: Epilepsy is a tendency to recurrent seizures.
  12. Antiepileptic drugs: Antiseizure medicines have narrow margins, many interactions and specific serious adverse effects.
  13. Parkinson's disease: Parkinson's disease causes slowness, stiffness and tremor from dopamine loss.
  14. Migraine: Migraine causes disabling headaches, often with nausea and sensitivity to light.
  15. Multiple sclerosis: Multiple sclerosis is an immune-mediated condition damaging the central nervous system.
  16. Pain physiology and assessment: Pain is a personal experience shaped by biology, psychology and circumstances.
  17. Analgesic ladder and simple analgesics: Paracetamol and NSAIDs are the foundation of treating acute pain, used at the right dose and duration.
  18. Opioids: Opioids relieve severe acute and cancer pain but have limited benefit and real harms in long-term non-cancer pain.
  19. Neuropathic pain: Neuropathic pain responds poorly to standard analgesics.
  20. Palliative care symptom control: In palliative and end-of-life care, the aim is comfort and dignity.

School 11: Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship

17 lessons

Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Microbiology for pharmacists: Choosing an antimicrobial well starts with understanding the organisms that cause infection.
  2. Antibiotic classes and mechanisms: Antibiotics act on bacterial cell walls, protein synthesis, nucleic acids or metabolism.
  3. Antimicrobial resistance: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) makes infections harder to treat and threatens modern medicine.
  4. Antimicrobial stewardship: Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated programme to use antimicrobials well: the right drug, dose, route and duration, reviewed regularly.
  5. Choosing an antibiotic: Choosing an antibiotic means matching the likely organism and site of infection with the person's factors: allergy, kidney function, pregnancy, interactions and route.
  6. Urinary tract infections: Urinary tract infections are among the commonest infections managed in primary care and pharmacies.
  7. Respiratory tract infections: Most respiratory tract infections are viral and self-limiting, but pneumonia and some bacterial infections need antibiotics.
  8. Skin and soft tissue infections: Skin infections range from impetigo and minor wound infections to cellulitis and bites.
  9. Sepsis: Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection that damages the body's own organs.
  10. Clostridioides difficile infection: C. difficile infection causes diarrhoea and colitis, usually after antibiotics disrupt the gut flora.
  11. Tuberculosis: Tuberculosis needs months of combination treatment to cure it and prevent resistance.
  12. Fungal infections: Fungal infections range from common skin, nail and vaginal infections to serious systemic infections in immunocompromised people.
  13. Antiviral drugs: Antivirals treat a growing range of infections, including herpes viruses, influenza and COVID-19.
  14. HIV: HIV is now a manageable long-term condition: effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) gives a near-normal life expectancy and prevents transmission.
  15. Viral hepatitis: Hepatitis B and C cause chronic liver disease, but hepatitis C can now be cured with short courses of tablets and hepatitis B is preventable by vaccination.
  16. Vaccines and immunisation: Vaccines prevent serious diseases and pharmacies give millions of doses every year.
  17. Travel health: Travellers may need advice on malaria, vaccines, food and water safety and existing medicines.

School 12: Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics

14 lessons

Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Dyspepsia and gastro-oesophageal reflux: Dyspepsia and reflux are very common and often managed in pharmacies.
  2. Peptic ulcer disease and Helicobacter pylori: Peptic ulcers are mostly caused by H. pylori or NSAIDs.
  3. Nausea and vomiting: Nausea and vomiting have many causes, from infections and pregnancy to medicines and raised intracranial pressure.
  4. Constipation: Constipation is common, especially in older people and those taking opioids.
  5. Diarrhoea: Acute diarrhoea is usually viral and self-limiting, but dehydration is dangerous, especially in infants and older people.
  6. Inflammatory bowel disease: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are long-term inflammatory conditions with flares and remissions.
  7. Irritable bowel syndrome: IBS causes abdominal pain with altered bowel habit and bloating, without structural disease.
  8. Acute kidney injury: Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function, often caused by illness plus medicines.
  9. Chronic kidney disease: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects millions of people and raises cardiovascular risk.
  10. Electrolyte disorders: Abnormal sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium levels can be life-threatening and are often caused or worsened by medicines.
  11. Fluid therapy: IV fluids are medicines: the wrong type or amount causes harm.
  12. Liver disease and drug handling: The liver metabolises many drugs, so liver disease changes how medicines work and how safe they are.
  13. Drug-induced liver injury: Many medicines can damage the liver, from paracetamol overdose to idiosyncratic reactions.
  14. Alcohol-related liver disease: Alcohol is the commonest cause of liver disease in the UK.

School 13: Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions

11 lessons

Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: NSAIDs relieve pain and inflammation and are among the most widely used medicines, but they cause significant gastrointestinal, kidney and cardiovascular harm.
  2. Osteoarthritis: Osteoarthritis is the commonest joint condition, causing pain and stiffness, mostly in knees, hips and hands.
  3. Rheumatoid arthritis: Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease causing joint inflammation and damage.
  4. Gout: Gout causes sudden severe joint pain from urate crystals.
  5. Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs: DMARDs control inflammatory disease but need careful monitoring.
  6. Eczema: Atopic eczema causes itchy, dry, inflamed skin and is very common in children.
  7. Psoriasis: Psoriasis is a long-term immune-mediated skin disease with raised, scaly plaques.
  8. Acne: Acne affects most teenagers and many adults, and can cause scarring and distress.
  9. Skin infections and infestations: Many skin infections and infestations are treated in pharmacies, including fungal infections, cold sores, warts, scabies and head lice.
  10. Eye conditions: Many eye problems are minor, but some threaten sight.
  11. Ear conditions: Ear problems such as wax, otitis externa and otitis media are common in pharmacy.

School 14: Oncology, Haematology and Immunology

10 lessons

Oncology, Haematology and Immunology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Anaemias: Anaemia is common and has many causes, from iron deficiency to B12 and folate deficiency and chronic disease.
  2. Blood products and haematological support: Blood transfusion and haematological support are complex areas with serious risks.
  3. Principles of cancer treatment: Cancer treatment combines surgery, radiotherapy and systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT).
  4. Cytotoxic chemotherapy: Cytotoxic drugs kill rapidly dividing cells, including healthy ones, causing predictable toxicities.
  5. Targeted therapies and immunotherapy: Targeted therapies and immunotherapies have transformed cancer treatment.
  6. Supportive care in cancer: Supportive care manages the symptoms and side effects of cancer and its treatment.
  7. Safe handling of cytotoxic medicines: Cytotoxic medicines can harm staff and others through exposure.
  8. Immunosuppressants: Immunosuppressants prevent transplant rejection and treat autoimmune disease, but carry risks of infection, cancer and toxicity.
  9. Transplantation: Organ transplantation saves lives but needs lifelong immunosuppression and careful medicines management.
  10. Biologics in inflammatory disease: Biological medicines such as TNF inhibitors have changed the treatment of inflammatory diseases.

School 15: Special Populations

12 lessons

Special Populations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Children and young people: Children aren't small adults: their bodies handle medicines differently and change rapidly as they grow.
  2. Neonates: Neonates, especially premature babies, are the most vulnerable patients.
  3. Pregnancy: Medicines in pregnancy can affect the fetus, but untreated illness also causes harm.
  4. Breastfeeding: Most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding, and stopping breastfeeding unnecessarily has harms.
  5. Older people: Older people take the most medicines and are most vulnerable to their harms.
  6. Prescribing in renal impairment: Kidney impairment causes many medicines to accumulate, and some worsen kidney function further.
  7. Prescribing in hepatic impairment: Liver disease alters drug metabolism, protein binding and clotting, but there's no simple test to adjust doses.
  8. Polypharmacy and deprescribing: Taking many medicines increases the risk of harm, interactions and burden.
  9. Frailty: Frailty is a state of reduced reserve, making people vulnerable to sudden decline from minor stresses.
  10. People with learning disabilities: People with learning disabilities have poorer health outcomes and are often overmedicated with psychotropic medicines.
  11. Swallowing difficulties and covert administration: Many people struggle to swallow tablets, and some lack capacity to agree to medicines.
  12. Care homes: Care home residents are frail, take many medicines and depend on others to give them.

School 16: Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance

20 lessons

Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The legal framework for medicines: Medicines in the UK are governed by a small number of key laws.
  2. Classes of medicines: POM, P and GSL: Every licensed medicine is classified as prescription-only, pharmacy or general sale.
  3. Prescriptions and prescription requirements: A prescription is a legal authority to supply a prescription-only medicine.
  4. Who can prescribe: Doctors and dentists aren't the only prescribers.
  5. Emergency supply: The law allows a pharmacist to supply a prescription-only medicine without a prescription in an emergency, at the request of a prescriber or of the patient, if strict conditions are met.
  6. Patient group directions and patient specific directions: Patient group directions (PGDs) let named, registered health professionals supply or administer specified medicines to groups of patients without a prescription.
  7. Wholesale dealing and supply: Supplying medicines to other businesses or professionals for them to sell or use is wholesale dealing, which is regulated separately from supply to patients.
  8. Labelling requirements: Dispensed medicines must carry a label with legally required information, plus advice that helps people take them safely.
  9. The Misuse of Drugs Regulations: schedules: Controlled drugs are divided into five schedules under the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001.
  10. Controlled drug prescriptions: Prescriptions for Schedule 2 and 3 controlled drugs have extra legal requirements designed to prevent misuse and forgery.
  11. Controlled drug registers and records: Schedule 2 controlled drugs must be recorded in a register every time they are received or supplied.
  12. Safe custody and destruction: Many controlled drugs must be kept in a locked cabinet that meets legal standards, and their destruction is tightly controlled.
  13. The responsible pharmacist: A registered pharmacy can only operate with a responsible pharmacist (RP) in charge.
  14. Records and record keeping: Pharmacies must keep various records for set periods, some by law and some as good practice.
  15. Fitness to practise: The GPhC can act when a pharmacy professional's fitness to practise is impaired, to protect the public and maintain confidence in the profession.
  16. Safeguarding in pharmacy: Pharmacy teams see people often and informally, so they may notice signs that a child or adult is at risk of abuse or neglect.
  17. Clinical governance: Clinical governance is the system that keeps pharmacy care safe and continually improving: procedures, audit, incident learning, staff training and patient feedback.
  18. Pharmacy across the four nations: Medicines law is largely UK-wide, but health services, funding, regulation and some pharmacy services differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  19. Ethics in pharmacy practice: Many pharmacy decisions are ethical as well as legal: balancing a patient's wishes against their safety, respecting confidentiality, and handling conflicts with personal beliefs.
  20. Veterinary medicines in pharmacy: Pharmacists can supply medicines for animals, which are governed by separate veterinary legislation with its own categories.

School 17: Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation

17 lessons

Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The clinical check: Every prescription gets a clinical check before supply: is this medicine, at this dose, right for this person now?
  2. Medicines reconciliation: Medicines reconciliation makes sure the list of medicines a person is taking is accurate when they move between care settings.
  3. Drug interactions in practice: Interactions are common, but most aren't clinically important.
  4. Identifying and managing adverse drug reactions: Many symptoms people report are adverse drug reactions (ADRs).
  5. Reporting adverse reactions: the Yellow Card scheme: The Yellow Card scheme is how the MHRA collects reports of suspected adverse reactions and problems with medicines and devices.
  6. Interventions and documentation: When a pharmacist identifies a problem and acts on it, that's an intervention.
  7. Medicines optimisation: Medicines optimisation looks at the whole of a person's medicines and their outcomes, not single prescriptions.
  8. Adherence and concordance: Many people don't take medicines as prescribed, for reasons that are often understandable.
  9. Structured medication reviews: A structured medication review (SMR) is a comprehensive, person-centred review of all of a person's medicines.
  10. Shared decision-making: Shared decision-making means the person and the professional decide together, using the evidence about benefits and harms and the person's own values.
  11. Counselling on medicines: Good counselling helps people take medicines safely and effectively, and know what to do if something goes wrong.
  12. Responding to symptoms: People often ask pharmacy teams for help with symptoms.
  13. Minor illness and referral: Many minor illnesses can be managed with self-care and over-the-counter treatment, freeing GP and emergency capacity.
  14. Pharmacy First and common clinical conditions: In England, the Pharmacy First service lets community pharmacists assess and treat seven common conditions, including supplying some prescription-only medicines under patient group directions.
  15. New Medicine Service and discharge services: Two of the most effective community pharmacy services in England support people at moments of high risk: starting a new medicine, and leaving hospital.
  16. Hospital clinical pharmacy: Hospital pharmacists work on wards and in specialist teams, making sure inpatients' medicines are safe and effective from admission to discharge.
  17. Transfers of care: Moving between hospital, home, care homes and community services is when medicines errors and confusion are most likely.

School 18: Prescribing and Clinical Assessment

16 lessons

Prescribing and Clinical Assessment: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The independent prescriber: Pharmacist independent prescribers can prescribe any medicine for any condition within their competence, including most controlled drugs.
  2. The RPS competency framework for all prescribers: The Royal Pharmaceutical Society's competency framework sets out what good prescribing looks like for every prescriber, whatever their profession.
  3. Prescribing governance and scope: Safe prescribing needs clear governance: defined scope, supervision, audit and accountability.
  4. Consultation models: Consultation models give structure to clinical conversations, helping prescribers gather information, build rapport and reach shared decisions.
  5. History taking: A thorough history leads to the diagnosis in most cases.
  6. Clinical examination skills: Clinical examination confirms or refutes hypotheses from the history.
  7. Vital signs and red flags: Vital signs give an objective picture of how unwell someone is.
  8. Interpreting blood tests: Prescribers order and interpret blood tests to diagnose, choose medicines and monitor safety.
  9. Diagnostic reasoning: Diagnostic reasoning is how clinicians move from symptoms to a diagnosis.
  10. Writing a prescription: A prescription is a legal document and an instruction to others.
  11. Prescribing safety and common errors: Prescribing errors are common and can be serious.
  12. Antimicrobial prescribing: Prescribers are central to antimicrobial stewardship.
  13. Remote prescribing: Prescribing by phone, video or online questionnaire is now common, but it brings risks: you can't examine, identity is harder to confirm, and some medicines are open to misuse.
  14. Influences on prescribing: Prescribing decisions are shaped by evidence, but also by patients, colleagues, industry, cost and our own habits.
  15. Monitoring and follow-up: Prescribing doesn't end when the prescription is signed.
  16. Prescribing in practice: case reviews: Case reviews bring the prescribing process together: assessment, decision, prescription, monitoring and reflection.

School 19: Registration Assessment and Foundation Training

11 lessons

Registration Assessment and Foundation Training: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The GPhC registration assessment: The registration assessment is the General Pharmaceutical Council's exam that trainees must pass to join the register.
  2. The assessment framework: The registration assessment framework sets out what can be assessed and how much weight each area carries.
  3. Single best answer questions: Single best answer (SBA) questions ask you to choose the most appropriate option from several plausible ones.
  4. Extended matching questions: Extended matching questions (EMQs) share a list of options across several scenarios.
  5. Using the BNF and resources efficiently: Fast, accurate use of the BNF and other references is essential in practice and in revision.
  6. Revision strategy: Passing the assessment depends on steady, active revision over months rather than cramming.
  7. The foundation training year: The foundation training year is the final stage of initial education and training: a supervised year in practice before registration.
  8. Learning outcomes and evidence: Trainees must show they meet the GPhC learning outcomes by the end of initial training.
  9. Working with your designated supervisor: Your designated supervisor guides, assesses and supports you through the foundation year.
  10. From trainee to registered pharmacist: After passing the assessment and completing training, you apply to join the GPhC register.
  11. GPhC revalidation: Registered pharmacists must revalidate every year to show they keep their practice up to date.
