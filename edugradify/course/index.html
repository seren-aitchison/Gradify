Free Teaching course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

EduGradify's free Teaching course runs from How Pupils Learn to Education: 228 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for teachers and educators.

Study material, not a teaching qualification. EduGradify is for trainee and early-career teachers. It does not award or count towards Qualified Teacher Status (QTS), and it doesn't replace your training provider, mentor or induction programme. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by teacher educators, so they may contain errors; check important points against the DfE's current guidance. Always follow your own school's policies, especially on safeguarding: if you are worried about a child, speak to your designated safeguarding lead straight away, and if a child is in immediate danger call 999.

Schools and lessons

School 1: How Pupils Learn

19 lessons

How Pupils Learn: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Learning as a change in long-term memory: The Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework starts from one idea: learning is a lasting change in what pupils know, understand or can do.
  2. Working memory and its limits: Working memory is where pupils hold and manipulate information while they think, and it is small.
  3. Long-term memory and schemas: Long-term memory is not a filing cabinet of separate facts but a web of connected knowledge called schemas.
  4. Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning: What pupils already know is the strongest single influence on what they learn next.
  5. Misconceptions and conceptual change: Pupils do not arrive as blank slates: they bring intuitive ideas that are often wrong and hard to shift.
  6. Cognitive load theory: Sweller 1988: Cognitive load theory, first set out by John Sweller in 1988, asks how instruction should be designed given the limits of working memory.
  7. Intrinsic and extraneous load: Cognitive load theory distinguishes the load that comes from the material itself from the load that comes from how it is taught.
  8. The worked example effect: Studying a worked example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem, is one of the most reliable ways for novices to learn a procedure.
  9. The expertise reversal effect: Guidance that helps novices can become a hindrance as pupils gain expertise.
  10. Split attention and redundancy: Two effects from cognitive load theory explain why some slides and worksheets make learning harder: split attention and redundancy.
  11. Dual coding: Dual coding means combining words with visuals such as diagrams, timelines and graphic organisers, so that pupils can process information through two connected channels.
  12. Retrieval practice: Retrieval practice means bringing information to mind from memory, for example through low-stakes quizzes, rather than re-reading it.
  13. Spaced practice: Spreading practice of the same material over time leads to better long-term retention than cramming it into one session.
  14. Interleaving: Interleaving means mixing different types of problem or topic in practice, rather than practising one type in a block.
  15. Elaboration and generative learning: Learning is deeper when pupils actively make sense of material: explaining why, connecting it to what they know, and transforming it into a new form.
  16. Forgetting and desirable difficulties: Forgetting is normal and, managed well, useful.
  17. Motivation and self-efficacy: Pupils are more likely to persist and succeed when they believe they can, value the work and feel some control and belonging.
  18. Metacognition and self-regulated learning: Metacognition is thinking about thinking: how pupils plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning.
  19. Learning styles and other neuromyths: Some popular ideas about the brain and learning have little or no evidence behind them.

School 2: Classroom Practice

18 lessons

Classroom Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction: In 2012 Barak Rosenshine summarised decades of research into ten principles of effective instruction.
  2. Explicit instruction and its critics: Explicit instruction means the teacher clearly explains, models and guides practice, rather than leaving pupils to discover new material for themselves.
  3. Daily, weekly and monthly review: Rosenshine's first and tenth principles concern review: begin lessons by reviewing previous learning, and return to material weekly and monthly.
  4. Presenting new material in small steps: Because working memory is limited, new material is best presented in small steps, with practice after each one.
  5. Modelling and thinking aloud: Modelling means showing pupils how an expert does a task, and thinking aloud makes the expert's hidden decisions visible.
  6. Worked examples and faded guidance: Worked examples are most effective when support is gradually removed, so that pupils move from studying full solutions to completing problems on their own.
  7. Questioning to check understanding: Questions are teachers' main tool for finding out what pupils understand.
  8. Cold calling and no opt out: Cold calling means choosing who answers rather than waiting for hands up; "no opt out" means a pupil who can't answer at first still ends up giving the right answer.
  9. Hinge questions and mini-whiteboards: A hinge question is a quick, carefully designed check at the point in a lesson where the teacher must decide whether to move on.
  10. Wait time and think-pair-share: Giving pupils time to think before they answer changes who answers and how well.
  11. Probing questions and extending answers: A pupil's first answer is often incomplete.
  12. Scaffolding: Scaffolding is temporary support that lets pupils do a task they could not yet do alone, removed as they become able to do it themselves.
  13. Guided practice: Guided practice is the stage between the teacher's model and pupils' independent work, when pupils practise with the teacher close by to prompt, check and correct.
  14. Independent practice and a high success rate: Pupils need extensive independent practice to become fluent, and they learn best when they succeed most of the time.
  15. Removing scaffolds: Scaffolds help only if they are eventually taken away.
  16. Teacher explanations and analogies: A clear explanation is one of the most powerful things a teacher does.
  17. Classroom discussion and dialogic teaching: Well-structured talk helps pupils reason, explain and learn from each other.
  18. Collaborative learning and groupwork: Pupils working together can learn more than they would alone, but only when groupwork is carefully structured.

School 3: Adaptive Teaching and SEND

20 lessons

Adaptive Teaching and SEND: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What adaptive teaching is: Adaptive teaching means keeping high expectations for every pupil while adjusting how you teach, in response to what pupils know and need, so that all of them can succeed.
  2. Adaptive teaching versus differentiation by task: For many years "differentiation" in English schools often meant planning different tasks for different groups.
  3. Responding to pupils within the lesson: Much of adaptive teaching happens in the moment: noticing that pupils are stuck and changing what you do.
  4. Attainment grouping and its evidence: Schools often group pupils by prior attainment, between classes (setting and streaming) or within a class.
  5. Disadvantaged pupils and the Pupil Premium: Pupils from disadvantaged backgrounds in England do less well at school, on average, than their peers.
  6. Pupils with English as an additional language: About one in five pupils in England speaks English as an additional language (EAL).
  7. Challenging high-attaining pupils: Pupils who already do well also need to be challenged, or they may coast and fall behind their potential.
  8. The SEND Code of Practice 2015: The Special Educational Needs and Disability Code of Practice: 0 to 25 years (2015) is the statutory guidance that sets out how schools must identify and support pupils with SEND in England.
  9. The four broad areas of need: The SEND Code of Practice groups special educational needs into four broad areas.
  10. The graduated approach: assess, plan, do, review: When a pupil needs SEN support, the Code of Practice expects schools to follow a graduated approach: a cycle of assess, plan, do and review.
  11. The role of the SENCO: Every mainstream school in England must have a special educational needs co-ordinator (SENCO).
  12. Education, health and care plans: An education, health and care (EHC) plan is a legal document for a child or young person aged 0 to 25 whose needs cannot be met through SEN support alone.
  13. Working with teaching assistants: Teaching assistants (TAs) make up a large part of the school workforce, but research has shown that how they are deployed matters greatly.
  14. The Equality Act 2010 and reasonable adjustments: The Equality Act 2010 protects pupils from discrimination, including disabled pupils, and requires schools to make reasonable adjustments.
  15. Communication and interaction needs: Speech, language and communication needs are among the most common special educational needs in primary schools, and they are often missed.
  16. Dyslexia and specific learning difficulties: Specific learning difficulties affect particular aspects of learning, such as reading, spelling, number or coordination, rather than learning as a whole.
  17. Autism in the classroom: Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication and interaction, often with focused interests, a preference for routine and sensory differences.
  18. ADHD in the classroom: Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) affects attention, activity and impulse control, and can have a large effect on learning and relationships at school.
  19. Social, emotional and mental health needs: Social, emotional and mental health (SEMH) needs range from anxiety and low mood to attachment difficulties and behaviour that challenges.
  20. Sensory and physical needs: Some pupils have a vision impairment, hearing impairment, multi-sensory impairment, physical disability or medical condition that affects access to learning.

School 4: Assessment and Feedback

16 lessons

Assessment and Feedback: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The purposes of assessment: Assessment is any process that gathers evidence of what pupils know and can do.
  2. Formative and summative assessment: Formative and summative assessment play different roles in teaching.
  3. Validity and reliability: Validity and reliability are the two key qualities of any assessment.
  4. Assessment for learning: Assessment for learning (AfL) uses evidence of learning, minute by minute and day by day, to help pupils improve.
  5. Designing assessment questions: Good assessment depends on good questions.
  6. Multiple-choice questions and diagnostic distractors: Well-written multiple-choice questions can assess understanding quickly and precisely, especially when the wrong options are based on real misconceptions.
  7. Effective feedback: the EEF guidance: Feedback is information that helps pupils close the gap between where they are and where they need to be.
  8. Written and verbal feedback: Feedback can be written or spoken, and each has strengths and costs.
  9. Marking, purpose and workload: Marking takes a large share of teachers' time, yet much of it has little effect on learning.
  10. Peer and self-assessment: When pupils assess their own and each other's work, they can learn what quality looks like and how to improve.
  11. Whole-class feedback: Whole-class feedback means reading pupils' work, identifying the common strengths and errors, and addressing them with the whole class.
  12. Using assessment data: Schools collect a great deal of assessment data.
  13. Standardised tests and scaled scores: Standardised tests compare a pupil's performance with a large reference group.
  14. Statutory assessment in England: Pupils in England take a series of statutory assessments from Reception to the end of key stage 2, followed by public qualifications.
  15. GCSEs, grading and examinations: GCSEs are the main qualifications taken at 16 in England.
  16. Reporting to parents and carers: Schools must report pupils' progress to parents, and good communication with families supports learning.

School 5: Learning and Behaviour

20 lessons

Learning and Behaviour: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Classical conditioning: Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response after being paired with a stimulus that triggers it naturally.
  2. Acquisition and extinction: Acquisition is the phase in which a conditioned response is learned; extinction is its gradual weakening when the conditioned stimulus is no longer followed by the unconditioned stimulus.
  3. Spontaneous recovery: Spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period.
  4. Generalisation and discrimination: Generalisation is responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus; discrimination is learning to respond only to the conditioned stimulus.
  5. Operant conditioning: Operant conditioning is learning from consequences: behaviours followed by good outcomes become more frequent, and those followed by bad outcomes less frequent.
  6. Positive and negative reinforcement: Reinforcement always increases behaviour.
  7. Punishment: Punishment is a consequence that decreases behaviour.
  8. Schedules of reinforcement: Schedules of reinforcement are rules about which responses are reinforced.
  9. Shaping: Shaping is training a new behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it.
  10. Behaviour modification: Behaviour modification applies learning principles to change behaviour, in schools, care settings, health and everyday life.
  11. Observational learning: Observational learning is learning by watching others, without needing to perform the behaviour or be reinforced directly.
  12. Social learning theory: Bandura's social learning theory explains how people learn through observation, combining behaviourist ideas with cognitive processes.
  13. Modelling and imitation: Not all models are equally likely to be imitated.
  14. Vicarious reinforcement: Vicarious reinforcement is learning about the consequences of behaviour by seeing what happens to others.
  15. Latent learning: Latent learning is learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it.
  16. Insight learning: Insight learning is the sudden solution of a problem through restructuring it mentally, rather than gradual trial and error.
  17. Biological constraints on learning: Not everything is equally easy to learn.
  18. Habituation and sensitisation: Habituation and sensitisation are the simplest forms of learning: responding less to a repeated harmless stimulus, or more to stimuli after something intense.
  19. Habit formation: Habits are behaviours triggered automatically by context, after being repeated in that context.
  20. Applications of learning theory: Learning theory has shaped therapy, education, parenting, health and the design of technology.

School 6: Curriculum and Subject Knowledge

17 lessons

Curriculum and Subject Knowledge: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What a curriculum is: The curriculum is what pupils are meant to learn, in what order and why.
  2. Intent, implementation and impact: Ofsted's 2019 framework described the curriculum through three words: intent, implementation and impact.
  3. The National Curriculum in England: The National Curriculum sets out the subjects and content that maintained schools in England must teach.
  4. Powerful knowledge and knowledge-rich curricula: Many schools in England describe their curriculum as knowledge-rich, drawing on ideas such as Michael Young's powerful knowledge and E. D. Hirsch's core knowledge.
  5. Substantive and disciplinary knowledge: Every subject has two kinds of knowledge: substantive knowledge, the content it has established, and disciplinary knowledge, how that knowledge is created, tested and debated.
  6. Sequencing and the curriculum as progression model: The order in which content is taught matters, because later learning depends on earlier learning.
  7. Subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge: Teachers need deep knowledge of their subject and also knowledge of how to teach it: which examples, explanations and representations work, and what pupils find hard.
  8. Threshold concepts: Some ideas in a subject act like doorways: once understood, they transform how a learner sees the subject.
  9. Anticipating misconceptions in your subject: Every subject has predictable misconceptions.
  10. Core knowledge and hinterland: Not everything taught in a topic needs to be memorised.
  11. Cultural capital and a diverse curriculum: Debates about curriculum often turn on whose knowledge is taught and who benefits.
  12. Curriculum mapping and coherence: A coherent curriculum fits together: within a subject from year to year, across units in a year, and with assessment.
  13. Schemes of work: A scheme of work turns the curriculum into a sequence of lessons for a unit.
  14. Textbooks and curriculum resources: High-quality textbooks and curriculum resources can support coherent teaching and reduce workload.
  15. Spiral and mastery curricula: Two influential ideas about curriculum structure are the spiral curriculum, which revisits topics at increasing depth, and mastery, which secures each step before moving on.
  16. Cross-curricular links: Making links between subjects can help pupils see connections and apply knowledge.
  17. Reviewing a curriculum: Curricula need regular review to check that they are working.

School 7: Planning Lessons and Sequences

16 lessons

Planning Lessons and Sequences: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Planning backwards from outcomes: Good planning starts from what pupils should know and be able to do at the end, not from an activity.
  2. Planning a sequence of lessons: Learning rarely fits into a single lesson, so teachers plan sequences that build knowledge over time.
  3. Learning intentions and success criteria: Learning intentions say what pupils will learn, and success criteria describe what successful learning looks like.
  4. Activating prior knowledge: New learning is built on what pupils already know, so lessons often begin by bringing relevant knowledge to mind.
  5. The structure of a lesson: Most lessons follow a recognisable shape, from review to new content, practice and checking.
  6. Choosing tasks and activities: The tasks pupils do determine what they think about, and so what they learn.
  7. Planning explanations and models: Clear explanations rarely happen by chance, so expert teachers plan the words, examples and models they will use.
  8. Planning questions in advance: Good questions are often prepared before the lesson rather than invented on the spot.
  9. Homework and independent study: Homework can extend learning beyond the lesson, but its value depends on its purpose and design.
  10. Planning for adaptive teaching: Adaptive teaching starts at the planning stage, by anticipating where pupils may struggle and planning support and challenge.
  11. Planning practical work and trips: Practical work and educational visits can make learning memorable, but only if they are planned with a clear purpose and managed safely.
  12. Evaluating a lesson: Teachers improve by reflecting on what happened in a lesson and why, then changing what they do next.
  13. Lesson observation and feedback: Trainees and early-career teachers are regularly observed, and the feedback can speed up their development.
  14. Planning and workload: Planning is essential, but it can also become one of the biggest drivers of teacher workload.
  15. Digital technology in the classroom: Digital technology can support explanations, practice and feedback, but it does not improve learning by itself.
  16. Artificial intelligence and teaching: Generative artificial intelligence tools can help teachers plan and prepare, but they also bring risks to accuracy, data protection and assessment.

School 8: Literacy and Oracy Across the Curriculum

14 lessons

Literacy and Oracy Across the Curriculum: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Literacy in every subject: Reading, writing and talk are the main ways pupils learn in every subject, so every teacher is a teacher of literacy.
  2. Disciplinary literacy: Each subject reads, writes and talks in its own way, and pupils need to be taught those ways.
  3. Reading fluency: Fluent readers read accurately, at a good pace and with expression, which frees attention for understanding.
  4. Reading comprehension strategies: Comprehension strategies, such as predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarising, help pupils actively make sense of texts.
  5. Vocabulary: tiers and explicit teaching: Vocabulary is closely linked to reading comprehension and to success in every subject.
  6. Systematic synthetic phonics and early reading: In England, early reading is taught through systematic synthetic phonics, which teaches children the links between sounds and letters and how to blend them.
  7. Teaching writing in subjects: Writing is hard because it asks pupils to manage ideas, language and structure at the same time.
  8. Modelling academic writing: Pupils learn to write in a subject by seeing how an expert writes and hearing the decisions behind it.
  9. Spelling, punctuation and grammar: Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar help writers communicate clearly, and they are assessed in national tests and examinations.
  10. What oracy is: Oracy is the ability to speak well and to use talk to think and learn.
  11. Learning to talk and learning through talk: Oracy has two sides: teaching pupils to talk well, and using talk as a way to learn content.
  12. Structuring classroom talk: Productive classroom talk rarely happens by accident; it is planned through routines, roles and clear expectations.
  13. Sentence stems and speaking frames: Sentence stems and speaking frames give pupils the language to start and structure what they want to say.
  14. Assessing oracy: Spoken language can be assessed, but it is harder to capture than writing because talk is fleeting and depends on context.

School 9: Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare

19 lessons

Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What safeguarding means: Safeguarding is the action taken to promote the welfare of children and protect them from harm, and it is everyone's responsibility.
  2. Keeping Children Safe in Education: Keeping Children Safe in Education is the statutory guidance that sets out what schools and colleges in England must do to safeguard children.
  3. The designated safeguarding lead: Every school must have a designated safeguarding lead who takes lead responsibility for safeguarding and child protection.
  4. Working Together to Safeguard Children: Working Together to Safeguard Children is the statutory guidance on how organisations and agencies should work together to protect children.
  5. The four categories of abuse: Statutory guidance describes four categories of abuse: physical, emotional, sexual abuse and neglect.
  6. Signs of abuse and neglect: Children rarely tell an adult directly that they are being abused, so staff need to notice signs and changes.
  7. Responding to a disclosure: When a child tells an adult about abuse, how the adult responds matters greatly.
  8. Recording and reporting concerns: Clear, accurate records help the DSL and other agencies protect children, and they may later be used in court.
  9. Child-on-child abuse: Children can abuse other children, and schools must take this seriously rather than dismiss it as banter or growing up.
  10. Online safety: Online life brings benefits and risks for children, and online safety is part of safeguarding.
  11. Child criminal and sexual exploitation: Children can be exploited by individuals or groups who take advantage of an imbalance of power to coerce them into criminal or sexual activity.
  12. Radicalisation and the Prevent duty: Schools have a legal duty to have due regard to the need to prevent people from being drawn into terrorism.
  13. Female genital mutilation: the mandatory reporting duty: Female genital mutilation is illegal in the UK and a form of child abuse with serious lifelong consequences.
  14. Children missing education: A child who is not on a school roll or not receiving suitable education may be at risk of harm and of falling behind.
  15. Pupil mental health: Many pupils experience mental health difficulties, and teachers are often the first to notice.
  16. Allegations against staff and low-level concerns: Schools must deal promptly and fairly with concerns about adults who work with children.
  17. Safer working practice: Safer working practice protects children and staff by setting clear professional boundaries.
  18. Safeguarding children with SEND: Children with special educational needs and disabilities are at greater risk of abuse, and face extra barriers to being recognised and protected.
  19. Attendance as a safeguarding issue: Regular attendance is vital for learning and is also one of the ways schools keep children safe.

School 10: Sleep, Stress and Rest

14 lessons

Sleep, Stress and Rest: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why we sleep: Sleep is essential for physical health, learning and emotional wellbeing.
  2. Sleep stages and cycles: Sleep isn't a single state but a cycle of distinct stages.
  3. Circadian rhythms: Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles in body processes, controlled by an internal clock and set by light.
  4. How much sleep we need: Sleep needs vary with age and between individuals.
  5. Sleep across the lifespan: Sleep changes dramatically from birth to old age.
  6. Insomnia: Insomnia is persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep that affects daytime functioning.
  7. Improving sleep: Many everyday habits affect sleep.
  8. Shift work and sleep: Shift work, especially at night, conflicts with the body clock.
  9. Sleep disorders beyond insomnia: Several sleep disorders other than insomnia are common and treatable.
  10. What stress is: Stress is the response to demands that challenge or exceed our ability to cope.
  11. The stress response: The body responds to stress through two linked systems: a fast nervous and hormonal response, and a slower hormonal response.
  12. Stress and health: Chronic stress affects both physical and mental health.
  13. Coping and stress management: Coping is what people do to manage stress.
  14. Work-related stress and burnout: Work is a major source of stress for many people.

School 11: Research Methods

34 lessons

Research Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Scientific reasoning in psychology: Scientific reasoning is the way psychologists move from ideas to evidence and back: making claims testable, collecting data systematically and drawing conclusions that go no further than the data allow.
  2. Research questions and hypotheses: A good study starts with a clear research question and a precise hypothesis.
  3. Operationalisation: Operationalisation turns an abstract concept, such as stress or aggression, into something that can be measured.
  4. Independent and dependent variables: In an experiment, the independent variable is what the researcher changes and the dependent variable is what they measure.
  5. Confounding variables: A confounding variable changes along with the independent variable, so it offers an alternative explanation for the results.
  6. Experimental design: Experimental design is how participants are allocated to conditions: independent groups, repeated measures or matched pairs.
  7. Laboratory experiments: Laboratory experiments manipulate an IV in a controlled setting, allowing strong causal conclusions at a possible cost to realism.
  8. Field experiments: Field experiments manipulate an IV in a natural setting, often without participants knowing they are in a study.
  9. Natural experiments: In a natural experiment, the independent variable changes naturally, without the researcher manipulating it, and the researcher studies its effects.
  10. Quasi-experiments: A quasi-experiment compares groups that differ on a pre-existing characteristic, such as age, sex or diagnosis, which the researcher can't assign.
  11. Correlational research: Correlational research measures two or more variables to see whether they are related, without manipulating anything.
  12. Cross-sectional and longitudinal research: To study change over time, researchers either compare different age groups at one point (cross-sectional) or follow the same people over years (longitudinal).
  13. Case studies: A case study is an in-depth investigation of one person, group or event, usually using several methods.
  14. Naturalistic observation: Naturalistic observation records behaviour as it happens in its everyday setting, without interfering.
  15. Structured observation: Structured observation uses predefined behavioural categories and sampling methods so that observations are systematic, countable and reliable.
  16. Surveys and questionnaires: Surveys collect self-report data from many people using questionnaires.
  17. Interviews: Interviews collect self-report data through conversation, from fully structured to unstructured.
  18. Focus groups: A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group about a topic, used to explore views and how people talk about an issue together.
  19. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods: Quantitative research measures and counts; qualitative research explores meanings in words and experiences; mixed methods combine them.
  20. Random and stratified sampling: Sampling decides who takes part.
  21. Opportunity and volunteer sampling: Opportunity and volunteer samples are quick and common in psychology, but they are rarely representative.
  22. Sample size: How many participants a study needs depends on the size of the effect it is looking for and how sure the researchers want to be of detecting it.
  23. Reliability: Reliability is the consistency of a measure: whether it gives the same results under the same conditions.
  24. Validity: Validity is whether a study or measure actually captures what it claims to.
  25. Generalisability: Generalisability is how far findings extend beyond the specific people, place and time studied.
  26. Bias in research: Bias is systematic error that pushes results in a particular direction.
  27. Replication: Replication means repeating a study to see whether its findings hold.
  28. Preregistration: Preregistration means publicly recording a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan before collecting or analysing data.
  29. Open science: Open science is a set of practices that make research transparent, accessible and checkable: sharing data, materials, code and papers, and making plans public.
  30. Informed consent: Informed consent means participants agree to take part knowing what the research involves, its risks and their rights.
  31. Confidentiality: Confidentiality means protecting participants' personal information and ensuring they can't be identified in reports.
  32. Deception and debriefing: Deception means misleading participants about a study, and debriefing means explaining the study fully afterwards.
  33. Vulnerable participants: Some participants need extra protection because their capacity to consent is limited or they face greater risk: children, people with some cognitive impairments or mental health conditions, prisoners…
  34. Research governance and ethics committees: Research governance is the system of rules, review and accountability that oversees research.

School 12: Education

21 lessons

Education: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Functionalist perspectives on education: Functionalists see education as serving the needs of society: building shared values, teaching skills and sorting people into roles.
  2. Marxist perspectives on education: Marxists see education as reproducing class inequality and legitimating it.
  3. Feminist perspectives on education: Feminist sociologists examine how education reproduces or challenges gender inequality.
  4. Education, socialisation and social control: Schools socialise young people and regulate their behaviour.
  5. The hidden curriculum: The hidden curriculum is what pupils learn from the way schools are organised, not from formal lessons.
  6. Educational achievement and social class: Social class is one of the strongest predictors of educational achievement in England.
  7. Educational achievement and gender: Girls have outperformed boys on average in English schools for decades.
  8. Educational achievement and ethnicity: Educational outcomes vary widely by ethnic group.
  9. Cultural capital and education: Cultural capital is the knowledge, tastes and confidence that bring educational advantage.
  10. Linguistic codes: Bernstein argued that differences in how families use language affect school success.
  11. Labelling in education: Labelling theory examines how teachers' judgements about pupils can shape pupils' identities and outcomes.
  12. Teacher expectations: Teacher expectations are beliefs teachers hold about pupils' future achievement.
  13. The self-fulfilling prophecy: A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true because it was made.
  14. Selection: Selection refers to how schools and systems sort pupils by ability or other criteria.
  15. Educational inequality: Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education.
  16. Private education: Private (independent) schools educate a small share of pupils but a large share of elites.
  17. Higher education: Higher education has expanded greatly, but access and outcomes remain unequal.
  18. Education policy: Education policy shapes how schools are organised, funded and held accountable.
  19. Global education: Access to and quality of education vary greatly around the world.
  20. Digital learning: Digital technology is changing how people learn, from online courses to AI tools.
  21. Educational reform: Educational reform aims to improve standards and reduce inequality.
