## Everyday materials
LV: Year 1 to Year 2 · Foundation
S: Everything around us is made from materials such as wood, plastic, metal and glass. This lesson explains the difference between an object and the material it is made from, and why we choose particular materials for particular jobs.
O: Distinguish between an object and the material it is made from
O: Identify and name everyday materials
O: Explain why a material is suitable for a particular use
H: Objects and materials
P: An **object** is a thing, such as a spoon, a window or a chair. A **material** is what the object is made from, such as **wood**, **plastic**, **glass**, **metal**, **fabric**, **paper**, **rubber**, **rock** or **water**. The same object can be made from different materials: a spoon could be metal, plastic or wood. And the same material can make lots of different objects: wood can become a table, a pencil or a door. In Year 1 the national curriculum asks children to tell an object apart from the material it is made from and to name everyday materials. In Year 2 they compare how suitable materials are for different uses, and learn about people such as **John McAdam**, who developed a new way of building roads, and **Charles Macintosh**, who invented a waterproof fabric.
H: Common materials
- **Wood**: comes from trees; used for furniture, doors and pencils.
- **Metal**: strong and shiny; used for keys, pans and cars.
- **Plastic**: can be made in any shape and colour; used for bottles and toys.
- **Glass**: transparent; used for windows and jars.
- **Fabric**: soft and bendy; used for clothes and curtains.
- **Rubber**: stretchy and waterproof; used for tyres and wellies.
H: Choosing the right material
- Ask what the object needs to do.
- A window needs to be **transparent** so we can see through it.
- An umbrella needs to be **waterproof**.
- A saucepan needs to be strong and not melt on the hob.
- A teapot made of chocolate would be a poor choice: it would melt.
X: Testing materials for a raincoat.
> A class wants to find the best material for a teddy's raincoat.
> They drip the same amount of water onto paper, cotton, foil and plastic.
> The paper and cotton soak it up; the water runs off the foil and plastic.
> Plastic is chosen because it is waterproof and also bendy enough to wear.
K: An object is a thing and a material is what it is made from, and we choose materials whose properties suit the object's job.
!: Calling an object a material, such as saying "the material is a chair". The chair is the object; wood or plastic is the material.
