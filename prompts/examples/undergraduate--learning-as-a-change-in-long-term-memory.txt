## Learning as a change in long-term memory
LV: Introductory · Foundation
S: The Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework starts from one idea: learning is a lasting change in what pupils know, understand or can do. This lesson explains that definition, why it matters for teaching, and why a good lesson is not the same as learning.
O: Explain what is meant by learning as a lasting change in long-term memory
O: Distinguish performance during a lesson from learning over time
O: Evaluate the use of in-lesson indicators as evidence that pupils have learned
H: A working definition
P: The ITTECF (DfE, 2024) states that learning involves a lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) put it more sharply: if nothing has changed in long-term memory, nothing has been learned. The definition is deliberately about what lasts. A pupil who can solve a problem at 10.15 and cannot at the start of next week has performed, but has not yet learned it securely.
P: **Long-term memory** is the store of everything a person knows, from facts and concepts to procedures and the links between them. Its capacity is, for practical purposes, unlimited. Learning adds to it and, just as importantly, reorganises it: new ideas are connected to what is already there.
K: Learning is a lasting change in long-term memory. A lesson succeeds when pupils can still use what was taught days and weeks later, not only when they can do it at the end of the lesson.
H: Learning versus performance
P: **Performance** is what a pupil can do now, often with support, prompts and the material fresh in mind. Soderstrom and Bjork (2015) reviewed decades of evidence that performance during practice is an unreliable guide to learning. Conditions that make practice feel smooth (massed repetition, constant hints, re-reading) often produce fast gains that fade, while conditions that make it harder (spacing, retrieval, mixing problem types) can depress performance in the lesson yet improve long-term retention.
- Busy pupils, completed worksheets and confident answers are **proxies** for learning, not learning itself (Coe, 2013, called these "poor proxies").
- Correct answers straight after an explanation show that pupils can follow, not that they will remember.
- Checking learning means checking later: at the start of the next lesson, the next week, the next term.
X: A Year 8 class completes ten simultaneous equations at the end of a lesson with 90% accuracy. A week later, a three-question starter on the same method gives 40%.
> The 90% was performance: the method was in working memory, with the worked example still on the board.
> The 40% is closer to a measure of learning, because it required retrieval from long-term memory after a delay.
> The teacher's response is not to reteach from scratch but to plan spaced retrieval and further practice, then check again later.
!: Judging a lesson by how well it went on the day. A lesson where pupils struggled productively may have produced more learning than one where everyone finished easily.
H: Why it matters for teachers
P: If learning is a change in long-term memory, then teaching must plan for what pupils will remember: sequencing so new material builds on secure prior knowledge, returning to content over time, and assessing after a delay. It also explains why the ITTECF places memory, prior knowledge and practice at the start of Standard 2 (how pupils learn).
EV: established | Performance during acquisition is an unreliable indicator of long-term learning; some conditions that slow acquisition improve retention (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015).
EV: contested | Whether all valuable school outcomes (creativity, disposition, wellbeing) can be described as changes in long-term memory is debated.
T: Learning | A lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding, held in long-term memory
T: Performance | What a learner can do at a given moment, which may not last
T: Proxy for learning | An observable feature of a lesson, such as engagement or finished work, that is easily mistaken for learning
SUM: Learning is a lasting change in long-term memory; performance in the lesson is not proof of it.
SUM: Check learning after a delay, and plan for retention, not just for completion.

Q: How does the ITTECF describe learning? | As a lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding.
Q: Why is end-of-lesson success a weak measure of learning? | It shows performance with the material fresh in working memory; it does not show what pupils will retain and use later.

Completion, repetition and engagement can happen without any lasting change in long-term memory.
