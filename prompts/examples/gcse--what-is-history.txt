## What is history?
LV: GCSE and A level · Foundation
S: History is the study of the human past through the evidence it left behind. This lesson separates "the past" (everything that happened) from "history" (the accounts historians build from evidence), and shows why those accounts are arguments rather than lists of facts.
O: Distinguish between the past and history as an account of it
O: Explain why historians select and interpret facts rather than simply recording them
O: Evaluate the claim that history is "just facts and dates"
H: The past and history
P: **The past** is everything that has ever happened. **History** is the account we make of it, built from the traces the past left behind: documents, buildings, objects, images, landscapes and memories. Most of the past left no trace at all, so every history is partial.
P: History is therefore an act of **enquiry**. A historian asks a question (why did the English Civil War break out in 1642?), searches for evidence, interprets it and argues for an answer. Two careful historians can reach different answers from the same evidence, because they weigh it differently or ask slightly different questions.
H: Facts, selection and interpretation
P: Facts matter: Charles I was executed on 30 January 1649, and no serious account can deny it. But facts alone don't make history. The historian E. H. Carr argued in **What is History?** (1961) that "the facts speak only when the historian calls on them": the historian decides which facts are significant and how they connect.
P: Carr's own example was Julius Caesar crossing the Rubicon in 49 BCE. Millions of people crossed that small river; Caesar's crossing became a "historical fact" because historians judged it significant, since it began a civil war.
X: Is "the Battle of Hastings was fought in 1066" history, or only a fact?
> On its own it is a fact: a dated event that the evidence firmly supports.
> It becomes history when it is used to answer a question, for example "Why did William of Normandy win?" or "How far did 1066 change England?"
> Answering those questions means selecting further facts, judging their importance and arguing for an explanation.
K: History is not the past itself but an evidence-based argument about it: historians select, interpret and explain, so their accounts can differ while still respecting the facts.
!: Thinking that because historians disagree, any opinion about the past is as good as any other. Interpretations must be supported by evidence and can be judged by how well they account for it.
H: Why study history?
- It explains how the present came to be: institutions, borders, laws and identities all have histories.
- It trains judgement: weighing evidence, spotting bias and building an argument transfer to law, journalism, policy and everyday life.
- It widens perspective: people in the past thought and lived differently, and understanding them on their own terms is a discipline in itself.
EV: established | Historical accounts are built from surviving evidence and are always selective.
EV: contested | How far historians can be objective is debated: Carr stressed the historian's role in shaping facts, while Richard Evans defended the possibility of reliable knowledge about the past.
T: The past | Everything that happened before now, whether or not it left any trace
T: History | An evidence-based account of the past, built by asking questions and interpreting sources
T: Historical enquiry | Investigating a question about the past by gathering and interpreting evidence to reach a supported answer
SUM: History is an evidence-based account of the past, not the past itself.
SUM: Historians select and interpret facts to answer questions, so accounts can differ but must fit the evidence.
