## Blood glucose regulation
LV: Level 3 · Application
S: Blood glucose must stay within narrow limits because the brain depends on it and high levels damage blood vessels. This lesson explains how insulin and glucagon from the pancreas regulate glucose, the normal ranges and diagnostic thresholds used in the UK, and what goes wrong in diabetes.
O: Describe the roles of insulin and glucagon in regulating blood glucose
O: Interpret blood glucose and HbA1c values against UK diagnostic thresholds
O: Explain how type 1 and type 2 diabetes disrupt glucose homeostasis
PRE: Homeostasis
H: Insulin and glucagon
P: The **islets of Langerhans** in the pancreas contain **beta cells**, which release **insulin**, and **alpha cells**, which release **glucagon**. Insulin lowers blood glucose by helping cells, especially muscle and fat, take up glucose and by stimulating the liver and muscles to store it as **glycogen**. Glucagon raises blood glucose by stimulating the liver to break glycogen down (glycogenolysis) and to make new glucose (gluconeogenesis). Adrenaline and cortisol also raise glucose during stress.
X: Glucose control between meals and after a meal.
> After a meal, glucose rises; beta cells release insulin; cells take up glucose and the liver stores glycogen; glucose falls.
> Several hours later, glucose falls; alpha cells release glucagon; the liver releases glucose; glucose rises.
> The two hormones act antagonistically, keeping glucose usually between about 4 and 7.8 mmol/L in people without diabetes.
K: Insulin lowers blood glucose and glucagon raises it; they work in opposition through negative feedback, with the liver as the main store.
H: Measuring glucose
P: Blood glucose is measured in millimoles per litre (mmol/L). **HbA1c** (glycated haemoglobin) reflects average glucose over the previous two to three months, because glucose binds to haemoglobin over a red cell's roughly 120-day life. In the UK, type 2 diabetes is diagnosed (in people without symptoms, with a confirming test) by an HbA1c of 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) or more, or a fasting plasma glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or more. An HbA1c of 42 to 47 mmol/mol indicates a high risk of developing diabetes ("prediabetes"), for which the NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme offers support.
P: Very high blood glucose exceeds the kidneys' ability to reabsorb glucose, so it appears in the urine, pulling water with it: this explains the classic symptoms of thirst and passing a lot of urine.
H: When regulation fails
P: In **type 1 diabetes** the immune system destroys the beta cells, so little or no insulin is made; people need insulin for life, and without it they can develop diabetic ketoacidosis. In **type 2 diabetes**, cells become resistant to insulin and the beta cells gradually cannot make enough to compensate; it is strongly linked to excess weight, inactivity, age, family history and South Asian, Black African or Black Caribbean ethnicity. **Hypoglycaemia** (glucose below 4 mmol/L, "4 is the floor") is a risk for people taking insulin or some tablets and needs treating promptly with fast-acting sugar. Both conditions are covered in School 8.
!: Thinking that insulin breaks glucose down. Insulin is a signal: it causes cells to take up and store glucose; it is not an enzyme.

EV: established | Type 2 diabetes risk can be reduced in people at high risk through weight loss and increased physical activity, as shown in randomised trials of lifestyle intervention.
