## What is research design?
LV: Postgraduate · Foundation
S: Research design is the overall plan that connects a research question to the evidence needed to answer it. This lesson explains what a design is, how it differs from methods, and the main families of design used in postgraduate research.
O: Define research design and distinguish it from research methods
O: Identify the main types of research design
O: Explain why design decisions should follow from the research question
H: Design as a logical plan
P: A **research design** is the framework that guides the collection and analysis of data so that the evidence obtained answers the research question as unambiguously as possible. De Vaus (2001) stresses that design is a **logical** problem, not a logistical one: before choosing questionnaires or interviews, a researcher must ask what kind of evidence would be convincing. A **research method** is a technique for collecting data, such as a survey instrument or a semi-structured interview, whereas the design is the structure that decides whose data are collected, when, how often and with what comparisons. Bryman (2021) lists five broad designs: **experimental**, **cross-sectional**, **longitudinal**, **case study** and **comparative**.
H: What a design specifies
- The **unit of analysis**: individuals, groups, organisations, documents or events.
- The **comparisons** that will be made, for example between groups or over time.
- The **timing** of data collection: once, repeatedly or before and after an event.
- How cases or participants will be **selected**.
- How data will be **analysed** to support the claims the study wants to make.
H: Common families of design
- **Experimental** and **quasi-experimental** designs test causal claims through comparison.
- **Cross-sectional** designs collect data from many cases at one point in time.
- **Longitudinal** designs follow the same cases over time.
- **Case study** designs examine one or a few cases in depth and in context.
- **Comparative** designs study two or more contrasting cases using similar methods.
X: Choosing a design for a question about remote working.
> Question: does moving to remote working change employees' reported wellbeing?
> A one-off survey of remote and office workers could show differences but not change.
> A longitudinal design measuring the same staff before and after the move tracks change directly.
> The question about change points to a longitudinal design; the survey is only the method.
K: Research design is the logical structure of a study, specifying units, comparisons, timing and selection so that the evidence can answer the question, and it is distinct from the methods used to collect data.
!: Treating "I will use interviews" as a research design. Interviews are a method; the design states which cases, comparisons and timing make the interview data able to answer the question.
