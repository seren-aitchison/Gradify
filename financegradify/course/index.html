Accounting and Finance: a free course on FinanceGradify

FinanceGradify's free Accounting and Finance course runs from Accounting and the Double-Entry System to Accounting, Technology and Society: 202 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for finance and accounting students.

Education, not financial, tax or investment advice. FinanceGradify teaches accounting and finance for study and exams. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a qualified accountant, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, the current standards and HMRC guidance. Tax rates, allowances and standards change every year, and worked examples use simplified figures. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, sell or hold any investment, or advice on your own tax or finances: for that, speak to a firm or adviser authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority, or a chartered accountant or chartered tax adviser.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Accounting and the Double-Entry System

17 lessons

Accounting and the Double-Entry System: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is accounting?: Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording and communicating financial information about an organisation so that people can make decisions.
  2. Users of accounting information: Different people use accounts for different decisions.
  3. Financial and management accounting: Accounting splits into two branches.
  4. Types of business organisation: How a business is organised decides who owns it, who is liable for its debts and what accounts it must keep.
  5. Accounting concepts and conventions: Accounts rest on a small set of basic assumptions and principles.
  6. The accounting equation: Everything in double entry follows from one identity: assets equal capital plus liabilities.
  7. Assets, liabilities and capital: The statement of financial position groups items into current and non-current assets, current and non-current liabilities, and capital.
  8. Income, expenses and drawings: Income increases capital and expenses reduce it; drawings reduce capital too but are not an expense.
  9. The principles of double entry: Double entry records every transaction twice, as a debit in one account and an equal credit in another.
  10. Recording cash and credit transactions: Most business transactions are purchases, sales, returns, payments and receipts, made either for cash or on credit.
  11. Balancing off accounts: At the end of a period each account is balanced off to find what it now holds: a debit or credit balance carried down to start the next period.
  12. The trial balance: A trial balance lists every ledger balance to check that total debits equal total credits.
  13. Books of prime entry: Transactions are first listed in books of prime entry, from source documents, before being posted to the ledger in totals.
  14. Sales tax in the accounts: VAT-registered businesses charge VAT on sales and reclaim VAT on purchases, paying the difference to HMRC.
  15. Control accounts: A control account summarises all the individual customer or supplier accounts in one total account.
  16. Bank reconciliations: The cash book and the bank statement rarely agree on a given day.
  17. Errors and the suspense account: When a trial balance doesn't agree, the difference goes temporarily to a suspense account until the errors are found.

School 2: Preparing Financial Statements

15 lessons

Preparing Financial Statements: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Capital and revenue expenditure: Spending that brings benefits over several years is capital expenditure and goes to the statement of financial position; spending used up in the year is revenue expenditure and goes to profit.
  2. Accruals and prepayments: The accruals concept matches income and expenses to the period they belong to, not to when cash moves.
  3. Depreciation: Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over the years it is used.
  4. Disposal of non-current assets: When a non-current asset is sold or scrapped, its cost and accumulated depreciation are removed and any profit or loss on disposal is calculated.
  5. Irrecoverable debts and allowances for receivables: Some customers never pay.
  6. Inventory valuation: Closing inventory directly changes cost of sales and profit.
  7. The extended trial balance: The extended trial balance is a worksheet that takes the trial balance, adds year-end adjustments and sorts every figure into the statement of profit or loss or the statement of financial position.
  8. The statement of profit or loss: The statement of profit or loss shows a business's income and expenses for a period and the profit that results.
  9. The statement of financial position: The statement of financial position shows what a business owns and owes, and the owner's capital, at one date.
  10. Incomplete records: Many small businesses don't keep full double-entry records.
  11. Profit and cash: A profitable business can run out of cash, and a loss-making one can have plenty.
  12. Partnership agreements and the Partnership Act 1890: A partnership's accounts follow its partnership agreement, and where there is none, the default rules of the Partnership Act 1890.
  13. The appropriation account: A partnership's profit is shared out in an appropriation account: interest on drawings is added, salaries and interest on capital are deducted, and the residue is split in the profit-sharing ratio.
  14. Partners' capital and current accounts: Partners usually keep fixed capital accounts for the money they have invested and current accounts for their shares of profit and their drawings.
  15. Goodwill and changes in partners: When a partner joins or leaves, or the profit-sharing ratio changes, the existing partners must be credited with the value they have built up.

School 3: Financial Reporting for Companies

22 lessons

Financial Reporting for Companies: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The regulatory framework for financial reporting: Company financial statements are shaped by law, accounting standards and the bodies that set and enforce them.
  2. The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting: The IASB's Conceptual Framework sets out the purpose of financial reporting and defines its building blocks.
  3. Qualitative characteristics of useful information: The Conceptual Framework identifies the qualities that make financial information useful.
  4. Recognition and measurement: Recognition decides whether an item appears in the financial statements; measurement decides at what amount.
  5. Share capital and reserves: A company's equity is made up of share capital and reserves.
  6. Issues of shares, rights issues and bonus issues: Companies raise capital by issuing shares and can reorganise equity without new cash through bonus issues.
  7. Presenting company financial statements: IAS 1 sets out what a complete set of financial statements contains and the general principles of presentation.
  8. Statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income: The statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income reports a company's performance for the period.
  9. Statement of changes in equity: The statement of changes in equity reconciles opening and closing equity for each component.
  10. Statement of cash flows: The statement of cash flows shows how a company generated and used cash in the period.
  11. Property, plant and equipment: IAS 16 sets out how to account for property, plant and equipment (PPE).
  12. Intangible assets: Intangible assets are identifiable non-monetary assets without physical substance, such as patents, software and licences.
  13. Impairment of assets: An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds what can be recovered from it.
  14. Inventories under IAS 2: IAS 2 requires inventories to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
  15. Revenue from contracts with customers: IFRS 15 sets out a single five-step model for recognising revenue from contracts with customers.
  16. Leases: IFRS 16 brings almost all leases onto the lessee's statement of financial position.
  17. Provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets: IAS 37 decides when uncertain obligations are recognised as provisions and when they are only disclosed.
  18. Events after the reporting period: IAS 10 decides how events between the reporting date and the date the accounts are authorised are treated.
  19. Accounting policies, estimates and errors: IAS 8 governs how companies select and change accounting policies, revise estimates and correct errors.
  20. Income taxes: IAS 12 covers current tax and deferred tax.
  21. Financial instruments: Financial instruments include loans, bonds, shares and derivatives.
  22. Earnings per share: Earnings per share (EPS) is one of the most widely used performance measures for listed companies.

School 4: Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

17 lessons

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why analyse financial statements?: Financial statement analysis turns published figures into information users can act on.
  2. Profitability ratios: Profitability ratios measure how well a company turns revenue and capital into profit.
  3. Liquidity ratios: Liquidity ratios show whether a company can meet its short-term obligations.
  4. Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle: Efficiency ratios show how quickly a business turns inventory into sales, collects cash from customers and pays suppliers.
  5. Gearing and investor ratios: Gearing ratios show how far a company is financed by debt, and investor ratios show returns to shareholders.
  6. Interpreting ratios in context: Ratios are only useful when interpreted in context.
  7. Limitations of ratio analysis: Ratio analysis is powerful but has important limitations.
  8. Analysing cash flows: Cash flow analysis shows whether a company generates enough cash to sustain itself and how it funds investment and returns to shareholders.
  9. Performance in not-for-profit organisations: Charities, public bodies and other not-for-profit organisations exist to achieve a mission, not to make profit.
  10. Groups and control: A group is a parent company and the subsidiaries it controls.
  11. Goodwill on acquisition: When a parent buys a subsidiary, it usually pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets: the excess is goodwill.
  12. The consolidated statement of financial position: The consolidated statement of financial position adds together the parent's and subsidiaries' assets and liabilities and shows the equity attributable to the parent and to non-controlling interests.
  13. Intra-group trading and unrealised profit: When group companies trade with each other, the sales, purchases, balances and any profit left in inventory must be eliminated on consolidation.
  14. The consolidated statement of profit or loss: The consolidated statement of profit or loss combines the income and expenses of the parent and subsidiaries for the period.
  15. Associates and joint arrangements: Investments that give significant influence or joint control aren't consolidated line by line.
  16. Creative accounting and earnings management: Creative accounting uses the flexibility in accounting rules, and sometimes breaks them, to present a better picture than reality.
  17. Narrative reporting and the strategic report: Annual reports contain much more than the financial statements.

School 5: Management Accounting: Costing

19 lessons

Management Accounting: Costing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The role of management accounting: Management accounting provides information to help managers plan, control and make decisions.
  2. Cost classification: Costs can be grouped in several ways, depending on the purpose.
  3. Cost behaviour: Costs respond differently to changes in activity.
  4. The high-low method: Semi-variable costs must be split into fixed and variable elements for planning.
  5. Accounting for materials: Materials are often the largest cost in manufacturing.
  6. Accounting for labour: Labour costs must be measured, controlled and charged to products.
  7. Overhead allocation, apportionment and reapportionment: Before overheads can be charged to products, they must be shared out among cost centres.
  8. Absorption costing: Absorption costing charges production overheads to cost units using a predetermined rate.
  9. Marginal costing: Marginal costing charges only variable costs to products and treats fixed costs as period costs.
  10. Marginal and absorption costing compared: Marginal and absorption costing can report different profits when inventory levels change.
  11. Activity-based costing: Activity-based costing (ABC) assigns overheads to products according to the activities they consume.
  12. Job and batch costing: Job and batch costing are used where work is done to customer order or in distinct batches.
  13. Process costing: Process costing is used where identical units are produced continuously through a series of processes.
  14. Process losses and equivalent units: Process accounts must deal with losses that differ from expectations and with work still in progress at the period end.
  15. Service costing: Service organisations need cost information as much as manufacturers, but their output is intangible.
  16. Target costing: Target costing starts with the price customers will pay and works back to the cost a product must achieve.
  17. Life-cycle costing: Life-cycle costing tracks all the costs of a product or asset from development to disposal.
  18. Throughput accounting: Throughput accounting, based on the theory of constraints, focuses on maximising the rate at which a business earns money through its bottleneck.
  19. Environmental management accounting: Environmental management accounting (EMA) identifies and manages environmental costs that conventional systems often hide.

School 6: Management Accounting: Planning, Control and Decisions

20 lessons

Management Accounting: Planning, Control and Decisions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Relevant costing: Good decisions depend on the costs and revenues that change as a result of them.
  2. Cost–volume–profit analysis: Cost–volume–profit (CVP) analysis shows how profit changes with sales volume.
  3. Break-even charts and the margin of safety: Break-even charts show CVP relationships visually, and the margin of safety measures how far sales can fall before losses occur.
  4. Limiting factor analysis: When a resource is scarce, a business must decide how to use it to maximise profit.
  5. Make-or-buy and other short-term decisions: Many decisions compare the relevant costs of alternatives.
  6. Pricing decisions: Setting prices combines cost information with understanding of demand.
  7. Decisions under risk and uncertainty: Most business decisions involve uncertain outcomes.
  8. Budgeting: purposes and process: A budget is a quantified plan for a future period.
  9. Functional budgets: Functional budgets translate the sales forecast into plans for production, materials and labour.
  10. Cash budgets: A cash budget forecasts cash receipts and payments to ensure an organisation can pay its bills.
  11. Fixed and flexible budgets: A fixed budget is set for one level of activity; a flexible budget adjusts for actual activity.
  12. Approaches to budgeting: Organisations prepare budgets in different ways, each with strengths and weaknesses.
  13. Behavioural aspects of budgeting: Budgets affect how people behave as well as recording plans.
  14. Standard costing: Standard costing sets predetermined costs for each unit, providing a benchmark for control.
  15. Material and labour variances: Variance analysis splits the difference between standard and actual cost into causes.
  16. Overhead and sales variances: Overhead and sales variances complete the picture of performance against standard.
  17. The operating statement: The operating statement reconciles budgeted profit with actual profit using variances.
  18. Financial performance indicators: Financial performance indicators measure how well an organisation or division is doing.
  19. The balanced scorecard: The balanced scorecard measures performance from financial and non-financial perspectives linked to strategy.
  20. Divisional performance and transfer pricing: Large organisations are divided into divisions whose performance must be measured fairly.

School 7: Financial Management

23 lessons

Financial Management: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The financial management function: Financial management makes decisions about raising and using funds to create value.
  2. Financial objectives and stakeholders: Companies pursue financial objectives on behalf of shareholders, but managers and other stakeholders have their own interests.
  3. The financial environment: Companies raise and invest funds through financial markets and institutions.
  4. The time value of money: Money received today is worth more than the same amount later, because it can be invested.
  5. Accounting rate of return and payback: Accounting rate of return (ARR) and payback are simple appraisal methods still widely used.
  6. Net present value: Net present value (NPV) is the theoretically preferred method of investment appraisal.
  7. Internal rate of return: The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which a project's NPV is zero.
  8. Inflation and taxation in investment appraisal: Inflation and tax affect project cash flows and discount rates.
  9. Risk and uncertainty in investment appraisal: Investment cash flows are uncertain, so appraisal must consider risk.
  10. Capital rationing and asset replacement: When capital is limited, firms must choose the best combination of projects.
  11. Working capital and its financing: Working capital is the capital tied up in day-to-day operations.
  12. Managing inventory: Inventory management balances the costs of holding stock against the risks of running out.
  13. Managing receivables and payables: Credit given to customers and taken from suppliers has a major effect on cash.
  14. Managing cash: Cash management keeps enough cash to meet obligations while investing any surplus.
  15. Sources of finance: Businesses can raise finance from many sources, each with different costs and risks.
  16. Islamic finance: Islamic finance follows Sharia principles, notably the prohibition of interest.
  17. Dividend policy: Dividend policy decides how much of its profit a company pays to shareholders.
  18. The cost of equity: The cost of equity is the return shareholders require.
  19. The cost of debt and WACC: The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) combines the costs of equity and debt into one discount rate.
  20. Capital structure: Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity finance.
  21. Business valuation: Businesses are valued for takeovers, share issues and investment decisions.
  22. Foreign exchange risk: Companies trading internationally face risks from exchange rate movements.
  23. Interest rate risk: Changes in interest rates affect borrowing costs and the value of investments.

School 8: Audit, Assurance and Ethics

17 lessons

Audit, Assurance and Ethics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is an audit?: An external audit gives users independent assurance that financial statements are fairly presented.
  2. The regulation of audit: Audit in the UK is governed by law, standards and professional bodies.
  3. Corporate governance: Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled.
  4. Internal audit: Internal audit is an independent function within an organisation that evaluates risk management, control and governance.
  5. Professional ethics: Auditors must be, and be seen to be, independent and ethical.
  6. Accepting and planning an engagement: Before an audit starts, the firm must decide whether to accept the client and then plan the work.
  7. Risk assessment and materiality: A risk-based audit concentrates effort where misstatement is most likely and most important.
  8. Internal control: Internal controls help a business run reliably and prevent errors and fraud.
  9. Tests of controls: When auditors plan to rely on a client's controls, they must test that the controls work.
  10. Audit evidence and substantive procedures: The auditor's opinion rests on sufficient appropriate evidence.
  11. Audit sampling and data analytics: Auditors rarely test every item, so they sample, and increasingly they analyse whole populations with data analytics.
  12. Auditing inventory, receivables and cash: Some balances need specific procedures.
  13. Completion and review: At the end of an audit, the auditor draws the evidence together before signing.
  14. The auditor's report: The auditor's report communicates the opinion to shareholders.
  15. Fraud and error: Misstatements can arise from honest error or deliberate fraud.
  16. The audit expectation gap: The public often expects more from auditors than they deliver or are required to deliver.
  17. Audit failures and reform: High-profile corporate collapses have repeatedly led to audit reform.

School 9: Taxation

12 lessons

Taxation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The UK tax system: Tax pays for public services and shapes economic behaviour.
  2. Tax administration and self-assessment: Most individuals with untaxed income and all companies calculate and report their own tax under self-assessment.
  3. Tax avoidance and tax evasion: Tax evasion is illegal; tax avoidance uses the law in ways Parliament did not intend.
  4. The income tax computation: Income tax is charged on an individual's taxable income for a tax year.
  5. Employment income and benefits: Employees are taxed on their earnings, including most benefits in kind.
  6. Trading income: The profits of a trade carried on by a sole trader or partner are taxed as trading income.
  7. Capital allowances: Capital allowances give tax relief for spending on plant and machinery and some structures, in place of depreciation.
  8. National Insurance contributions: National Insurance contributions are charged on earnings and self-employed profits and build entitlement to some state benefits.
  9. Corporation tax: UK resident companies pay corporation tax on their worldwide taxable total profits.
  10. Capital gains tax: Individuals pay capital gains tax when they dispose of chargeable assets at a gain.
  11. Inheritance tax: Inheritance tax is charged on a person's estate at death and on some lifetime gifts.
  12. Value added tax: VAT is a tax on consumer spending collected by businesses at each stage of supply.

School 10: Business and Company Law

14 lessons

Business and Company Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The English legal system: Business law in England and Wales rests on legislation and case law applied through a hierarchy of courts.
  2. Contract formation: A contract is a legally binding agreement.
  3. Contract terms and breach: The terms of a contract set out each party's obligations.
  4. Contract remedies: When a contract is broken, the law provides remedies to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in.
  5. Negligence and professional liability: The tort of negligence imposes liability for careless acts and statements that cause loss.
  6. Employment law: Employment law governs the relationship between employers and employees.
  7. Agency and partnership law: Businesses act through agents, and many professional firms are partnerships.
  8. Forming a company: A registered company is a separate legal person distinct from its members.
  9. Share capital and capital maintenance: Share capital is the money members invest in a company, and the law protects it for creditors.
  10. Loan capital and charges: Companies borrow as well as issuing shares, and lenders often take security.
  11. Directors' duties: Directors manage the company and owe it legal duties.
  12. Company meetings and resolutions: Members make key decisions at general meetings or by written resolution.
  13. Insolvency: When a company cannot pay its debts, insolvency law decides whether it can be rescued and how its assets are shared.
  14. Fraudulent behaviour: insider dealing, money laundering and bribery: Some business misconduct is criminal.

School 11: Finance and Financial Markets

14 lessons

Finance and Financial Markets: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The financial system: The financial system moves money from those who save to those who want to borrow and invest.
  2. Banks and money markets: Banks are at the centre of the financial system and depend on short-term money markets.
  3. Bonds and bond valuation: Bonds are a major way for governments and firms to borrow.
  4. Equities and stock markets: Shares give investors ownership of companies and a claim on their profits.
  5. Derivatives: Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset.
  6. Risk and return: Investors expect higher returns for taking more risk.
  7. Portfolio theory and diversification: Combining investments can reduce risk without reducing expected return.
  8. The capital asset pricing model: The capital asset pricing model links an investment's required return to its market risk.
  9. Market efficiency: If markets are efficient, prices already reflect available information and it is hard to beat the market.
  10. Behavioural finance: Behavioural finance studies how psychology affects investors and markets.
  11. Option pricing: Options have value because they give rights without obligations.
  12. Financial regulation: Financial regulation aims to keep banks safe, markets fair and consumers protected.
  13. Financial crises: Financial crises recur throughout history and can cause deep recessions.
  14. Fintech and digital finance: Technology is reshaping financial markets and services.

School 12: Accounting, Technology and Society

12 lessons

Accounting, Technology and Society: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Accounting information systems: An accounting information system collects, stores and processes transaction data and turns it into information for decisions and reports.
  2. Data analytics in accounting: Accountants increasingly analyse whole populations of data rather than samples.
  3. Automation, AI and the accountant: Robotic process automation and artificial intelligence are changing what accountants do.
  4. Cyber security and data protection: Finance teams hold valuable data and control payments, so they are prime targets for cyber attack.
  5. The history of accounting: Accounting has developed over thousands of years in response to trade, industry and regulation.
  6. Accounting theory: Accounting theory tries to explain and guide accounting practice.
  7. Critical perspectives on accounting: Critical accounting research argues that accounting is not a neutral technical activity but shapes organisations and society.
  8. Public sector accounting: Government bodies account for taxpayers' money under rules adapted from IFRS.
  9. Charity accounting: Charities must show donors and regulators how funds have been raised and spent.
  10. Sustainability and integrated reporting: Investors and regulators now expect companies to report on climate and other sustainability matters.
  11. Ethics and the public interest: Accountants have a duty to act in the public interest, not only for their clients or employers.
  12. The accountancy profession and its qualifications: The UK has several professional accountancy bodies with different qualifications and specialisms.
