Professional Skills: a free course on ProGradify

ProGradify's free Professional Skills course runs from Professional Communication to Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing: 169 lessons in 10 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for working professionals.

General guidance, not legal or HR advice. ProGradify teaches professional skills for study and self-development. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against official sources. It is not a CMI, CIPD, APM or PRINCE2 qualification and is not endorsed by those bodies. Lessons on employment rights and equality describe the law of Great Britain in general terms only: for a problem at work, read your employer's policies and contact ACAS (0300 123 1100), Citizens Advice, a trade union or a qualified employment adviser or solicitor. If work is affecting your health, speak to your GP.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Professional Communication

14 lessons

Professional Communication: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Communication at work: purpose and audience: Every message at work exists to make something happen: a decision, an action, a change in understanding.
  2. Active listening: Listening is the communication skill people use most and are taught least.
  3. Questioning skills: Good questions uncover facts, test assumptions and help people think.
  4. Non-verbal communication: Tone of voice, facial expression, posture, gesture and timing all carry meaning alongside words.
  5. Plain English and clear writing: Clear writing gets read, understood and acted on.
  6. Structuring reports and briefings: A report or briefing exists to support a decision.
  7. Writing effective emails: Email remains the main written channel at work, and most professionals receive more than they can read carefully.
  8. Tone, etiquette and choosing the channel: The same message can land well or badly depending on its tone and the channel it travels through.
  9. Running effective meetings: Meetings take a large share of professional time and are often judged a poor use of it.
  10. Minutes, decisions and action points: The record of a meeting is what turns talk into action and accountability.
  11. Planning a presentation: Most presentations succeed or fail before they begin, in how clearly the message is defined and structured.
  12. Delivering a presentation: Delivery turns a well-planned talk into one people remember.
  13. Communicating in virtual and hybrid teams: Many teams now work partly or fully remotely.
  14. Giving and receiving feedback: Feedback helps people learn, but badly given feedback can reduce performance.

School 2: Social Psychology

26 lessons

Social Psychology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Social cognition: Social cognition is how people think about themselves, other people and social situations.
  2. Social perception and impressions: People form impressions of others quickly from small amounts of information.
  3. Attribution: Attribution is how people explain the causes of behaviour.
  4. The fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias: Attributions are often biased in predictable ways.
  5. Attitude formation: An attitude is an evaluation of a person, object or idea.
  6. Attitude change and persuasion: Persuasion is deliberately changing attitudes through communication.
  7. Cognitive dissonance: Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort felt when beliefs and actions conflict.
  8. Conformity: Conformity is changing behaviour or beliefs to match a group.
  9. Obedience: Obedience is following orders from an authority figure.
  10. Compliance: Compliance is going along with a direct request.
  11. Social norms: Social norms are shared expectations about how people behave.
  12. Group dynamics: Group dynamics are the processes that operate within groups.
  13. Group decision-making: Groups often make decisions together, sometimes better and sometimes worse than individuals.
  14. Leadership: Leadership is influencing a group towards shared goals.
  15. Power: Power is the capacity to influence others and control resources.
  16. Social identity theory: Social identity theory explains how group memberships become part of who we are and shape intergroup behaviour.
  17. Self-categorisation: Self-categorisation theory explains when people see themselves as individuals and when as group members.
  18. In-groups and out-groups: An in-group is a group we belong to; an out-group is one we don't.
  19. Prejudice, stereotyping and discrimination: Stereotypes are beliefs about groups, prejudice is an attitude towards them, and discrimination is unfair behaviour.
  20. Intergroup relations and conflict: Intergroup conflict arises between groups competing for resources, status or identity.
  21. Bystander behaviour: Bystander behaviour is how people respond when they witness an emergency.
  22. Prosocial behaviour and altruism: Prosocial behaviour is any action that benefits others; altruism is helping without expectation of reward.
  23. Aggression: Aggression is behaviour intended to harm someone who wants to avoid it.
  24. Attraction: Interpersonal attraction is the liking that draws people together.
  25. Close relationships: Close relationships include friendships, romantic partnerships and family bonds.
  26. Culture and social behaviour: Culture shapes how people think, feel and behave.

School 3: Management and Organisational Behaviour

18 lessons

Management and Organisational Behaviour: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is management?: Management is getting things done through other people and through the organisation's resources.
  2. Classical management theory: Taylor and Fayol: Classical management theory, developed around 1900–1920, tried to find the one best way to run work and organisations.
  3. Mintzberg's managerial roles: Henry Mintzberg watched what managers actually did and found work very different from the tidy functions of classical theory.
  4. The human relations movement: The human relations movement shifted attention from tasks to people.
  5. Organisational structure: Structure is how an organisation divides, groups and co-ordinates its work.
  6. Centralisation and decentralisation: Centralisation and decentralisation describe where decisions are made.
  7. Bureaucracy and Weber: Max Weber described bureaucracy as the most rational and efficient form of organisation for large-scale administration.
  8. Contingency theory and organisational design: Contingency theory says there is no one best way to organise: the right structure depends on the situation.
  9. Motivation: content theories: Content theories of motivation ask what needs drive people at work.
  10. Motivation: process theories: Process theories explain how people decide how much effort to put in.
  11. Leadership theories: Leadership theories try to explain what makes a leader effective.
  12. Leadership styles: A leadership style is a typical way a leader makes decisions and treats followers.
  13. Teams and group working: Much work is done in groups and teams.
  14. Communication in organisations: Communication is how information and meaning pass between people in an organisation.
  15. Organisational culture: Organisational culture is "the way we do things around here".
  16. Power and politics in organisations: Power is the capacity to influence others, and organisational politics is the use of power to pursue interests.
  17. Managing change: Organisations must change to survive, but change often fails.
  18. Decision-making in organisations: Managers make decisions under uncertainty and with limited time and information.

School 4: Project Management

17 lessons

Project Management: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What a project is: Projects are temporary efforts to create something new, unlike the routine work of running an organisation.
  2. Project life cycles: Projects move through phases from idea to closure.
  3. Project roles and governance: Projects need clear roles and decision-making structures.
  4. The business case: The business case explains why a project is worth doing.
  5. Scope and requirements: Scope defines what a project will and will not deliver.
  6. The work breakdown structure: A work breakdown structure breaks a project into manageable pieces.
  7. Scheduling and the critical path: A schedule shows when work will happen.
  8. Estimating and budgeting: Projects need realistic estimates of time and cost.
  9. Risk management: Risks are uncertain events that could affect a project.
  10. Stakeholders and communication in projects: Projects succeed or fail through people.
  11. PRINCE2 principles: PRINCE2 is a widely used project management method, especially in the UK.
  12. Agile and the Agile Manifesto: Agile approaches deliver value in small increments and respond to change.
  13. Scrum: Scrum is the most widely used agile framework.
  14. Kanban: Kanban is a method for visualising and improving the flow of work.
  15. Choosing a delivery approach: No single approach suits every project.
  16. Monitoring and controlling progress: Projects rarely go exactly to plan.
  17. Closing a project and learning lessons: Closing a project properly is as important as starting it well.

School 5: Productivity and Personal Effectiveness

12 lessons

Productivity and Personal Effectiveness: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Setting goals: SMART objectives and OKRs: Clear goals direct effort and make progress visible.
  2. Prioritisation: urgency and importance: Not all tasks deserve equal attention.
  3. Planning your week: A short weekly plan connects big goals to daily action.
  4. Focus, interruptions and deep work: Knowledge work depends on sustained concentration.
  5. Procrastination: Procrastination is common and costly but is not simply laziness.
  6. Managing email and information overload: Email and messaging can swallow the working day.
  7. Task management systems: A trusted system for tracking tasks frees the mind to focus.
  8. Saying no and managing expectations: Taking on everything leads to overload and missed commitments.
  9. Working remotely and flexibly: Remote, hybrid and flexible working are now common in the UK.
  10. How adults learn: Continuing to learn is part of professional life.
  11. Reflective practice: Reflecting on experience turns everyday work into learning.
  12. Learning from mistakes: Mistakes are inevitable, but organisations differ in whether they learn from them.

School 6: Strategy

16 lessons

Strategy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is strategy?: Strategy is the long-term direction of an organisation: the choices about where it competes and how it will win.
  2. Mission, vision and objectives: Mission and vision statements express why an organisation exists and what it hopes to become, and strategic objectives turn them into targets.
  3. Planned and emergent strategy: Some strategies are deliberately planned; others emerge from patterns of action.
  4. Porter's five forces: Porter's five forces framework analyses the competitive structure of an industry to judge how attractive it is.
  5. The industry life cycle: Industries, like products, pass through stages of introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
  6. SWOT analysis: SWOT analysis summarises an organisation's internal strengths and weaknesses and the external opportunities and threats it faces.
  7. Resources and capabilities: The resource-based view explains competitive advantage by what a firm has and can do, not just by the industry it is in.
  8. The value chain: Porter's value chain breaks a firm's activities into primary and support activities to show where value and cost are created.
  9. Core competences and the VRIO framework: Not every strength gives lasting advantage.
  10. Porter's generic strategies: Porter argued that a firm can achieve competitive advantage through lower cost or differentiation, across a broad market or a narrow focus.
  11. The Ansoff matrix: The Ansoff matrix sets out four directions for growth based on whether products and markets are existing or new.
  12. The Boston Matrix and portfolio analysis: Portfolio analysis helps a firm decide how to allocate resources between its products or business units.
  13. Mergers, acquisitions and alliances: Firms can grow organically or by combining with others through mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures and strategic alliances.
  14. Blue ocean strategy: Blue ocean strategy argues that firms can create uncontested market space rather than competing head-on.
  15. Strategy implementation: A strategy only creates value once it is put into practice.
  16. The balanced scorecard: The balanced scorecard measures performance from four perspectives, not just financial results.

School 7: Data and Digital Skills at Work

14 lessons

Data and Digital Skills at Work: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Data literacy: Almost every job now involves reading, using or questioning data.
  2. Spreadsheet essentials: Spreadsheets are the most widely used data tool at work.
  3. Descriptive statistics for work: A few simple statistics summarise most workplace data.
  4. Charts and data visualisation: The right chart makes a pattern obvious; the wrong one hides or distorts it.
  5. Telling a story with data: Data persuades when it is part of a clear message.
  6. Spotting misleading statistics: Statistics can mislead by accident or by design.
  7. Digital collaboration tools: Teams now share files, chat and meet through digital platforms.
  8. Information management and version control: Finding the right, current file wastes a surprising amount of working time.
  9. Cyber security at work: Most cyber attacks on organisations exploit everyday behaviour.
  10. Data protection: UK GDPR basics: Almost every employee handles personal data.
  11. Artificial intelligence at work: Generative AI tools are changing how many jobs are done.
  12. Digital accessibility: Digital content must work for everyone, including disabled people.
  13. Your online professional presence: What people find about you online shapes how colleagues, clients and employers see you.
  14. Digital wellbeing: Constant connection can blur work and home and wear people down.

School 8: The Business Environment

16 lessons

The Business Environment: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is a business?: A business is an organisation that combines resources to produce goods or services that meet customers' needs, usually in exchange for payment.
  2. Business purpose and objectives: Every organisation needs to know what it is for and what it is trying to achieve.
  3. Stakeholders: Stakeholders are the individuals and groups who affect or are affected by a business.
  4. The private, public and third sectors: The UK economy is organised into three broad sectors by who owns organisations and why they exist: the private sector, the public sector and the third (voluntary and community) sector.
  5. Sole traders and partnerships: Most UK businesses are run by one person or a small group without a separate legal identity.
  6. Private and public limited companies: A company is a separate legal person, owned by shareholders and run by directors, whose owners have limited liability.
  7. Social enterprises and co-operatives: Not every business is owned by investors seeking returns.
  8. Franchising: Franchising lets a business grow by licensing others to run outlets under its brand and system.
  9. PESTLE analysis: Businesses operate in an external environment they can't control but must anticipate.
  10. Markets and competition: A market is where buyers and sellers exchange goods or services.
  11. The economic environment: Every business is affected by the state of the wider economy.
  12. The legal and regulatory environment: Law sets the rules within which businesses compete.
  13. Technology and social change: Technological and social change create new markets and destroy old ones.
  14. Globalisation and international business: Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, technology and people.
  15. Business size and growth: Businesses range from one-person enterprises to global corporations.
  16. Why businesses fail: Many new businesses close within a few years, and even large, famous firms collapse.

School 9: Ethics, Professionalism and Employment Rights

16 lessons

Ethics, Professionalism and Employment Rights: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What professionalism means: Professionalism is about how you do your job, not just what job you do.
  2. Professional codes of conduct: Codes of conduct turn values into expected behaviour.
  3. Ethical decision making: Ethical dilemmas at work rarely come labelled.
  4. Integrity and conflicts of interest: A conflict of interest can undermine trust even when nothing goes wrong.
  5. Whistleblowing: Whistleblowers expose wrongdoing that might otherwise stay hidden.
  6. The Equality Act 2010: The Equality Act 2010 is the main law protecting people from discrimination in Great Britain.
  7. Discrimination, harassment and victimisation: The Equality Act defines several forms of prohibited conduct.
  8. Reasonable adjustments: Employers must remove barriers that disabled people face at work.
  9. Inclusion and unconscious bias: Legal compliance is a minimum; inclusion is about everyone being able to contribute fully.
  10. Employment status: Your rights at work depend on your legal employment status.
  11. The contract of employment: The contract of employment sets out the rights and duties of employer and employee.
  12. Working time, pay and holiday: Core rights on hours, pay and holiday apply to almost all workers.
  13. Family-friendly rights: UK law gives working parents and carers rights to leave and pay.
  14. Discipline and grievance: the ACAS Code: Problems at work should be handled fairly and consistently.
  15. Dismissal and redundancy basics: Ending employment fairly protects both employees and employers.
  16. Getting help: ACAS and advice services: Knowing where to get help early can resolve problems before they escalate.

School 10: Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

20 lessons

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation: Motivation is what energises and directs behaviour.
  2. Drive theories: Drive theories explain motivation as a push to reduce internal tension caused by unmet needs.
  3. Incentive theories: Incentive theories explain motivation by the pull of external goals and rewards.
  4. Self-determination theory: Self-determination theory explains how social environments support or undermine motivation and wellbeing through three basic psychological needs.
  5. Goal-setting theory: Goal-setting theory explains how specific, challenging goals improve performance.
  6. Achievement motivation in context: Achievement motivation is shaped by context: classrooms, cultures, stereotypes and expectations.
  7. Theories of emotion: James–Lange and Cannon–Bard: Early theories of emotion asked whether bodily changes cause emotional feelings or occur alongside them.
  8. The two-factor theory: Schachter–Singer: Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory proposes that emotion depends on physiological arousal plus a cognitive label.
  9. Emotion regulation: Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them.
  10. Stress: Stress arises when demands are perceived to exceed resources.
  11. The physiology of stress: Stress triggers bodily responses that prepare for action.
  12. Coping: Coping is how people manage stressful demands.
  13. Resilience: Resilience is the ability to adapt well in the face of adversity.
  14. Subjective wellbeing: Subjective wellbeing is how people evaluate and experience their own lives.
  15. Happiness: What makes people happy?
  16. Positive psychology in practice: Positive psychology studies what helps people thrive and develops interventions to increase wellbeing.
  17. Flow: Flow is a state of deep absorption in a challenging activity.
  18. Gratitude: Gratitude is noticing and appreciating the good things in life, including what others do for us.
  19. Mindfulness research: Mindfulness means paying attention to present-moment experience without judgement.
  20. Flourishing: Flourishing means living well: feeling good and functioning well.
