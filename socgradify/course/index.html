Free Sociology course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

SocGradify's free Sociology course runs from Foundations of Sociology to Social Statistics and Data Science: 438 lessons in 18 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for social science students.

Study material, not professional advice. SocGradify explains sociological theories, evidence and debates. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors. Lessons separate empirical findings from theoretical interpretations and from normative arguments, and statistics carry their source and year: check the latest release before relying on a figure.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Sociology

19 lessons

Foundations of Sociology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is sociology?: Sociology is the systematic study of social life: how societies are organised, how they change and how they shape, and are shaped by, the people in them.
  2. Sociology as a discipline: Sociology is an academic discipline with its own concepts, theories, methods and professional bodies.
  3. The sociological imagination: C. Wright Mills called the ability to connect personal experience to wider social forces "the sociological imagination".
  4. Micro and macro sociology: Sociologists study social life at different scales: micro sociology studies face-to-face interaction; macro sociology studies large-scale structures and whole societies.
  5. The individual and society: Is society just a collection of individuals, or something more?
  6. Structure and agency: How far are our lives shaped by social structures, and how far by our own choices?
  7. Social institutions: Institutions are the established patterns of social life that organise how societies meet their needs: the family, education, religion, the economy, politics and law, health and the media.
  8. Social relationships: Social life is made of relationships: between friends, family members, colleagues, strangers and citizens.
  9. Norms and values: Values are shared ideas about what is good and desirable; norms are the expected behaviours that follow from them.
  10. Culture: Culture is the shared way of life of a group: its values, norms, beliefs, language, knowledge and material objects.
  11. Primary socialisation: Primary socialisation is the first and most intensive stage of learning the culture of a society, usually within the family.
  12. Secondary socialisation: Secondary socialisation continues throughout life as people learn the norms of wider society through school, peer groups, the media, religion and work.
  13. Roles and status: Status is a social position; a role is the behaviour expected of someone in that position.
  14. Identity: Identity is our sense of who we are and how others see us.
  15. Groups and communities: Much of social life happens in groups: families, friendship circles, teams, organisations and communities.
  16. Social interaction: Social interaction is how people act towards and respond to one another.
  17. Sociological research: an introduction: Sociologists study society using a range of methods, from large surveys to long-term participant observation.
  18. Contemporary sociology: Contemporary sociology studies a world reshaped by globalisation, digital technology, climate change, migration and changing inequalities.
  19. Applying sociology: Sociology is used in policy, public services, business, campaigning and everyday citizenship.

School 2: Classical Sociological Theory

23 lessons

Classical Sociological Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The origins of sociology: Sociology emerged in 19th-century Europe as thinkers tried to understand the upheavals of revolution, industrialisation and urbanisation.
  2. The Enlightenment and social thought: The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement that championed reason, science and progress.
  3. Industrialisation and social change: Industrialisation transformed work, family, cities and class.
  4. Comte and positivism: Auguste Comte named sociology and argued it should be a positive science, discovering laws of society as natural sciences discover laws of nature.
  5. Marx: historical materialism: Historical materialism is Marx's theory that the way a society produces its material needs shapes its social relations, politics and ideas, and that history moves through conflict between classes.
  6. Marx: class conflict: Marx argued that the history of all societies so far has been the history of class struggle.
  7. Marx: capitalism: Marx's analysis of capitalism explains profit as the result of exploitation, through the concept of surplus value, and argues that capitalism is dynamic but prone to crisis.
  8. Marx: alienation: Alienation is Marx's term for the way capitalism estranges workers from their work, their products, their own humanity and each other.
  9. Engels: Friedrich Engels was Marx's closest collaborator and an important social researcher in his own right.
  10. Durkheim: social facts: Durkheim argued that sociology has its own subject matter, social facts, which must be studied as things and explained by other social facts.
  11. Durkheim: social solidarity: Durkheim's central question was what holds society together.
  12. Mechanical and organic solidarity: Durkheim distinguished two types of solidarity: mechanical, based on likeness in simple societies, and organic, based on interdependence in complex ones.
  13. Durkheim: anomie: Anomie is Durkheim's term for a condition of normlessness, in which rapid change weakens the rules that regulate people's desires.
  14. Durkheim's Suicide: In Suicide (1897), Durkheim set out to show that even the most personal act has social causes, by analysing official suicide statistics across groups and countries.
  15. Weber: social action: Max Weber defined sociology as the study of social action: behaviour to which individuals attach meaning and which takes account of others.
  16. Weber: Verstehen: Verstehen, German for "understanding", is Weber's term for grasping the meaning of social action from the actor's point of view.
  17. Weber: rationalisation: Rationalisation is Weber's term for the long-term process by which calculation, efficiency and rules replace tradition, emotion and magic in social life.
  18. Weber: bureaucracy: Weber saw bureaucracy as the most technically efficient form of organisation and the characteristic institution of modern rational society.
  19. Weber: class, status and party: Weber argued that inequality has three distinct dimensions: class (economic position), status (social honour) and party (political power).
  20. Simmel: Georg Simmel studied the forms of social interaction that recur across different settings, from the significance of group size to the experience of city life and money.
  21. W. E. B. Du Bois: W. E. B. Du Bois was a pioneering American sociologist who studied race, inequality and Black urban life with rigorous empirical methods.
  22. Harriet Martineau: Harriet Martineau was a British writer and social theorist who wrote one of the first books on sociological method, translated Comte and analysed slavery, marriage and women's position.
  23. Classical debates compared: The founders disagreed about what holds society together, what drives change, how society should be studied and whether modernity is progress.

School 3: Contemporary Sociological Theory

24 lessons

Contemporary Sociological Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Functionalism: Functionalism sees society as a system of interrelated parts, each contributing to the stability and survival of the whole.
  2. Parsons: Talcott Parsons built the most ambitious functionalist theory of society, the social system.
  3. Merton: manifest and latent functions: Robert Merton revised functionalism into a more flexible, testable approach.
  4. Conflict theory: Conflict theory sees society as shaped by inequality and struggle between groups with different interests and power.
  5. Neo-Marxism: Neo-Marxists revised Marx to explain why revolution didn't happen in advanced capitalist societies, giving more weight to culture, ideology and the state.
  6. Critical theory and the Frankfurt School: Critical theory, developed by the Frankfurt School, combined Marx, Weber and Freud to criticise modern capitalist society, its culture and its forms of reason.
  7. Symbolic interactionism: Mead and Blumer: Symbolic interactionism explains society as built from interaction, in which people create and act on shared meanings.
  8. Goffman and dramaturgy: Erving Goffman studied the fine detail of everyday interaction, from self-presentation to stigma and total institutions.
  9. Ethnomethodology: Ethnomethodology studies the methods people use to make sense of everyday life and produce social order moment by moment.
  10. Phenomenology: Phenomenological sociology studies how people experience and understand the everyday world, using shared, taken-for-granted categories.
  11. Social constructionism: Social constructionism argues that much of what we treat as natural or objective, from gender to mental illness, is created through social processes.
  12. Liberal feminism: Liberal feminism seeks equal rights and opportunities for women and men through legal and policy reform, education and changing attitudes.
  13. Radical feminism: Radical feminism argues that patriarchy, men's domination of women, is the most fundamental form of inequality, rooted in family, sexuality and violence as well as public life.
  14. Marxist feminism: Marxist feminism links women's oppression to capitalism: women's unpaid domestic labour and position in the labour market serve capitalist interests.
  15. Black feminism: Black feminism analyses how racism, sexism and class oppression combine in Black women's lives, and criticises feminisms that treat white middle-class women's experience as universal.
  16. Intersectionality: Intersectionality analyses how categories such as race, gender, class, sexuality and disability combine to shape distinctive experiences of privilege and disadvantage.
  17. Postmodernism: Postmodernism argues that contemporary societies have moved beyond modernity: grand theories have lost credibility, identities are fluid and media images blur with reality.
  18. Poststructuralism: Poststructuralism holds that meaning is unstable and produced through language and discourse, and that subjects are shaped by the discourses that describe them.
  19. Foucault: Michel Foucault analysed how power operates through knowledge, institutions and everyday practices, shaping how people are seen and how they see themselves.
  20. Bourdieu: habitus, capital and field: Pierre Bourdieu explained how social inequality is reproduced through taste, education and everyday dispositions, using the concepts of habitus, capital and field.
  21. Giddens and structuration theory: Anthony Giddens' structuration theory argues that structure and agency are not opposites but two sides of the same process.
  22. Bauman: Zygmunt Bauman analysed how modern society has become "liquid": relationships, identities and institutions are fluid, temporary and uncertain.
  23. Beck: Ulrich Beck argued that late modern societies are "risk societies", organised around manufactured risks produced by modernisation itself.
  24. Theoretical synthesis: Contemporary sociologists often try to combine insights from different theories rather than choose one.

School 4: Culture, Identity and Socialisation

24 lessons

Culture, Identity and Socialisation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Material and non-material culture: Culture is the shared way of life of a group, including both objects and ideas.
  2. Values: Values are shared ideas about what is good, right and desirable.
  3. Norms and sanctions: Norms are shared rules for behaviour, and sanctions are the rewards and punishments that enforce them.
  4. Subcultures: A subculture is a group with distinctive values and norms within a wider culture.
  5. Countercultures: A counterculture rejects key values and norms of the dominant culture and offers an alternative.
  6. Cultural transmission: Cultural transmission is how culture is passed between people and across generations.
  7. The family as an agent of socialisation: The family is usually the first and most influential agent of socialisation.
  8. Education as an agent of socialisation: Schools teach not only subjects but also values, norms and ways of behaving.
  9. Peer groups: Peer groups are people of similar age and status.
  10. The media: The media is a powerful agent of socialisation, shaping knowledge, norms and identities.
  11. Religion as an agent of socialisation: Religion teaches beliefs, values and moral codes and shapes identity and community.
  12. Personal and social identity: Identity is how people see themselves and how others see them.
  13. National identity: National identity is a sense of belonging to a nation.
  14. Ethnic identity: Ethnic identity is a sense of belonging to a group sharing culture, ancestry or history.
  15. Gender identity: Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender.
  16. Class identity: Class identity is how people see their own social class and how class shapes their sense of self.
  17. Cultural identity: Cultural identity is a sense of belonging based on shared culture, such as language, traditions, religion or lifestyle.
  18. Hybrid identities: Hybrid identities combine elements from different cultures into new forms.
  19. Globalised identities: Globalisation connects people across the world and creates new sources of identity.
  20. Consumer culture: Consumer culture is a culture in which buying and using goods is central to identity and social life.
  21. Popular culture: Popular culture is the culture enjoyed by large numbers of people, such as music, television, sport and social media.
  22. Cultural change: Cultures change through technology, migration, social movements and economic change.
  23. Cultural diversity: Cultural diversity is the existence of many cultures within a society.
  24. Multiculturalism: Multiculturalism refers both to cultural diversity and to policies that recognise and support it.

School 5: Social Stratification and Inequality

26 lessons

Social Stratification and Inequality: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Social stratification: Social stratification is the division of a society into layers with unequal access to resources, power and prestige.
  2. Social hierarchy: Societies have organised hierarchy in different ways: slavery, caste, estates and class.
  3. Marxist theories of class: Marx argued class is defined by relationship to the means of production and that class conflict drives history.
  4. Weberian theories of class: Weber saw stratification as having three dimensions: class, status and party.
  5. Contemporary class analysis: Contemporary class analysis asks how class should be measured today.
  6. Social mobility: Social mobility is movement between positions in a stratification system.
  7. Absolute and relative mobility: Absolute mobility is how many people move; relative mobility is how unequal people's chances are.
  8. Intergenerational and intragenerational mobility: Intergenerational mobility compares children with parents; intragenerational mobility follows changes within one person's career.
  9. Meritocracy: A meritocracy rewards people according to ability and effort rather than background.
  10. Life chances: Life chances are the opportunities a person has to improve their quality of life.
  11. Wealth inequality: Wealth is the stock of assets a household owns, minus its debts.
  12. Income inequality: Income inequality is how unevenly income is shared across a population.
  13. Absolute poverty: Absolute poverty is lacking the resources to meet basic physical needs.
  14. Relative poverty: Relative poverty is having too little to take part in the normal life of one's society.
  15. Social exclusion: Social exclusion is being shut out from the social, economic and political life of society.
  16. Deprivation: Deprivation is lacking things that are widely regarded as necessities.
  17. Status: Status is social honour or prestige.
  18. Occupational inequality: Occupational inequality is unequal access to jobs, pay and progression.
  19. Housing inequality: Housing inequality is unequal access to secure, affordable and decent homes.
  20. Health inequality: Health inequalities are systematic differences in health between social groups.
  21. Educational inequality: Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education.
  22. Digital inequality: Digital inequality is unequal access to and use of digital technologies.
  23. Regional inequality: Regional inequality is unequal economic and social conditions between parts of a country.
  24. Global inequality: Global inequality is inequality between countries and between all the world's people.
  25. Social reproduction: Social reproduction is how inequality is passed from one generation to the next.
  26. Welfare and inequality: Welfare states redistribute resources and protect people against risks.

School 6: Gender and Sexuality

24 lessons

Gender and Sexuality: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Sex and gender: Sociologists distinguish sex, usually referring to biological characteristics, from gender, the social meanings attached to being a woman or a man.
  2. The social construction of gender: Social constructionists argue that gender is produced through social processes rather than simply expressing biology.
  3. Gender socialisation: Gender socialisation is the process through which people learn gender norms and roles.
  4. Gender roles: Gender roles are the behaviours and responsibilities expected of women and men.
  5. Gender stereotypes: Gender stereotypes are generalised beliefs about the characteristics of women and men.
  6. Gender identity in society: Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender.
  7. Gender performativity: Butler: Judith Butler argued that gender is performative: it is produced through repeated acts rather than expressing an inner essence.
  8. Masculinities: Sociologists study masculinities: the different ways of being a man.
  9. Hegemonic masculinity: Connell's concept of hegemonic masculinity explains how one form of masculinity becomes dominant and legitimates men's power.
  10. Feminist theory: Feminist theory explains gender inequality and how to change it.
  11. Patriarchy: Patriarchy is a system of social structures and practices in which men dominate women.
  12. Gender inequality: Gender inequality is unequal access to resources, power and opportunities based on gender.
  13. Gender and work: Work is a central site of gender inequality, from pay and segregation to harassment and care.
  14. Gender and education: Gender shapes educational achievement, subject choices and experiences.
  15. Gender and the family: Families are central sites where gender roles, power and care are organised.
  16. Gender and politics: Gender shapes who holds political power, how political issues are defined and how people participate.
  17. Gender and the media: Media representations shape and reflect ideas about gender.
  18. Sexuality as a social phenomenon: Sociologists study sexuality not only as biology but as something shaped by society, culture, law and history.
  19. Sexual identities: Sexual identity is how people understand and describe their own sexuality.
  20. Heteronormativity: Heteronormativity is the assumption that heterosexuality is normal and natural, and the social arrangements built on it.
  21. LGBTQ+ sociology: LGBTQ+ sociology studies the lives, communities, movements and inequalities of lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans and queer people.
  22. Gender and intersectionality: Intersectionality examines how gender combines with race, class, sexuality, disability and other social divisions.
  23. Gender-based violence: Gender-based violence is violence directed at people because of their gender, or that affects one gender disproportionately.
  24. Current debates in gender and sexuality: Gender and sexuality remain areas of active social and political debate.

School 7: Race, Ethnicity and Migration

27 lessons

Race, Ethnicity and Migration: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Race as a social construct: Sociologists treat race as a social construct: a way of classifying people that has no firm biological basis but has powerful social consequences.
  2. Ethnicity: Ethnicity refers to shared cultural identity based on ancestry, language, religion or traditions.
  3. Racialisation: Racialisation is the process by which groups come to be defined and treated in racial terms.
  4. Racism: Racism is prejudice, discrimination or structural disadvantage based on perceived race or ethnicity.
  5. Institutional racism: Institutional racism is racism built into the practices and cultures of organisations.
  6. Structural racism: Structural racism refers to the way racial inequalities are produced and reinforced across interconnected institutions over time.
  7. Prejudice and discrimination: Prejudice is a negative attitude towards a group; discrimination is unfair treatment.
  8. Ethnic identity and belonging: Ethnic identity shapes people's sense of belonging, and belonging can be affirmed or denied by others.
  9. Minority groups: In sociology, a minority group is defined by disadvantage and subordination rather than simply by numbers.
  10. Colonialism: Colonialism is the domination of one people or territory by another, involving political control, economic exploitation and cultural imposition.
  11. Postcolonial theory: Postcolonial theory examines how colonialism shaped knowledge, identity and culture, and how its effects continue after formal independence.
  12. Decolonisation: Decolonisation refers both to the political end of colonial rule and to efforts to challenge colonial legacies in knowledge, culture and institutions.
  13. International migration: International migration is the movement of people across national borders to live.
  14. Forced migration: Forced migration is movement caused by conflict, persecution, disaster or development projects.
  15. Refugees and asylum: Refugees are protected under international law.
  16. Integration and assimilation: Integration and assimilation describe how migrants and their descendants become part of a receiving society.
  17. Multiculturalism and migration: Migration has made societies more diverse, and governments have responded with different policies.
  18. Diaspora: A diaspora is a community dispersed from an original homeland that maintains a collective identity and links with it.
  19. Transnationalism: Transnationalism describes migrants' ongoing connections across national borders.
  20. Borders: Borders are the lines and practices that control movement between states.
  21. Citizenship: Citizenship is legal membership of a state, with rights and duties, and a sense of belonging.
  22. Race and inequality: Ethnic inequalities appear across income, wealth, housing, education, work, health and justice.
  23. Race and education: Educational outcomes differ widely between ethnic groups.
  24. Race and employment: Ethnic minorities in Britain face different employment rates, occupations and pay.
  25. Race and criminal justice: Ethnic minorities, especially Black people, are over-represented at several stages of the criminal justice system in England and Wales.
  26. Race and health: Health outcomes differ between ethnic groups in Britain.
  27. Debates about migration: Migration is one of the most debated issues in British politics.

School 8: Family and Relationships

21 lessons

Family and Relationships: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is a family?: Defining the family is harder than it seems.
  2. Family structures: Family structure refers to the composition of families: who lives together and how they are related.
  3. Nuclear and extended families: The nuclear family is parents and their children; the extended family includes wider kin.
  4. Lone-parent families: Lone-parent families are headed by one parent living with at least one child.
  5. Blended families: Blended families, or step-families, form when partners bring children from previous relationships.
  6. Same-sex families: Same-sex families are headed by same-sex couples, with or without children.
  7. Marriage: Marriage is a legally and socially recognised union.
  8. Cohabitation: Cohabitation is an unmarried couple living together.
  9. Divorce: Divorce is the legal ending of a marriage.
  10. Fertility and family size: Fertility refers to the number of children born.
  11. Parenting: Parenting is shaped by class, culture, gender and policy.
  12. Childhood as a social construction: Sociologists argue childhood is socially constructed: what it means to be a child varies across time and cultures.
  13. Domestic labour: Domestic labour is the unpaid work of running a home and caring for its members.
  14. Power in the family: Power in families concerns who makes decisions, controls resources and shapes others' lives.
  15. Domestic violence: Domestic violence and abuse is harmful behaviour between people who are or have been intimate partners or family members.
  16. Family diversity: Families in the UK are increasingly diverse.
  17. Intergenerational relationships: Intergenerational relationships link grandparents, parents, children and grandchildren.
  18. Ageing and care: An ageing population increases the need for care, much of it provided unpaid by families.
  19. Family policy: Family policies are laws and measures that affect families, from benefits and parental leave to childcare and child protection.
  20. Global families: Families around the world differ in structure, marriage practices and roles, and globalisation is changing family life.
  21. Family change: Family life has changed greatly since the mid-20th century.

School 9: Education

21 lessons

Education: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Functionalist perspectives on education: Functionalists see education as serving the needs of society: building shared values, teaching skills and sorting people into roles.
  2. Marxist perspectives on education: Marxists see education as reproducing class inequality and legitimating it.
  3. Feminist perspectives on education: Feminist sociologists examine how education reproduces or challenges gender inequality.
  4. Education, socialisation and social control: Schools socialise young people and regulate their behaviour.
  5. The hidden curriculum: The hidden curriculum is what pupils learn from the way schools are organised, not from formal lessons.
  6. Educational achievement and social class: Social class is one of the strongest predictors of educational achievement in England.
  7. Educational achievement and gender: Girls have outperformed boys on average in English schools for decades.
  8. Educational achievement and ethnicity: Educational outcomes vary widely by ethnic group.
  9. Cultural capital and education: Cultural capital is the knowledge, tastes and confidence that bring educational advantage.
  10. Linguistic codes: Bernstein argued that differences in how families use language affect school success.
  11. Labelling in education: Labelling theory examines how teachers' judgements about pupils can shape pupils' identities and outcomes.
  12. Teacher expectations: Teacher expectations are beliefs teachers hold about pupils' future achievement.
  13. The self-fulfilling prophecy: A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true because it was made.
  14. Selection: Selection refers to how schools and systems sort pupils by ability or other criteria.
  15. Educational inequality: Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education.
  16. Private education: Private (independent) schools educate a small share of pupils but a large share of elites.
  17. Higher education: Higher education has expanded greatly, but access and outcomes remain unequal.
  18. Education policy: Education policy shapes how schools are organised, funded and held accountable.
  19. Global education: Access to and quality of education vary greatly around the world.
  20. Digital learning: Digital technology is changing how people learn, from online courses to AI tools.
  21. Educational reform: Educational reform aims to improve standards and reduce inequality.

School 10: Crime and Deviance

34 lessons

Crime and Deviance: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Crime and deviance: Crime is behaviour that breaks the criminal law; deviance is behaviour that breaks social norms.
  2. The social construction of crime: Crime is socially constructed: what counts as crime depends on laws, enforcement and social reactions that change over time and differ between places.
  3. Functionalist theories of crime: Functionalists argue crime is normal and can even be useful for society.
  4. Marxist theories of crime: Marxists argue crime and the law reflect the interests of the ruling class under capitalism.
  5. Interactionist theories of crime: Interactionists study how deviance is created through social interaction and reactions.
  6. Labelling theory: Labelling theory explains how being labelled as deviant can create a deviant identity and further deviance.
  7. Strain theory: Strain theory explains crime as a response to the gap between socially approved goals and the legitimate means to achieve them.
  8. Subcultural theories: Subcultural theories explain crime as a collective response by groups whose values differ from the mainstream.
  9. Differential association: Differential association theory proposes that criminal behaviour is learned through interaction with others.
  10. Social learning and crime: Social learning theory explains crime through observation, imitation and reinforcement.
  11. Control theory: Control theories ask not why people commit crime but why most people don't.
  12. Feminist criminology: Feminist criminology examines how gender shapes offending, victimisation and justice, and criticises "malestream" criminology.
  13. Critical criminology: Critical criminology examines how power, inequality and the state shape crime, laws and justice.
  14. Realist criminology: Realist criminologists argue crime is a real problem that harms people, especially the most vulnerable, and that practical solutions are needed.
  15. Postmodern criminology: Postmodern criminology questions grand theories of crime and examines how crime is shaped by identity, consumption, media and risk in late modern society.
  16. Crime and social class: Official statistics suggest working-class people are more likely to be convicted of crime.
  17. Crime and gender: Men commit most recorded crime, especially violence.
  18. Crime and ethnicity: Some ethnic minority groups are over-represented in the criminal justice system.
  19. Youth crime: Young people, especially young men, are over-represented in recorded crime, but most grow out of offending.
  20. White-collar crime: White-collar crime is crime committed by respectable people in the course of their occupations.
  21. Corporate crime: Corporate crime is illegal or harmful acts committed by organisations for their benefit.
  22. Organised crime: Organised crime is serious crime planned and carried out by groups for profit.
  23. Cybercrime: Cybercrime is crime committed using computers and the internet.
  24. Victimology: Victimology studies victims of crime: who becomes a victim, how victimisation affects people and how the justice system treats victims.
  25. Policing: The police are the main formal agency of social control and the gateway to the criminal justice system.
  26. The criminal justice system: The criminal justice system (CJS) is the set of agencies that respond to crime, from police to prisons.
  27. Prisons: Prison is the most severe punishment used in the UK.
  28. Punishment: Punishment is the deliberate imposition of pain or loss on offenders.
  29. Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation aims to change offenders so they stop offending.
  30. Social control: Social control covers all the ways societies encourage conformity and respond to deviance.
  31. Surveillance: Surveillance is the monitoring of people's behaviour to control, manage or protect them.
  32. Moral panics: A moral panic is an exaggerated public reaction to a group or behaviour seen as a threat to society.
  33. Crime and the media: The media shape how people understand crime.
  34. Crime prevention: Crime prevention aims to stop crime before it happens.

School 11: Politics and Power

21 lessons

Politics and Power: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Power: Power is the ability to get what you want, even against resistance.
  2. Authority: traditional, charismatic and legal-rational: Authority is power that people accept as legitimate.
  3. Legitimacy: Legitimacy is the belief that those in power have the right to rule.
  4. The state: The state is the set of institutions that make and enforce binding rules over a territory.
  5. Nation states: A nation state combines a state with a nation, a community that sees itself as sharing an identity.
  6. Citizenship and rights: Citizenship is membership of a political community, bringing rights and duties.
  7. Democracy: Democracy means rule by the people, but it takes many forms.
  8. Political participation: Political participation includes all the ways people try to influence politics, from voting to boycotts.
  9. Political inequality: Political inequality means some groups have more influence over decisions than others.
  10. Political ideologies: A political ideology is a set of ideas about how society is and should be organised.
  11. Social movements: Social movements are sustained, organised collective efforts to bring about or resist social change.
  12. Collective action: Collective action is people acting together to achieve shared goals.
  13. Civil society: Civil society is the space between the state, the market and the family where people organise voluntarily.
  14. Protest: Protest is public action to express opposition or demand change.
  15. Political institutions: Political institutions are the formal and informal rules and organisations through which power is exercised.
  16. Bureaucracy and the state: Modern states rely on large bureaucracies to make and deliver policy.
  17. Global governance: Global governance is the set of institutions, rules and networks that manage problems crossing national borders.
  18. Power and knowledge: Knowledge is not neutral: who produces it, and whose knowledge counts, is shaped by power.
  19. Political surveillance: Political surveillance is the monitoring of political activity by states and other actors.
  20. Political sociology: Political sociology studies the social bases of politics: how class, education, age and identity shape political attitudes and behaviour.
  21. Political change: Political systems change through reform, revolution, democratisation and backsliding.

School 12: Religion

19 lessons

Religion: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Defining religion: Sociologists study religion as a social phenomenon, not to judge whether beliefs are true.
  2. Functionalist approaches to religion: Functionalists explain religion by the functions it performs for society and individuals.
  3. Marxist approaches to religion: Marxists see religion as ideology that helps maintain class inequality.
  4. Weberian approaches to religion: Weber argued religious ideas can shape economic and social change.
  5. Secularisation: Secularisation is the decline in the social significance of religion.
  6. Belief and practice: Religion involves both what people believe and what they do.
  7. Religious institutions: Religious organisations take different forms, from large established churches to small sects.
  8. Religious authority: Religious authority is the recognised right to interpret beliefs and guide others.
  9. Religious identity: Religion can be a key source of identity, especially for minority communities.
  10. Religious pluralism: Religious pluralism describes societies with many religions and the acceptance of that diversity.
  11. Fundamentalism: Fundamentalism describes movements that seek to return to what they see as the basic truths of a religion, often in reaction to modern change.
  12. Civil religion: Civil religion is a set of beliefs, symbols and rituals that sacralise the nation and unite citizens across different faiths.
  13. Religion and social change: Is religion a conservative force or a force for change?
  14. Religion and politics: Religion and politics interact through laws, parties, voting and public debate.
  15. Religion and gender: Religion has often been shaped by patriarchy, yet women are more religious than men in many societies.
  16. Religion and inequality: Religion intersects with class, ethnicity and other inequalities.
  17. Religion and globalisation: Globalisation spreads religions across borders, connects believers worldwide and changes how religion is practised.
  18. New religious movements: New religious movements (NRMs) are religious or spiritual groups that have emerged relatively recently.
  19. Contemporary religious change: Religion in contemporary societies is changing in form: becoming more individual, spiritual, mediated and diverse.

School 13: Work and Economy

24 lessons

Work and Economy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Industrial society: Industrial society is organised around factory production, wage labour and mass employment in manufacturing.
  2. Post-industrial society: Post-industrial society is one where services and knowledge, rather than manufacturing, dominate employment.
  3. Capitalism: Capitalism is an economic system based on private ownership, markets and wage labour, aimed at profit.
  4. Labour markets: A labour market is where workers sell their labour and employers buy it.
  5. The division of labour: The division of labour is how work is split into specialised tasks and roles.
  6. Alienation at work: Alienation describes workers' estrangement from their work, its products, other people and themselves.
  7. Workplace hierarchy: Workplaces are organised into hierarchies of authority, pay and status.
  8. Bureaucracy and organisations: Most paid work happens inside formal organisations.
  9. Professionalisation: Professions such as medicine and law hold high status, autonomy and rewards.
  10. Occupational identity: Work shapes how people see themselves and how others see them.
  11. Work and gender: Men and women experience paid and unpaid work differently.
  12. Work and class: Occupation is the main basis for measuring social class.
  13. Work and ethnicity: People from different ethnic groups have different experiences of employment, pay and progression.
  14. Unemployment: Unemployment means being without paid work while looking for it.
  15. Precarity: Precarity describes work that is insecure, unpredictable and lacking protections.
  16. The gig economy: The gig economy is work arranged through digital platforms for short tasks or "gigs".
  17. Automation and artificial intelligence: Automation and artificial intelligence (AI) are changing which tasks humans do at work.
  18. Workplace surveillance: Employers increasingly use technology to monitor workers.
  19. Trade unions and industrial relations: Trade unions are organisations of workers that bargain collectively with employers.
  20. Global production: Many goods are produced through chains of firms spread across several countries.
  21. Consumer society: In consumer societies, buying goods and services is central to everyday life and identity.
  22. Economic inequality: Economic inequality concerns the unequal distribution of income and wealth.
  23. Welfare capitalism: Welfare states protect people against risks such as unemployment, illness and old age.
  24. The future of work: Work is changing through technology, demography, climate policy and new ways of organising.

School 14: Globalisation and Social Change

26 lessons

Globalisation and Social Change: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is globalisation?: Globalisation is the growing interconnectedness of the world through flows of goods, money, people, information and ideas.
  2. Economic globalisation: Economic globalisation is the integration of national economies through trade, investment, finance and production.
  3. Political globalisation: Political globalisation is the growth of political authority, cooperation and activism beyond the nation state.
  4. Cultural globalisation: Cultural globalisation is the worldwide flow of ideas, media, products and practices.
  5. Technological globalisation: Technology has driven globalisation by making communication, transport and coordination faster and cheaper.
  6. Global migration: Migration is a central part of globalisation.
  7. Global inequality and development: Development is the process by which societies improve living standards and capabilities.
  8. Global capitalism: Global capitalism describes a world economy in which capital, production and markets are organised across borders.
  9. Transnational corporations: Transnational corporations (TNCs) operate in many countries and control much of world trade and investment.
  10. Global supply chains: A global supply chain is the network of firms and workers that produce and deliver a product across countries.
  11. Cultural homogenisation: Cultural homogenisation is the idea that globalisation makes cultures more alike.
  12. Cultural hybridisation: Cultural hybridisation is the mixing of cultures to create new forms.
  13. Glocalisation: Glocalisation describes how global products and ideas are adapted to local contexts.
  14. Digital society: In a digital society, digital technologies shape everyday life, work, relationships and power.
  15. The network society: Castells argued that information technology has produced a network society in which networks are the main form of social organisation.
  16. Social media and society: Social media platforms let people create and share content and connect with others.
  17. Global social movements: Global social movements organise across borders on issues such as climate change, trade justice and human rights.
  18. Climate change and society: Climate change is a social as well as a physical problem: human activity causes it, and its impacts and solutions are shaped by inequality and politics.
  19. Environmental sociology: Environmental sociology studies the relationship between society and the natural environment.
  20. Sustainable development: Sustainable development aims to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  21. Consumerism and the environment: High levels of consumption, especially in rich countries, drive resource use and emissions.
  22. Risk society: Beck argued that late modern societies are increasingly organised around producing and managing risks they have created themselves.
  23. Modernity: Modernity is the social order that emerged with industrialisation, capitalism, the nation state and Enlightenment thought.
  24. Postmodernity: Postmodernity describes a proposed new era following modernity, marked by media saturation, consumer culture, fragmented identities and distrust of grand theories.
  25. Social transformation: Social transformation refers to deep, long-term change in social structures and relationships.
  26. Future societies: Sociologists study trends to think about possible futures, while recognising uncertainty.

School 15: Urban Sociology and Population

22 lessons

Urban Sociology and Population: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Urbanisation: Urbanisation is the growth in the share of people living in towns and cities.
  2. Cities and modernity: Classical sociologists saw the modern city as a new form of social life.
  3. Urban ecology and the Chicago School: The Chicago School of sociology studied the city as an ecosystem, where groups compete for space.
  4. Urban inequality: Cities concentrate both wealth and poverty.
  5. Segregation: Residential segregation is the separation of groups into different neighbourhoods by income, ethnicity or religion.
  6. Gentrification: Gentrification is the movement of wealthier residents and investment into working-class areas, changing their character.
  7. Housing: Housing shapes health, wealth, family life and opportunity.
  8. Homelessness: Homelessness ranges from sleeping rough to living in temporary accommodation or sofa-surfing.
  9. Suburbanisation: Suburbanisation is the growth of residential areas on the edges of cities.
  10. Rural sociology: Rural sociology studies life in the countryside, including agriculture, community and rural change.
  11. Community: Community refers to people bound by shared place, identity or interest.
  12. Social networks in places: Social networks link people to resources, information and support.
  13. Population change: Population size changes through births, deaths and migration.
  14. The demographic transition: The demographic transition model describes how birth and death rates change as societies develop.
  15. Fertility: Fertility refers to the number of children people have.
  16. Mortality: Mortality refers to deaths in a population.
  17. Population ageing: Population ageing means older people make up a growing share of the population.
  18. Migration and population: Migration changes the size, age structure and diversity of populations.
  19. Urban planning: Urban planning shapes how cities are designed and developed.
  20. Sustainable cities: Sustainable cities aim to reduce environmental harm while improving residents' quality of life.
  21. Global cities: Global cities are command centres of the world economy.
  22. Urban futures: Cities face challenges from climate change, technology, inequality and demographic change.

School 16: Health and Medicine

20 lessons

Health and Medicine: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Social determinants of health: Health is shaped by the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.
  2. Health inequalities: Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable differences in health between social groups.
  3. Medical sociology: Medical sociology studies health, illness and health care as social phenomena.
  4. Medical and social models of health: The biomedical model sees disease as a biological fault to be fixed by medicine, while social models stress social causes and meanings.
  5. The experience of illness: Sociologists study how people experience and make sense of illness, especially long-term conditions.
  6. Medicalisation: Medicalisation is the process by which non-medical problems come to be defined and treated as medical.
  7. The medical profession: The medical profession has high status, autonomy and power over health care.
  8. Healthcare institutions: Healthcare institutions organise how care is funded and delivered.
  9. Patients and power: Relationships between patients and doctors involve power and communication.
  10. Disability and the social model: Disability can be understood as an individual impairment or as a product of social barriers.
  11. Stigma and health: Stigma is a mark of disgrace that sets a person apart.
  12. Mental health and social control: Mental health is an area where medicine has the power to define abnormality and detain people.
  13. Health behaviours: Health behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and exercise influence health, but they are shaped by social circumstances.
  14. Public health: Public health aims to protect and improve the health of whole populations.
  15. Health policy: Health policy shapes how health care is funded, organised and delivered.
  16. Global health: Global health studies health issues that cross borders and inequalities between countries.
  17. Ageing and health: Older people use health services more, but ageing is not the same as illness.
  18. Gender and health: Men and women differ in health, illness and use of services.
  19. Race and health inequalities: Health differs between ethnic groups in ways linked to deprivation, racism and access to care.
  20. Digital healthcare: Digital technology is changing how people access and receive health care.

School 17: Research Methods

38 lessons

Research Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Research design: A research design is the overall plan that links a research question to the evidence needed to answer it: what will be studied, how, with whom and when.
  2. Research questions and hypotheses: Sociological research begins with a question.
  3. Positivism: Positivism holds that sociology should study society scientifically, as natural sciences study the physical world, seeking objective facts and law-like patterns.
  4. Interpretivism: Interpretivism holds that sociology must understand the meanings people give to their actions, because human behaviour differs from the behaviour of natural objects.
  5. Realism: Realism (critical realism) argues that society has real structures and mechanisms that may not be directly observable, so research must go beyond observed patterns and participants' accounts.
  6. Quantitative research: Quantitative research collects numerical data to measure social phenomena, identify patterns and test relationships.
  7. Qualitative research: Qualitative research collects non-numerical data, such as words, observations and documents, to understand meanings, experiences and processes in depth.
  8. Mixed methods: Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative methods in one study, to gain both breadth and depth.
  9. Surveys: Surveys collect standardised information from many people, usually by questionnaire or structured interview.
  10. Questionnaire design: The quality of survey data depends on the questions asked.
  11. Structured interviews: A structured interview is a survey delivered by an interviewer: the same questions, in the same order and wording, for every respondent.
  12. Semi-structured interviews: Semi-structured interviews use a guide of key questions but allow the interviewer to follow up and respondents to answer in their own words.
  13. Unstructured interviews: An unstructured interview is a guided conversation in which the respondent largely leads, allowing researchers to explore experiences in great depth.
  14. Focus groups: Focus groups are group interviews in which participants discuss a topic together, guided by a moderator.
  15. Participant observation: In participant observation, the researcher joins a group or setting, taking part in its activities while observing.
  16. Ethnography: Ethnography is the in-depth study of a culture or community over an extended period, combining observation, conversation and documents to produce a detailed account of how life is lived.
  17. Case studies: A case study investigates one case, such as a community, organisation, event or policy, in depth and in its context, often with several methods.
  18. Experiments in sociology: Experiments are rare in sociology but powerful when they can be used, especially field experiments that test discrimination in real life.
  19. Social network analysis: Social network analysis (SNA) studies the patterns of relationships between people, groups or organisations, using measures such as density and centrality.
  20. Content analysis: Content analysis systematically analyses the content of media and documents.
  21. Discourse analysis: Discourse analysis studies how language constructs social reality, identities and power, rather than simply describing them.
  22. Secondary data: Secondary data are data collected by someone else, such as official statistics, existing surveys, documents and archives, that sociologists reanalyse for new questions.
  23. Official statistics: Official statistics are numerical data collected by governments and public bodies on topics such as population, crime, employment and health.
  24. Longitudinal research: Longitudinal research follows the same people, households or units over time, showing how lives change and in what order events happen.
  25. Comparative research: Comparative research compares societies, regions, institutions or time periods to understand similarities and differences and test explanations.
  26. Probability sampling: In probability sampling every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection, which allows sample results to be generalised with measurable precision.
  27. Non-probability sampling: Non-probability sampling selects participants without giving everyone a known chance of selection.
  28. Reliability: Reliability concerns consistency: whether a method would produce the same results if repeated.
  29. Validity: Validity concerns whether research captures what it claims to: a true picture of the social reality studied.
  30. Generalisability: Generalisability is how far findings from a study apply beyond the people and settings studied.
  31. Bias: Bias is systematic distortion in research.
  32. Positionality: Positionality is the researcher's social position, such as class, gender, ethnicity, age and experience, and how it shapes access, relationships and interpretation in research.
  33. Reflexivity: Reflexivity means researchers critically reflecting on how they, their methods and their assumptions shape the research and its findings.
  34. Informed consent: Informed consent means participants agree to take part freely, understanding what the research involves.
  35. Anonymity and confidentiality: Anonymity and confidentiality protect participants from being identified and from harm.
  36. Researching vulnerable groups: Some participants are vulnerable because of age, capacity, circumstances or power relations.
  37. Interpreting findings: Interpreting findings means deciding what the evidence shows and does not show.
  38. Disseminating research: Research matters only if it reaches people who can use or challenge it.

School 18: Social Statistics and Data Science

25 lessons

Social Statistics and Data Science: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Types of social data: Social data come in different forms: categorical or numerical, individual or aggregate, survey or administrative, structured or unstructured.
  2. Central tendency: Measures of central tendency summarise typical values.
  3. Dispersion: Dispersion describes how spread out values are.
  4. Frequency distributions: A frequency distribution shows how many cases fall into each category or range.
  5. Visualising social data: Good charts make social patterns visible and honest.
  6. Probability for social research: Probability underpins sampling and inference in social research.
  7. Sampling distributions: If a survey were repeated many times with new random samples, the results would vary.
  8. Hypothesis testing: Hypothesis testing assesses whether patterns in sample data are strong enough to infer patterns in the population.
  9. Statistical significance: Statistical significance tells us whether a result would be surprising if there were no real effect, not whether it matters.
  10. Confidence intervals: Confidence intervals give a range of plausible values for a population figure, showing how precise a survey estimate is.
  11. Effect sizes: Effect sizes measure how big a difference or association is.
  12. Correlation: Correlation measures how strongly two numerical variables are related.
  13. Regression: Regression models how an outcome changes with one or more explanatory variables, allowing sociologists to estimate associations while holding other factors constant.
  14. Categorical data: Much social data is categorical: class, ethnicity, tenure, voting.
  15. Chi-square tests: The chi-square test of independence checks whether an association in a crosstabulation is larger than sampling chance would produce.
  16. Social indicators: Social indicators are statistics used to measure social conditions and track progress, from unemployment rates to life expectancy and wellbeing.
  17. Measuring inequality: the Gini coefficient: The Gini coefficient summarises inequality in a single number from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality).
  18. Measuring poverty: How poverty is measured shapes how much of it we see.
  19. Demographic analysis: Demographic analysis measures and explains population size, structure and change through births, deaths and migration.
  20. Census data: A census counts every person and household in a country at one point in time.
  21. Survey data: Large social surveys are the workhorses of quantitative sociology.
  22. Social network analysis in practice: This lesson takes social network analysis from concepts to practice: collecting network data, choosing measures and interpreting results, with classic and contemporary examples.
  23. Computational sociology: Computational sociology uses computers to model and analyse social processes, from agent-based simulations to large-scale analysis of digital data.
  24. Digital trace data: Digital trace data are records left by online and digital activity: posts, searches, clicks, transactions and phone movements.
  25. Reproducible research: Reproducible research means others can follow and repeat every step from raw data to published findings.
