Professional Skills: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Professional Skills lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Professional Communication

  • Writing for yourself: putting things in the order you discovered them, or using the vocabulary of your own team, rather than in the order and words the reader needs. From “Communication at work: purpose and audience”
  • Jumping straight to advice. Solving the problem you assume someone has, before they've finished, often solves the wrong problem and signals that you weren't listening. From “Active listening”
  • Asking a leading question in an investigation or interview ("You saw him take it, didn't you?"), which can contaminate the answer and undermine fairness. From “Questioning skills”
  • Treating body-language "rules" as lie detection. Research gives little support to the idea that people can reliably spot deception from non-verbal cues. From “Non-verbal communication”

Social Psychology

  • Assuming famous priming findings are solid. Several high-profile social priming effects haven't replicated. From “Social cognition”
  • Treating fast impressions as accurate. Confidence in snap judgements often exceeds their accuracy. From “Social perception and impressions”
  • Thinking people always use covariation information systematically. People often have incomplete information and use shortcuts. From “Attribution”
  • Calling the FAE universal. It is weaker in some cultures and situations, which is one reason "fundamental" has been questioned. From “The fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias”

Management and Organisational Behaviour

  • Treating management and leadership as the same thing. Kotter's distinction is useful: management copes with complexity (plans, budgets, control); leadership copes with change (direction, alignment, inspiration). Organisations need both. From “What is management?”
  • Saying classical theory is obsolete. Its ideas live on in fast-food production lines, call-centre scripts, lean timings and warehouse picking targets, where work is still measured and standardised. From “Classical management theory: Taylor and Fayol”
  • Thinking the roles are separate jobs done one after another. They form an integrated whole: one meeting can involve several roles at once. From “Mintzberg's managerial roles”
  • Overstating the evidence. The studies had tiny samples, changed several things at once and were partly reinterpreted to fit Mayo's ideas. Re-analyses of the original lighting data find the "Hawthorne effect" much weaker than the legend. From “The human relations movement”

Project Management

  • Calling all work a project. Ongoing routine operations are business as usual, not projects. From “What a project is”
  • Assuming stopping a project at a stage gate is a failure. Stopping a project that no longer makes sense saves money. From “Project life cycles”
  • Giving a task several "accountable" people. Accountability should rest with one person. From “Project roles and governance”
  • Writing the business case once and forgetting it. It should be reviewed throughout the project. From “The business case”

Productivity and Personal Effectiveness

  • Writing key results as a to-do list. A key result is a measurable outcome that shows success, not an activity you plan to carry out. From “Setting goals: SMART objectives and OKRs”
  • Treating every urgent request as important. Ask whether the task advances your goals or responsibilities before letting it push out planned work. From “Prioritisation: urgency and importance”
  • Filling every hour of the calendar. A plan with no buffer collapses at the first interruption, so leave unscheduled time. From “Planning your week”
  • Believing you can multitask on demanding work. The brain switches between tasks rather than doing both at once, and each switch carries a cost. From “Focus, interruptions and deep work”

Strategy

  • Confusing strategy with being efficient. Doing things better than rivals (operational effectiveness) matters, but rivals can copy it; strategy requires a distinct position and deliberate trade-offs. From “What is strategy?”
  • Assuming a published mission statement shows what an organisation actually does. Statements are often generic; judge them against decisions, resource allocation and behaviour. From “Mission, vision and objectives”
  • Treating emergent strategy as having no strategy. Emergent strategy is a consistent pattern that develops through action and learning, often recognised and then formalised by managers. From “Planned and emergent strategy”
  • Confusing substitutes with competitors. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (trains for domestic flights); a competitor offers a similar product (another airline). From “Porter's five forces”

Data and Digital Skills at Work

  • Assuming data is correct because it is in a system or a spreadsheet. Always ask where it came from and check its quality before relying on it. From “Data literacy”
  • Typing fixed numbers such as rates directly into formulas. Put them in one labelled cell and use an absolute reference so they can be changed safely. From “Spreadsheet essentials”
  • Confusing percentage points with percentages. A rise from 10% to 12% is 2 percentage points but a 20% increase. From “Descriptive statistics for work”
  • Using a pie chart for many similar categories. People judge angles poorly, so use a sorted bar chart instead. From “Charts and data visualisation”

The Business Environment

  • Confusing added value with profit. Added value is selling price minus bought-in inputs; profit is what remains after all costs, including wages and overheads, have also been paid. From “What is a business?”
  • Treating a mission statement as an objective. "To delight our customers" is an aim or mission; without a measure and a deadline it can't guide decisions or show whether the business succeeded. From “Business purpose and objectives”
  • Assuming stakeholder positions are fixed. Interest and power change with the decision and over time; a matrix is a snapshot to revisit. From “Stakeholders”
  • Mixing up the public sector with public limited companies. A plc such as BP is in the private sector: "public" refers to its shares being offered to the public, not to state ownership. From “The private, public and third sectors”

Ethics, Professionalism and Employment Rights

  • Equating professionalism with formal dress or a job title. It is shown through behaviour, such as honesty, reliability and owning mistakes. From “What professionalism means”
  • Assuming that anything not banned by the code is acceptable. Codes cannot list every case, so use their spirit and your judgement. From “Professional codes of conduct”
  • Framing a decision only in business terms. Ask explicitly whether it has an ethical dimension, or ethical fading may lead you astray. From “Ethical decision making”
  • Thinking a conflict only matters if you are actually influenced. Perceived conflicts also damage trust, so declare them too. From “Integrity and conflicts of interest”

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

  • Assuming rewards always increase motivation. For enjoyable tasks, expected tangible rewards can reduce interest. From “Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation”
  • Assuming all behaviour reduces tension. People often seek stimulation, novelty and challenge. From “Drive theories”
  • Thinking dopamine is the brain's "pleasure chemical". It is more closely linked to wanting and motivation. From “Incentive theories”
  • Equating autonomy with independence. In SDT, autonomy means acting from self-endorsed values, which can include following others' guidance. From “Self-determination theory”
