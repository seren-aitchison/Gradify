Health and Wellbeing: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Health and Wellbeing lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

  • Treating the WHO definition as the only "correct" one. It is the most quoted, but it is one definition among several, and its word "complete" is widely criticised. From “What is health?”
  • Thinking the biopsychosocial model rejects biology or medicine. It adds psychological and social factors to biological ones; it doesn't replace them. From “Models of health: biomedical and biopsychosocial”
  • Assuming a high wellbeing score means the absence of illness. People with long-term conditions can report high wellbeing, and people without any diagnosis can report low wellbeing. From “Wellbeing and how it is measured”
  • Confusing "holistic" with "alternative" medicine. In health and social care, holistic means attending to the whole person; it is part of mainstream evidence-based care. From “Holistic health: physical, mental and social”

The Human Body

  • Thinking each organ belongs to only one system. The pancreas is part of both the digestive and endocrine systems; the kidneys belong to the renal system but also control blood pressure and make a hormone. From “Cells, tissues and organs”
  • Thinking that homeostasis keeps variables at one exact value. Variables fluctuate within a normal range around a set point, and some set points change, for example temperature during a fever. From “Homeostasis”
  • Thinking that a fever is a failure of temperature control. In fever the hypothalamus deliberately raises its set point; the temperature is still regulated, just around a higher value. From “Body temperature regulation”
  • Thinking that insulin breaks glucose down. Insulin is a signal: it causes cells to take up and store glucose; it is not an enzyme. From “Blood glucose regulation”

Nutrition

  • Treating all sugar as the same. The sugars in whole fruit and plain milk come with fibre or other nutrients and aren't counted as free sugars; fruit juice and honey are. From “Carbohydrates and dietary fibre”
  • Assuming more protein always builds more muscle. Beyond requirements, extra protein is used for energy or excreted; muscle growth depends on resistance training and overall energy intake. From “Protein”
  • Assuming "low fat" always means healthier. Some low-fat products replace fat with sugar or refined starch; replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrate doesn't lower heart disease risk. From “Fats”
  • Assuming high-dose supplements are always safe. Fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate; excess vitamin A in pregnancy is harmful, so pregnant women should avoid liver and vitamin A supplements. From “Vitamins”

Physical Activity

  • Thinking only sport or gym exercise counts. All movement counts towards activity guidelines, including active travel, housework and play. From “Physical activity, exercise and fitness”
  • Forgetting the strength recommendation. Many people who meet the aerobic target don't do muscle-strengthening activity, which is a separate guideline. From “The UK physical activity guidelines”
  • Assuming a daily workout cancels out a sedentary day. High sitting time carries risk unless offset by large amounts of activity; reducing and breaking up sitting matters too. From “Sedentary behaviour”
  • Expecting exercise alone to produce large weight loss. Activity has many health benefits even without weight loss; for weight loss, diet changes usually matter more. From “The benefits of physical activity”

Sleep, Stress and Rest

  • Believing people can adapt fully to short sleep. Studies show performance keeps declining with chronic restriction even when people report feeling only a little tired. From “Why we sleep”
  • Thinking dreaming only happens in REM sleep. Dreams can occur in NREM sleep too, though REM dreams are usually more vivid and story-like. From “Sleep stages and cycles”
  • Assuming teenagers stay up late only by choice. The adolescent body clock shifts later biologically, though screens and social life can add to the delay. From “Circadian rhythms”
  • Concluding that long sleep causes ill health from observational data. Long sleep often reflects underlying illness, depression or poor-quality sleep. From “How much sleep we need”

Mental Health and Wellbeing

  • Treating mental health as a yes-or-no state. People move along a spectrum, and wellbeing can be supported whether or not someone has a diagnosis. From “Mental health and mental illness”
  • Assuming rising figures only reflect more willingness to talk. Changes in surveys using consistent methods suggest real increases in some groups, especially young women. From “Mental health in the UK”
  • Treating ACEs as destiny. ACEs increase risk on average, but many people with ACEs do well, especially with supportive relationships. From “Risk and protective factors for mental health”
  • Using mental illness terms casually, such as "I'm so OCD". Casual misuse trivialises real conditions and reinforces stigma. From “Stigma and discrimination”

Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change

  • Blaming individuals alone for unhealthy behaviour. Environments, income and stress strongly shape what people do. From “Why health behaviours matter”
  • Assuming that increasing fear always changes behaviour. Without lowering barriers and building self-efficacy, threat messages can backfire. From “The health belief model”
  • Assuming strong intentions guarantee behaviour. Many people intend to act but do not, so planning and support are needed. From “The theory of planned behaviour”
  • Refusing to help people who are not "ready". Many people make sudden, unplanned quit attempts that succeed. From “The transtheoretical model”

Disease and Long-term Conditions

  • Expecting antibiotics to cure viral infections such as colds and flu. Antibiotics act only on bacteria. From “Pathogens and how infections spread”
  • Relying on alcohol hand gel for every infection. Soap and water are needed for norovirus and Clostridioides difficile. From “Breaking the chain of infection”
  • Believing herd immunity means unvaccinated people are always safe. When coverage falls below the threshold, outbreaks occur, as with measles. From “Vaccination and herd immunity”
  • Thinking people become resistant to antibiotics. It is the bacteria that become resistant. From “Antimicrobial resistance”

Public Health and Epidemiology

  • Thinking public health is only about individual lifestyle choices. Most public health action changes environments, services and policies that shape everyone's choices. From “What public health is”
  • Assuming that medical treatment caused most of the fall in deaths in the nineteenth century. Clean water, sanitation, better nutrition and housing played a much larger part. From “A short history of public health”
  • Thinking each domain works separately. Most problems, from measles to obesity, need action across all three. From “The three domains of public health”
  • Calling screening a guarantee. Screening reduces risk but misses some cases and can cause harm through false positives and overdiagnosis. From “Levels of prevention”

Health and Social Care Systems

  • Thinking the NHS has always been completely free for everything. Charges for prescriptions (in England), dental care and some other services have existed since the early 1950s. From “The founding of the NHS”
  • Thinking the NHS is one single organisation. It is a system of many separate bodies with different roles. From “How the NHS in England is organised”
  • NHS structures in England are being reformed, including the planned merger of NHS England into the DHSC announced in 2025; check the current arrangements. From “How the NHS in England is organised”
  • Assuming NHS policy in England applies across the UK. Health policy is decided separately in each nation. From “Health care across the four UK nations”

Health Across the Life Course

  • Starting folic acid only once pregnancy is confirmed. The neural tube forms very early, so it should start before conception where possible. From “Preconception and pregnancy”
  • This lesson is for study; anyone pregnant or planning pregnancy should follow advice from their midwife or GP. From “Preconception and pregnancy”
  • Assuming the blood spot test diagnoses conditions. It is screening; positive results need confirmatory tests. From “Birth and the newborn”
  • Treating a single late milestone as a problem. Development varies widely; patterns of delay matter more. From “Infant health and development”
