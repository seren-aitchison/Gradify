Sport and Exercise Science: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Sport and Exercise Science lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System

  • Matching a plane with the axis of the same name. The sagittal plane's movements turn about the frontal axis, and frontal-plane movements about the sagittal axis. Note that OCR and some texts call the frontal axis "transverse"; check your specification's terms. From “Anatomical language: planes, axes and positions”
  • Writing "the skeleton produces blood". It produces blood cells, in red marrow; plasma is mostly water and is not made in bone. From “Functions of the skeleton”
  • Calling the patella a short bone. It is a sesamoid bone, formed within the quadriceps tendon. From “Bone structure and types of bone”
  • Placing the fibula on the medial side or treating it as the main weight-bearing bone. The tibia is medial and bears most of the load; the fibula is lateral and mainly anchors muscles. From “The axial and appendicular skeleton”

The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise

  • Saying all arteries carry oxygenated blood. Arteries carry blood away from the heart: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs. From “Structure of the heart”
  • Saying the brain starts each heartbeat. The SA node does; nerves and hormones only change how fast it fires. From “The cardiac conduction system”
  • Saying the valves open because the muscles pull them. Valves open and close passively because of pressure differences either side of them. From “The cardiac cycle”
  • Thinking training increases maximal heart rate. Maximal heart rate is mostly set by age and genetics and is unchanged or slightly lower after training; the gain in maximal cardiac output comes from stroke volume. From “Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate”

Energy Systems and Exercise Metabolism

  • Saying food "gives muscles energy" directly. Food energy is used to resynthesise ATP; only ATP powers contraction. From “ATP: the energy currency”
  • Saying the ATP–PC system lasts 10 seconds in all exercise. It is the predominant source for maximal efforts up to about 10 s; in submaximal or intermittent exercise PC is used and restored repeatedly. From “The ATP–PC system”
  • Saying "lactic acid causes muscle soreness the next day". Lactate is cleared within an hour or so; delayed-onset soreness is caused by muscle damage, not lactate. From “The anaerobic glycolytic system”
  • Saying the Krebs cycle produces most of the ATP. Most ATP is made by the electron transport chain; the Krebs cycle mainly releases CO₂ and supplies hydrogen carriers. From “The aerobic system”

Exercise Physiology: Environment, Adaptation and Populations

  • Thinking sweating cools the body by itself. Cooling comes from the evaporation of sweat, not from sweat dripping off. From “Thermoregulation during exercise”
  • Assuming a collapsed athlete in the heat is just tired. Confusion or collapse may mean heat stroke, which needs emergency treatment and rapid cooling. From “Exercise in the heat and acclimatisation”
  • Rubbing frostbitten skin to warm it up. Rubbing can damage tissue further; seek medical help. From “Exercise in the cold”
  • Saying there is less oxygen percentage at altitude. The percentage is the same; the partial pressure is lower. From “Altitude and hypoxia”

Biomechanics

  • Using speed and velocity as if they always mean the same thing. They are equal only when the path is a straight line in one direction; velocity depends on displacement, not distance. From “Linear motion: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration”
  • Reading a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph as standing still. A horizontal line on a velocity–time graph means constant velocity; only a horizontal line on a distance–time graph means stationary. From “Distance–time and velocity–time graphs”
  • Thinking any force makes a body spin. Only a force acting outside the centre of mass produces rotation; a force through the centre of mass gives linear motion. From “Angular motion”
  • Assuming athletes always want maximum stability. Sprint and swim starts deliberately reduce stability so the athlete can move off quickly. From “Centre of mass and stability”

Training Principles and Programme Design

  • Thinking any hard training improves all sports equally. Adaptations are specific, so a programme must reflect the demands of the particular sport and position. From “Specificity”
  • Increasing frequency, intensity and time all at once. This raises injury risk and makes it hard to tell which change caused the response. From “Progressive overload and FITT”
  • Assuming fitness is kept for months without training. Aerobic fitness can decline noticeably within a few weeks, so maintenance work matters. From “Reversibility”
  • Copying an elite athlete's programme exactly. Their training reflects their own history, genetics and recovery capacity, so it may overload or underload someone else. From “Individual differences, variation and recovery”

Fitness Testing and Performance Analysis

  • Using a single test score to judge a player's overall ability. Tests measure isolated components and should be combined with match performance and other information. From “Why test?”
  • Thinking a reliable test must be valid. A test can give consistent results while measuring the wrong thing, such as using a 12-minute run to assess sprint speed. From “Validity, reliability and objectivity”
  • Testing everyone maximally without screening. Screening identifies people at increased risk who need medical clearance first. From “Pre-test procedures and health screening”
  • Treating a signature as enough for consent. Consent is only informed if the person understood the purpose, risks and right to withdraw, and agreed without pressure. From “Ethics and informed consent in testing”

Sport and Exercise Nutrition

  • Treating all sugar as the same. The sugars in whole fruit and plain milk come with fibre or other nutrients and aren't counted as free sugars; fruit juice and honey are. From “Carbohydrates and dietary fibre”
  • Assuming more protein always builds more muscle. Beyond requirements, extra protein is used for energy or excreted; muscle growth depends on resistance training and overall energy intake. From “Protein”
  • Assuming "low fat" always means healthier. Some low-fat products replace fat with sugar or refined starch; replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrate doesn't lower heart disease risk. From “Fats”
  • Assuming high-dose supplements are always safe. Fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate; excess vitamin A in pregnancy is harmful, so pregnant women should avoid liver and vitamin A supplements. From “Vitamins”

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

  • Assuming rewards always increase motivation. For enjoyable tasks, expected tangible rewards can reduce interest. From “Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation”
  • Assuming all behaviour reduces tension. People often seek stimulation, novelty and challenge. From “Drive theories”
  • Thinking dopamine is the brain's "pleasure chemical". It is more closely linked to wanting and motivation. From “Incentive theories”
  • Equating autonomy with independence. In SDT, autonomy means acting from self-endorsed values, which can include following others' guidance. From “Self-determination theory”

Skill Acquisition and Coaching

  • Using skill and ability as if they mean the same. Abilities are largely innate traits; skills are learned through practice and depend on those abilities. From “The nature of skill”
  • Classifying a skill without a reason. A placement on a continuum must be justified by a feature of the skill, such as the stability of the environment. From “Classifying skills”
  • Assuming a person with a high general sporting ability will be good at every sport. Abilities are specific, so a person may have the abilities for one sport but not another. From “Abilities”
  • Using massed practice for beginners learning a complex skill. Distributed practice allows rest, feedback and mental rehearsal, which helps beginners. From “Types of practice”

Injury Prevention and Rehabilitation

  • Ignoring early overuse pain. Mild pain after training can be the first sign of a stress fracture or tendinopathy. From “Acute and chronic injuries”
  • Confusing strains and sprains. Strains affect muscles or tendons; sprains affect ligaments. From “Soft-tissue injuries”
  • Assuming a child who can move a limb cannot have a fracture. Greenstick and growth-plate fractures can still allow some movement. From “Hard-tissue injuries”
  • Thinking concussion requires loss of consciousness. Most concussions occur without it. From “Concussion”

Physical Activity

  • Thinking only sport or gym exercise counts. All movement counts towards activity guidelines, including active travel, housework and play. From “Physical activity, exercise and fitness”
  • Forgetting the strength recommendation. Many people who meet the aerobic target don't do muscle-strengthening activity, which is a separate guideline. From “The UK physical activity guidelines”
  • Assuming a daily workout cancels out a sedentary day. High sitting time carries risk unless offset by large amounts of activity; reducing and breaking up sitting matters too. From “Sedentary behaviour”
  • Expecting exercise alone to produce large weight loss. Activity has many health benefits even without weight loss; for weight loss, diet changes usually matter more. From “The benefits of physical activity”
