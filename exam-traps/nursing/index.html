Nursing: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Nursing lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Nursing and Professional Practice

  • Posting about patients, even without names, can breach confidentiality if they could be identified from the details. From “Professional identity”
  • Silence or a lack of objection isn't consent. Check understanding and agreement. From “Consent”
  • Viewing your own family member's record "just to check" without a care reason is still a breach. From “Data protection”
  • Never document care in advance of giving it, or alter a record after an event without making the change clear. From “Documentation”

Human Anatomy and Physiology

  • Describing "left" as the left side of your view when facing the patient. Anatomical left is always the patient's left. From “Anatomical terminology”
  • Thinking osmosis moves solutes. Osmosis is the movement of water, towards the side with more dissolved particles. From “Cells and tissues”
  • Being reassured by a normal blood pressure in a patient who is tachycardic and breathing fast. They may be compensating. From “Homeostasis”
  • Relying on redness alone to detect early pressure damage. On darker skin, check temperature, firmness and the person's own report. From “The integumentary system”

Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning

  • A normal score on a tool does not overrule your clinical concern. If you are worried, escalate. From “Introduction to clinical assessment”
  • Diagnostic overshadowing is a known cause of avoidable deaths in people with learning disabilities. Assess new symptoms as new. From “Person-centred assessment”
  • Treating a fever with antipyretics doesn't treat its cause. Look for the source and escalate as needed. From “Temperature”
  • Never estimate or write "18" by habit. Inaccurate respiratory rates hide deterioration. From “Respiratory rate”

Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills

  • Using alcohol rub after caring for someone with diarrhoea. C. difficile spores survive alcohol: wash with soap and water. From “Hand hygiene”
  • Thinking SICPs only matter for "infectious" patients. Most transmission comes from people not yet known to carry an infection. From “Standard infection precautions”
  • Wearing the same gloves between tasks or patients. Gloves become contaminated; change them and clean your hands. From “Personal protective equipment”
  • Assuming sterile gloves make any touch acceptable. Non-touch technique still matters, because gloves can be contaminated during the procedure. From “Aseptic technique”

Clinical Practice and Care Planning

  • Treating the care plan as paperwork completed on admission and never revisited. A plan that isn't evaluated and updated stops reflecting the person. From “Nursing process”
  • Letting a risk tool score override your clinical judgement. Tools support decisions; escalate a concern even if the score is low. From “Assessment”
  • Writing a medical diagnosis, such as "COPD", as a nursing problem. Describe the person's response, such as breathlessness limiting self-care. From “Nursing diagnoses and problem identification”
  • Copying a standard care plan without tailoring it. Standard plans must be individualised to the person's needs and goals. From “Care planning”

Infection Prevention and Control

  • Treating colonisation as infection. A positive swab without signs of infection doesn't always need treatment, though it may need precautions. From “Microorganisms”
  • Using alcohol products alone where spore-forming bacteria are involved. Spores survive alcohol. From “Bacteria”
  • Relying on alcohol hand rub during a norovirus outbreak. Norovirus is non-enveloped and resists alcohol; wash with soap and water. From “Viruses”
  • Ignoring oral thrush as trivial. It causes discomfort, affects eating and can signal immunosuppression or antibiotic effects. From “Fungi”

Acute, Emergency and Critical Care Nursing

  • Fixating on one task while a patient's condition changes around you. Step back regularly and reassess the whole picture. From “Acute nursing principles”
  • Dismissing a high respiratory rate because the patient "always breathes fast". Changes from baseline matter; never normalise without review. From “Recognition of deterioration”
  • Moving on to breathing while an airway problem is untreated. Treat each problem before moving to the next. From “ABCDE assessment”
  • Using NEWS2 for a pregnant woman. Use the maternity early warning score, which accounts for the physiological changes of pregnancy. From “Early warning scores”

Adult Nursing

  • Recording observations without acting on the score. A NEWS2 of 5 or a single red score of 3 needs urgent review. From “Adult health assessment”
  • Focusing only on the medical diagnosis. Delirium, falls and deconditioning often decide how a medical patient's stay ends. From “Medical nursing”
  • Fasting patients from midnight regardless of operation time. Prolonged fasting causes dehydration and distress; follow current fasting guidance. From “Surgical nursing”
  • Giving high-flow oxygen to every patient with chest pain. Oxygen is given only if saturation is below target. From “Cardiovascular nursing”

Children's and Young People's Nursing

  • Being reassured by a normal blood pressure in an unwell child. Children maintain blood pressure until late; tachycardia, tachypnoea and capillary refill change earlier. From “Introduction to paediatric nursing”
  • Explaining a procedure to a 4-year-old using abstract ideas. Preschool children think concretely and magically; use simple words, play and reassurance. From “Child development”
  • Plotting a premature baby's measurements at their actual age. Correct for prematurity, or growth will look falsely poor. From “Growth and development”
  • Reassuring parents that a child "will catch up" without assessment. Red flags and parental concern need proper assessment and referral. From “Developmental assessment”

Mental Health Nursing

  • Using labels such as "a schizophrenic" or "a self-harmer". Use person-first, respectful language. From “Foundations of mental health nursing”
  • Assuming new confusion or hallucinations are psychiatric without checking physical causes. Infection, delirium or other illness may be responsible. From “Mental health assessment”
  • Recording conclusions such as "psychotic" instead of describing what was observed. Describe specific behaviours and statements. From “Mental state examination”
  • Promising to always be available. It sets up disappointment; be reliable about what you can offer and plan endings. From “Therapeutic relationships”

Learning Disability Nursing

  • Assuming poorer health outcomes are inevitable in learning disability. Many early deaths are avoidable with good, adjusted care. From “Foundations of learning disability nursing”
  • Diagnosing learning disability from an IQ score alone. Adaptive functioning and onset before adulthood are also required. From “Intellectual disability”
  • Attributing all difficulties to one known condition. Co-occurring conditions such as ADHD or sensory impairment may be missed. From “Neurodevelopmental conditions”
  • Interpreting a meltdown as deliberate bad behaviour. It is a response to overwhelm; reduce demands and stimulation. From “Autism”

Community, Public Health and Health Promotion

  • Thinking public health is only for specialists. Every nurse protects and promotes health in daily practice. From “Public health foundations”
  • Blaming individuals for unhealthy behaviours without considering their living conditions. Behaviour is shaped by social and economic circumstances. From “Social determinants of health”
  • Treating everyone identically and expecting equal outcomes. Proportionate universalism gives more support where need is greater. From “Health inequalities”
  • Being persuaded by a large relative risk without checking the absolute risk. A doubling of a very rare risk may still be small. From “Epidemiology”

Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing

  • Assuming normal blood results mean normal kidney function in an older person. Low muscle mass can make creatinine look normal despite reduced function. From “Ageing physiology”
  • Assuming frailty is fixed and irreversible. Exercise, nutrition and treating reversible causes can improve it. From “Frailty”
  • Measuring blood pressure only lying down. Lying and standing measurements are needed to detect orthostatic hypotension. From “Falls”
  • Giving an antipsychotic to a person with dementia with Lewy bodies for hallucinations without specialist advice. Severe, sometimes fatal reactions can occur. From “Dementia”

Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership

  • Assuming any published study is trustworthy. Publication doesn't guarantee quality; appraise it. From “Research literacy”
  • Treating "statistically significant" as meaning "clinically important". A tiny difference can be statistically significant in a large study. From “Quantitative research”
  • Criticising a qualitative study for having a small sample that isn't statistically representative. Qualitative research aims for depth, not statistical generalisation. From “Qualitative research”
  • Assuming mixed methods are automatically better. They add value only when integration answers the question better than one method alone. From “Mixed methods”

Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding

  • Thinking the NMC's main purpose is to represent nurses. It regulates to protect the public; trade unions and professional bodies such as the RCN represent nurses. From “Professional regulation”
  • Treating the Code as applying only at work. Conduct outside work, including online, can affect fitness to practise. From “The NMC Code”
  • Thinking inexperience lowers the standard expected. The standard is that of the post; seek help when unsure. From “Duty of care”
  • Thinking a signed form proves consent. Consent depends on capacity, information and voluntariness; the form is only evidence. From “Consent”
