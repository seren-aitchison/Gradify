Design Thinking and Creativity: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Design Thinking and Creativity lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Design Thinking

  • Equating design with appearance. A beautiful chair that collapses, or an app that looks polished but confuses users, is badly designed. Appearance matters because it affects use and emotion, but it is one requirement among many. From “What is design?”
  • Treating movements as tidy, sequential periods. They overlapped, many designers worked across them, and dates are approximate markers. From “A short history of design”
  • Treating design thinking as a fixed five-step recipe. The models are scaffolds; experienced designers move back and forth between activities as they learn. From “Design thinking defined”
  • Assuming a process that loops back has failed. Iteration is the normal, efficient route to a good design: it is cheaper to learn from a sketch than from a finished product. From “Design as a process, not a product”

The Psychology of Creativity

  • Thinking creativity is only for geniuses or artists. Research treats creativity as a normal capacity found at every level, from mini-c to Big-C. From “What is creativity?”
  • Studying only the person. Treating creativity as an individual gift ignores the methods and environments that research shows make a large difference. From “The four Ps of creativity”
  • Treating a divergent thinking score as "your creativity". It measures one component, idea generation, not the knowledge, judgement and persistence that creative achievement also needs. From “Divergent thinking and its measurement”
  • Waiting for inspiration. The model shows illumination follows preparation; doing nothing without first working on the problem rarely produces insight. From “Stages of the creative process”

Ideation Methods

  • Assuming a lively, noisy meeting means a productive one. Energy is not output; production blocking means many ideas are never voiced. From “Brainstorming and its rules”
  • Treating the 108 ideas as 108 good ideas. Brainwriting produces quantity; convergence, clustering and evaluation are still needed. From “Brainwriting and 6-3-5”
  • Using SCAMPER without a problem in mind. The prompts generate variations; you still need research and criteria to know which variations matter to users. From “SCAMPER”
  • Producing a decorative mind map as a portfolio requirement and never using it. A mind map is a thinking tool; its ideas should feed into research questions and design directions. From “Mind mapping”

User Research and Empathy

  • Treating research as a box to tick at the start. Research is most valuable when it shapes decisions throughout, from framing to testing. From “Why research users”
  • Choosing a method first ("let's do a survey") and then deciding what to ask. Method should follow question. From “Research questions and plans”
  • Treating qualitative findings as statistics ("4 of 6 users said..., so 67% of customers...") or treating numbers as explanations. Small samples reveal issues; they don't estimate percentages. From “Qualitative and quantitative research”
  • Assuming public behaviour can be recorded freely. Filming identifiable people for research still raises consent and data protection issues. From “Research ethics and consent”

Defining Problems

  • Reframing endlessly. Reframing is valuable early, but a team must eventually commit to a frame, test it and act. From “Problem framing and reframing”
  • Writing the need as a noun or a feature ("needs an app", "needs a better website"). That decides the solution before ideation. From “Point-of-view statements”
  • Writing the solution into the brief ("design a phone app that..."). Unless the solution type is a genuine constraint, it narrows creativity too early. From “Design briefs”
  • Writing specification points that describe a solution ("must be made of wood with a hinge") rather than requirements, unless the material is truly required. From “Design specifications”

Prototyping, Testing and Iteration

  • Building a polished prototype before knowing what question it should answer. Effort spent on detail early is often wasted when the concept changes. From “Why prototype”
  • Showing a polished prototype too early. People comment on colours and fonts and hesitate to criticise something that looks finished, so concept problems go unnoticed. From “Prototype fidelity”
  • Making paper prototypes too neat. Rough sketches are faster and signal that feedback is welcome. From “Paper prototyping”
  • Treating a wireframe as a finished design to hand over. Wireframes need annotation, testing and visual design before build. From “Wireframes”

The Visual Language of Art and Design

  • Treating "art" as meaning only painting and sculpture. Exam specifications and galleries include photography, film, installation, performance, textiles, ceramics and digital work. From “What is art and design?”
  • Thinking AO3 means only drawing. Recording includes photography, written annotation, digital capture, maquettes and notes, so long as they record observations and ideas relevant to your intentions. From “How art and design are assessed: the four assessment objectives”
  • Jumping straight to meaning ("it's about loneliness") without describing and analysing first. Examiners reward interpretations that are built on visual evidence. From “Looking closely: an approach to visual analysis”
  • Thinking line only means outline. Most expressive drawing uses varied, broken and internal contour lines; a single even outline flattens a form. From “Line”

Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Treating accessibility as a final check before launch. Retrofitting is expensive; accessibility is cheapest when designed in from the start. From “Why accessibility matters”
  • Assuming language preferences are universal. Many UK disabled people prefer "disabled people", while elsewhere "people with disabilities" is common; some prefer identity-first terms such as "autistic person". Ask, and follow the person's preference. From “Models of disability”
  • Equating inclusive design with meeting WCAG. Standards matter, but a design can pass checks and still exclude people in practice. From “Inclusive design principles”
  • Assuming one design can suit absolutely everyone. Universal design aims for "the greatest extent possible"; some needs still require options or assistive technology. From “Universal design”

Mechanics of Materials

  • Mixing units. Using N with mm2 gives N/mm2, which equals MPa; using N with m2 gives Pa. Keep units consistent throughout a calculation. From “Normal stress and strain”
  • Forgetting double shear. If a bolt passes through three plates with the load shared across two planes, using the single-shear area doubles the calculated stress. From “Shear stress and strain”
  • Confusing stiffness with strength. Young's modulus measures stiffness (resistance to elastic deformation); yield and ultimate strength measure resistance to permanent deformation and fracture. From “Hooke's law and elastic constants”
  • Using the total load for every segment of a stepped bar. Each segment's internal force must be found by equilibrium at a section through that segment. From “Axially loaded members”

Service and Systems Design

  • Treating service design as designing a website or app. Digital touchpoints are part of a service, but the policies, staff and systems behind them often matter more. From “What is service design?”
  • Drawing a blueprint from what the process manual says. Blueprints should be based on research into how the service really runs, including workarounds. From “Service blueprints”
  • Designing each channel separately. Users move between channels, so information, language and status must carry across them. From “Touchpoints and channels”
  • Calling a feedback survey on a finished design co-design. Co-design involves people in decisions throughout the process, not just commenting at the end. From “Co-design and participatory design”

Sustainable and Ethical Design

  • Thinking environmental impact is mainly a manufacturing or recycling problem. Designers set the material, energy and lifespan choices that determine impact across the whole life cycle. From “Design and the environment”
  • Comparing products per item rather than per functional unit. A reusable product must be compared with the number of disposable items it replaces. From “Life cycle assessment”
  • Equating the circular economy with recycling. Recycling is the outermost loop; keeping products in use through sharing, repair and reuse retains more value. From “The circular economy”
  • Thinking recyclable packaging solves the waste problem. Avoiding or reusing packaging prevents waste, which is better than recycling it. From “Designing out waste”

Collaboration, Communication and Intellectual Property

  • Avoiding all disagreement to keep the peace. Constructive conflict about ideas improves designs; the aim is to keep it about the work, not the person. From “Creative collaboration”
  • Running a workshop without a clear purpose or outcome. Without them, activities fill time but decisions are not made. From “Workshops and facilitation”
  • Treating critique as a personal attack or a list of instructions. It is an analysis of the work against its goals, and the designer decides how to respond. From “Giving and receiving critique”
  • Presenting finished designs to stakeholders for the first time at the end. Late involvement leads to surprises, rework and resistance. From “Working with stakeholders”
