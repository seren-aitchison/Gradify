History: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our History lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Historical Thinking and Methods

  • Thinking that because historians disagree, any opinion about the past is as good as any other. Interpretations must be supported by evidence and can be judged by how well they account for it. From “What is history?”
  • Assuming primary sources are always more reliable than secondary ones. A primary source can be mistaken, partial or propaganda; a good secondary source may weigh hundreds of primary sources. From “Primary and secondary sources”
  • Treating a single source as proof. One source supports an inference; a claim becomes secure when several independent sources point the same way. From “Historical evidence”
  • Writing "the source is biased" and stopping. Provenance analysis should explain how the author's position shapes the content and what the source is still useful for. From “Source provenance”

Medieval Britain

  • Picturing the Anglo-Saxons as primitive. Late Anglo-Saxon England had sophisticated government, coinage, law and art. From “Anglo-Saxon England”
  • Thinking Vikings were only raiders. Many were farmers, traders and settlers whose communities became part of English society. From “The Viking invasions”
  • Remembering Alfred mainly for the story of the burnt cakes. The story first appears in later sources, long after his death. From “Alfred the Great”
  • Assuming William's victory proves his claim was the strongest. Victory decided the throne, not the legal merits. From “The Norman Conquest”

Tudor England

  • Thinking Henry VII's throne was safe after Bosworth. He faced rebellions and pretenders for over a decade. From “Henry VII and dynastic security”
  • Thinking Henry VIII ran the daily business of government himself. His ministers did most of the work. From “Henry VIII's government”
  • Thinking Henry VIII made England Protestant. He rejected the Pope's authority but kept much Catholic doctrine. From “The English Reformation”
  • Thinking the dissolution made the crown permanently rich. Much of the land was sold quickly to pay for war. From “The dissolution of the monasteries”

Stuart Britain and the Civil War

  • Thinking the Union of the Crowns in 1603 created one United Kingdom. The parliaments of England and Scotland were not united until 1707. From “James I and the Union of the Crowns”
  • Thinking divine right meant James claimed he could ignore the law whenever he liked. He usually said a good king would rule by law. From “The divine right of kings”
  • Thinking the Petition of Right stopped Charles I. He ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640. From “Parliament and taxation”
  • Thinking Laud was a Catholic. He was a Protestant churchman, but his reforms looked Catholic to his critics. From “Religious tensions under the early Stuarts”

The Industrial Revolution

  • Crediting a few famous innovators with transforming farming. Change depended on thousands of farmers and spread slowly. From “Agricultural change”
  • Assuming population grew mainly because people lived longer. Wrigley and Schofield found that rising fertility mattered more. From “Population growth”
  • Thinking most British workers worked in factories by 1850. Agriculture, domestic service and small workshops still employed more people. From “The factory system”
  • Thinking the Luddites simply hated machines. They protested against low wages and the loss of skilled work. From “The textile industry”

Nineteenth-Century Britain

  • Thinking the empire was a single system. Settler colonies, India, crown colonies and informal influence were governed very differently. From “The British Empire in the nineteenth century”
  • Thinking Victoria had no influence. She could still be consulted, encourage and warn, and she tried to influence ministers. From “Victorian monarchy”
  • Thinking all men could vote after 1884. Around four in ten adult men, and all women, still could not. From “Parliamentary reform, 1867–1884”
  • Dating British democracy to a single act. It developed in stages from 1867 to 1928, and some would add 1948 or 1969. From “The development of democracy”

The First World War

  • Thinking the Great Powers were divided into two fixed camps for decades. The alignments of 1914 formed gradually, and as late as the 1890s Britain was at odds with France and Russia over empire. From “European power politics”
  • Thinking militarism means only that countries had armies. The point is the arms race, the influence of military planning on policy, and plans that left little time for diplomacy. From “Militarism”
  • Assuming Britain was bound by alliance to fight in 1914. The ententes were understandings; Britain entered over the invasion of Belgium. From “Alliances”
  • Treating imperial rivalry as only about overseas colonies. The most dangerous rivalry was in the Balkans, on Europe's own doorstep. From “Imperial rivalry”

The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union

  • Thinking the Russian Empire was mainly Russian. Fewer than half its people were Russians in 1897. From “Tsarist Russia”
  • Thinking Rasputin ran the government. He influenced Alexandra and some appointments, but rumour exaggerated his power. From “Nicholas II”
  • Confusing the Bolsheviks with all Russian socialists. They were one faction of the Social Democrats, alongside the Mensheviks and the Socialist Revolutionaries. From “Russian society and inequality”
  • Thinking the October Manifesto made Russia a constitutional monarchy. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 kept supreme power with the tsar. From “The 1905 Revolution”

The Rise of Fascism and Nazism

  • Thinking Italy was on the losing side in the war. Italy won, but many Italians felt the peace denied them what they had been promised. From “Post-war Italy”
  • Believing the March on Rome overthrew the government by force. The king appointed Mussolini legally; the march was a threat that worked. From “Mussolini”
  • Assuming Mussolini had absolute power from 1922. He led a coalition until the dictatorship was built in 1925–1926, and the king and Church remained independent forces. From “The development of Italian Fascism”
  • Blaming the constitution alone for Weimar's collapse. Its weaknesses mattered most in combination with economic crisis and the choices of its leaders. From “The Weimar Republic”

The Second World War

  • Thinking war in 1939 began over the Sudetenland. It began when Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. From “The causes of the Second World War”
  • Assuming appeasement had no supporters. In 1938 it was popular, and Chamberlain was cheered on his return from Munich. From “Appeasement”
  • Thinking Poland was defeated by Germany alone. The Soviet invasion from the east sealed its defeat. From “The invasion of Poland”
  • Assuming Germany won because it had many more tanks. The Allies had as many tanks; German planning and speed made the difference. From “The fall of France”

The Cold War

  • Thinking the Cold War began on a single day. It developed between 1945 and 1947 through a series of decisions on both sides. From “Origins of the Cold War”
  • Thinking communism and capitalism were only economic systems. Each was also a set of political beliefs about how society should be run. From “Capitalism and communism”
  • Thinking every eastern European communist state took orders from Moscow. Tito's Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948. From “The division of Europe”
  • Assuming Churchill was prime minister when he gave the speech. He had lost the 1945 election and spoke as leader of the opposition. From “The Iron Curtain”

Britain since 1945

  • Thinking rationing ended with the war. Some rationing tightened after 1945, and it did not finally end until 1954. From “Post-war reconstruction”
  • Thinking the welfare state was created entirely by Labour from nothing. It built on earlier reforms, the wartime coalition's plans and the 1944 Education Act passed under the coalition. From “The welfare state”
  • Thinking the NHS has always been completely free. Charges for dentures and spectacles began in 1951 and prescription charges in 1952. From “The creation of the NHS”
  • Thinking comprehensives replaced grammar schools everywhere. Some areas kept selection, and grammar schools still exist in parts of England. From “Housing and education reform”

The United States in the Twentieth Century

  • Thinking Progressive reform helped all Americans equally. Segregation deepened in the same years. From “Progressive reform”
  • Thinking the crash alone caused the Depression. Weak banks, inequality, overproduction and policy mistakes turned a crash into a depression. From “The Great Depression”
  • Thinking Roosevelt opposed segregation. He avoided challenging it to keep the support of Southern Democrats. From “Franklin D. Roosevelt”
  • Thinking the New Deal ended the Depression. Unemployment was still around 15% in 1940; war spending ended it. From “The New Deal”

Global History and Empires

  • Assuming the Ottoman Empire was in decline for three centuries. Recent historians stress its adaptation and reform. From “The Ottoman Empire”
  • Blaming the Mughal decline on Aurangzeb's religious policies alone. Overstretch, succession disputes and regional rivals mattered at least as much. From “The Mughal Empire”
  • Thinking China was weak and isolated throughout Qing rule. In the eighteenth century it was one of the world's largest and most prosperous states. From “The Qing dynasty”
  • Thinking Japan simply copied the West. It adapted Western models selectively, keeping imperial ideology and its own traditions. From “The rise of modern Japan”

Revolutions and Political Change

  • Assuming that people in 1642 set out to make a revolution. Few did; radical changes came as the war and its aftermath unfolded. From “The English Revolution”
  • Thinking "all men are created equal" applied to everyone in 1776. Slavery continued, and women and Indigenous peoples were excluded. From “The American Revolution”
  • Thinking the revolution aimed at a republic from 1789. Most early revolutionaries wanted a constitutional monarchy. From “The French Revolution”
  • Treating the Atlantic revolutions as a story only of Europe and North America. Haiti tested their ideals most radically. From “The Haitian Revolution in comparison”

Religion, Culture and Society

  • Thinking the medieval Church was unchanging and unchallenged. It was reformed repeatedly and criticised by figures such as Wycliffe and Hus. From “Medieval Christianity”
  • Thinking Luther meant to split the Church in 1517. He sought reform; division followed from conflict over authority. From “The European Reformation”
  • Treating the wars of religion as purely religious. Catholic France fought the Catholic Habsburgs for political reasons. From “Religious conflict in Europe”
  • Treating the Enlightenment as a single agreed programme. Its thinkers disagreed about religion, politics and human nature. From “The Enlightenment”

A Level Historical Investigation

  • Choosing a topic rather than a question. Without a question, research becomes description. From “Constructing historical enquiries”
  • Keeping the first version of a question unchanged. Research questions usually need refining as evidence is found. From “Developing research questions”
  • Choosing only sources that support the argument. A strong investigation also uses sources that challenge it. From “Selecting primary sources”
  • Dismissing a biased or forged source as useless. Even a forgery is valuable evidence for some questions. From “Evaluating provenance in depth”

Historical Examination and Revision

  • Writing "the source is biased so it is not useful". A biased source is useful evidence of its maker's aims and methods. From “Source analysis questions”
  • Simply agreeing or disagreeing with an interpretation. Explain why it says what it does, then test it. From “Interpretation analysis questions”
  • Listing causes in separate paragraphs without linking them. Show how causes interacted to produce the event. From “Causation questions”
  • Assuming the Treaty of Versailles alone caused the Second World War. Many later events, including the Depression, intervened. From “Consequence questions”
