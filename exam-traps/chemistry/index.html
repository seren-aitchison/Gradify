Chemistry: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Chemistry lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Chemistry

  • Thinking "chemicals" are only artificial substances. Everything, including water and the food we eat, is made of chemicals. From “What is chemistry?”
  • Calling O₂ a compound because it contains two atoms. Both atoms are oxygen, so it is an element made of molecules. From “Matter and its classification”
  • Thinking a compound keeps the properties of its elements, like a mixture does. Sodium chloride isn't reactive or toxic like its elements. From “Elements and compounds”
  • Calling dissolving a chemical change. Dissolving salt is a physical process: evaporating the water gets the salt back. From “Mixtures”

Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

  • Picturing electrons as tiny planets in fixed orbits close to the nucleus. Electrons occupy a large region around it, described by probabilities, as later lessons explain. From “Structure of the atom”
  • Changing the number of protons to make an ion. Ions form by electron transfer; changing the protons would make a different element. From “Protons, neutrons and electrons”
  • Saying isotopes are different elements. They are the same element, because the number of protons is the same. From “Isotopes”
  • Using relative atomic mass as the mass number. Mass number is always a whole number for one nucleus. From “Atomic number and mass number”

Periodic Table and Periodicity

  • Thinking Mendeleev ordered elements strictly by atomic mass. He broke mass order when properties required it. From “Development of the periodic table”
  • Thinking the table is ordered by mass. It is ordered by atomic number, the number of protons. From “Modern periodic law”
  • Thinking elements in the same period have similar chemistry. Similar chemistry comes from being in the same group. From “Periods and groups”
  • Thinking s-block metals are found as pure metals in nature. They are too reactive and occur as compounds. From “s-block elements”

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

  • Thinking atoms "want" a full shell. Bonding happens because it lowers energy, not because atoms have desires. From “Introduction to chemical bonding”
  • Thinking ionic compounds conduct when solid. Ions are fixed in the solid lattice and cannot move. From “Ionic bonding”
  • Thinking covalent bonds break when water boils. Boiling separates molecules; the O–H bonds remain intact. From “Covalent bonding”
  • Thinking a dative bond stays different after forming. It becomes indistinguishable from other covalent bonds. From “Coordinate bonding”

States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

  • Using degrees Celsius in gas law calculations. Temperature must be converted to kelvin. From “Gases”
  • Doubling the Celsius temperature and expecting volume to double. Only doubling the kelvin temperature doubles volume. From “Gas laws”
  • Using volume in dm³ with pressure in pascals. Volume must be in m³ when pressure is in pascals. From “Ideal gas equation”
  • Thinking real gases always behave ideally. Deviations are significant at high pressure and low temperature. From “Real gases”

Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

  • Thinking energy is "used up" in reactions. It is transferred, not destroyed. From “Energy in chemical systems”
  • Thinking exothermic reactions need no energy to start. Many need activation energy first. From “Exothermic reactions”
  • Thinking endothermic reactions cannot happen without heating. Some happen spontaneously because entropy increases. From “Endothermic reactions”
  • Ignoring state symbols in thermochemical equations. Different states give different ΔH values. From “Enthalpy”

Chemical Kinetics

  • Thinking rate stays constant throughout a reaction. It usually decreases as reactants are used up. From “Introduction to reaction rates”
  • Thinking every collision causes a reaction. Most collisions lack enough energy or the right orientation. From “Collision theory”
  • Thinking catalysts give particles more energy. Catalysts lower the activation energy instead. From “Activation energy”
  • Confusing transition states with intermediates. Transition states are peaks; intermediates are troughs. From “Reaction profiles”

Chemical Equilibrium

  • Thinking reversible reactions stop when products form. Both directions continue to occur. From “Reversible reactions”
  • Thinking concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not necessarily equal. From “Dynamic equilibrium”
  • Thinking adding more reactant changes K. Only temperature changes K; the position shifts but K stays the same. From “Equilibrium constants”
  • Using initial concentrations in Kc. Only equilibrium concentrations are used. From “Kc”

Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

  • Thinking all bases are alkalis. Only soluble bases are alkalis. From “Introduction to acids and bases”
  • Thinking H⁺ ions exist freely in water. They bond to water molecules as H₃O⁺. From “Arrhenius theory”
  • Thinking a conjugate base must contain OH⁻. It is simply the species left after the acid loses a proton. From “Brønsted-Lowry theory”
  • Thinking Lewis acids must contain hydrogen. BF₃ and metal ions are Lewis acids without hydrogen. From “Lewis acid-base theory”

Redox and Electrochemistry

  • Thinking oxidation always involves oxygen. Oxidation is fundamentally loss of electrons. From “Oxidation and reduction”
  • Thinking every element changes oxidation state in a redox reaction. Spectator elements stay the same. From “Oxidation states”
  • Leaving electrons in the overall equation. Electrons must cancel when half-equations are combined. From “Redox equations”
  • Adding O₂ or O atoms to balance oxygen. Use water molecules instead. From “Balancing redox reactions”

Inorganic Chemistry

  • Thinking inorganic chemistry only covers metals. It includes non-metals and their compounds too. From “Introduction to inorganic chemistry”
  • Thinking all chlorides simply dissolve in water. Covalent chlorides such as SiCl₄ react with it. From “Main-group chemistry”
  • Thinking all group 1 metals form the same oxide. Lithium, sodium and potassium form different oxygen compounds. From “s-block chemistry”
  • Thinking all bonds are two-centre two-electron bonds. Boranes contain three-centre two-electron bonds. From “p-block chemistry”

Organic Chemistry Foundations

  • Thinking "organic" means natural or chemical-free. In chemistry, it simply means compounds based on carbon. From “Introduction to organic chemistry”
  • Thinking all carbon atoms in a molecule have the same shape. Each carbon's geometry depends on its own bonds. From “Carbon bonding”
  • Thinking any compound with carbon and hydrogen is a hydrocarbon. Hydrocarbons contain only those two elements. From “Hydrocarbons”
  • Thinking free-radical substitution gives a single clean product. It gives a mixture of substituted products. From “Alkanes”

Organic Reaction Mechanisms

  • Drawing an arrow from a positive charge. Positive charges have no electrons to give. From “Curly arrow notation”
  • Thinking nucleophiles must be negatively charged. Neutral molecules such as ammonia and water are nucleophiles too. From “Electrophiles and nucleophiles”
  • Thinking heterolysis puts the electrons on either atom at random. The more electronegative atom usually takes them. From “Homolytic and heterolytic cleavage”
  • Thinking primary carbocations are the most stable because they are least crowded. Alkyl groups stabilise the charge. From “Carbocations”

Functional Group Chemistry

  • Thinking alcohols are alkaline because they contain OH. The –OH is covalently bonded and not released as hydroxide. From “Alcohols”
  • Thinking phenol is an alcohol. The –OH on a ring changes its acidity and reactions. From “Phenols”
  • Using a tertiary haloalkane in a Williamson synthesis. Elimination wins instead of substitution. From “Ethers”
  • Thinking 2,4-DNPH distinguishes aldehydes from ketones. It reacts with both; Tollens' tells them apart. From “Aldehydes”

Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy

  • Thinking the instrument is the most important step. Poor sampling or preparation ruins even precise measurements. From “Foundations of analytical chemistry”
  • Adding barium chloride without acid first. Carbonate would also give a white precipitate. From “Qualitative analysis”
  • Thinking a detected substance can always be quantified. Between the LOD and LOQ it is detected but not reliably measured. From “Quantitative analysis”
  • Thinking precise results must be accurate. Results can be tightly grouped but all wrong. From “Accuracy and precision”

Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science

  • Thinking molecules are rigid with fixed bond lengths. Vibration means measured values are averages. From “Molecular structure”
  • Using Lewis structures to predict magnetism. They fail for O₂, which MO theory explains. From “Molecular orbitals”
  • Thinking quantum chemistry solves molecules exactly. Only one-electron systems have exact solutions. From “Quantum chemistry”
  • Thinking ψ itself is a probability. The probability density is |ψ|². From “Schrödinger equation”

Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry

  • Thinking one material is best for every job. Each application needs a different balance of properties. From “Materials chemistry”
  • Thinking an alloy is a chemical compound. It is a mixture with variable composition. From “Metals and alloys”
  • Thinking all plastics can be remelted. Thermosets are cross-linked and cannot be melted. From “Polymers”
  • Drawing the repeat unit with the C=C still present. The double bond opens during polymerisation. From “Polymerisation”

Synthetic Chemistry and Laboratory Methods

  • Relying only on gloves and goggles. Removing or substituting hazards comes first in the hierarchy of controls. From “Laboratory safety”
  • Changing more than one variable at once. It becomes impossible to tell which caused the effect. From “Experimental planning”
  • Pouring unused reagent back into the bottle. This can contaminate the whole stock. From “Reagent handling”
  • Heating a sealed system. Pressure can build up and break the glassware. From “Reaction setup”
