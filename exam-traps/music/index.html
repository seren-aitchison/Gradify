Music: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Music lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Notation and Rhythm

  • Reading bass clef as if it were treble. A note on the bottom line is E in treble clef but G in bass clef; learners who memorise only treble positions misread every bass note by a third (two letter names). From “Pitch, the stave and clefs”
  • Assuming the octave number changes at A. In scientific pitch notation it changes at C: B3 is directly below C4, so the A just below middle C is A3 and the A just above is A4. From “Note names, ledger lines and octave registers”
  • Carrying an accidental into the next bar. Players often keep a sharp from the previous bar; the rule is that the barline cancels it, which is exactly why editors add cautionary accidentals there. From “Accidentals: sharps, flats and naturals”
  • Treating enharmonic spellings as interchangeable. In an exam a G♭ written where the key demands F♯ is marked wrong, because the spelling changes the interval and shows the wrong function. From “Tones, semitones and enharmonic equivalents”

Scales, Keys and Intervals

  • Spelling with the wrong letter names. F major is F G A B♭ C D E F, not F G A A♯ C D E F: A♯ repeats the letter A and skips B. From “The major scale”
  • Writing sharps or flats in a key signature out of order. B♭ major is always B♭ then E♭; writing E♭ then B♭ is incorrect even though the same two notes are flattened. From “Key signatures and the circle of fifths”
  • Thinking the subdominant is named because it is "just below the dominant". It is a fifth below the tonic, mirroring the dominant a fifth above; that it sits a step below the dominant is a consequence. From “Scale degrees and their names”
  • Writing the raised seventh in the key signature. Key signatures follow the natural minor; the raised notes of harmonic and melodic forms are written as accidentals. From “Natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales”

Chords and Harmony

  • Assuming the lowest note is always the root. In inversions and spread chords, another note can be in the bass; rearrange the notes into thirds to find the root. From “Triads: root, third and fifth”
  • Changing letter names when altering a triad. C diminished is C–E♭–G♭, not C–D♯–F♯: keep the letter names of a stack of thirds and change only the accidentals. From “Major, minor, diminished and augmented triads”
  • Using second-inversion chords freely. In common-practice harmony, 6/4 chords are unstable and appear mainly as cadential, passing or pedal 6/4s. From “Inversions and figured bass symbols”
  • Writing V as minor in a minor key. In tonal music the dominant in a minor key is normally major, using the raised leading note, so that it pulls strongly to the tonic. From “Diatonic triads and Roman numerals”

Voice Leading and Cadences

  • Writing stems by note height, as in piano music. In SATB, stem direction shows which voice a note belongs to, so it never changes. From “SATB layout and vocal ranges”
  • Doubling the third of a major chord in root position, especially the dominant. If the third is the leading note, it creates a strong fault; in other chords it thins the sound. From “Doubling and spacing”
  • Checking only the outer voices. Consecutives can occur between any pair of the four voices; check all six pairs. From “Parallel fifths and octaves”
  • Leaping in every voice to reach a chord. The nearest available chord note usually gives the smoothest result. From “Principles of voice leading”

Melody and Form

  • Confusing range with tessitura. A melody may have a wide range but sit mostly in a narrow band of notes. From “Melodic contour and range”
  • Assuming all phrases are four bars long. Composers frequently extend or shorten phrases for expressive effect. From “Phrases and periods”
  • Calling any repetition a sequence. A sequence repeats an idea at a different pitch level, usually several times in succession. From “Motif and sequence”
  • Confusing inversion with retrograde. Inversion flips the direction of intervals; retrograde reverses the order of notes. From “Melodic development”

Ear Training and Musicianship

  • Confusing tempo with metre. A fast piece in 3/4 is still triple; speed changes the pulse rate, not the grouping. From “Hearing pulse and metre”
  • Writing notes before fixing the metre. Without a frame of beats, durations are easy to misjudge. From “Rhythmic dictation”
  • Stopping to fix every wrong note. In sight-singing, as in performance, keeping the pulse matters more than a single pitch. From “Sight-singing and pitch matching”
  • Mixing fixed and movable do. In movable do, do changes with the key; in fixed do, do is always C. From “Solfège and movable do”

Counterpoint and Texture

  • Calling a hymn polyphonic because it has four parts. If the parts move together in rhythm, the texture is homophonic. From “Monophony, homophony and polyphony”
  • Treating heterophony as poor ensemble playing. In many traditions it is a deliberate, skilled texture. From “Heterophony and other textures”
  • Giving only a single word, such as "homophonic". Examiners expect detail about layers, register and change. From “Describing texture”
  • Treating the perfect fourth as consonant in two-part writing. In strict two-part counterpoint above the bass, it is a dissonance. From “Principles of species counterpoint”

Sound, Instruments and Orchestration

  • Thinking higher notes travel faster. All audible frequencies travel at the same speed in air; higher frequency means shorter wavelength. From “Sound waves: frequency, amplitude and pitch”
  • Assuming harmonics match piano pitches exactly. Several, such as the 7th and 11th, are noticeably out of tune with equal temperament. From “The harmonic series and timbre”
  • Saying the clarinet overblows an octave. As an approximately closed cylinder it overblows a twelfth, to the third harmonic. From “Standing waves in strings and pipes”
  • Thinking equal temperament makes every interval pure. It makes every key equal by making every interval except the octave slightly impure. From “Beats, tuning and temperament”

Music History: Medieval to Classical

  • Assuming Pope Gregory composed Gregorian chant. The repertory developed over centuries and was named after him later. From “Plainchant”
  • Thinking the tenor was the highest voice. In medieval polyphony the tenor held the chant and was usually the lowest part. From “Organum and early polyphony”
  • Assuming all medieval music was sacred. Secular songs of love and politics flourished alongside church music. From “Ars nova and medieval song”
  • Believing Palestrina single-handedly saved church polyphony at Trent. This is a later legend, though his style became a model. From “Renaissance sacred polyphony”

Music History: Romantic to the Present

  • Thinking Romantic music is about romantic love only. "Romantic" refers to a broad movement valuing emotion, imagination and the individual. From “Romanticism in music”
  • Treating the piano in a Lied as a mere accompaniment. It depicts images and emotions as an equal partner to the voice. From “The Lied and art song”
  • Assuming listeners can hear the story without the programme. Much programme music depends on titles and written descriptions. From “Programme music and the symphonic poem”
  • Thinking études are only technical exercises. Chopin's and Liszt's études are concert works of great musical depth. From “Romantic piano music”

Popular Music, Jazz and World Music

  • Thinking every blues is twelve bars long. Eight-bar and sixteen-bar blues are common, and early rural players often stretched or shortened bars to fit the words. From “The blues”
  • Assuming early jazz was mainly solo improvisation. In New Orleans style the front line improvised together; the extended solo became central only in the later 1920s. From “The origins of jazz”
  • Thinking big band music was fully improvised. Most of it was arranged and written down; improvisation was confined to solos set within the arrangement. From “Swing and the big band”
  • Believing bebop had no link to swing. Bebop players came from swing bands, used the same popular-song and blues forms, and Parker's style grew from blues and Kansas City swing. From “Bebop and modern jazz”

Composition and Analysis

  • Treating analysis as labelling. Naming chords and sections is only a starting point; analysis should explain how features relate and what effect they have. From “Approaches to analysis”
  • Labelling a modulation from one accidental. A single sharp may be a secondary dominant or a chromatic note; a modulation needs confirmation by a cadence in the new key. From “Harmonic analysis”
  • Confusing a sentence with a period. A period has two parallel phrases with weak then strong cadences; a sentence repeats a basic idea and then develops it in a continuation. From “Analysing melody and motif”
  • Forcing a piece into a textbook form. Models are norms, not rules; describe what actually happens and treat departures as meaningful rather than errors. From “Analysing form”
