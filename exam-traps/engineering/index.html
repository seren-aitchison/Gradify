Engineering: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Engineering lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Engineering Mathematics

  • Mixing unit systems. In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter after ground software reported thruster impulse in pound-force seconds where newton seconds were expected; the investigation board identified the units mismatch as the root cause. From “SI units and dimensional analysis”
  • Reporting every digit from the calculator. A result of 6.0247 Ω with an uncertainty of 0.078 Ω should be written 6.02 ± 0.08 Ω: the extra digits imply precision that does not exist. From “Uncertainty, significant figures and estimation”
  • Trusting a numerical model because it produces detailed, colourful output. A finite element result with the wrong boundary conditions is precisely wrong; check reactions balance the loads and compare with a hand calculation. From “Engineering models and assumptions”
  • Fitting a straight line to curved data because a spreadsheet will draw one. Plot the residuals: a systematic curve in them means the model is wrong, however high R2 is. From “Functions and graphs in engineering”

Statics

  • Using the wrong trigonometric function after measuring the angle from the y-axis. Always check which axis θ is measured from: sin and cos swap if it is measured from the vertical. From “Forces as vectors”
  • Multiplying force by the distance to the point of application rather than the perpendicular distance to the line of action. Only the perpendicular distance gives the moment. From “Moments and couples”
  • Forgetting the couple when moving a force. Sliding a force along its own line of action changes nothing, but moving it sideways changes its moment, which must be added back as a couple. From “Equivalent force systems”
  • Including internal forces or forces the body exerts on something else. A free-body diagram shows only forces acting on the body from outside. From “Free-body diagrams”

Dynamics

  • Using the constant-acceleration equations when acceleration varies. Check that a is constant before using them; otherwise integrate. From “Rectilinear motion”
  • Adding speeds instead of velocity components. Speed is √(vx2 + vy2), not vx + vy. From “Curvilinear motion: rectangular components”
  • Thinking an object moving at constant speed on a curve has no acceleration. Its direction is changing, so it has normal acceleration v2/ρ. From “Normal and tangential components”
  • Using the level-ground range formula when launch and landing heights differ. Solve the vertical equation for the actual landing height instead. From “Projectile motion”

Mechanics of Materials

  • Mixing units. Using N with mm2 gives N/mm2, which equals MPa; using N with m2 gives Pa. Keep units consistent throughout a calculation. From “Normal stress and strain”
  • Forgetting double shear. If a bolt passes through three plates with the load shared across two planes, using the single-shear area doubles the calculated stress. From “Shear stress and strain”
  • Confusing stiffness with strength. Young's modulus measures stiffness (resistance to elastic deformation); yield and ultimate strength measure resistance to permanent deformation and fracture. From “Hooke's law and elastic constants”
  • Using the total load for every segment of a stepped bar. Each segment's internal force must be found by equilibrium at a section through that segment. From “Axially loaded members”

Materials Science

  • Thinking polymers are weak because their chains are weak. The carbon–carbon backbone is strong; it is the secondary bonds between chains that are weak. From “Atomic bonding in materials”
  • Assuming close-packed means ductile. HCP is as densely packed as FCC but has fewer active slip systems, so it is often less ductile. From “Crystal structures”
  • Thinking defects only weaken materials. Controlled defects, such as fine grains and precipitates, are the main way metals are made stronger. From “Defects and dislocations”
  • Reading phase fractions from the tie-line end nearest the alloy. In the lever rule, each phase's fraction is the length of the opposite arm divided by the total. From “Phase diagrams”

Thermodynamics

  • Treating absolute and gauge pressure as the same. Thermodynamic equations use absolute pressure: absolute = gauge + atmospheric (about 101.3 kPa at sea level). From “Systems, properties and states”
  • Saying a hot object "has a lot of heat". It has high internal energy; heat is only the energy transferred because of a temperature difference. From “Energy, work and heat”
  • Using temperature and pressure to fix the state of wet steam. In the two-phase region they are not independent; you need x or another property. From “Properties of pure substances and steam tables”
  • Using degrees Celsius in pV = mRT. Always convert to kelvin by adding 273.15. From “Ideal gases”

Fluid Mechanics

  • Confusing dynamic and kinematic viscosity. Check the units: Pa s for μ, m2/s for ν. From “Fluid properties”
  • Mixing absolute and gauge pressure in one calculation. Decide which you are using and convert consistently. From “Pressure and manometry”
  • Placing the resultant force at the centroid. It acts lower, at the centre of pressure. From “Hydrostatic forces on surfaces”
  • Assuming a floating body is unstable whenever G is above B. Floating bodies can be stable if the metacentre lies above G. From “Buoyancy and stability”

Electrical Circuits and Electronics

  • Confusing power (W) with energy (J or kWh). Electricity bills charge for energy, in kilowatt-hours, not for power. From “Charge, current, voltage and power”
  • Using the voltage divider rule when a load is connected across the output. The load is in parallel with R2 and changes the result. From “Ohm's law and resistor networks”
  • Mixing sign conventions within one loop equation. Choose a direction round the loop and apply rises and drops consistently. From “Kirchhoff's laws”
  • Forgetting that a shared resistor carries the difference of two mesh currents. Write the voltage across it as R(I1 − I2) in mesh 1 and R(I2 − I1) in mesh 2. From “Nodal and mesh analysis”

Control Systems and Signals

  • Assuming feedback always improves a system. Poorly designed feedback can cause oscillation or instability. From “Introduction to control systems”
  • Applying the final value theorem to an unstable or oscillating system. It is valid only if the system actually settles. From “Laplace transforms”
  • Forgetting the sign convention. With positive feedback the closed-loop transfer function is G/(1 − GH). From “Transfer functions and block diagrams”
  • Forgetting that models are approximations. Friction, saturation and other non-linearities mean a linear model is valid only near its operating point. From “Modelling physical systems”

Design and Manufacturing

  • Treating design as a straight line from idea to product. Real design loops back as testing reveals problems. From “The engineering design process”
  • Writing a solution into the specification, such as "use an aluminium frame". This rules out better alternatives before concepts are explored. From “Requirements and specifications”
  • Treating the highest weighted score as an objective answer. Weights and scores are judgements, and close results need further testing. From “Concept generation and selection”
  • Misreading the projection method. Always check the projection symbol before interpreting views. From “Engineering drawing and CAD”

Engineering in Society

  • Thinking that ethics only matters for senior engineers. Every registered engineer and technician, including graduates, is expected to follow the principles from the start of their career. From “The statement of ethical principles”
  • Going straight to the press with a concern. Media disclosures are protected only in limited circumstances; internal routes and prescribed regulators usually come first. From “Professional responsibility and whistleblowing”
  • Blaming a single person or component. Most failures have several interacting causes, including organisational ones, and investigations look for root causes. From “Learning from engineering failures”
  • Assuming that a standard is the law. Most standards are voluntary; it is the regulations that are binding, although following designated standards is strong evidence of compliance. From “Codes, standards and regulation”

Projects and Professional Practice

  • Starting work before scope is agreed. Unclear scope leads to scope creep, rework and overruns; define and baseline scope first. From “The project lifecycle and planning”
  • Thinking the critical path is the path with the most activities. It is the path with the longest total duration. From “Scheduling: Gantt charts and the critical path”
  • Adding future savings without discounting. Money received in later years is worth less today and must be discounted to its present value. From “Engineering economics”
  • Confusing risks with issues. A risk may happen; an issue has already happened and needs action now. From “Risk management in projects”
