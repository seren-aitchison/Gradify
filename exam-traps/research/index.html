Research and the Dissertation: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Research and the Dissertation lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

The Research Landscape and Research Degrees

  • Treating "finding information" as research. Collecting what is already known, however thoroughly, is study; research must add something that wasn't known, by a method others could follow. From “What is research?”
  • Equating originality with size. A narrow, well-defined contribution that is clearly new and rigorously established is safer than a broad claim that cannot be supported. From “An original contribution to knowledge”
  • Assuming interdisciplinary means "using two literatures". Integration is the test: show how concepts or methods from each field change the answer. From “Disciplines and interdisciplinarity”
  • Thinking universities' research is funded mainly by tuition fees. Research funding comes chiefly from public grants, charities and industry, although universities do cross-subsidise research from other income, which has become a growing concern. From “The UK research system and its funders”

Research Questions and the Literature Review

  • Choosing a topic so broad that it cannot be investigated in depth. Narrow first; you can situate the work in the wider area later. From “Choosing a research topic”
  • Writing questions that can be answered with yes or no without investigation. Questions should require evidence and analysis to answer. From “Formulating research questions”
  • Writing objectives that are just methods ("to conduct interviews"). Objectives should state what will be found out or achieved. From “Aims, objectives and hypotheses”
  • Claiming a gap because you could not find studies after a quick search. Gaps must be established by systematic searching. From “Finding the gap: gap-spotting and problematisation”

Philosophy of Research

  • Thinking ontology is irrelevant to empirical research. Every study rests on assumptions about what exists. From “Ontology”
  • Equating qualitative methods with constructionism and quantitative with objectivism. Methods can serve different epistemologies. From “Epistemology”
  • Treating paradigms as labels to attach to a thesis. They should genuinely shape and justify the design. From “Research paradigms”
  • Using "positivist" to describe any quantitative study. Quantitative methods can be used within post-positivist and other paradigms. From “Positivism”

Research Design

  • Treating "I will use interviews" as a research design. Interviews are a method; the design states which cases, comparisons and timing make the interview data able to answer the question. From “What is research design?”
  • Describing methods without justifying them. Examiners expect to see why the methods fit the question and the philosophical stance, not just what was done. From “Aligning question, philosophy and method”
  • Calling any study with a survey and a few interviews "mixed methods". Genuine mixed methods requires a rationale and planned integration of the strands. From “Quantitative, qualitative and mixed approaches”
  • Confusing random allocation with random sampling. Random allocation assigns participants to groups and supports internal validity; random sampling selects participants from a population and supports generalisation. From “Experimental designs”

Quantitative Methods and Statistics

  • Calculating a mean of nominal codes, such as "average discipline = 1.8". Numbers used as labels for categories have no arithmetic meaning. From “Variables and levels of measurement”
  • Writing new questions when validated scales exist. Validated instruments allow comparison with other studies and come with evidence of reliability and validity. From “Questionnaire design”
  • Running EFA and CFA on the same sample. Confirming a structure on the data that generated it overstates fit; use separate samples. From “Measurement scales and psychometrics”
  • Deleting outliers simply because they are inconvenient. Outliers should be investigated, and genuine values removed only with clear justification and sensitivity analysis. From “Data screening and cleaning”

Qualitative Methods

  • Judging qualitative studies by whether their samples are statistically representative. Qualitative research aims for depth and conceptual insight, not population estimates. From “What is qualitative research?”
  • Naming a tradition such as grounded theory without following its procedures. Using a label without its methods weakens coherence; a generic qualitative design may be more honest. From “Qualitative research designs”
  • Describing any qualitative sample as "theoretical". Theoretical sampling requires sampling decisions driven by ongoing analysis; a sample fixed in advance is purposive. From “Purposive and theoretical sampling”
  • Asking the research question directly as an interview question. Participants answer better when research questions are translated into concrete, experience-based questions. From “Semi-structured interviews”

Mixed Methods and Evidence Synthesis

  • Treating any study using two methods as mixed methods. Mixed methods requires both qualitative and quantitative strands and their integration. From “What is mixed methods research?”
  • Treating divergence between strands as a failure. Disagreement often reveals something important and should be explored and reported. From “Convergent designs”
  • Planning the qualitative phase in full before seeing quantitative results. The second phase should be shaped by what the first phase finds. From “Explanatory sequential designs”
  • Using the small qualitative sample to validate a new instrument. Validation requires a separate, larger quantitative sample. From “Exploratory sequential designs”

Research Ethics and Integrity

  • Treating ethics as a form to complete once. Ethical issues arise throughout research and need ongoing attention. From “Principles of research ethics”
  • Assuming historical abuses are irrelevant today. Pressures that led to them, such as exploiting vulnerable groups, can still arise. From “The history of research ethics”
  • Treating a signed form as proof of consent. Consent depends on understanding and voluntariness, not just a signature. From “Informed consent”
  • Promising complete confidentiality. Researchers may have to break confidentiality to prevent serious harm. From “Confidentiality and anonymity”

Research Data Management and Open Research

  • Leaving data management until the end of a project. Decisions about formats, storage and documentation are much harder to fix later. From “Research data management”
  • Writing a DMP that promises data sharing not covered in consent forms. Participants must know how their data will be used. From “Data management plans”
  • Editing raw data files directly. Always keep an unchanged copy of raw data and work on copies. From “File organisation and version control”
  • Using personal cloud accounts for participant data. These may not meet data protection or institutional requirements. From “Secure storage and backup”

Academic Writing and the Thesis

  • Confusing complex language with good writing. Clear, direct prose communicates expertise better. From “Academic writing style”
  • Waiting for inspiration or large blocks of time. Regular short sessions are more productive. From “Writing habits and productivity”
  • Listing sources without making claims. Literature should support an argument, not replace it. From “Building an argument”
  • Writing very long paragraphs with several ideas. Split them so each paragraph makes one point. From “Paragraphs and signposting”

Publishing, Presenting and Impact

  • Expecting publication to be quick. Review and revision often take many months. From “The academic publishing system”
  • Choosing a journal on prestige alone. A poor fit leads to desk rejection and wasted time. From “Choosing a journal”
  • Submitting a thesis chapter almost unchanged. Articles need a tighter focus and different framing. From “Writing a journal article”
  • Writing a harsh, vague review. Constructive, specific comments help authors improve their work. From “Peer review”

The Viva and Beyond

  • Leaving formatting and references to the last day. These checks take longer than expected. From “Preparing to submit”
  • Seeing the viva as an attack. Examiners usually want candidates to succeed and to discuss the research in depth. From “Examiners and the viva”
  • Not rereading the thesis because you wrote it. Months may pass between submission and viva, and details fade. From “Preparing for the viva”
  • Becoming defensive when challenged. Examiners value thoughtful engagement with criticism. From “In the viva”
