Accounting and Finance: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Accounting and Finance lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Accounting and the Double-Entry System

  • Thinking accounts capture everything of value. Only events that can be measured reliably in money are recorded, so a firm's reputation, staff skill and customer loyalty usually appear nowhere in its accounts. From “What is accounting?”
  • Assuming managers need published accounts. Managers are internal users who get detailed, frequent management information; the annual published accounts are mainly for outsiders. From “Users of accounting information”
  • Treating the two as separate record-keeping systems. Both usually draw on the same underlying transaction records; they differ in how the data is analysed and presented. From “Financial and management accounting”
  • Thinking limited liability means the company's debts are limited. The company is liable for all its debts; it is the shareholders' liability that is limited to what they agreed to pay for their shares. From “Types of business organisation”

Preparing Financial Statements

  • Capitalising repairs because they are large. Size doesn't decide the treatment: a costly repair that restores the original condition is still revenue expenditure. From “Capital and revenue expenditure”
  • Mixing up the direction. Ask "has the business had the benefit yet?" An accrual is a benefit received but not paid; a prepayment is a payment made for a benefit still to come. From “Accruals and prepayments”
  • Thinking depreciation provides money to replace the asset. It is a book entry: no cash moves when it is charged. From “Depreciation”
  • Recording the new asset at the cash paid in a part-exchange. The new asset is recorded at its full cost; the allowance is the proceeds of the old asset. From “Disposal of non-current assets”

Financial Reporting for Companies

  • Thinking IFRS and UK GAAP are the same. FRS 102 is based on IFRS principles but differs in many areas, such as goodwill amortisation and simpler disclosures. From “The regulatory framework for financial reporting”
  • Defining an asset as something the entity owns. The 2018 Framework focuses on control of an economic resource (a right), which includes leased items and excludes things the entity owns but can't benefit from. From “The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting”
  • Treating prudence as permission to understate profits. Deliberate understatement isn't neutral; it misleads users just as overstatement does. From “Qualitative characteristics of useful information”
  • Treating fair value as "what the company thinks it is worth". Fair value is a market-based exit price from the perspective of market participants, not the entity's own estimate of its value to the business (which is value in use). From “Recognition and measurement”

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

  • Drawing conclusions from one ratio for one year. Ratios need context: the industry, the company's strategy, the economy and the trend over several years. From “Why analyse financial statements?”
  • Comparing ROCE across companies that calculate capital employed differently. Check definitions, and note that revaluations or old, heavily depreciated assets can distort ROCE. From “Profitability ratios”
  • Treating the quick ratio as always more reliable. It excludes inventory, but receivables may also be slow or doubtful; check receivables days and cash flows too. From “Liquidity ratios”
  • Using year-end balances without considering seasonality. A year-end at a quiet point may make inventory or receivables look unusually low; averages give a better picture. From “Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle”

Management Accounting: Costing

  • Treating management information as useful just because it is precise. Information that arrives too late, or costs more than it saves, fails the test. From “The role of management accounting”
  • Including selling or administration costs in inventory. IAS 2 limits inventory cost to the costs of purchase, conversion and bringing inventory to its present location and condition. From “Cost classification”
  • Treating unit cost as constant at all output levels. Because fixed cost per unit falls with volume, a unit cost calculated at one level misleads at another. From “Cost behaviour”
  • Choosing the highest and lowest costs instead of the highest and lowest activity levels. The method is driven by activity. From “The high-low method”

Management Accounting: Planning, Control and Decisions

  • Including absorbed fixed overheads in a one-off decision. If total fixed costs do not change, they are irrelevant. From “Relevant costing”
  • Using CVP far outside the relevant range. Prices, variable costs and fixed costs may change at very different volumes. From “Cost–volume–profit analysis”
  • Treating a large margin of safety as guaranteeing profit. Budgeted sales may be optimistic, and costs may change. From “Break-even charts and the margin of safety”
  • Ranking by contribution per unit. A product with high unit contribution may use so much of the scarce resource that it earns less per hour. From “Limiting factor analysis”

Financial Management

  • Treating the three decisions separately. Investment plans affect how much finance is needed and what dividends can be paid. From “The financial management function”
  • Equating profit maximisation with shareholder wealth. Profit ignores risk, timing and cash flows. From “Financial objectives and stakeholders”
  • Assuming efficient markets mean prices are always "right". Efficiency means prices quickly reflect information, not that they never err. From “The financial environment”
  • Adding cash flows from different years without discounting. A pound in five years is worth less than a pound today. From “The time value of money”

Audit, Assurance and Ethics

  • Thinking auditors prepare the accounts or guarantee them. Directors prepare the financial statements; the auditor gives an opinion with reasonable assurance. From “What is an audit?”
  • Assuming every company must be audited. Many small private companies are exempt under the Companies Act. From “The regulation of audit”
  • Thinking the Code is law. It is applied through the listing rules on a comply-or-explain basis, not as statute. From “Corporate governance”
  • Treating internal auditors as part of finance staff who check bookkeeping. Their remit covers risk and control across the whole organisation. From “Internal audit”

Taxation

  • Treating HMRC guidance as law. A manual or concession shows HMRC's view and practice; a tribunal decides on the statute itself. From “The UK tax system”
  • Basing payments on account on the current year's tax. They are based on the previous year's liability not deducted at source, and the difference is settled by the balancing payment. From “Tax administration and self-assessment”
  • Penalty amounts and Making Tax Digital requirements change; check the current HMRC position before applying figures. From “Tax administration and self-assessment”
  • Assuming avoidance is safe because it is legal. Schemes can be defeated under Ramsay or the GAAR, with the tax, interest, penalties and accelerated payments to follow. From “Tax avoidance and tax evasion”

Business and Company Law

  • Writing "UK law" when only English law is meant. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate legal systems; this school describes the law of England and Wales. From “The English legal system”
  • Treating a price display as an offer. Displays and most adverts are invitations to treat; the customer makes the offer. From “Contract formation”
  • Assuming a signed document always binds every term. Signature incorporates terms, but statutory controls can still make exclusion clauses ineffective. From “Contract terms and breach”
  • Thinking damages punish the party in breach. Contract damages compensate; they are not meant to punish. From “Contract remedies”

Finance and Financial Markets

  • Thinking secondary markets raise money for companies. Only primary issues raise new funds; secondary trading gives investors liquidity. From “The financial system”
  • Believing banks simply lend out existing deposits. In the modern system, new loans create new deposits. From “Banks and money markets”
  • Thinking a rise in interest rates increases the price of existing bonds. Prices and yields move inversely. From “Bonds and bond valuation”
  • Treating a high P/E ratio as proof a share is overpriced. It may reflect justified expectations of growth. From “Equities and stock markets”

Accounting, Technology and Society

  • Thinking an AIS is just the accounting software. The software is only one component; procedures, people and controls determine whether the information is reliable. From “Accounting information systems”
  • Treating a Benford's law deviation as evidence of fraud. Some data sets, such as prices set at £9.99 or amounts with fixed limits, do not follow Benford's law, so deviations need explaining before conclusions are drawn. From “Data analytics in accounting”
  • Assuming AI output is correct because it is fluent. Generative AI can invent figures, standards or citations, so every output used professionally must be checked against reliable sources. From “Automation, AI and the accountant”
  • Thinking cyber security is purely an IT matter. Many losses come from staff acting on fraudulent emails, so finance procedures such as independent call-back verification are key controls. From “Cyber security and data protection”
