English Literature: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our English Literature lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Literary Study

  • Thinking literature means only novels. Poetry, drama, essays, letters, diaries, sermons and oral forms such as ballads are all studied as literature. From “What is literature?”
  • Listing genre features without saying why they matter. Name the convention, then explain its effect or how the writer adapts it. From “Genre and form”
  • Writing "Wordsworth feels lonely" about "I wandered lonely as a cloud". Write "the speaker describes feeling lonely", then discuss how Wordsworth presents it. From “Author, narrator and reader”
  • Bolting on context ("In Victorian times there was lots of poverty") in a separate paragraph. Integrate it with a quotation and an interpretive point. From “Text and context”

Poetry and Poetics

  • Treating a poem as prose with line breaks added. The line, the sound and the form are part of what the poem says, not decoration around a message. From “What makes a poem?”
  • Assuming a poem written in the first person tells us the poet's own feelings or experiences. Even confessional poetry shapes and selects; treat biography as context, not as the meaning. From “The speaker and the poetic voice”
  • Listing images without saying what they do. Identify the image, then explain its connotations and its effect in context. From “Imagery in poetry”
  • Claiming a sound has a fixed meaning, such as "sibilance always sounds sinister". Sounds take their effect from context; the same s sounds can be soothing or menacing. From “Rhyme and sound patterning”

Drama and Shakespeare

  • Writing about a play as if it were a novel. Avoid phrases such as "the reader learns"; consider the audience, the stage and how a moment might be played. From “Drama as performance”
  • Translating hamartia simply as a "fatal flaw" of character. Aristotle's term suggests an error or misjudgement, which may involve ignorance rather than vice. From “Tragedy”
  • Treating comedy as trivial. Many comedies satirise society, question gender roles or contain darker elements that complicate their happy endings. From “Comedy”
  • Applying Freytag's pyramid rigidly to every play. It describes many classical and Shakespearean tragedies, but modern plays often use different shapes, circular structures or fragmented time. From “Dramatic structure”

Prose Fiction and the Novel

  • Retelling the story in an essay instead of analysing the plot. Marks come from explaining how the arrangement of events creates effects, not from summary. From “Story, plot and narrative”
  • Treating a first-person narrator's judgements as the author's. The narrator is a character, and authors often create distance between their views and their narrators'. From “Narrators and point of view”
  • Attributing every judgement in free indirect passages to the narrator or author. The thoughts may be the character's, and the irony lies in the gap. From “Free indirect discourse”
  • Writing about characters as if they were real people. Analyse how the author constructs them and why. From “Characterisation”

Medieval to Eighteenth-Century Literature

  • Treating Beowulf as purely pagan. It was composed and copied in a Christian culture, and its narrator interprets events in Christian terms. From “Old English literature”
  • Taking the narrator's praise of pilgrims at face value. Chaucer's narrator often admires characters whom the details expose. From “Chaucer and Middle English”
  • Assuming romance means a love story. Medieval romance is a broader genre of chivalric adventure, in which love is one element. From “Medieval romance and drama”
  • Treating the Renaissance as a sudden break with the Middle Ages. Medieval forms and beliefs continued alongside the new learning. From “The English Renaissance”

Romantic to Contemporary Literature

  • Equating "Romantic" with love poetry. The movement is about imagination, nature and the self, and is often political. From “Romanticism: an overview”
  • Assuming Wordsworth simply describes landscapes. His nature poetry is about the mind's relationship with nature and the work of memory. From “Wordsworth, Coleridge and Lyrical Ballads”
  • Treating the second-generation Romantics as identical. Byron's satire, Shelley's radicalism and Keats's sensuous lyricism differ greatly. From “The second-generation Romantics”
  • Assuming Romanticism was only the "big six" male poets. Women were among the best-selling and most influential writers of the period. From “Women writers of the Romantic period”

Literary Theory and Criticism

  • Treating theory as a set of fixed answers. Theories are lenses that raise questions, and many readings combine several. From “What is literary theory?”
  • Thinking close reading belongs to one school. It began with practical criticism but is used by almost every theoretical approach. From “Liberal humanism and practical criticism”
  • Thinking New Critics ignored meaning. They pursued meaning intensely, but located it in the text's form and language. From “New Criticism”
  • Confusing the signifier with the thing itself. In Saussure's model, the signified is a concept, not an object in the world. From “Formalism and structuralism”

Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose

  • Bolting on paragraphs of history. Every contextual point should be tied to a specific moment, character or quotation. From “Macbeth: context”
  • Blaming the witches or Lady Macbeth entirely. The text gives Macbeth knowledge and choice, especially in the later murders. From “Macbeth: ambition and power”
  • Saying the witches make Macbeth kill Duncan. They never instruct him to murder; they predict, and he interprets. From “Macbeth: the supernatural”
  • Calling Lady Macbeth simply evil or a "fiend-like queen". That is Malcolm's verdict; her guilt, breakdown and death make her more complex. From “Macbeth: gender and Lady Macbeth”

Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry

  • Reading Innocence as simply good and Experience as simply bad. Blake presents both as limited perspectives, and Innocence can be naive or exploited. From “Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience”
  • Reading Wordsworth's nature poems as simple description. They are about the mind's relationship with nature over time. From “Wordsworth: nature and memory”
  • Reducing the poem to its final moral. The poem's imagery, isolation and lasting guilt suggest more complex meanings. From “Coleridge: The Rime of the Ancient Mariner”
  • Treating "Ozymandias" only as a poem about time. It is also a pointed attack on tyranny and political pride. From “Shelley: Ozymandias and the political poems”

Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing

  • Working through the poem line by line. Organise your answer by ideas so that it builds an argument. From “Approaching an unseen poem”
  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing. Every point should explain how and why the writer creates an effect. From “Approaching unseen prose”
  • Ignoring stage directions. They are part of the text and show how meaning is created in performance. From “Approaching unseen drama”
  • Writing about one text then the other with a single comparative sentence at the end. Integrate comparison throughout. From “Comparative analysis: principles”
