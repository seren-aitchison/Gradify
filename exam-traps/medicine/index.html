Medicine: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Medicine lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Medical Science

  • Assuming all body cells can divide to replace losses. Neurones and cardiac myocytes are essentially permanent (non-dividing) cells, so their loss is replaced by scar, not new tissue. From “Cell structure and function in health”
  • Thinking hyperkalaemia makes cells more excitable for long. A small depolarisation first brings cells nearer threshold, but sustained depolarisation inactivates Na⁺ channels, so the net effect in the heart is depressed conduction. From “Membranes, transport and the resting potential”
  • Calling any loop that ends a process "positive feedback". Positive feedback amplifies the original change; the event that stops it (such as delivery of the baby) is outside the loop. From “Homeostasis and negative feedback”
  • Treating every low sodium as a sodium deficit. Hyponatraemia must be assessed with the patient's volume status, plasma and urine osmolality and urine sodium; giving saline to someone with fluid overload or SIADH can make things worse. From “Body fluids, osmolality and electrolytes”

Clinical Method and Reasoning

  • Recording "allergic to penicillin" without the reaction. Diarrhoea or nausea is an intolerance; a rash or anaphylaxis is an allergy. The difference decides whether a patient can safely receive a whole class of antibiotics, and unverified labels lead to broader, less effective prescribing. From “The medical history”
  • Thinking patient-centred means doing whatever the patient asks. It means understanding the patient's perspective and involving them in decisions; the doctor still gives honest advice and may decline requests that aren't in the patient's interest, such as antibiotics for a viral illness. From “Consultation models and patient-centred care”
  • Thinking consent is a signature on a form. Consent is a process of dialogue in which the patient understands the options, material risks and alternatives; the form only records it. From “Communicating risk and shared decision-making”
  • Giving false reassurance to soften the blow ("I'm sure it will be fine"). It damages trust when the truth emerges and prevents patients planning; hope can be honest ("We'll do everything we can to control this"). From “Breaking bad news”

Cardiovascular Medicine

  • Equating ejection fraction with cardiac output. A patient can have a normal ejection fraction and still have heart failure (HFpEF), or a low EF with a resting output maintained by dilation and a fast rate. From “The cardiac cycle and cardiac output”
  • Forgetting that the ECG shows electrical, not mechanical, activity. A normal-looking rhythm without a pulse (pulseless electrical activity) is a cardiac arrest. From “Cardiac electrophysiology and conduction”
  • Assuming a normal blood pressure rules out significant blood loss. Compensatory tachycardia and vasoconstriction can hold pressure up until a large loss, especially in young people, so falling pressure is a late sign. From “Blood pressure regulation”
  • Judging risk from cholesterol alone. Absolute risk depends on all risk factors together; a modest cholesterol in a smoker with diabetes can carry higher risk than a high cholesterol in a young non-smoker. From “Atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk”

Respiratory Medicine

  • Interpreting PaO2 without knowing the inspired oxygen. A PaO2 of 12 kPa is normal on air but shows a large gas exchange defect on 60% oxygen. From “Ventilation, gas exchange and oxygen transport”
  • Labelling a restrictive pattern on spirometry as proven restrictive lung disease. Spirometry suggests restriction; full lung volumes (total lung capacity) are needed to confirm it. From “Lung function tests”
  • Withholding oxygen from a hypoxic patient with COPD for fear of CO2 retention. Hypoxaemia kills; give controlled oxygen to the 88–92% target and monitor with blood gases. From “Respiratory failure: type 1 and type 2”
  • Being reassured by a normal PaCO2 in acute asthma. In a severe attack, CO2 should be low from hyperventilation; a normal or raised level suggests exhaustion and near-fatal asthma. From “Asthma”

Gastroenterology and Hepatology

  • Assuming B12 deficiency always means dietary lack. Loss of intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia, gastrectomy) or terminal ileal disease are common causes. From “Digestion and absorption”
  • Starting full feeding immediately in a starved patient. High-risk patients need cautious introduction, vitamin supplementation and electrolyte monitoring. From “Nutrition, malnutrition and refeeding”
  • Attributing new dysphagia to reflux or anxiety without investigation. NICE recommends urgent endoscopy for dysphagia at any age. From “Dysphagia and oesophageal disease”
  • Continuing high-dose PPIs indefinitely without review. Long-term use should be at the lowest effective dose and reviewed, given possible adverse effects. From “Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease”

Renal, Endocrine and Metabolic Medicine

  • Treating a normal serum creatinine as proof of normal kidney function. Creatinine depends on muscle mass and rises only after substantial loss of GFR. From “Renal physiology and glomerular filtration”
  • Continuing nephrotoxic medicines during AKI. NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, diuretics and some others should be reviewed and often paused. From “Acute kidney injury”
  • Classifying CKD on eGFR alone. Albuminuria is a powerful predictor of risk and must be measured. From “Chronic kidney disease”
  • Treating haematuria and proteinuria with a rising creatinine as a simple UTI. These features may signal glomerulonephritis needing urgent assessment. From “Glomerulonephritis and nephrotic syndrome”

Neurology and Psychiatry

  • Treating a sudden, severe "worst ever" headache as migraine. Thunderclap headache needs emergency investigation for subarachnoid haemorrhage. From “Headache”
  • Waiting to see whether stroke symptoms improve before calling 999. Delays reduce the benefit of time-critical treatment. From “Stroke and transient ischaemic attack”
  • Putting something in the mouth of a person having a seizure. This can cause injury; keep them safe and time the seizure. From “Epilepsy and seizures”
  • Diagnosing MS from a single episode without evidence of dissemination in space and time. Many conditions can cause one episode of neurological symptoms. From “Multiple sclerosis”

Infection, Haematology and Oncology

  • Waiting for culture results before treating suspected sepsis. Take cultures quickly, but give antibiotics without delay. From “Principles of microbiology”
  • Continuing IV antibiotics by default. Review at 48–72 hours and switch to oral when the patient meets criteria. From “Antimicrobial therapy and stewardship”
  • Being reassured by a normal temperature. Sepsis can occur without fever, especially in older or immunosuppressed people. From “Sepsis”
  • Dismissing malaria because the patient took prophylaxis. No prophylaxis is fully protective, and adherence is often incomplete. From “Fever in the returning traveller”

Musculoskeletal, Skin and the Special Senses

  • Ordering an X-ray to confirm typical OA. NICE advises a clinical diagnosis; imaging is for atypical features or diagnostic doubt. From “Osteoarthritis”
  • Waiting for a positive rheumatoid factor before referral. Seronegative RA is common; refer on clinical suspicion of synovitis. From “Rheumatoid arthritis”
  • Prescribing methotrexate needs specialist protocols and monitoring; doses here are not given and must follow the BNF and local shared-care guidance. From “Rheumatoid arthritis”
  • Ruling out gout because the serum urate is normal during an attack. Urate often falls during acute attacks; aspiration and later recheck are more reliable. From “Gout and pseudogout”

Acute, Surgical and Palliative Care

  • Moving on before fixing a problem. An airway or breathing problem must be treated before assessing circulation. From “The ABCDE assessment of the acutely ill patient”
  • This lesson describes the approach for study. In practice, follow local escalation policies and call for emergency help when needed. From “The ABCDE assessment of the acutely ill patient”
  • Waiting for hypotension before diagnosing shock. Young people can maintain blood pressure until they are severely shocked. From “Shock”
  • Treatment choices and doses follow local protocols and senior or critical care advice. From “Shock”

Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Paediatrics

  • Interpreting a falling haemoglobin in pregnancy as always pathological. Some fall is dilutional, though true iron deficiency anaemia is also common. From “Normal pregnancy and antenatal care”
  • This lesson is for study; clinical care must follow current local and national guidance. From “Normal pregnancy and antenatal care”
  • Assuming a woman with abdominal pain cannot be pregnant. Always perform a pregnancy test in women of reproductive age. From “Bleeding in early pregnancy”
  • Assuming pre-eclampsia resolves immediately after birth. It can worsen or first appear postpartum, including eclampsia. From “Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy”

Professional Practice, Prescribing and Population Health

  • Thinking professional standards only start at graduation. Students are expected to meet the values of Good medical practice, and concerns can affect provisional registration. From “Good medical practice and professionalism”
  • Treating beneficence as overriding autonomy. A person with capacity may refuse treatment even when doctors believe it would benefit them. From “Medical ethics: principles and frameworks”
  • Assuming a patient lacks capacity because they make an unwise decision or have a diagnosis such as dementia. Capacity must be assessed for the specific decision at the time it is needed. From “Consent and capacity”
  • Believing that confidentiality ends when a patient dies. The duty of confidentiality continues after death. From “Confidentiality and information governance”
