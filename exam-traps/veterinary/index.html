Veterinary Science: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Veterinary Science lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Comparative Anatomy

  • Using anterior and posterior for the limbs or trunk of a quadruped. In veterinary anatomy they are reserved for the eye (the anterior chamber, the posterior segment) and a few human-derived terms; elsewhere they are ambiguous. From “Anatomical terms of position and direction”
  • Forgetting the open physis. In a young animal it is mechanically weaker than the surrounding bone and ligaments, so trauma that would sprain an adult joint often fractures the growth plate instead (Salter–Harris fractures), with a risk of shortened or angular growth. From “The skeleton and joints compared”
  • Treating the equine suspensory ligament as "just a ligament". It is a modified muscle (interosseous III) that still contains some muscle fibres, and it works with the flexor tendons as a passive support of the fetlock. From “Muscles, tendons and fascia”
  • Assuming the cat's humerus is the same as the dog's. The feline supracondylar foramen carries the median nerve and brachial artery, so fracture repair of the distal humerus must avoid them. From “The thoracic limb of the dog and cat”

Veterinary Physiology

  • Thinking a dehydrated animal has lost only plasma. Most fluid losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, reduced intake) come from the whole ECF and, with time, the ICF; plasma volume is a small part of the deficit. From “Homeostasis and body fluids”
  • Saying hyperkalaemia makes cells more excitable because the resting potential is closer to threshold. Sustained depolarisation inactivates sodium channels, so excitability falls; this is why severe hyperkalaemia causes bradycardia and cardiac arrest. From “Membrane potentials and nerve conduction”
  • Confusing tetanus with botulism. Tetanus blocks inhibition centrally, causing spastic rigidity; botulism blocks ACh release peripherally, causing flaccid weakness. From “The neuromuscular junction and muscle contraction”
  • Assuming drugs act the same way in every species. Responses to autonomic drugs differ widely (α2-agonists in ruminants, atropine in rabbits), so species-specific data sheets and formularies are essential; nothing here is a dose. From “The autonomic nervous system”

Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics

  • Assuming every cell has every organelle in the same amount. Mammalian red cells lose their nucleus and mitochondria, plasma cells are packed with RER, and hepatocytes with SER and mitochondria; structure follows function. From “The animal cell and its organelles”
  • Reading a liver enzyme rise as liver failure. Enzymes measure cell damage or induction, not function; a dog with high ALT may have normal liver function, and an end-stage liver may have normal enzymes. Function is tested with bile acids, albumin, glucose, urea and clotting. From “Proteins and enzymes”
  • Assuming sugar-free means safe for pets. Xylitol is highly toxic to dogs even in small amounts; any ingestion needs prompt veterinary advice. From “Carbohydrate metabolism”
  • Assuming a fat animal can safely go without food. Obese cats, ponies and donkeys are the most at risk of fatal lipid mobilisation disorders when they stop eating; inappetence in these animals needs a vet promptly. From “Lipid metabolism and ketogenesis”

Animal Husbandry, Nutrition and Welfare

  • Equating good productivity with good welfare. A productive animal can still experience pain, fear or frustration. From “What animal welfare means”
  • Treating the Five Freedoms as fully achievable. They are ideals; the aim is to minimise negative and promote positive experiences. From “The Five Freedoms and the Five Domains”
  • Thinking the law only acts once an animal is suffering. The duty of care allows intervention when welfare needs are not being met. From “The Animal Welfare Act 2006”
  • Assuming that meeting space and feed standards guarantees good welfare. Only animal-based measures show how animals actually fare. From “Assessing welfare in practice”

Veterinary Pathology

  • Treating necrosis and apoptosis as the same. Necrosis causes inflammation and affects groups of cells; apoptosis does not. From “Cell injury and cell death”
  • Thinking inflammation is always harmful. It is a protective response, although excessive inflammation can cause damage. From “Acute inflammation”
  • Expecting a chronic granuloma to resolve without removing its cause. Persistent agents such as foreign bodies must be removed. From “Chronic inflammation and repair”
  • Confusing hyperaemia with congestion. Hyperaemia is active inflow; congestion is passive build-up from impaired outflow. From “Disturbances of circulation”

Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology

  • Assuming Gram-negative means less dangerous. Gram-negative bacteria carry endotoxin, which can cause life-threatening shock. From “Bacteria of veterinary importance”
  • Thinking all viruses are fragile. Non-enveloped viruses such as parvovirus survive for months and need disinfectants proven against them. From “Viruses of veterinary importance”
  • Relying on a negative Wood's lamp to rule out ringworm. Many dermatophyte strains do not fluoresce, so culture or PCR is needed. From “Fungi and prions”
  • Thinking resistance develops in the animal being treated. Bacteria become resistant; selection pressure favours resistant bacteria in every treated animal. From “Antimicrobial resistance”

Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics

  • Thinking half-life depends only on the liver. Half-life depends on both clearance and volume of distribution, and kidney excretion is often important. From “Pharmacokinetics”
  • Confusing potency with efficacy. A potent drug works at low amounts, but a less potent drug may still produce a bigger maximum effect. From “Pharmacodynamics”
  • Assuming that a drug safe in dogs is safe in cats. Cats metabolise many drugs differently and can be poisoned by products safe for dogs. From “Species differences in drug handling”
  • Waiting for proof before reporting. Pharmacovigilance relies on reports of suspected reactions; patterns only emerge when many reports are pooled. From “Adverse drug reactions and pharmacovigilance”

Clinical Method and Reasoning

  • Recording "allergic to penicillin" without the reaction. Diarrhoea or nausea is an intolerance; a rash or anaphylaxis is an allergy. The difference decides whether a patient can safely receive a whole class of antibiotics, and unverified labels lead to broader, less effective prescribing. From “The medical history”
  • Thinking patient-centred means doing whatever the patient asks. It means understanding the patient's perspective and involving them in decisions; the doctor still gives honest advice and may decline requests that aren't in the patient's interest, such as antibiotics for a viral illness. From “Consultation models and patient-centred care”
  • Thinking consent is a signature on a form. Consent is a process of dialogue in which the patient understands the options, material risks and alternatives; the form only records it. From “Communicating risk and shared decision-making”
  • Giving false reassurance to soften the blow ("I'm sure it will be fine"). It damages trust when the truth emerges and prevents patients planning; hope can be honest ("We'll do everything we can to control this"). From “Breaking bad news”

Small Animal Medicine and Surgery

  • Treating every case of acute diarrhoea with antimicrobials. Most mild acute cases resolve with supportive care alone. From “Vomiting and diarrhoea in dogs and cats”
  • Thinking a cat without a murmur has a normal heart. Many cats with HCM have no murmur, so imaging is needed when heart disease is suspected. From “Cardiac disease in dogs and cats”
  • Thinking snoring in flat-faced dogs is normal for the breed. It is a sign of airway obstruction that affects welfare and can lead to collapse. From “Respiratory disease in dogs and cats”
  • Diagnosing CKD from a single high creatinine in a dehydrated animal. Dehydration raises creatinine, so tests should be repeated once hydrated, with urine concentration. From “Chronic kidney disease”

Equine Practice

  • Waiting to see if a colicking horse improves before calling the vet. Early assessment matters, because strangulating lesions need surgery within hours. From “Colic”
  • Treating equine asthma with medicines alone. Without reducing dust exposure, signs return when medication stops. From “Equine respiratory disease”
  • Thinking strangles must be reported to APHA. Strangles is not notifiable in the UK, but HBLB codes and good biosecurity should still be followed. From “Equine infectious and notifiable diseases”
  • Thinking laminitis is mainly caused by eating too much grain. Most cases are endocrinopathic, linked to insulin dysregulation, so hormonal testing is essential. From “Laminitis and equine metabolic syndrome”

Farm Animal Practice and Herd Health

  • Treating every cow with antibiotics at drying off. Selective dry cow therapy with teat sealants reduces antibiotic use without harming udder health when well managed. From “Mastitis”
  • Waiting until a cow is severely lame before treating. Early detection and prompt treatment improve recovery. From “Lameness in cattle”
  • Giving intravenous calcium quickly without monitoring the heart. Rapid infusion can cause dangerous arrhythmias. From “Metabolic disease of dairy cows”
  • Stopping milk feeding in scouring calves. Calves need continued milk for energy alongside electrolytes. From “Calf health and the colostrum”

Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice

  • Treating antimicrobial resistance as only a human medicine problem. Resistance moves between animals, people and the environment. From “One Health”
  • Thinking only exotic diseases are zoonotic. Many common infections, such as ringworm and Campylobacter, pass between animals and people. From “Zoonoses”
  • Thinking meat inspection protects only human health. It also protects animal welfare and provides health feedback to farms. From “Food safety and meat inspection”
  • Assuming vaccination always solves outbreaks. It can reduce culling, but may affect trade and must distinguish vaccinated from infected animals. From “Notifiable diseases and disease control”
