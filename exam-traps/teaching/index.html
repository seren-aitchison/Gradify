Teaching: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Teaching lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

How Pupils Learn

  • Judging a lesson by how well it went on the day. A lesson where pupils struggled productively may have produced more learning than one where everyone finished easily. From “Learning as a change in long-term memory”
  • Thinking pupils with a poor working memory "just need to concentrate". Overload is a design problem: reduce the number of new elements, give steps one at a time and provide external memory aids. From “Working memory and its limits”
  • Teaching topics as unconnected chunks and assuming pupils will join them up. Make the links explicit: refer back, compare, and show how the new idea fits the big picture. From “Long-term memory and schemas”
  • Assuming that because a topic was "covered" last year, pupils know it. Coverage is not retention: always check. From “Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning”

Classroom Practice

  • Treating the principles as a lesson-observation checklist where every principle must appear in every lesson. They describe teaching over time; a single lesson may emphasise only some. From “Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction”
  • Confusing explicit instruction with "chalk and talk". Explicit teaching is short steps with frequent questions and practice, not a long monologue. From “Explicit instruction and its critics”
  • Running a review as "What did we do last lesson?" and taking an answer from one volunteer. It checks one pupil's memory and gives everyone else nothing to retrieve. From “Daily, weekly and monthly review”
  • Breaking a skill into steps but never recombining them. Pupils also need practice of the whole task, or the steps remain fragments. From “Presenting new material in small steps”

Adaptive Teaching and SEND

  • Planning three different worksheets for "low, middle and high" pupils. This can cap expectations and fix pupils' attainment, and it is time-consuming; the ITTECF warns against artificially distinct tasks. From “What adaptive teaching is”
  • Reducing the content of a task because a pupil finds reading or writing hard. Remove the barrier to access, not the learning. From “Adaptive teaching versus differentiation by task”
  • Noticing that pupils are confused and carrying on because "I need to get through the plan". Covering content that pupils haven't understood wastes the rest of the lesson. From “Responding to pupils within the lesson”
  • Assuming that setting helps everyone because it narrows the range in each class. On average it helps little and can harm lower-attaining pupils. From “Attainment grouping and its evidence”

Assessment and Feedback

  • Assuming that more testing always gives more useful information. If an assessment isn't designed for the decision you need to make, its results can mislead. From “The purposes of assessment”
  • Using end-of-unit tests only to record a score. Analyse which questions pupils got wrong and plan to revisit them. From “Formative and summative assessment”
  • Treating a single score as a precise measure. Small differences between pupils, or between one test and the next, are often within measurement error. From “Validity and reliability”
  • Writing the learning objective on the board and assuming it has been "shared". Pupils understand quality by seeing and discussing examples, not by copying a sentence. From “Assessment for learning”

Learning and Behaviour

  • Mixing up UCS and CS. Ask: did this stimulus trigger the response before any learning? If yes, it is unconditioned. From “Classical conditioning”
  • Thinking extinction means the association has been unlearned. The evidence shows it is suppressed by new learning, which is why relapse is common after exposure therapy. From “Acquisition and extinction”
  • Treating spontaneous recovery as a failure of the experiment or therapy. It is a predictable property of extinction learning. From “Spontaneous recovery”
  • Confusing generalisation with spontaneous recovery. Generalisation is about similar stimuli; spontaneous recovery is about time after extinction. From “Generalisation and discrimination”

Curriculum and Subject Knowledge

  • Treating the curriculum as the list of topics on a plan. The real test is what pupils know and can do as a result. From “What a curriculum is”
  • Writing a long intent statement and stopping there. Intent matters only if it changes what is taught and what pupils learn. From “Intent, implementation and impact”
  • Assuming every state school must follow the National Curriculum. Academies and free schools need not, though most use it as a basis. From “The National Curriculum in England”
  • Equating a knowledge-rich curriculum with memorising lists of facts. Powerful knowledge is connected and conceptual, and must be understood and used, not just recalled. From “Powerful knowledge and knowledge-rich curricula”

Planning Lessons and Sequences

  • Starting with a fun activity and then looking for an objective to attach to it. Decide what pupils should learn and how you will know first, then choose the activity. From “Planning backwards from outcomes”
  • Planning each lesson as a stand-alone performance. Plan the sequence first so that each lesson builds on the previous one and prepares for the next. From “Planning a sequence of lessons”
  • Writing the task as the learning intention, such as to complete the worksheet. State the learning the task is meant to produce. From “Learning intentions and success criteria”
  • Using a starter that is fun but unrelated to the lesson. Choose a starter that brings to mind the knowledge the new content will build on. From “Activating prior knowledge”

Literacy and Oracy Across the Curriculum

  • Assuming literacy is the English department's job. Every subject has its own reading, writing and vocabulary demands that its teachers are best placed to teach. From “Literacy in every subject”
  • Thinking literacy is the same in every subject. The way a historian reads a source is different from the way a scientist reads data, and both must be taught. From “Disciplinary literacy”
  • Treating fast reading as fluent reading. Fluency also needs accuracy and expression; racing through a text without phrasing does not support understanding. From “Reading fluency”
  • Teaching strategies as stand-alone exercises with short, easy texts. Model them with real subject texts and remember that knowledge of the topic matters too. From “Reading comprehension strategies”

Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare

  • Thinking safeguarding is only the DSL's job. Every adult in school must notice, act and pass on concerns; the DSL then decides what happens next. From “What safeguarding means”
  • Relying on last year's version of KCSIE. It is updated regularly, so always check the current version and your school's policy. From “Keeping Children Safe in Education”
  • Waiting until the next day because the DSL has gone home. Speak to a deputy DSL or senior leader, and call 999 if a child is in immediate danger. From “The designated safeguarding lead”
  • Thinking schools only matter once abuse is confirmed. Schools are vital in spotting early signs and supporting early help before problems escalate. From “Working Together to Safeguard Children”

Sleep, Stress and Rest

  • Believing people can adapt fully to short sleep. Studies show performance keeps declining with chronic restriction even when people report feeling only a little tired. From “Why we sleep”
  • Thinking dreaming only happens in REM sleep. Dreams can occur in NREM sleep too, though REM dreams are usually more vivid and story-like. From “Sleep stages and cycles”
  • Assuming teenagers stay up late only by choice. The adolescent body clock shifts later biologically, though screens and social life can add to the delay. From “Circadian rhythms”
  • Concluding that long sleep causes ill health from observational data. Long sleep often reflects underlying illness, depression or poor-quality sleep. From “How much sleep we need”

Research Methods

  • Saying a study "proves" a theory. Evidence supports or fails to support; a later study may overturn it. From “Scientific reasoning in psychology”
  • Writing vague hypotheses such as "caffeine affects people". A hypothesis must name the operationalised variables and the predicted relationship. From “Research questions and hypotheses”
  • Treating the measure as if it were the concept. A memory test score measures performance on that test, not "memory" in every sense. From “Operationalisation”
  • Calling a measured characteristic like gender an IV in a true experiment. It can be a grouping variable, but it isn't manipulated, so the study is a quasi-experiment. From “Independent and dependent variables”

Education

  • Assuming education is fully meritocratic. Background strongly influences outcomes. From “Functionalist perspectives on education”
  • Assuming pupils passively absorb school values. Many resist, as Willis showed. From “Marxist perspectives on education”
  • Assuming girls' higher average achievement means gender inequality in education is over. Harassment, subject segregation and later pay gaps persist. From “Feminist perspectives on education”
  • Treating school discipline as neutral. Rules and sanctions affect some groups more than others. From “Education, socialisation and social control”
