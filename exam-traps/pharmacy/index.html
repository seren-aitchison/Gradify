Pharmacy: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Pharmacy lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Pharmacy Practice

  • Thinking of pharmacy as only dispensing: most of the profession's value is in clinical judgement and advice. From “Introduction to pharmacy”
  • Assuming another sector "knows" about a change in medicines: communicate changes at every transition. From “Pharmacy across sectors”
  • Delegating a task to someone who hasn't been trained for it because the pharmacy is busy. From “The pharmacy team”
  • Thinking the GPhC represents pharmacists' interests: it regulates in the public interest. From “The GPhC and professional regulation”

Pharmacology: How Drugs Act

  • Learning drugs as lists of names and doses without their mechanisms: mechanisms are what let you predict effects, interactions and harms. From “What pharmacology is”
  • Assuming a "selective" drug has no effect on other targets: selectivity is a matter of degree. From “Drug targets”
  • Expecting an oral corticosteroid to work as quickly as an inhaled bronchodilator: nuclear receptor effects take hours. From “Receptors and receptor families”
  • Assuming one dose of naloxone is enough after a long-acting opioid overdose: its effect can wear off first. From “Agonists and antagonists”

Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics

  • Assuming everyone given the same dose reaches the same concentration: kidney function, age and interactions change it. From “Introduction to pharmacokinetics”
  • Assuming oral and IV doses are interchangeable: oral bioavailability is often lower, so the doses can differ. From “Routes of administration”
  • Taking a tetracycline or quinolone with milk, antacids or iron, which chelate it and reduce absorption. From “Absorption and bioavailability”
  • Converting between oral and IV morphine as if the doses were equivalent: oral doses are larger because of first-pass metabolism. From “First-pass metabolism”

Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry

  • Treating a drug as one fixed property. Each functional group contributes separately to binding, ionisation and breakdown. From “Functional groups in drugs”
  • Reading a base's pKa as if it were an acid's. A high pKa means a strong base, which is mostly ionised at plasma pH. From “Acids, bases and pKa”
  • Using the acid form of the equation for a base. Write out which form is ionised before calculating. From “Ionisation and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation”
  • Assuming more lipophilic always means better absorption. Very lipophilic drugs may not dissolve well enough to be absorbed. From “Lipophilicity and partition coefficients”

Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms

  • Treating different formulations of the same drug as interchangeable. Release rate, bioavailability and dose can differ. From “Introduction to pharmaceutics”
  • Confusing solubility with dissolution rate. Solubility is how much can dissolve; dissolution rate is how fast it does. From “Solubility and dissolution”
  • Assuming smaller particles are always better. Very fine powders dissolve faster but often flow and handle poorly. From “Particle size and powders”
  • Assuming viscosity is fixed. Many pharmaceutical products change viscosity with shaking, rubbing or temperature. From “Rheology”

Pharmaceutical Calculations

  • Abbreviating micrograms as "mcg" or "µg" on a label or record: the convention in UK practice is to write it in full. From “Units and conversions”
  • Rounding an intermediate step (for example a concentration) and then using the rounded figure in the next step. From “Significant figures and rounding”
  • Inverting the ratio (dividing the stock by the dose), which gives an answer that is wrong by a large factor. From “Ratio and proportion”
  • Treating a w/w cream strength as if it were w/v, or forgetting that the percentage refers to the final product, not the solvent. From “Percentage concentrations”

Cardiovascular Therapeutics

  • Starting long-term treatment on a single raised clinic reading without confirming it with ambulatory or home readings. From “Hypertension”
  • Starting an ACE inhibitor without checking kidney function and potassium before and after. From “Antihypertensive drugs”
  • Stopping a statin at the first mention of aches without assessing the symptoms or trying another statin or dose. From “Lipid management”
  • Calculating QRISK3 for someone who has already had a heart attack: they need secondary prevention regardless. From “Cardiovascular risk assessment”

Respiratory Therapeutics

  • Supplying repeated salbutamol inhalers to someone without an inhaled corticosteroid, without referring them for review. From “Asthma in adults”
  • Giving a child a dry powder inhaler they can't inhale through forcefully enough to use. From “Asthma in children”
  • Starting an ICS in COPD without asthmatic features or frequent exacerbations, adding pneumonia risk without clear benefit. From “Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”
  • Assuming someone knows how to use an inhaler because they've had it for years. From “Inhaler devices and technique”

Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics

  • Advising someone with type 1 diabetes who isn't eating because of a stomach bug to stop their insulin. From “Type 1 diabetes”
  • Writing "10U" on a prescription, which can be read as 100 units. From “Insulins”
  • Chasing a tight HbA1c target in a frail older person, causing hypoglycaemia and falls. From “Type 2 diabetes”
  • Reassuring someone on an SGLT2 inhibitor with vomiting and abdominal pain because their glucose is only modestly raised: euglycaemic DKA is possible. From “Glucose-lowering drugs”

Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics

  • Stopping an antidepressant as soon as the person feels better, increasing the risk of relapse. From “Depression”
  • Supplying tramadol to someone on an SSRI without considering serotonin syndrome and seizure risk. From “Antidepressants”
  • Repeating benzodiazepine prescriptions for months for generalised anxiety without a plan to reduce. From “Anxiety disorders”
  • Recommending ibuprofen for back pain to someone on lithium. From “Bipolar disorder and lithium”

Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship

  • Starting antibiotics before sending a sample, when the result would have guided better treatment. From “Microbiology for pharmacists”
  • Choosing a quinolone for an uncomplicated infection when another antibiotic would work. From “Antibiotic classes and mechanisms”
  • Supplying leftover antibiotics or encouraging people to keep antibiotics "just in case". From “Antimicrobial resistance”
  • Continuing IV antibiotics past 72 hours without review, when the person could switch to oral treatment. From “Antimicrobial stewardship”

Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics

  • Selling antacids repeatedly to a 60-year-old with weight loss and difficulty swallowing. From “Dyspepsia and gastro-oesophageal reflux”
  • Testing for H. pylori while the person is still taking a PPI. From “Peptic ulcer disease and Helicobacter pylori”
  • Supplying metoclopramide long term to a young woman. From “Nausea and vomiting”
  • Recommending ispaghula for someone with opioid constipation. From “Constipation”

Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions

  • Selling ibuprofen to someone with heart failure on furosemide and ramipril. From “Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs”
  • Recommending glucosamine supplements instead of exercise. From “Osteoarthritis”
  • Treating swollen hand joints for months with ibuprofen alone without referral. From “Rheumatoid arthritis”
  • Starting allopurinol in someone taking azathioprine without specialist advice. From “Gout”

Oncology, Haematology and Immunology

  • Giving folic acid alone to someone with undiagnosed B12 deficiency. From “Anaemias”
  • Delaying vitamin K and PCC for someone on warfarin with a major bleed. From “Blood products and haematological support”
  • Treating oral chemotherapy as low-risk because it's a tablet. From “Principles of cancer treatment”
  • Advising someone on chemotherapy with a fever to take paracetamol and wait. From “Cytotoxic chemotherapy”

Special Populations

  • Using a kitchen teaspoon to measure a child's antibiotic dose. From “Children and young people”
  • Confusing caffeine citrate with caffeine base when calculating a dose. From “Neonates”
  • Advising a pregnant woman to stop her antiepileptic immediately without specialist advice. From “Pregnancy”
  • Advising a mother to stop breastfeeding to take amoxicillin. From “Breastfeeding”

Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance

  • Assuming the GPhC regulates pharmacy in Northern Ireland: that's the PSNI. From “The legal framework for medicines”
  • Assuming a product is P because the medicine is P: the same drug can be POM at a higher strength or larger pack. From “Classes of medicines: POM, P and GSL”
  • Supplying a Schedule 4 controlled drug on a prescription dated over 28 days ago, treating it like an ordinary 6-month POM prescription. From “Prescriptions and prescription requirements”
  • Assuming any independent prescriber can prescribe any controlled drug for addiction: diamorphine, dipipanone and cocaine for addiction need a Home Office licence. From “Who can prescribe”

Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation

  • Checking only that the dose is within BNF limits, without asking whether the medicine is right for this person. From “The clinical check”
  • Restarting a medicine that was deliberately stopped before admission, because it's still on an old list. From “Medicines reconciliation”
  • Contacting the prescriber about every flagged interaction, including trivial ones, so that the important ones lose impact. From “Drug interactions in practice”
  • Missing a prescribing cascade, where a new drug is added to treat an unrecognised side effect of another. From “Identifying and managing adverse drug reactions”

Prescribing and Clinical Assessment

  • Prescribing outside your area of competence because the law technically allows it. From “The independent prescriber”
  • Treating the framework as a one-off checklist rather than a tool for ongoing development. From “The RPS competency framework for all prescribers”
  • Prescribing for a friend at a social event without a proper assessment. From “Prescribing governance and scope”
  • Ending a consultation without telling the person what to do if symptoms worsen. From “Consultation models”

Registration Assessment and Foundation Training

  • Assuming the pass mark is always the same percentage between sittings. From “The GPhC registration assessment”
  • Revising only favourite topics instead of covering the framework. From “The assessment framework”
  • Choosing an option that's true but doesn't answer the specific lead-in. From “Single best answer questions”
  • Ruling out the right answer because it was used in the previous question. From “Extended matching questions”
