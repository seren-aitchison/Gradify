Investing: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Investing lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Money Foundations

  • Thinking investing is just "saving with a better rate". Investments carry no promise of getting your money back, and the higher expected return is payment for accepting that risk. From “Saving and investing: what is the difference?”
  • Budgeting from gross salary. Tax, National Insurance and pension deductions can take a large share of gross pay, so a budget based on gross income will overstate what you have. From “Budgeting and cash flow”
  • Counting investments as the emergency fund. A share fund may be down 20% exactly when you lose your job, because recessions hit both at once. From “The emergency fund”
  • Treating a 0% balance transfer as free. When the promotional period ends the rate often jumps, and missing a payment can end the deal early; plan to clear it before then. From “Debt before investing”

How Financial Markets Work

  • Thinking financial markets create returns out of nothing. Over the long run, returns come from the cash flows of real businesses, borrowers and assets. From “The role of the financial system”
  • Assuming money in a bank sits in a vault. Banks lend most of it; deposit protection (FSCS) and regulation exist because they do. From “Savers, borrowers and financial intermediaries”
  • Believing banks simply lend out existing savers' money. Lending creates new deposits; savers' deposits and capital matter for funding and safety, but the order is the other way round. From “Banks and the Bank of England”
  • Expecting markets to move on a rate decision itself. Prices react to the difference between the decision and what was expected; a widely predicted rise may barely move them. From “Interest rates and monetary policy”

Asset Classes

  • Leaving large amounts of uninvested cash on a platform earning little interest. Check what rate, if any, the platform pays. From “Cash as an asset class”
  • Confusing the coupon rate with the yield. The coupon is fixed on nominal value; the yield depends on the price you pay. From “What a bond is”
  • Thinking gilts cannot lose value. Their prices move with interest rates, and long gilts fell heavily in 2022. From “Gilts”
  • Treating a high yield as free income. A high yield signals high risk of default or price falls. From “Corporate bonds and credit ratings”

Risk and Return

  • Dividing a cumulative return by the number of years. That ignores compounding and overstates the annual rate. From “Holding period return”
  • Projecting wealth by compounding an arithmetic average return. That overstates the likely outcome when returns are volatile. From “Arithmetic and geometric average returns”
  • Comparing a fund's total return with an index's price return, or judging long-run share performance from price charts alone. From “Total return and reinvested income”
  • Comparing returns from a high-inflation period with those from a low-inflation period without adjusting for inflation. From “Nominal and real returns in practice”

Diversification and Portfolio Theory

  • Thinking you are diversified because you own many funds. If they all hold the same large companies, the overlap leaves you concentrated. From “Why diversification works”
  • Assuming correlation means causation, or that a historical correlation will hold in the next crisis. From “Correlation between assets”
  • Holding a large stake in your employer's shares because you "know the company". Your income already depends on it. From “Specific and systematic risk”
  • Counting holdings rather than exposures. Ten funds can hold the same companies; thirty shares can sit in one sector. From “How many holdings are enough?”

Valuation

  • Assuming a share that has fallen a long way must now be cheap. A lower price says nothing about value on its own; the business may have become worth less. From “Price and value”
  • Presenting a DCF value to the penny as if it were precise. Show a range, because modest changes in inputs produce large changes in value. From “Discounted cash flow valuation”
  • Concluding that a company paying no dividend is worthless under the DDM. Its value comes from dividends it may pay in future, or from other payouts, which the model must forecast. From “The dividend discount model”
  • Using the dividend just paid in place of next year's dividend. The formula needs D1, so multiply D0 by (1 + g) first. From “The Gordon growth model”

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

  • Drawing conclusions from one ratio for one year. Ratios need context: the industry, the company's strategy, the economy and the trend over several years. From “Why analyse financial statements?”
  • Comparing ROCE across companies that calculate capital employed differently. Check definitions, and note that revaluations or old, heavily depreciated assets can distort ROCE. From “Profitability ratios”
  • Treating the quick ratio as always more reliable. It excludes inventory, but receivables may also be slow or doubtful; check receivables days and cash flows too. From “Liquidity ratios”
  • Using year-end balances without considering seasonality. A year-end at a quiet point may make inventory or receivables look unusually low; averages give a better picture. From “Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle”

Funds, Index Investing and Costs

  • Thinking that a fund protects you from market losses. Pooling spreads specific risk, but if the market falls, the fund falls too, and FSCS does not compensate for investment losses. From “Why pool investments?”
  • Thinking an open-ended fund's price rises because many people are buying it. New money simply creates new units at NAV; the price only moves with the value of the underlying assets. From “Unit trusts and OEICs”
  • Assuming a wide discount is free money. Discounts can stay wide or widen further, especially in falling markets, so a discount is only an advantage if it narrows or at least does not widen. From “Investment trusts”
  • Treating every exchange-traded product as a diversified fund. ETCs and ETNs are debt of an issuer, and leveraged or inverse products behave very differently from a simple tracker over time. From “Exchange-traded funds”

Behavioural Finance

  • Assuming that being clever or well informed removes bias. Biases are features of how all people think, including experts; good processes help more than intelligence. From “Rational investors and their limits”
  • Judging an investment by whether it is above or below the price you paid. The purchase price is irrelevant to future returns; what matters is the investment's prospects from today. From “Loss aversion and prospect theory”
  • Treating a run of good results as proof of skill. In a rising market most portfolios gain; compare your returns with a tracker before concluding you have an edge. From “Overconfidence”
  • Believing a share must be cheap because it is far below its previous high. Past prices reflect past expectations; what matters is the value of future cash flows. From “Anchoring and investment decisions”

UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax

  • Assuming all investment returns are taxed the same way. Interest, dividends and gains have different allowances and rates, and the account they are held in changes the tax completely. From “How investments are taxed in the UK”
  • Thinking the dividend allowance means the first £500 of dividends does not count towards your tax band. It is taxed at 0%, but it still uses up part of your band. From “Income tax on savings and dividends”
  • Thinking you avoid CGT by selling and buying straight back. Under the 30-day rule, a repurchase within 30 days is matched with the sale, so no gain or loss is crystallised; Bed and ISA works because the new holding is inside an ISA. From “Capital gains tax”
  • Leaving large sums in a GIA for years while ISA allowances go unused. Each year's ISA allowance is lost if not used, and sheltering investments early saves tax for decades. From “General investment accounts”

Regulation, Protection and Ethics

  • Thinking regulation means an investment is safe. Regulation controls how firms behave; it does not guarantee returns. From “Why financial services are regulated”
  • Assuming a firm is genuine because it uses a real company's name. Clone firms copy names of authorised firms, so check contact details on the register. From “The FCA and the PRA”
  • Thinking the Consumer Duty guarantees good investment returns. It sets standards for firms' conduct, not for market performance. From “The Consumer Duty”
  • Treating guidance as advice. Guidance services do not recommend what you should do, so you cannot complain that a recommendation was unsuitable. From “Advice, guidance and execution-only”

Building Your Plan

  • Investing before building any cash buffer. Without an emergency fund, a surprise bill may force you to sell investments at a bad time. From “The personal financial plan”
  • Rewriting the plan every time markets fall. Change the IPS when goals or circumstances change, not because of short-term market moves. From “An investment policy statement”
  • Using the same asset allocation for every goal. Short-term goals need safer assets than long-term goals. From “Choosing an asset allocation”
  • Thinking more funds always means more diversification. Overlapping funds often hold the same companies. From “Choosing funds for a portfolio”
