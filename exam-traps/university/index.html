University Study Skills: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our University Study Skills lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Starting University and Independent Learning

  • Treating timetabled hours as the whole workload. A light timetable usually means more independent study is expected, not less work. From “What is different about university study”
  • Thinking that asking a tutor or peer for help is "not independent". Choosing to seek help when you need it is part of self-direction. From “Independent learning”
  • Assuming the first year "doesn't count". It often carries no weight in the degree classification, but you must usually pass it to progress, and its credits count towards the award. From “How degrees are structured: credits, levels and modules”
  • Ignoring the outcomes in the module handbook and studying only from the lecture slides. The outcomes, not the slides, define what you must be able to do. From “Learning outcomes and the QAA level descriptors”

How Pupils Learn

  • Judging a lesson by how well it went on the day. A lesson where pupils struggled productively may have produced more learning than one where everyone finished easily. From “Learning as a change in long-term memory”
  • Thinking pupils with a poor working memory "just need to concentrate". Overload is a design problem: reduce the number of new elements, give steps one at a time and provide external memory aids. From “Working memory and its limits”
  • Teaching topics as unconnected chunks and assuming pupils will join them up. Make the links explicit: refer back, compare, and show how the new idea fits the big picture. From “Long-term memory and schemas”
  • Assuming that because a topic was "covered" last year, pupils know it. Coverage is not retention: always check. From “Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning”

Time Management, Lectures and Note-making

  • Treating timetabled classes as the whole of a course. Most learning at university happens in independent study. From “How students use their time”
  • Planning to start an assignment "when I have time". Without scheduled steps, work is pushed to the last minute. From “Semester and weekly planning”
  • Spending the day on urgent but unimportant tasks such as messages. Schedule important work first. From “Prioritising tasks”
  • Believing you work best under last-minute pressure. Research links procrastination to more stress and poorer performance for most people. From “Procrastination”

Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking

  • Relying only on textbooks and websites for an essay. Use them to get started, then cite peer-reviewed articles and primary sources for your evidence. From “Types of academic text”
  • Starting at page one of every book on the list. Set a purpose, check the contents and index, and read only what serves that purpose. From “Reading strategically: purpose and selection”
  • Reading only the abstract and citing the article as if you had read it. Abstracts compress and can overstate; read at least the discussion and, for key evidence, the methods. From “Reading a journal article”
  • Highlighting most of a page and calling it studying. Highlighting alone is passive; turn the text into questions and test yourself. From “Reading actively: SQ3R and questioning”

Academic Writing

  • Thinking that academic writing means long words and complicated sentences. Precision and clarity are valued more than complexity. From “What academic writing is”
  • Treating "evaluate" or "to what extent" as "describe". These questions require a judgement supported by evidence, not just information. From “Analysing the question”
  • Starting to write without a plan and hoping an argument will emerge. Write at least a brief outline with a thesis first. From “Planning and outlining”
  • Polishing every sentence before moving on. This slows drafting and fuels block; draft roughly first, then edit. From “Drafting”

Referencing and Academic Integrity

  • Seeing referencing only as a way to avoid plagiarism penalties. It is also what makes your evidence visible and your argument credible. From “Why we reference”
  • Assuming that information found online is common knowledge. Being freely available does not make something common knowledge; specific facts and ideas still need a source. From “What needs a reference”
  • Mixing styles in one piece of work, for example using footnotes in some places and Harvard in others. Choose the required style and be consistent. From “Referencing styles: an overview”
  • Writing the author's first name or full name in the in-text citation. Use the surname only, with the year. From “Harvard referencing”

Research and Information Literacy

  • Thinking information literacy just means being good at searching. Evaluating and using information ethically are equally important. From “Information literacy”
  • Choosing a question so broad it could fill a library. Focused questions lead to deeper, more manageable work. From “Defining a research question”
  • Typing a whole sentence into a database. Databases work best with keywords combined by Boolean operators. From “Search strategies: keywords and Boolean operators”
  • Assuming that if Google does not show the full text, the library cannot provide it. Library logins and interlibrary loan give access to much more. From “Library catalogues and discovery tools”

Numeracy and Data Skills

  • Believing some people are just "not maths people". Number skills improve with practice and support. From “Maths anxiety and number confidence”
  • Confusing percentage points with percentage change. A rise from 20% to 25% is 5 points but a 25% relative increase. From “Percentages, ratios and proportions”
  • Reporting results with false precision. Too many decimal places suggest more accuracy than the data support. From “Rounding, significant figures and estimation”
  • Forgetting to square or cube conversion factors for area and volume. 1 m² is 10,000 cm², not 100 cm². From “Units and conversions”

Professional Communication

  • Writing for yourself: putting things in the order you discovered them, or using the vocabulary of your own team, rather than in the order and words the reader needs. From “Communication at work: purpose and audience”
  • Jumping straight to advice. Solving the problem you assume someone has, before they've finished, often solves the wrong problem and signals that you weren't listening. From “Active listening”
  • Asking a leading question in an investigation or interview ("You saw him take it, didn't you?"), which can contaminate the answer and undermine fairness. From “Questioning skills”
  • Treating body-language "rules" as lie detection. Research gives little support to the idea that people can reliably spot deception from non-verbal cues. From “Non-verbal communication”

Exams and Revision

  • Thinking open-book exams need no revision. Without good understanding and organised notes, there is not enough time to look everything up. From “Types of exam”
  • Leaving revision until the last few days. Cramming may help short-term recall but leads to weaker long-term memory and more stress. From “Planning revision”
  • Choosing techniques because they feel easy. Effortful techniques such as self-testing are more effective, even if they feel harder. From “Revision techniques that work”
  • Trying to predict exam questions and revising only those topics. Exams may cover any part of the syllabus. From “Past papers and practice questions”

Sleep, Stress and Rest

  • Believing people can adapt fully to short sleep. Studies show performance keeps declining with chronic restriction even when people report feeling only a little tired. From “Why we sleep”
  • Thinking dreaming only happens in REM sleep. Dreams can occur in NREM sleep too, though REM dreams are usually more vivid and story-like. From “Sleep stages and cycles”
  • Assuming teenagers stay up late only by choice. The adolescent body clock shifts later biologically, though screens and social life can add to the delay. From “Circadian rhythms”
  • Concluding that long sleep causes ill health from observational data. Long sleep often reflects underlying illness, depression or poor-quality sleep. From “How much sleep we need”

Knowing Yourself

  • Thinking career development means choosing the "right" job once. Most people's careers involve many decisions and transitions, so the skills of managing a career matter more than any single choice. From “What career development is”
  • Treating a matching quiz result as the answer. Matching theories assume stable people and stable jobs; real careers also involve development, chance and changing labour markets. From “Career theories: matching, development and chance”
  • Assuming careers guidance just means one meeting with an adviser. Personal guidance is only one benchmark; the others are about programmes, information, encounters and experiences over time. From “The Gatsby Benchmarks and careers support”
  • Listing skills as bare adjectives ("excellent communicator, team player"). Without evidence a recruiter can't tell claim from reality; every skill needs an example. From “Identifying your skills”
