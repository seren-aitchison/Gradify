Mathematics: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Mathematics lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Number and Arithmetic

  • Treating subtraction as commutative: 3 − 7 isn't the same as 7 − 3. From “Arithmetic”
  • Doing all multiplication before any division: they have equal priority and go left to right. From “Order of Operations”
  • Forgetting the zero placeholder, writing 2,046 as 246. From “Number Systems”
  • Assuming everyone agrees whether 0 is a natural number. From “Natural Numbers”

Number Theory and Cryptography

  • Checking a few values of n and calling it a proof. From “Elementary Number Theory”
  • Dividing both sides of a congruence by a number that isn't coprime to the modulus. From “Modular Arithmetic”
  • Assuming every linear congruence has exactly one solution. From “Congruences”
  • Stopping at the zero remainder and reporting 0 instead of the previous remainder. From “Euclidean Algorithm”

Algebra

  • Writing "5 less than n" as 5 − n: it's n − 5. From “Basic Algebra”
  • Dividing both sides by an expression that might be zero, which can lose solutions. From “Elementary Algebra”
  • Squaring before applying the coefficient's sign wrongly: −x2 with x = −3 is −9, not 9. From “Algebraic Expressions”
  • Combining unlike terms, such as x2 + x2 = x4 (it's 2x2). From “Simplifying Expressions”

Abstract Algebra

  • Assuming a property of one example (such as commutativity) holds for every structure of that type. From “Abstract Algebra”
  • Forgetting to check associativity because it's true for familiar operations. From “Algebraic Structures”
  • Assuming the converse of Lagrange: a divisor of |G| need not be the order of a subgroup. From “Group Theory”
  • Forgetting to check the operation is preserved for all pairs, not just a few. From “Homomorphisms”

Geometry

  • Treating a diagram as a proof: a diagram can mislead, while reasoning from axioms can't. From “Euclidean Geometry”
  • Assuming a shape has a property (equal sides, right angles) because it looks that way in a diagram. From “Plane Geometry”
  • Assuming that two lines that aren't parallel must meet: in 3D they may be skew. From “Points, Lines and Planes”
  • Reading the wrong scale on a protractor. From “Angles”

Trigonometry

  • Labelling sides before choosing the angle, or calling the hypotenuse the adjacent side. From “Basic Trigonometry”
  • Using degree values in a calculator set to radians. From “Trigonometric Ratios”
  • Giving only the calculator's principal value when an equation has several solutions in the range. From “Sine”
  • Assuming cos x = −0.5 means x = −60°, which isn't in the usual range. From “Cosine”

Calculus

  • Using degrees in calculus: d/dx (sin x) = cos x only when x is in radians. From “Precalculus”
  • Treating dy/dx as an ordinary fraction in every situation: it's a limit, though it often behaves like a fraction. From “Infinitesimal Calculus”
  • Concluding that a 0/0 limit is 0, 1 or undefined without further work. From “Limits”
  • Using a sign change to claim a root when the function has an asymptote in the interval. From “Continuity”

Multivariable and Vector Calculus

  • Concluding a limit exists after checking only one or two paths. From “Multivariable Calculus”
  • Forgetting to normalise the direction vector to length 1. From “Directional Derivatives”
  • Using the gradient as a tangent direction instead of a normal to the level curve. From “Gradient”
  • Using constant limits for the inner integral when the region isn't a rectangle. From “Multiple Integrals”

Analysis

  • Choosing N that depends on n rather than only on ε. From “Real Analysis”
  • Including poles outside the contour in the sum of residues. From “Complex Analysis”
  • Assuming every Fourier series converges at every point. From “Harmonic Analysis”
  • Assuming that a set with measure zero must be countable. From “Measure Theory”

Differential Equations

  • Forgetting that the general solution of an nth-order equation needs n constants. From “Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs)”
  • Using the actual temperature T instead of the difference T − Ts in Newton's law. From “First-Order Differential Equations”
  • Dividing by h(y) and losing the solution where h(y) = 0. From “Separable Differential Equations”
  • Forgetting to put the equation in standard form (coefficient of y′ equal to 1) before finding μ. From “Linear Differential Equations”

Linear Algebra and Matrices

  • Writing the vector from A to B as a − b instead of b − a. From “Vectors”
  • Taking the cross product of 2D vectors or confusing its order (it's anticommutative). From “Vector Algebra”
  • Forgetting that a vector space must contain the zero vector. From “Vector Spaces”
  • Checking only closure under addition and forgetting scaling (or the zero vector). From “Subspaces”

Probability

  • Treating outcomes as equally likely when they aren't, such as the totals 2 to 12 on two dice. From “Probability Theory”
  • Adding P(A) and P(B) for events that overlap. From “Foundations of Probability”
  • Confusing independent with mutually exclusive. From “Independent Events”
  • Using the same probabilities for the second draw when sampling without replacement. From “Conditional Probability”

Statistics

  • Reporting the mean alone for heavily skewed data. From “Descriptive Statistics”
  • Concluding that one variable causes the other from a correlation alone. From “Correlation”
  • Reading a 50% relative increase as a 50% chance of harm. From “Data Interpretation”
  • Treating a poll lead smaller than the margin of error as decisive. From “Statistics in Everyday Life”

Discrete Mathematics and Combinatorics

  • Proving only the base case and the step for one specific k rather than general k. From “Discrete Mathematics”
  • Counting lattice paths as m × n instead of choosing step positions. From “Combinatorics”
  • Adding counts of overlapping cases. From “Counting Principles”
  • Forgetting to divide by the factorials of repeated letters. From “Permutations”

Graph Theory and Networks

  • Assuming two different drawings show different graphs. From “Graph Theory”
  • Concluding two graphs are isomorphic because they have the same number of vertices and edges. From “Undirected Graphs”
  • Treating a directed graph's adjacency matrix as symmetric. From “Directed Graphs”
  • Comparing paths by number of edges when the question is about total weight. From “Weighted Graphs”

Logic, Sets and Foundations

  • Confusing ∈ with ⊆, for example writing 1 ⊆ {1, 2}. From “Elementary Set Theory”
  • Assuming any property defines a set (it may only carve a subset from an existing set). From “Set Theory”
  • Treating A \ B and B \ A as equal. From “Set Operations”
  • Assuming an intersection of nested non-empty sets must be non-empty. From “Union and Intersection”

Topology and Advanced Pure Mathematics

  • Treating geometric properties like length or curvature as topological. From “General Topology”
  • Thinking every set is either open or closed (many are neither, like [0, 1)). From “Point-Set Topology”
  • Requiring arbitrary intersections of open sets to be open. From “Topological Spaces”
  • Forgetting to check the triangle inequality when proposing a metric. From “Metric Spaces”

Optimisation and Operations Research

  • Forgetting to check that a stationary point is a maximum and is feasible. From “Mathematical Optimisation”
  • Concluding a point is a minimum from ∇f = 0 alone. From “Unconstrained Optimisation”
  • Taking the unconstrained optimum without checking it's feasible. From “Constrained Optimisation”
  • Forgetting to include the constraint equation itself when solving. From “Lagrange Multipliers”

Numerical Analysis and Scientific Computing

  • Assuming more digits in the output means a more accurate answer. From “Numerical Analysis”
  • Judging a method by one run instead of checking errors under refinement. From “Numerical Methods”
  • Expecting a Taylor polynomial to be accurate across a whole interval. From “Numerical Approximation”
  • Reporting absolute error for quantities of very different sizes, where relative error is meaningful. From “Error Analysis”

Mathematical Modelling and Applied Mathematics

  • Thinking applied mathematics is only about using existing formulas. From “Applied Mathematics”
  • Fitting a model to all the data and then claiming it's validated by the same data. From “Mathematical Modelling”
  • Forgetting that dimensional analysis can't give dimensionless constants such as 2π. From “Dimensional Analysis”
  • Testing a scale model without matching the dimensionless numbers that govern the flow. From “Similarity Analysis”

Financial Mathematics and Economics

  • Comparing cash flows at different dates without discounting them to a common date. From “Financial Mathematics”
  • Using months as t without converting to years. From “Simple Interest”
  • Comparing nominal rates with different compounding frequencies without converting to AER. From “Compound Interest”
  • Adding cash flows due at different dates without moving them to a common date. From “Time Value of Money”

Time Series and Forecasting

  • Randomly splitting a time series into training and test sets, which leaks future information. From “Time Series Analysis”
  • Using an additive decomposition when seasonal swings grow with the level. From “Time Series Decomposition”
  • Regressing two independent random walks on each other and reporting a strong relationship. From “Trend Analysis”
  • Including a dummy for every season as well as an intercept, causing perfect collinearity. From “Seasonality”

Information Theory and Signal Processing

  • Confusing information in Shannon's sense with meaning or importance. From “Information Theory”
  • Forgetting that 0 log 0 is taken as 0, so impossible outcomes add nothing. From “Entropy”
  • Thinking that observing any particular value of X must reduce uncertainty about Y. From “Conditional Entropy”
  • Concluding independence from zero correlation. From “Mutual Information”

Mathematics for Machine Learning and AI

  • Judging a model by its training error alone. From “Mathematics for Machine Learning”
  • Assuming more parameters always means worse generalisation. From “Statistical Learning Theory”
  • Using linear regression for a binary outcome, which can predict probabilities outside [0, 1]. From “Linear Models”
  • Keeping a fixed large learning rate throughout training, which prevents fine convergence. From “Optimisation Algorithms”

Control Theory and Systems

  • Designing a controller without considering sensor noise and actuator limits. From “Control Theory”
  • Applying superposition to a system with saturation or other non-linearity. From “Linear Systems”
  • Concluding global stability from a linearisation, which only describes behaviour near the equilibrium. From “Nonlinear Systems”
  • Choosing too few state variables, so the model can't capture the system's memory. From “State-Space Representation”

Mathematical Reasoning and Research Methods

  • Treating a pattern that fits several cases as proved. From “Mathematical Reasoning”
  • Dividing by an expression without checking that it can't be zero. From “Mathematical Proofs”
  • Affirming the consequent: reasoning from Q back to P. From “Deductive Reasoning”
  • Confusing inductive generalisation with mathematical induction. From “Inductive Reasoning”

Mathematical Software and Tools

  • Expecting Simplify[Sqrt[x^2]] to return x without stating that x is positive. From “Mathematica”
  • Using = when a delayed definition := is needed, so the right-hand side is evaluated too early. From “Wolfram Language”
  • Accepting an answer without checking how Wolfram|Alpha interpreted the query. From “Wolfram Alpha”
  • Relying on definitions from cells that were deleted or evaluated out of order. From “Mathematica Notebooks”

School and Examination Mathematics

  • Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column instead of exchanging. From “Primary Mathematics”
  • Answering the first calculation (the area) and forgetting the rest of the question (waste and cost). From “Functional Skills Mathematics”
  • Dividing only part of an expression when rearranging, such as writing t = v − u/a. From “Foundation Mathematics”
  • Writing only the final answer, which loses method marks if it's wrong. From “GCSE Mathematics”

Everyday and Practical Mathematics

  • Choosing a loan by the lowest monthly payment without looking at the total cost. From “Financial Literacy”
  • Converting weekly to monthly by multiplying by 4, which understates yearly costs. From “Personal Budgeting”
  • Forgetting the standing charge, which is paid even with no use. From “Household Budgeting”
  • Taxing the whole salary at the highest rate it reaches. From “Tax Calculations”
