Dentistry: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Dentistry lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy

  • Thinking the facial skeleton is uniformly strong. It is built of strong vertical buttresses (nasomaxillary, zygomaticomaxillary, pterygomaxillary) with thin bone between, including the walls of the maxillary sinus, which fracture easily. From “The skull and facial skeleton”
  • Assuming upper molar pain is always dental. Maxillary sinusitis can cause aching of several upper posterior teeth, because the superior alveolar nerves run in the sinus walls; tenderness of several adjacent teeth, worse on bending forward, points to the sinus. From “The maxilla and palate”
  • Placing the needle for an inferior alveolar block too low. The mandibular foramen lies roughly level with the occlusal plane in adults (higher than in children), so a low injection misses the nerve before it enters the canal. From “The mandible”
  • Treating every click as disease. Joint sounds are common in people without pain; management is guided by pain and function, not the sound alone. From “The temporomandibular joint”

Teeth: Morphology, Development and Oral Histology

  • Treating "11" in FDI as the eleventh tooth. In FDI, 11 is the upper right central incisor; in Universal, 11 is the upper left canine. Always state which system a record uses, because the same number means different teeth. From “Dental notation systems”
  • Calling the inner surface of an upper tooth "lingual" in a record where the local convention is "palatal". Both are understood, but use the convention consistently; mixing them causes confusion in charting. From “The primary and permanent dentitions”
  • Assuming every lower incisor has one canal. A second, lingual canal is common; missing it is a frequent cause of root canal treatment failure in lower incisors. From “Permanent incisors and canines”
  • Overlooking the mesial concavity of the upper first premolar. A straight matrix band leaves a gap or overhang at the cervical margin there; wedges and contoured matrices are needed. From “Permanent premolars”

Foundations of Medical Science

  • Assuming all body cells can divide to replace losses. Neurones and cardiac myocytes are essentially permanent (non-dividing) cells, so their loss is replaced by scar, not new tissue. From “Cell structure and function in health”
  • Thinking hyperkalaemia makes cells more excitable for long. A small depolarisation first brings cells nearer threshold, but sustained depolarisation inactivates Na⁺ channels, so the net effect in the heart is depressed conduction. From “Membranes, transport and the resting potential”
  • Calling any loop that ends a process "positive feedback". Positive feedback amplifies the original change; the event that stops it (such as delivery of the baby) is outside the loop. From “Homeostasis and negative feedback”
  • Treating every low sodium as a sodium deficit. Hyponatraemia must be assessed with the patient's volume status, plasma and urine osmolality and urine sodium; giving saline to someone with fluid overload or SIADH can make things worse. From “Body fluids, osmolality and electrolytes”

Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health

  • Equating "caries" with "a hole". Most of the disease process happens before cavitation, when it is reversible or arrestable by prevention alone. From “What dental caries is”
  • Aiming to "kill the caries bacteria" with long courses of antimicrobials. The resident microbiota recolonises quickly; without changing sugar exposure and pH, the cariogenic community returns. From “The ecological plaque hypothesis”
  • Using a sharp probe to "stick" in fissures to diagnose caries. It can cavitate the fragile surface zone of an early lesion and transfer bacteria; caries is assessed visually on clean, dry teeth with radiographs as needed. From “Demineralisation and remineralisation”
  • Recommending fruit juice and smoothies as healthy between-meal drinks for teeth. They contain free sugars and are acidic; they are best limited and taken with meals. From “Diet, sugars and caries”

Periodontology

  • Equating a 1–3 mm probing depth with the histological sulcus depth. The probe tip penetrates the junctional epithelium, so clinical probing depth is greater than the true sulcus, especially when tissues are inflamed. From “The periodontium in health”
  • Interpreting little bleeding in a smoker as health. Nicotine causes vasoconstriction, so smokers bleed less despite disease; probing depths and attachment levels must be checked. From “Gingivitis”
  • Thinking periodontitis is simply an infection to be cured with antibiotics. Bacteria are necessary, but most destruction is host-mediated, and disruption of the biofilm with control of risk factors is the basis of treatment. From “Pathogenesis of periodontitis”
  • Telling smokers their gums look healthy because they bleed little. Smoking masks the key sign of inflammation; disease must be assessed by probing and radiographs. From “Risk factors for periodontitis”

Clinical Method and Reasoning

  • Recording "allergic to penicillin" without the reaction. Diarrhoea or nausea is an intolerance; a rash or anaphylaxis is an allergy. The difference decides whether a patient can safely receive a whole class of antibiotics, and unverified labels lead to broader, less effective prescribing. From “The medical history”
  • Thinking patient-centred means doing whatever the patient asks. It means understanding the patient's perspective and involving them in decisions; the doctor still gives honest advice and may decline requests that aren't in the patient's interest, such as antibiotics for a viral illness. From “Consultation models and patient-centred care”
  • Thinking consent is a signature on a form. Consent is a process of dialogue in which the patient understands the options, material risks and alternatives; the form only records it. From “Communicating risk and shared decision-making”
  • Giving false reassurance to soften the blow ("I'm sure it will be fine"). It damages trust when the truth emerges and prevents patients planning; hope can be honest ("We'll do everything we can to control this"). From “Breaking bad news”

Dental Materials

  • Equating strength with stiffness. A material can be stiff but brittle, like ceramic, or flexible but tough; modulus and strength are different properties. From “Properties of dental materials”
  • Believing gloves fully protect against resin monomers. Methacrylates penetrate most gloves quickly, so a no-touch technique is needed. From “Biocompatibility of dental materials”
  • Drying etched dentine until it is chalky. Over-drying collapses the collagen network; leave dentine slightly moist with etch-and-rinse systems. From “Adhesion to enamel and dentine”
  • Advising patients to have sound amalgams replaced for health reasons. There is no evidence of benefit, and removal itself releases mercury vapour and removes tooth tissue. From “Dental amalgam”

Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection

  • Thinking that higher mA makes X-rays more penetrating. Tube current changes the number of photons; kilovoltage changes their energy. From “Production of X-rays”
  • Assuming radiolucent areas are where the beam was absorbed. Dark areas are where many photons passed through; white areas are where they were absorbed. From “Interaction of X-rays with matter”
  • Saying dental X-rays carry no risk. The stochastic risk is very small but not zero, so exposures must be justified and optimised. From “Biological effects of ionising radiation”
  • Treating effective dose as the exact dose to one patient. It is a risk-weighted estimate for a reference person, useful for comparison only. From “Radiation doses in dentistry”

Restorative Dentistry: Operative, Endodontics and Prosthodontics

  • Planning crowns and bridges before disease is controlled. Stabilise caries and periodontal disease and reassess first, or definitive work may fail. From “History, examination and treatment planning”
  • Treating a signed form as consent. Consent is an ongoing conversation; the signature only records it. From “Consent and shared decision-making in treatment”
  • Adjusting a new restoration only with articulating paper marks. Marks can mislead; check that shim stock holds on neighbouring teeth as it did before. From “Occlusion in restorative dentistry”
  • Removing all stained or soft dentine over the pulp in a deep lesion. This risks exposure; selective removal with a sealed restoration has better outcomes. From “Principles of cavity preparation”

Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics

  • Using vague threats or the word "hurt" to gain cooperation. Frightening words increase anxiety; use positive, simple language. From “Child development and behaviour management”
  • Assuming carious primary teeth do not matter because they will fall out. Untreated caries causes pain, infection and GA admissions, and primary molars are needed for years. From “Caries in children”
  • Relying on cold tests in primary teeth. They are unreliable; use history, clinical signs and radiographs. From “Pulp therapy in primary teeth”
  • Replanting an avulsed primary incisor. This can damage the developing permanent tooth; primary teeth are not replanted. From “Dental trauma in children”

Oral Surgery, Pain and Anxiety Control

  • Forgetting to calculate the maximum dose in small children. Toxicity can occur with only a few cartridges. From “Local anaesthetics: pharmacology”
  • Assuming a numb lip means the tooth is anaesthetised. Lip numbness shows the nerve has been reached, but pulpal anaesthesia may still be incomplete. From “Local anaesthetic techniques”
  • Labelling a patient allergic to all local anaesthetics after a faint. Fainting and adrenaline effects are often mistaken for allergy. From “Complications of local anaesthesia”
  • Relying only on sedation for very anxious patients. Sedation helps with treatment but does not usually reduce anxiety for the future. From “Dental anxiety”

Professionalism, Ethics and Law

  • Thinking the standards apply only to dentists. They apply to all registered members of the dental team. From “The GDC and its Standards for the Dental Team”
  • Thinking anything in a group's scope can be done by every member of it. Each registrant must also be trained and competent for the task. From “The dental team and scope of practice”
  • Treating a signed form as proof of valid consent. Consent depends on the quality of the discussion and the patient's understanding. From “Communication and consent”
  • Assuming someone with dementia or a learning disability lacks capacity. Capacity must be assessed for each decision. From “Mental capacity and consent”
