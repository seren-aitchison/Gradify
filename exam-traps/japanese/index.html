Japanese: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Japanese lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana

  • Squashing the special morae. Nihon said as two beats, or Tōkyō as "Tokyo", loses beats that Japanese ears expect, and in other words the lost beat changes the meaning. From “The sounds of Japanese and the mora”
  • Treating long vowels as optional. Kōkō (high school) said as koko (here) is a different word; listeners rely on length as much as on consonants. From “Vowels, long vowels and devoicing”
  • Adding English stress. Making one syllable louder and longer, as in "to-KYO", distorts mora length; change the pitch, not the volume. From “Pitch accent basics”
  • Writing し as si. In Hepburn it is shi, and it is pronounced like English "she": sushi, not "susi". Kunrei-shiki romanisation does write si, but pronunciation is the same. From “Hiragana: the vowel, k and s rows”

Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence

  • Writing シ and ツ (or ソ and ン) with the strokes in the wrong direction. In print they look alike, but by hand the direction of the long stroke is the only difference: up from the bottom for シ and ン, down from the top for ツ and ソ. From “Katakana: the basic chart”
  • Spelling a loanword's long vowel with a vowel letter, as in コオヒイ for coffee. Loanwords take the bar: コーヒー. Vowel letters for length belong to hiragana spelling. From “Katakana: voiced sounds, combinations and the long vowel mark”
  • Assuming every katakana word means what it does in English. マンション is a flat, not a mansion, and アルバイト comes from German; check the meaning before you use a loanword. From “Loanwords and extended katakana”
  • Learning one reading per kanji and expecting it everywhere. Learn each kanji in words, such as 山 in 山 (yama) and 富士山 (Fujisan), so that each reading is tied to real vocabulary. From “What kanji are: on and kun readings”

Verbs, Particles and Daily Routine

  • Treating every verb ending in -eru or -iru as a ru-verb. かえる, はいる, はしる and しる are u-verbs and conjugate differently. From “Verb groups: ru-verbs, u-verbs and irregular verbs”
  • Forgetting to change the u sound in u-verbs. のむ becomes のみます, not のむます. From “The masu form: present and future”
  • Saying ませんでした as ましたでした or ませんました. The past negative is always ません plus でした. From “Negative and past masu forms”
  • Taking ちょっと… as "a little" in a refusal. It signals a polite no. From “Mashou and masen ka: suggestions and invitations”

Adjectives, Counters and Description

  • Saying いくないです for "not good". The negative of いい is よくないです. From “I-adjectives: present and negative”
  • Treating きれい as an い-adjective, as in きれくないです. It is a な-adjective: きれいじゃないです. From “Na-adjectives: present and negative”
  • Saying おいしいでした. い-adjectives change their own ending: おいしかったです. From “Past tense of adjectives”
  • Dropping な before a noun, as in しずか まち. な-adjectives need な: しずかな まち. From “Adjectives before nouns and joining adjectives”

The Te-form and Plain Forms

  • Writing いいて for いく. The て-form of いく is the exception いって. From “Making the te-form”
  • Using ～て ください to ask a senior person a favour. ～て くださいませんか sounds more polite. From “Te kudasai: requests”
  • Saying けっこんします to mean "I am married". That means "I will marry"; "I am married" is けっこんして います. From “Te imasu: ongoing actions and resulting states”
  • Answering ～ても いいですか with いけません to a guest. This sounds harsh; a softer refusal with a reason is more polite. From “Te mo ii desu and te wa ikemasen”

Everyday Life: GCSE Themes

  • Calling your own mother おかあさん when talking about her to others. Use はは; おかあさん is for addressing her or for others' mothers. From “Family and relationships”
  • Saying あには たかいです to mean tall. This can mean "expensive"; for height, say せが たかいです. From “Describing people: appearance and personality”
  • Using する with the piano, as in ピアノを します. The verb is ひく: ピアノを ひきます. From “Hobbies, sport and free time”
  • Saying あたまが いたいでした for a past headache. い-adjectives change: あたまが いたかったです. From “Health, the body and illness”

N4 Grammar: Ability, Intention and Conditions

  • Using を routinely with potential verbs in exams, as in にほんごを はなせます. Textbook standard is が: にほんごが はなせます. From “The potential form”
  • Saying いくと おもって います for "I'm thinking of going". Use the volitional: いこうと おもって います. From “The volitional form and to omotte imasu”
  • Using the masu form before つもり, as in いきます つもりです. Use the dictionary form: いく つもりです. From “Tsumori desu: plans”
  • Using the masu form before こと, as in のりますことが できます. Use the dictionary form: のることが できます. From “Koto ga dekiru”

Keigo and Social Register

  • Using respectful verbs for your own actions, as in わたしが いらっしゃいます. Respectful language is only for others. From “Plain, polite and honorific speech”
  • Saying たなかさんは いらっしゃいません to a client about your own boss. To outsiders, say たなかは おりません. From “Uchi and soto: in-group and out-group”
  • Saying わたしの おなまえは. Do not use respectful お for your own name. From “Beautifying o and go”
  • Saying いらっしゃります. The masu form is いらっしゃいます. From “Sonkeigo: respectful verbs”

N3 Grammar: Nuance and Viewpoint

  • Using ～て しまう for neutral events with no nuance. It adds completion or regret, so use the plain past for neutral facts. From “Te shimau: completion and regret”
  • Confusing ～て おく with ～て いる. ～て おく focuses on preparing for a later purpose. From “Te oku: doing in advance”
  • Using ～て ある with intransitive verbs, as in まどが あいて あります. Use the transitive verb: あけて あります. From “Te aru: deliberate results”
  • Using ～て いく for change up to the present. Change up to now uses ～て くる. From “Te iku and te kuru”

Kanji and Reading to N3

  • Memorising every reading of each kanji in isolation. Learning kanji through common words is more efficient. From “The Joyo kanji list and the JLPT levels”
  • Assuming the sound component always gives the exact reading. Sound families have exceptions. From “Radicals and semantic-phonetic kanji”
  • Reading 大人 as だいにん. It has the special reading おとな. From “Kanji compounds: on and kun readings together”
  • Expecting rendaku in every compound. It is common but irregular, so learn compounds individually. From “Rendaku and sound changes in compounds”

Japanese Culture and Society

  • Thinking Tokyo has always been the capital. Kyoto was the imperial capital from 794 until the Meiji era. From “A short history of Japan”
  • Clapping at a Buddhist temple. Clapping is for shrines; at temples, put your hands together quietly. From “Shinto and Buddhism in everyday life”
  • Thinking hiragana and katakana were invented separately from kanji. Both developed from kanji. From “The history of the writing system”
  • Writing 令和1年 for 2019. The first year of an era is written 元年: 令和元年. From “Festivals and the Japanese calendar”

Exam Skills: GCSE and JLPT

  • Assuming passing the JLPT shows speaking ability. It tests only reading, listening and language knowledge. From “The JLPT: levels and format”
  • Revising only reading and writing. Each of the four skills is worth 25%. From “GCSE Japanese: the four skills”
  • Choosing the first option mentioned. Speakers often change their minds, so listen to the end. From “Listening strategies”
  • Answering from general knowledge rather than the text. Answers must be based on what the text says. From “Reading strategies for exams”
