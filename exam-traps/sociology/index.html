Sociology: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Sociology lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Sociology

  • Thinking a sociological explanation denies individual choice. It shows how choices are made within social conditions, which makes some choices easier or more likely than others. From “What is sociology?”
  • Treating sociology as only about "society" in the abstract. Much sociology studies concrete things: a school, a hospital ward, a workplace, a neighbourhood. From “Sociology as a discipline”
  • Using "public issue" to mean "a problem that's in the news". Mills meant a problem rooted in how society is organised, whether or not it gets attention. From “The sociological imagination”
  • Assuming micro means "unimportant" or macro means "more scientific". Each answers different questions with appropriate methods. From “Micro and macro sociology”

Classical Sociological Theory

  • Thinking sociology began with a single founder or date. Comte named it, but several thinkers in different countries developed sociological ideas at once. From “The origins of sociology”
  • Treating the Enlightenment as a single agreed doctrine. Its thinkers disagreed widely, about religion, politics and human nature. From “The Enlightenment and social thought”
  • Assuming industrialisation affected everyone the same way. Effects differed by class, gender, region and between countries. From “Industrialisation and social change”
  • Confusing Comte's positivism with logical positivism, a 20th-century philosophy of science developed by the Vienna Circle. They share a name and a respect for science but differ. From “Comte and positivism”

Contemporary Sociological Theory

  • Assuming functionalists claim every institution is good. Merton recognised dysfunctions, and functions can benefit the system while harming individuals. From “Functionalism”
  • Treating AGIL as describing four separate buildings or organisations. They are functional problems that institutions help solve, often several at once. From “Parsons”
  • Treating latent functions as secret or sinister. They are simply consequences that aren't intended or recognised. From “Merton: manifest and latent functions”
  • Equating conflict theory only with Marxism. Weber, Dahrendorf, Coser and Mills developed conflict theories without Marx's economic focus. From “Conflict theory”

Culture, Identity and Socialisation

  • Treating culture as only "high culture" such as art and opera. Sociologically, culture is a whole way of life, including everyday practices. From “Material and non-material culture”
  • Assuming everyone in a society holds the same values. Values vary by age, class, religion and region. From “Values”
  • Assuming norms are only laws. Most norms are unwritten and enforced informally. From “Norms and sanctions”
  • Assuming subcultures always reject the mainstream. Many share most mainstream values and differ in style or interests. From “Subcultures”

Social Stratification and Inequality

  • Treating stratification as differences between individuals. It refers to structured inequality between groups, built into institutions. From “Social stratification”
  • Assuming slavery and caste are only historical. Forced labour and caste discrimination persist today. From “Social hierarchy”
  • Assuming Marxist class analysis is only about factory workers. Neo-Marxists apply it to managers, professionals and service workers. From “Marxist theories of class”
  • Thinking Weber rejected the importance of property. He saw property as central, but not the only basis of class. From “Weberian theories of class”

Gender and Sexuality

  • Assuming every difference between women and men is either fully biological or fully social. Most outcomes involve both. From “Sex and gender”
  • Thinking "socially constructed" means "not real". Social constructs have real effects on people's lives. From “The social construction of gender”
  • Assuming gender socialisation is a one-way process. Children actively learn, police and challenge gender norms. From “Gender socialisation”
  • Assuming equal employment means equal roles. Unpaid work and care are still unevenly shared. From “Gender roles”

Race, Ethnicity and Migration

  • Thinking "socially constructed" means "imaginary" or unimportant. Constructed categories have real effects. From “Race as a social construct”
  • Using "ethnic" to mean "minority". Majority groups also have ethnicities. From “Ethnicity”
  • Assuming racism only concerns skin colour. Religion, culture and nationality can be racialised too. From “Racialisation”
  • Thinking racism exists only where there is conscious hatred. Much racism is covert or built into systems. From “Racism”

Family and Relationships

  • Treating "household" and "family" as the same. Many households, such as people living alone or sharing flats, aren't families. From “What is a family?”
  • Treating every year-to-year change in survey data as a trend. Some changes aren't statistically significant. From “Family structures”
  • Assuming the extended family has disappeared. Support across generations remains strong, often at a distance. From “Nuclear and extended families”
  • Assuming lone parenthood itself harms children. Poverty, conflict and instability explain much of the difference. From “Lone-parent families”

Education

  • Assuming education is fully meritocratic. Background strongly influences outcomes. From “Functionalist perspectives on education”
  • Assuming pupils passively absorb school values. Many resist, as Willis showed. From “Marxist perspectives on education”
  • Assuming girls' higher average achievement means gender inequality in education is over. Harassment, subject segregation and later pay gaps persist. From “Feminist perspectives on education”
  • Treating school discipline as neutral. Rules and sanctions affect some groups more than others. From “Education, socialisation and social control”

Crime and Deviance

  • Treating police recorded crime as an exact measure of crime. Much crime is never reported or recorded. From “Crime and deviance”
  • Assuming "socially constructed" means crime causes no real harm. Constructed categories still describe harmful acts with real victims. From “The social construction of crime”
  • Reading functionalism as approving of crime. It argues some crime is inevitable and has social effects, not that crime is good. From “Functionalist theories of crime”
  • Assuming Marxists only explain working-class crime. They also stress crimes of the powerful. From “Marxist theories of crime”

Politics and Power

  • Measuring power only through visible decisions. Agenda-setting and shaping beliefs are also power. From “Power”
  • Treating ideal types as perfect descriptions. They are models for comparison. From “Authority: traditional, charismatic and legal-rational”
  • Equating low trust with rejecting democracy. Critical citizens may support democracy while distrusting politicians. From “Legitimacy”
  • Treating the state as only the government. It includes courts, police, military and bureaucracy. From “The state”

Religion

  • Assuming religion always means belief in God. Many traditions and definitions don't require it. From “Defining religion”
  • Assuming religion always unites. In diverse societies it can also divide. From “Functionalist approaches to religion”
  • Equating Marxist views with dismissing religion as unimportant. Marx saw it as a powerful response to suffering. From “Marxist approaches to religion”
  • Thinking Weber said religion alone caused capitalism. He said it was one necessary factor among several. From “Weberian approaches to religion”

Work and Economy

  • Assuming industrial work replaced all other work. Domestic and agricultural work continued, often unrecognised. From “Industrial society”
  • Assuming all service jobs are skilled knowledge work. Many are routine and closely monitored. From “Post-industrial society”
  • Treating capitalism as identical everywhere. Institutions create very different varieties. From “Capitalism”
  • Assuming falling unemployment always means more people in work. Inactivity may have risen instead. From “Labour markets”

Globalisation and Social Change

  • Treating globalisation as a single, inevitable force. It is uneven and shaped by policy. From “What is globalisation?”
  • Assuming economic globalisation benefits everyone equally. Gains and losses are unevenly spread. From “Economic globalisation”
  • Assuming globalisation makes states powerless. States create and enforce many global rules. From “Political globalisation”
  • Assuming cultural globalisation means Americanisation only. Flows run in many directions. From “Cultural globalisation”

Urban Sociology and Population

  • Assuming urbanisation always follows industrialisation. In many places, cities grew faster than industrial jobs. From “Urbanisation”
  • Assuming cities have no community. Urban villages and subcultures show otherwise. From “Cities and modernity”
  • Treating urban patterns as natural. Policy, markets and discrimination shape them. From “Urban ecology and the Chicago School”
  • Assuming deprived areas are caused by their residents' choices. Markets, policy and history shape places. From “Urban inequality”

Health and Medicine

  • Assuming health depends mainly on individual choices and health care. Social conditions shape both. From “Social determinants of health”
  • Explaining health inequalities only by individual lifestyles. Lifestyles are shaped by material conditions. From “Health inequalities”
  • Treating illness as purely biological. Its meaning, distribution and treatment are social. From “Medical sociology”
  • Rejecting medicine entirely. The social model challenges biomedicine's limits, not its achievements. From “Medical and social models of health”

Research Methods

  • Choosing a method first and then finding a question to fit it. The question should drive the design. From “Research design”
  • Writing questions that contain their own answer, such as "Why are young people addicted to phones?". Good questions don't assume the conclusion. From “Research questions and hypotheses”
  • Equating positivism with "using numbers". Positivism is a set of assumptions about knowledge; interpretivists sometimes use numbers too. From “Positivism”
  • Assuming interpretivist research is "just opinion". It follows systematic procedures, and its quality is judged by credibility, transparency and reflexivity. From “Interpretivism”

Social Statistics and Data Science

  • Treating class categories as numbers and averaging them. Ordinal and nominal categories aren't quantities. From “Types of social data”
  • Reporting "average income" without saying whether it is the mean or the median. The two can differ greatly. From “Central tendency”
  • Using the standard deviation alone to describe highly skewed income. Percentile-based measures are more informative. From “Dispersion”
  • Comparing raw counts between groups of different sizes. Use percentages. From “Frequency distributions”
