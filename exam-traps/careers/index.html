Careers and Employability: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Careers and Employability lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Knowing Yourself

  • Thinking career development means choosing the "right" job once. Most people's careers involve many decisions and transitions, so the skills of managing a career matter more than any single choice. From “What career development is”
  • Treating a matching quiz result as the answer. Matching theories assume stable people and stable jobs; real careers also involve development, chance and changing labour markets. From “Career theories: matching, development and chance”
  • Assuming careers guidance just means one meeting with an adviser. Personal guidance is only one benchmark; the others are about programmes, information, encounters and experiences over time. From “The Gatsby Benchmarks and careers support”
  • Listing skills as bare adjectives ("excellent communicator, team player"). Without evidence a recruiter can't tell claim from reality; every skill needs an example. From “Identifying your skills”

Exploring Sectors and Roles

  • Treating one figure, such as a national average salary or a growth projection, as a promise. LMI describes averages and trends, not what will happen to you. From “What labour market information is”
  • Calculating the employment rate as 100% minus the unemployment rate. The two use different denominators because economically inactive people are in neither group. From “Using ONS and official labour market data”
  • Ruling yourself out because a profile says "most entrants have" a qualification or experience you lack. Typical isn't always essential; check employers' actual requirements. From “Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service”
  • Assuming "shortage" means "easy to get into". Shortages are often for experienced or specialist staff, or result from pay and conditions rather than a lack of applicants. From “Skills shortages and growth areas”

Work Experience, Internships and Placements

  • Treating a list of placements as the evidence. Admissions tutors and employers want to know what you observed, did and learned, not just where you went. From “Work experience and work shadowing”
  • Waiting until summer to look for summer internships. Large employers often open applications the previous autumn and fill places on a rolling basis. From “Internships”
  • Assuming the higher final grades of placement students prove placements cause better grades. More motivated students may be more likely to take placements, so some of the difference is selection. From “Placement years and sandwich degrees”
  • Treating an insight day as a passive tour. Many firms assess participants informally or formally, and a fast-track offer can depend on how you engage. From “Insight days and spring weeks”

Networking and Your Professional Presence

  • Thinking networking means asking strangers for jobs. It is about relationships, information and mutual help over time; job leads follow from trust. From “What networking is and why it works”
  • Believing you have no network. Most people have contacts who know people in their field of interest; mapping helps you see them. From “Building a network from where you are”
  • Collecting freebies and leaflets without talking to anyone. The value of an event is the conversations and contacts, which need preparation and follow-up. From “Careers fairs and networking events”
  • Writing long messages asking for a job or a referral from a stranger. A short request for advice is easier to say yes to and starts a relationship. From “Speculative approaches and follow-up”

CVs and Cover Letters

  • Treating a CV as a complete life history. It should be selective and focused on the role. From “What a CV is for”
  • Using elaborate templates with columns and graphics. They can confuse applicant tracking systems and distract readers. From “CV structure and sections”
  • Starting bullets with "Responsible for". It describes a duty, not what you achieved. From “Writing achievement statements”
  • Sending the same generic CV to every vacancy. Tailored CVs show employers clearly how you fit their role. From “Tailoring a CV to a role”

Applications and Psychometric Tests

  • Leaving applications until the deadline. With rolling recruitment, places may already be filled; and rushed answers are easy to spot. From “How application forms work”
  • Answering with what you would do or usually do. Competency questions ask for one specific past situation; hypothetical answers score poorly. From “Competency-based questions”
  • Writing a long story about the situation and squeezing the actions into one line. Assessors mark the actions, so give them most of your words. From “The STAR method”
  • Trying to guess and fake the "right" strengths. Assessors watch for energy and consistency, and a role that doesn't fit your strengths is unlikely to suit you anyway. From “Strengths-based questions”

Interviews and Assessment Centres

  • Assuming every interview is an informal chat. Most graduate interviews are structured and scored, so prepare evidence for each criterion. From “Interview formats”
  • Preparing answers but never saying them aloud. Practising out loud reveals rambling and gaps that silent rehearsal misses. From “Preparing for an interview”
  • Treating a follow-up question as a sign of failure. Probes are standard in structured interviews and help you add evidence. From “Answering competency and strengths questions”
  • Treating a phone or video interview as less important. Remote interviews are usually scored just like in-person ones. From “Telephone and live video interviews”

Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes

  • Assuming graduate scheme salaries are typical. Large employer surveys cover a small, well-paid part of the market. From “The graduate recruitment market”
  • Assuming a scheme is the only good start. Many successful careers begin with direct-entry jobs at smaller employers. From “Graduate schemes”
  • Waiting for schemes to reopen. Direct-entry roles are advertised all year, so you can apply whenever you are ready. From “Graduate jobs outside schemes”
  • Waiting until final-year spring to look for graduate schemes. Many open in September and fill well before their deadlines. From “The recruitment calendar”

Starting Work: Contracts and Rights

  • Assuming the contract's label settles your status. Tribunals look at the reality of the working relationship. From “Employment status: employee, worker and self-employed”
  • Thinking the written statement is the whole contract. Contracts can also include verbal, handbook and implied terms. From “The written statement of employment particulars”
  • Not checking your payslip. Errors in hours, rates or tax codes are common, so check every time and raise problems early. From “Pay, the minimum wage and payslips”
  • Thinking student loan repayments depend on how much you owe. They depend on what you earn above the threshold. From “Tax, National Insurance and student loan repayments”

Marketing

  • Equating marketing with advertising or selling. Promotion is one part of marketing, which begins with understanding customers and shapes products, prices and distribution. From “What is marketing?”
  • Assuming a large sample is always reliable. A large convenience sample can be more biased than a small random one. From “Market research”
  • Choosing segments only because they are large. A segment must also be reachable and respond to a distinct offer the firm can deliver profitably. From “Segmentation, targeting and positioning”
  • Assuming consumers always act rationally. Habits, emotions and mental shortcuts shape many purchases. From “Consumer behaviour”

Career Planning and Resilience

  • Treating a career plan as fixed once written. Plans should be reviewed and adapted as circumstances change. From “Career planning models”
  • Assuming good job skills are enough to manage a career. You also need skills to explore, decide, network and adapt. From “Career management skills”
  • Writing a goal with no first step. Identify a small action you can take this week to start momentum. From “Action planning”
  • Thinking CPD only means formal courses. Reading, mentoring, projects and reflection all count if they develop your practice. From “Lifelong learning and CPD”
