Primary: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Primary lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Number and Place Value

  • Counting too fast, so your voice and your finger get out of step. Slow down and touch each object as you say its number. From “Counting objects to 20”
  • Saying "twenty-nine, twenty-ten" instead of "thirty". After 9 ones, the next number starts a new ten. From “Counting forwards and backwards”
  • Writing "fourteen" as 41 because you hear "four" first. In the teens the word says the ones first, but the digits are still written tens first: 1 then 4. From “Reading and writing numbers to 100”
  • Thinking a number is odd because its first digit is odd. 34 starts with 3 but ends in 4, so it is even: only the last digit counts. From “Odd and even numbers”

The Four Operations and Times Tables

  • Thinking the bond to 100 for 58 is 52, by making the tens 10 and the ones 10. The ones make 10 (8 + 2), which adds an extra ten, so the tens only need to make 90: 50 + 40. From “Number bonds”
  • Rounding up and then forgetting to adjust, or adjusting the wrong way. If you add 20 instead of 19, you have added 1 too many, so take 1 away. From “Adding in your head”
  • Thinking subtraction can be done in any order. 9 − 4 is 5, but 4 − 9 is not 5: the order matters, unlike in addition. From “Subtracting in your head”
  • Lining the numbers up on the left instead of the right. In 3,507 + 46, the 46 must go under the 07, or you will add tens to thousands. From “Column addition”

Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

  • Calling any one of two pieces "a half" even when the pieces are different sizes. Check the parts are equal first. From “Halves and quarters”
  • Thinking ⅕ is bigger than ⅓ because 5 is bigger than 3. Sharing a cake between 5 people gives smaller slices than sharing it between 3. From “Unit and non-unit fractions”
  • Adding the same number to top and bottom. 1/2 + 1 on each part gives 2/3, which isn't equal to 1/2. Only multiplying or dividing keeps the value. From “Equivalent fractions”
  • Comparing only the denominators, or only the numerators, when both are different. 2/3 and 3/5 need a common denominator: 10/15 and 9/15. From “Comparing fractions”

Measurement, Geometry and Statistics

  • Starting to measure from the edge of the ruler or from 1 instead of from 0. If you start at 1, the object looks 1 cm longer than it is. From “Length and height”
  • Counting every mark as 1 or as 10. Always work out what one step is worth first. From “Mass and capacity”
  • Writing both £ and p together, like £1.50p. Use one or the other: £1.50 or 150p. From “Money”
  • Treating time like ordinary numbers, for example working out 6:20 − 4:45 as 175. There are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100, so count on through the hours instead. From “Telling the time”

Phonics and Spelling

  • Adding "uh" to sounds, so "c-a-t" becomes "cuh-a-tuh", which blends to "cuhatuh" instead of "cat". Keep the sounds pure. From “Letters and sounds”
  • Guessing a word from its first letter or a picture. Look at every letter, all the way through the word, and blend. From “Blending to read”
  • Missing sounds in the middle of words, like writing "tet" for "tent". Say the word slowly and touch a finger for every sound. From “Segmenting to spell”
  • Sounding out each letter in a digraph separately, so "ship" becomes "s-h-i-p". The s and h make one sound together: /sh/. From “Digraphs and trigraphs”

Reading and Comprehension

  • Thinking only long chapter books "count". Picture books, comics, poems and non-fiction all build reading skills and enjoyment. From “Reading for pleasure”
  • Thinking fast means fluent. Racing through and ignoring punctuation makes it harder to understand; aim for the speed of natural talking. From “Reading fluently”
  • Skipping every word you don't know. Skipping one or two is fine, but if you skip many, you lose the meaning, so stop and use the clues. From “Understanding new words”
  • Answering from your own knowledge instead of the text. If the text says red squirrels eat fungi, write that, even if you think they eat something else. From “Retrieving information”

Grammar, Punctuation and Writing

  • Forgetting capital letters on proper nouns such as days, months, people's names and places: "on monday we went to wales" should be "On Monday we went to Wales." From “Nouns and noun phrases”
  • Switching tense by accident: "I went to the shop and I buy some milk." Keep both verbs in the past: went, bought. From “Verbs and tenses”
  • Using an adjective where an adverb is needed: "She sang beautiful." The word describes how she sang, so it needs the adverb "beautifully". From “Adjectives and adverbs”
  • Writing "me and Josh went" in formal writing. Use "Josh and I went" as the subject, and "with Josh and me" after a preposition. From “Pronouns”

Plants, Animals and Habitats

  • Thinking plants take in food through their roots. Roots take in water and nutrients, but the plant makes its own food in its leaves using sunlight. From “Parts of a plant”
  • Changing two things at once, such as light and water, in a test. Then you cannot tell which one made the difference, so change only one. From “What plants need to grow”
  • Thinking seeds need soil to start growing. Seeds need water and warmth to germinate, and can start growing on damp kitchen roll. From “How seeds and bulbs grow”
  • Mixing up pollination and seed dispersal. Pollination is moving pollen to make seeds; dispersal is spreading the seeds once they are made. From “Pollination and seed dispersal”

Materials, Forces and Magnets

  • Calling an object a material, such as saying "the material is a chair". The chair is the object; wood or plastic is the material. From “Everyday materials”
  • Thinking all metals are magnetic. Iron, nickel and cobalt are magnetic, but aluminium, copper and gold are not. From “Properties of materials”
  • Thinking that steam you can see is a gas. Water vapour is invisible: the white cloud is tiny droplets of liquid water that have condensed. From “Solids, liquids and gases”
  • Thinking water only evaporates when it boils. Puddles and washing dry at normal temperatures because water evaporates slowly all the time. From “The water cycle”

Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space

  • Thinking the Moon is a light source. The Moon gives out no light of its own; it reflects light from the Sun. From “Light and dark”
  • Thinking a shadow is a reflection or shows colours and faces. A shadow is just a dark area where light is blocked, so it only shows the outline shape. From “Shadows”
  • Drawing arrows from the eye to the object, as if eyes send out light. Light travels from the source, to the object and then into the eye. From “How we see”
  • Thinking sound travels through empty space, as in films where spaceships roar. With no particles to vibrate, space is silent. From “How sounds are made”

History

  • Thinking "old" always means hundreds of years ago. Living memory can be just 50 or 60 years. From “Changes within living memory”
  • Thinking Stone Age people lived alongside dinosaurs. Dinosaurs died out about 66 million years before humans existed. From “The Stone Age to the Iron Age”
  • Thinking all Britons welcomed the Romans. Many resisted, while others adopted Roman ways. From “Roman Britain”
  • Thinking the Anglo-Saxons were uncivilised because they followed the Romans. They produced fine metalwork, books, laws and kingdoms. From “Anglo-Saxons and Scots”

Geography

  • Thinking the UK and Great Britain mean the same thing. Great Britain is the island of England, Scotland and Wales; the UK also includes Northern Ireland. From “The United Kingdom”
  • Thinking flat maps show sizes exactly. Flat maps stretch some areas; a globe shows the true shapes. From “Continents and oceans”
  • Assuming a country's largest city is its capital. Washington, D.C., Ottawa, Canberra and Brasília are not their countries' biggest cities. From “Countries and capitals”
  • Confusing latitude and longitude. Latitude lines run across, like a ladder's rungs; longitude lines run from pole to pole. From “Latitude, longitude and time zones”
