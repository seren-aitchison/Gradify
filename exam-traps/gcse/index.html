GCSE Core: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our GCSE Core lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Revision and Exam Skills

  • Thinking that if notes feel familiar you know them. Recognising information when you see it is much easier than recalling it from a blank page, which is what an exam asks you to do. From “How memory works”
  • Looking at the answer too soon. If you glance at your notes the moment you hesitate, you skip the effortful part that does the work. Give yourself time to struggle, then check. From “Retrieval practice”
  • Making a timetable so packed that it collapses in the first week. A plan that leaves time for meals, exercise, friends and sleep is one you will actually follow. From “Spaced practice and revision timetables”
  • Staying on blocked practice because it feels good. Getting every question right when they are all the same type can hide the fact that you couldn't pick the method on a mixed paper. From “Interleaving and worked examples”

Foundations of Literary Study

  • Thinking literature means only novels. Poetry, drama, essays, letters, diaries, sermons and oral forms such as ballads are all studied as literature. From “What is literature?”
  • Listing genre features without saying why they matter. Name the convention, then explain its effect or how the writer adapts it. From “Genre and form”
  • Writing "Wordsworth feels lonely" about "I wandered lonely as a cloud". Write "the speaker describes feeling lonely", then discuss how Wordsworth presents it. From “Author, narrator and reader”
  • Bolting on context ("In Victorian times there was lots of poverty") in a separate paragraph. Integrate it with a quotation and an interpretive point. From “Text and context”

Drama and Shakespeare

  • Writing about a play as if it were a novel. Avoid phrases such as "the reader learns"; consider the audience, the stage and how a moment might be played. From “Drama as performance”
  • Translating hamartia simply as a "fatal flaw" of character. Aristotle's term suggests an error or misjudgement, which may involve ignorance rather than vice. From “Tragedy”
  • Treating comedy as trivial. Many comedies satirise society, question gender roles or contain darker elements that complicate their happy endings. From “Comedy”
  • Applying Freytag's pyramid rigidly to every play. It describes many classical and Shakespearean tragedies, but modern plays often use different shapes, circular structures or fragmented time. From “Dramatic structure”

The Human Body

  • Thinking each organ belongs to only one system. The pancreas is part of both the digestive and endocrine systems; the kidneys belong to the renal system but also control blood pressure and make a hormone. From “Cells, tissues and organs”
  • Thinking that homeostasis keeps variables at one exact value. Variables fluctuate within a normal range around a set point, and some set points change, for example temperature during a fever. From “Homeostasis”
  • Thinking that a fever is a failure of temperature control. In fever the hypothalamus deliberately raises its set point; the temperature is still regulated, just around a higher value. From “Body temperature regulation”
  • Thinking that insulin breaks glucose down. Insulin is a signal: it causes cells to take up and store glucose; it is not an enzyme. From “Blood glucose regulation”

Chemistry

  • Thinking isotopes have different chemical properties. They have the same electron arrangement, so they react the same way. From “Atomic structure and isotopes”
  • Saying Rutherford discovered the electron. Thomson discovered the electron; Rutherford's work led to the nuclear model. From “The development of the atomic model”
  • Saying all groups become more reactive down the table. Group 1 does, but Group 7 becomes less reactive. From “The periodic table and groups”
  • Thinking ionic compounds are made of molecules. They form giant lattices of ions, not separate molecules. From “Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding”

Physics

  • Saying energy is "used up" or "lost". Energy is conserved; it is dissipated into less useful stores, usually the thermal store of the surroundings. From “Energy stores and transfers”
  • Dividing input by output when calculating efficiency. Always divide useful output by total input, so the answer is less than 1. From “Work, power and efficiency”
  • Calling nuclear power renewable because it emits no carbon dioxide. Uranium is a finite resource, so nuclear fuel is non-renewable. From “Energy resources”
  • Connecting a voltmeter in series with a component. A voltmeter measures the difference between two points, so it must be connected across (in parallel with) the component. From “Circuits, current and potential difference”

Foundations of Ecology

  • Using "ecological" to mean "environmentally friendly". In the science, ecological simply means "to do with the interactions of organisms and environment". From “What is ecology?”
  • Confusing community and ecosystem. A community is living organisms only; an ecosystem includes the community and its abiotic environment. From “Levels of ecological organisation”
  • Calling all the animals in a pond a "population". A population is one species; all the species together are a community. From “Levels of ecological organisation”
  • Generalising from a one-season, one-site study. Ecological systems vary from place to place and year to year, so short, small studies may miss the pattern. From “Ecology as a science: hypotheses and scale”

Geography: Human Environments and Skills

  • Confusing urbanisation with urban growth. Urbanisation means the proportion of people in urban areas rises; a city can grow in size without the country urbanising if the rural population grows just as fast. From “Global urbanisation”
  • Describing squatter settlements as places with no positive features. Many have strong communities, small businesses and residents who improve their homes over time. From “Cities in lower-income countries”
  • Assuming regeneration helps everyone equally. New homes and jobs may suit incoming professionals better than existing residents, who can be priced out. From “UK cities: change and regeneration”
  • Treating "sustainable" as meaning only environmentally friendly. Sustainability also includes social and economic needs, such as affordable housing and jobs. From “Sustainable urban living”

Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

  • Treating the WHO definition as the only "correct" one. It is the most quoted, but it is one definition among several, and its word "complete" is widely criticised. From “What is health?”
  • Thinking the biopsychosocial model rejects biology or medicine. It adds psychological and social factors to biological ones; it doesn't replace them. From “Models of health: biomedical and biopsychosocial”
  • Assuming a high wellbeing score means the absence of illness. People with long-term conditions can report high wellbeing, and people without any diagnosis can report low wellbeing. From “Wellbeing and how it is measured”
  • Confusing "holistic" with "alternative" medicine. In health and social care, holistic means attending to the whole person; it is part of mainstream evidence-based care. From “Holistic health: physical, mental and social”
