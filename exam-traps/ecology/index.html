Ecology and Environmental Science: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Ecology and Environmental Science lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Ecology

  • Using "ecological" to mean "environmentally friendly". In the science, ecological simply means "to do with the interactions of organisms and environment". From “What is ecology?”
  • Confusing community and ecosystem. A community is living organisms only; an ecosystem includes the community and its abiotic environment. From “Levels of ecological organisation”
  • Calling all the animals in a pond a "population". A population is one species; all the species together are a community. From “Levels of ecological organisation”
  • Generalising from a one-season, one-site study. Ecological systems vary from place to place and year to year, so short, small studies may miss the pattern. From “Ecology as a science: hypotheses and scale”

Population Ecology

  • Calling all the animals in a habitat a population. Several species together form a community; a population is always a single species. From “What is a population?”
  • Placing quadrats where the organisms look most common. Non-random placement biases the estimate; random coordinates or a systematic grid avoid this. From “Population size and density”
  • Forgetting scale. Gannets are uniformly spaced within a colony, but colonies are clumped along coasts. The pattern you find depends on quadrat size and study area. From “Dispersion patterns”
  • Ignoring trap-happy or trap-shy animals. If marked animals return to bait (trap-happy), R is too high and N is underestimated; if they avoid traps (trap-shy), N is overestimated. From “Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture”

Communities and Species Interactions

  • Assuming species that overlap in resources must be competing now. Competition only occurs if the resource is limiting; overlap alone isn't evidence. From “Intraspecific and interspecific competition”
  • Using the principle to claim no two similar species ever share a habitat. Many similar species coexist because they differ in resource use, respond to variation or are held below competitive levels by predators. From “The competitive exclusion principle”
  • Assuming all differences between species are caused by present-day competition. Differences may reflect past competition ("the ghost of competition past") or unrelated adaptations. From “Resource partitioning and niche differentiation”
  • Thinking Batesian mimics are protected regardless of their numbers. Protection depends on predators meeting the harmful model often enough to learn avoidance. From “Predation”

Ecosystems and Energy Flow

  • Saying that energy is recycled in ecosystems. Energy flows one way and is lost as heat; only matter cycles. From “What is an ecosystem?”
  • Using "decomposer" for every organism that eats dead matter. Detritivores ingest detritus; decomposers digest it externally and absorb the products. From “Producers, consumers and decomposers”
  • Drawing food chain arrows from predator to prey. Arrows point in the direction of energy flow, from the organism eaten to the organism eating it. From “Food chains and food webs”
  • Assuming every organism fits neatly into one level. Omnivores and many predators feed at more than one level. From “Trophic levels”

Biogeochemical Cycles

  • Confusing the size of a reservoir with the speed of its flux. A small reservoir with a large flux, such as atmospheric water, turns over very quickly. From “Cycling of matter”
  • Treating the water cycle as purely physical. Plants move vast amounts of water through transpiration and shape local rainfall and flooding. From “The water cycle”
  • Assuming plants and oceans will absorb all extra CO2. They take up roughly half of emissions; the rest accumulates in the atmosphere. From “The carbon cycle”
  • Assuming natural sinks will keep pace with emissions. Their uptake depends on climate, and warming may weaken land and ocean sinks. From “Carbon stores and fluxes”

Biodiversity and Evolution

  • Equating biodiversity with the number of species. It also includes genetic variation within species and the diversity of ecosystems. From “Defining biodiversity”
  • Assuming a large population always has high genetic diversity. Populations that passed through bottlenecks can remain genetically uniform even after recovery. From “Genetic, species and ecosystem diversity”
  • Assuming species always look different. Cryptic species can be almost identical in appearance. From “Species concepts”
  • Writing scientific names without italics or with a capitalised species name. The convention is Genus species. From “Classification and naming”

Conservation Biology

  • Seeing conservation as purely a biological science. Most threats are driven by human activity, so social science and policy are central. From “What is conservation biology?”
  • Assuming putting a monetary value on nature means it can be sold. Valuation is a tool for decisions, not a market price. From “Conservation values and ethics”
  • Using the census count to judge genetic risk. The effective population size is often a fraction of the number of animals counted. From “Conservation genetics”
  • Treating an MVP figure as a precise number for every species. MVPs depend on the species, the time frame and the level of risk accepted. From “Minimum viable populations”

Climate Change

  • Using a single weather event, hot or cold, as proof for or against climate change. Climate is judged on long-term trends and changing frequencies of extremes. From “Weather and climate”
  • Assuming gases present in tiny amounts cannot matter. Carbon dioxide at around 0.04% strongly affects Earth's energy balance. From “The atmosphere”
  • Thinking the Sun is causing current warming. Solar output has shown no upward trend since the 1980s while temperatures have risen. From “Earth's energy balance”
  • Confusing the greenhouse effect with the ozone hole. Ozone depletion lets in more ultraviolet; it is a separate problem with a different cause. From “The greenhouse effect”

Field Methods and Statistics

  • Calling a hypothesis "proved". Data can support or fail to support a hypothesis, but further evidence can always revise it. From “The scientific method in ecology”
  • Saying the null hypothesis is "accepted" or "proved". It is either rejected or not rejected on the evidence. From “Hypotheses and null hypotheses”
  • Concluding cause from a correlation. A third, confounding variable may explain both. From “Experimental and observational studies”
  • Counting quadrats within one site as independent replicates of a site-level treatment. That is pseudoreplication. From “Replication and pseudoreplication”

Resources and Pollution

  • Equating energy with electricity. Heating and transport make up much of energy use and still depend largely on fossil fuels. From “Energy resources”
  • Thinking reserves are a fixed quantity. Reserves rise and fall with prices and technology. From “Fossil fuels”
  • Assuming nuclear power stations emit large amounts of CO2. Their life-cycle emissions are among the lowest of any source. From “Nuclear power”
  • Assuming all biomass is carbon neutral. It depends on sourcing, regrowth time and emissions in harvesting and transport. From “Renewable energy”

Sustainability and Environmental Policy

  • Treating sustainability as purely environmental. The Brundtland definition stresses human needs and fairness as well as environmental limits. From “What is sustainability?”
  • Thinking the SDGs apply only to developing countries. Unlike the MDGs, they are universal and apply to the UK. From “Sustainable development and the SDGs”
  • Thinking the footprint measures all environmental impacts. It excludes many pressures, including biodiversity loss, water scarcity and toxic pollution. From “Ecological footprints”
  • Assuming anthropocentrism means not caring about nature. Enlightened anthropocentrism can strongly support conservation for the sake of present and future people. From “Environmental ethics”
