Law: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Law lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Legal Foundations and the English Legal System

  • Don't assume "illegal" means "immoral" or that everything immoral is illegal. Exam answers that blur the two lose marks for imprecision. From “What is law?”
  • "The purpose of law is justice" is too vague to score marks. Name the specific purpose and show how the rule serves it. From “The nature and purposes of law”
  • Don't cite a textbook as authority for a legal rule. Cite the statute or the case; use the textbook only for commentary. From “Sources of law”
  • Damages are available as of right; equitable remedies are not. Say "the court may grant" an injunction, not "the claimant is entitled to" one. From “Common law and equity”

Constitutional and Administrative Law

  • Saying the UK has "no constitution": it has one, just not codified in a single document. From “The UK constitution”
  • Arguing that the Sewel convention is legally enforceable because it appears in the Scotland Act. From “Constitutional conventions”
  • Assuming constitutional statutes can never be changed: they can, but only by clear words. From “Constitutional statutes”
  • Treating the Jackson obiter as settled law that courts can strike down Acts. From “Parliamentary sovereignty”

Criminal Law

  • Jumping straight to defences before establishing that the elements of the offence are made out. From “Introduction to criminal liability”
  • Assuming a bystander who fails to help commits an offence: without a duty, there is generally no liability. From “Actus reus”
  • Treating foresight of virtual certainty as automatically equal to intention: it is evidence from which the jury may find it. From “Mens rea”
  • Concluding there is no liability simply because the fatal act came after the defendant believed the victim was already dead. From “Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea”

Criminal Law and Practice

  • Assuming the police decide all charges: the CPS decides most. From “The criminal justice system”
  • Assuming every Code breach makes the evidence inadmissible. From “Police powers”
  • Treating a person's age or clothing alone as reasonable grounds to search. From “Stop and search”
  • Arresting someone who is fully cooperating and could be interviewed voluntarily, without considering necessity. From “Arrest”

Land Law

  • Treating every agreement about land as creating a proprietary right: many create only personal rights. From “Introduction to land law”
  • Assuming everything attached to a house is a fixture: the purpose of the attachment decides. From “Nature of land”
  • Assuming a life interest can exist as a legal estate. From “Estates in land”
  • Confusing a determinable fee (ends automatically) with a conditional fee (gives a right of re-entry). From “Freehold estates”

Property Law and Practice

  • Assuming the Standard Conditions apply automatically: they must be incorporated into the contract. From “Residential conveyancing”
  • Ignoring VAT on a commercial purchase: if the seller has opted to tax, VAT may add 20% to the price. From “Commercial conveyancing”
  • Starting work before completing customer due diligence. From “Client onboarding”
  • Relying on photocopied ID without verifying it, especially for a mortgage-free, unoccupied property. From “Identity verification”

Equity and Trusts

  • Thinking equity was abolished in 1875: only the separate courts were. From “Historical development of equity”
  • Treating the maxims as rigid rules rather than guiding principles. From “Equitable principles”
  • Confusing a power (discretion) with a trust (obligation). From “Trusts”
  • Overlooking s 53(1)(c) when a beneficiary tries to transfer their equitable interest orally. From “Express trusts”

Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates

  • Assuming a will controls a jointly owned home held as beneficial joint tenants. From “Introduction to succession law”
  • Telling a client they can't disinherit a child: they can, though a 1975 Act claim may follow. From “Testamentary freedom”
  • Applying the Mental Capacity Act test instead of Banks v Goodfellow to testamentary capacity. From “Capacity to make a will”
  • Letting a beneficiary's spouse act as a witness. From “Formalities of wills”

Business Law and Practice

  • Assuming a limited company always gives complete protection: banks often require personal guarantees. From “Business organisations”
  • Treating the sole trader's business as a separate person that can be sued independently. From “Sole traders”
  • Assuming no partnership exists because nothing was signed. From “Partnerships”
  • Treating an LLP as a type of partnership under the 1890 Act: it is a body corporate. From “Limited liability partnerships”

Dispute Resolution and Civil Litigation

  • Treating the CPR as a rigid code: courts interpret them to further the overriding objective. From “Civil justice system”
  • Issuing proceedings without any pre-action letter in an ordinary claim. From “Pre-action protocols”
  • Threatening to report the defendant to the police unless they pay: improper and potentially a conduct breach. From “Letter before claim”
  • Applying the six-year tort period to a personal injury claim. From “Limitation”

Human Rights Law

  • Treating all Convention rights as qualified and subject to balancing. From “Human rights theory”
  • Arguing that the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child is directly enforceable in English courts. From “Universal human rights”
  • Assuming national labels decide whether proceedings involve a "criminal charge". From “European Convention on Human Rights”
  • Missing the one-year time limit for a standalone HRA claim. From “Human Rights Act 1998”

European Union and International Law

  • Assuming the EU can legislate on any subject: it needs a legal basis in the Treaties. From “Foundations of EU law”
  • Confusing the Council of the EU (ministers) with the Council of Europe (the ECHR body). From “EU institutions”
  • Treating recommendations and opinions as binding EU law. From “Sources of EU law”
  • Assuming a directive applies automatically like a regulation. From “EU legislation”

Legal Research, Writing and Practical Skills

  • Citing a newspaper report or summary instead of the judgment itself. From “Finding primary legal sources”
  • Quoting a textbook's summary of a case without reading the judgment. From “Finding secondary legal sources”
  • Relying on an amended provision when the events happened before the amendment came into force. From “Legislation research”
  • Relying on a case that has been overruled because you didn't check its subsequent history. From “Case law research”

Routes to Qualification

  • A true statement of law can be the wrong answer if it doesn't answer the question asked. From “Answering single-best-answer questions”
