Physics: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Physics lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Physics

  • Physics does not "prove" theories true. It tests them, and a theory survives only as long as no reliable measurement contradicts it within its domain. From “Scope and aims of physics”
  • Do not treat a unit as optional. "The length is 3.2" is meaningless; units carry half the information. From “Physical quantities and observables”
  • The radian and steradian are dimensionless derived units. Leaving "rad" out of ω in s-1 is legal but can hide errors between angular frequency ω and frequency f. From “SI base and derived units”
  • A dimensionally correct equation can still be wrong (it may miss a factor of 2π, or a whole term). Dimensional analysis can rule things out, never fully rule them in. From “Dimensional analysis”

Mathematics for Physics

  • √(a2 + b2) is not a + b, and 1/(a + b) is not 1/a + 1/b. From “Algebraic manipulation”
  • Don't divide both sides by an expression that could be zero; you can lose solutions (x2 = x gives x = 0 as well as x = 1). From “Equations and inequalities”
  • Check that equations are independent: three Kirchhoff loop equations in a two-loop circuit include one that's redundant. From “Simultaneous equations”
  • Calculators default to degrees on many devices; calculus results like d(sin x)/dx = cos x only hold in radians. From “Trigonometry and circular functions”

Measurement, Experimental Physics and Uncertainty

  • A high-resolution digital display doesn't make an instrument accurate; an uncalibrated 6-digit meter can be wrong in the second digit. From “Measurement instruments and calibration”
  • Precision isn't accuracy. Averaging a thousand readings from a miscalibrated instrument gives a very precise wrong answer. From “Accuracy, precision and resolution”
  • Taking more repeats only reduces random error. If your result disagrees with an accepted value by many standard errors, look for a systematic error. From “Random and systematic errors”
  • "Error" is the (unknown) difference from the true value; "uncertainty" is your estimate of how big it could be. They aren't the same thing. From “Measurement uncertainty”

Classical Mechanics

  • Displacement can be zero or negative even when an object has moved a long way. From “Position, displacement, distance and time”
  • Speedometers show speed, not velocity: a car going round a bend at a steady 50 km h-1 is accelerating. From “Speed and velocity”
  • Zero velocity doesn't mean zero acceleration: a ball at the top of its flight has v = 0 but a = g downward. From “Acceleration”
  • Area under the time axis on a v–t graph is negative displacement; add magnitudes only when you want distance. From “Motion in one dimension”

Rotational Mechanics

  • Rotational formulas need radians; using degrees in v = ωr or s = rθ gives wrong answers. From “Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration”
  • For a wheel rolling along the ground, the contact point is momentarily at rest; v = ωr gives the speed of the centre, not of every point. From “Angular and linear motion relationships”
  • Torque depends on where the axis is; always say about which point you take moments. From “Torque and moment of force”
  • I depends on the axis chosen. The same rod has I = ML2/12 about its centre and ML2/3 about an end. From “Moment of inertia”

Oscillations and Vibrations

  • Frequency f and angular frequency ω differ by 2π; mixing them is one of the commonest errors. From “Periodic motion”
  • SHM requires a ∝ −x exactly. A bouncing ball has periodic motion but isn't SHM (its acceleration is constant while airborne). From “Simple harmonic motion”
  • Phase is often quoted in degrees or fractions of a cycle; check which, and remember 2π rad = 360° = 1 cycle. From “Amplitude, frequency, period and phase”
  • Cutting a spring in half doubles its spring constant; each half stretches half as much under the same force. From “Mass-spring systems”

Gravitation and Orbital Mechanics

  • r is measured between centres, not between surfaces. From “Newton's law of universal gravitation”
  • The real Earth isn't uniform: its dense core makes g increase slightly with depth at first, peaking at the core–mantle boundary. From “Gravitational fields”
  • Weightlessness in orbit doesn't mean zero g; it means everything around you falls with the same acceleration. From “Gravitational field strength”
  • Potential is negative and becomes less negative (increases) with height. "Higher potential" means closer to zero. From “Gravitational potential and potential energy”

Fluid Mechanics

  • Pressure isn't a vector; force on a surface is p times area, directed along the surface normal. From “Density and pressure”
  • The pressure on a dam depends on the water depth, not on how far the reservoir stretches behind it. From “Hydrostatic equilibrium”
  • Hydraulics don't give free energy: the large piston moves a much smaller distance. From “Pascal's principle”
  • Buoyant force depends on the displaced volume, not on the object's weight or depth (for incompressible fluids). From “Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle”

Elasticity and Solid Mechanics

  • Strain has no units; percentages are common (0.05% here). From “Stress and strain”
  • Stiffness (E) isn't strength. Glass is stiff but brittle; rubber is flexible but tough. From “Young's modulus”
  • Shear strain is an angle (in radians, for small values), not a fractional length change. From “Shear modulus”
  • A gas's bulk modulus depends on how it's compressed: isothermally K = p, adiabatically K = γp. Newton got the speed of sound wrong by assuming isothermal. From “Bulk modulus”

Waves and Wave Motion

  • Particles of the medium oscillate about fixed points; only energy and the pattern travel. From “Wave properties”
  • Don't confuse a graph of displacement against position with a picture of the wave; for sound, the "peaks" are where molecules are displaced most, not where pressure is highest (pressure peaks are 90° out of phase). From “Transverse and longitudinal waves”
  • Frequency doesn't change at a boundary; wavelength and speed do. The colour we see depends on frequency. From “Wavelength, frequency, period and speed”
  • kx − ωt moves right; kx + ωt moves left. Sign errors reverse the direction of travel. From “Wave equations”

Acoustics and Sound

  • Sound travels faster in warm air, not in "thicker" air; humidity raises it slightly too. From “Sound wave propagation”
  • Microphones measure pressure; intensity also needs the particle velocity (or an assumption of plane waves). From “Sound pressure and intensity”
  • Decibels don't add arithmetically: 80 dB + 80 dB = 83 dB. From “Decibel scale”
  • Doubling intensity doesn't double perceived loudness; you need about 10 times the intensity. From “Pitch, loudness and timbre”

Thermodynamics

  • Temperature is not heat. A bath of warm water contains more internal energy than a red-hot spark, though the spark is hotter. From “Temperature and thermal equilibrium”
  • Thermal equilibrium means no net heat flow, not no microscopic energy exchange: molecules still collide constantly. From “Zeroth law of thermodynamics”
  • "Heat content" is a misleading phrase. A body has internal energy; heat is energy crossing a boundary. From “Heat and internal energy”
  • Specific heat capacity isn't constant; it changes with temperature, especially at low temperatures. From “Specific heat capacity”

Statistical Mechanics

  • The equal-probability postulate applies to microstates, not macrostates; macrostates differ enormously in probability. From “Microstates and macrostates”
  • Ensembles can disagree for small systems or near phase transitions with long-range forces (like self-gravitating systems). From “Statistical ensembles”
  • The partition function sums over states, not energy levels; degenerate levels count once per state. From “Microcanonical, canonical and grand canonical ensembles”
  • The Boltzmann factor compares individual states; with degeneracies, higher levels can hold more particles if they have many more states. From “Boltzmann distribution”

Heat Transfer and Thermal Physics

  • Materials that feel cold (metal, stone) aren't colder; they conduct heat away from your hand faster. From “Conduction”
  • Convection can't happen in solids or in a vacuum; it needs a fluid that can flow. From “Convection”
  • A hot object in a hot room radiates just as much as in a cold room; it's the net exchange that changes. From “Thermal radiation”
  • Fourier's law assumes local equilibrium; it fails at nanoscales or very short times (ballistic phonons, second sound). From “Fourier's law of heat conduction”

Electricity and Electrostatics

  • Rubbing doesn't create charge; it transfers electrons from one material to the other. From “Electric charge”
  • Quarks have fractional charge but are never seen free; observed charges remain integer multiples of e. From “Conservation and quantisation of charge”
  • Dielectrics don't conduct, but they still respond to fields; that response is what makes capacitors and microwave heating work. From “Conductors, insulators and dielectrics”
  • Coulomb's law is for point charges (or spherically symmetric ones); extended shapes need integration. From “Coulomb's law”

Electric Circuits

  • Current isn't "used up" in a component; the same current enters and leaves. Energy is what's transferred. From “Electric current”
  • Resistance depends on shape; resistivity doesn't. Stretching a wire to twice its length (same volume) quadruples its resistance. From “Resistance and resistivity”
  • V = IR defines resistance for any component at a point; Ohm's law is the claim that R stays constant. From “Ohm's law”
  • High conductivity doesn't always mean high mobility: metals have huge carrier densities but modest mobilities. From “Conductivity”

Magnetism and Electromagnetism

  • Earth's geographic North Pole is near a magnetic south pole (it attracts compass north poles). From “Magnetic fields”
  • A magnetic force can't speed up or slow down a charge; it only changes direction. Accelerators use electric fields to add energy. From “Magnetic forces on charges”
  • Whether a force is "electric" or "magnetic" depends on the observer's frame; the total Lorentz force transforms consistently. From “Lorentz force”
  • A wire parallel to the field feels no force however large the current. From “Magnetic forces on current-carrying wires”

Electromagnetic Waves

  • EM waves don't need a medium; the 19th-century "luminiferous ether" was abandoned after Michelson–Morley and special relativity. From “Electromagnetic wave propagation”
  • "Light-year" is a distance, not a time. From “Speed of light”
  • B in a wave is numerically small in tesla, but its energy density equals E's: don't conclude that the magnetic part is negligible. From “Electric and magnetic field relationships”
  • The wave equation in vacuum is non-dispersive: all frequencies travel at c. From “Electromagnetic wave equation”

Optics

  • Rays are a model; light isn't really made of infinitely thin lines, and the model fails at small scales. From “Geometrical optics”
  • We see most objects by diffuse reflection; a perfect mirror is itself almost invisible. From “Reflection and refraction”
  • Use sines of angles, not the angles themselves; θ1/θ2 ≠ n2/n1 except at small angles. From “Snell's law”
  • TIR only happens going from higher to lower index; light entering a denser medium always refracts in. From “Total internal reflection”

Quantum Mechanics

  • Quantum mechanics doesn't stop applying to large objects; its effects are just too small to notice, unless carefully isolated. From “Origins of quantum theory”
  • Planck initially viewed quantisation as a mathematical trick; Einstein took it physically, as quanta of light. From “Blackbody radiation”
  • Brighter light below threshold still ejects no electrons; a classical wave picture can't explain this. From “Photoelectric effect”
  • The Compton shift is independent of the incident wavelength; only its relative size changes. From “Compton scattering”

Atomic Physics

  • Atoms are mostly empty space in terms of mass, but the electron cloud fills that space; you can't push atoms through each other because of Pauli exclusion and electrostatics. From “Atomic structure”
  • Rutherford scattering is pure Coulomb repulsion; the alphas never touch the nucleus at the energies Geiger and Marsden used. From “Rutherford scattering”
  • Bohr orbits don't exist; electrons in atoms aren't on circular tracks. The model's energies are right; its picture isn't. From “Bohr model”
  • Orbitals are one-electron approximations; the true many-electron wavefunction isn't a simple product of orbitals. From “Quantum mechanical atomic models”

Molecular Physics

  • Vibrational and rotational energies aren't independent exactly; vibration changes the bond length and so the rotational constant. From “Molecular energy levels”
  • Homonuclear molecules (N2, O2, H2) have no permanent dipole, so no pure rotational absorption spectrum; they're seen by Raman spectroscopy. From “Rotational spectra”
  • Not every vibration absorbs infrared light; symmetric vibrations with no dipole change are IR-inactive (but often Raman-active). From “Vibrational spectra”
  • The colour we see is complementary to what's absorbed: chlorophyll absorbs red and blue, so leaves look green. From “Electronic molecular transitions”

Nuclear Physics

  • The nucleus isn't a static bag of balls; nucleons move rapidly (~0.25c) in quantum orbits. From “Nuclear structure”
  • Neutrons are stable inside most nuclei, because decay there would leave the nucleus in a higher energy state. From “Protons, neutrons and nucleons”
  • Nuclear "size" depends on what's probed: charge radius and matter radius can differ (neutron skins in heavy nuclei). From “Nuclear size and density”
  • Binding energy is energy released when a nucleus forms, so more binding means less mass, not more. From “Nuclear binding energy”

Particle Physics

  • Protons and neutrons aren't fundamental; they're bound states of quarks and gluons. From “Fundamental particles”
  • Quark masses inside protons are tiny; most of the proton's mass comes from gluon field energy and quark kinetic energy. From “Quarks and leptons”
  • Being a boson isn't the same as being a force carrier; mesons and helium-4 atoms are bosons too. From “Fermions and bosons”
  • The weak force isn't intrinsically weak: its coupling is similar to electromagnetism's; it seems weak at low energy because its carriers are heavy. From “Fundamental interactions”

Special Relativity

  • Einstein didn't need the Michelson–Morley result specifically; his reasoning started from the symmetry of electromagnetism. From “Historical foundations of relativity”
  • Galilean relativity isn't "wrong" for everyday use; its errors are far below anything measurable at normal speeds. From “Galilean transformations”
  • "Observing" in relativity isn't seeing; seeing includes light-travel delays, which produce different visual effects from length contraction (Terrell rotation). From “Inertial reference frames”
  • Relativity doesn't say "everything is relative": c, the spacetime interval, proper time and physical laws are absolute. From “Postulates of special relativity”

General Relativity

  • The equivalence is only local: over large regions tidal forces (differences in gravity) reveal true curvature. From “Equivalence principle”
  • Special relativity handles acceleration perfectly well; general relativity is needed for gravity (curved spacetime), not for acceleration itself. From “Accelerated reference frames”
  • The "rubber sheet" picture of curved space shows spatial curvature, but in reality it's mostly time curvature that makes things fall. From “Gravity as spacetime curvature”
  • The metric's components depend on coordinates; curvature (computed from them) is what's physically meaningful. From “Metric tensors”

Condensed Matter Physics

  • Glass isn't a slow-flowing liquid; medieval windows thicker at the bottom were made that way, not shaped by flow. From “Crystalline and amorphous solids”
  • A lattice is a set of points, not atoms; the crystal structure is lattice + basis. From “Crystal lattices and unit cells”
  • Base-centred cubic isn't a separate Bravais lattice; it breaks cubic symmetry and is equivalent to simple tetragonal. From “Bravais lattices”
  • Miller indices describe orientation, not position: parallel planes share the same indices. From “Miller indices”

Semiconductor Physics and Electronics

  • The band gap isn't a gap in space; it's a range of energies with no allowed electron states. From “Energy bands in solids”
  • Doped semiconductors are electrically neutral overall; ionised dopants balance the free carriers. From “Intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors”
  • A dopant concentration of 1 part per million can raise silicon's conductivity by a factor of ~105. From “Doping”
  • A hole isn't a positron or a real particle; it's a collective description of many electrons' behaviour in a nearly full band. From “Electrons and holes”

Plasma Physics

  • Ionisation alone doesn't make a plasma; collective behaviour does. From “Plasma as a state of matter”
  • Ionisation fraction depends on density too: lower density favours ionisation at a given temperature. From “Ionisation and recombination”
  • Shielding is statistical; individual charges still move through the plasma and interact. From “Debye shielding”
  • The Debye length uses the electron temperature (in most cases); hot electrons and cold ions are common in labs. From “Debye length”

Astrophysics

  • Planets don't orbit the Sun's centre exactly; they orbit the Solar System's centre of mass, which is sometimes outside the Sun. From “Celestial mechanics”
  • More fuel doesn't mean a longer life: massive stars burn their fuel disproportionately fast. From “Stellar structure and evolution”
  • Star formation is inefficient: only a few per cent of a molecular cloud's mass becomes stars. From “Star formation”
  • Losing energy makes stars hotter, not cooler: a consequence of gravity's negative heat capacity. From “Hydrostatic equilibrium in stars”

Cosmology

  • Distant galaxies receding faster than light don't violate relativity; it's space expanding, not motion through space. From “The observable universe”
  • The universe is very inhomogeneous on small scales (galaxies, voids); the principle applies only on very large scales. From “Cosmological principles”
  • The expansion isn't an explosion into pre-existing space; space itself grows. From “Expansion of the universe”
  • Hubble's law is linear only at small distances; at high redshift, the relation depends on the expansion history. From “Hubble's law”

Geophysics

  • The mantle isn't liquid magma; it's solid rock that flows slowly like a very viscous material over millions of years. From “Earth's internal structure”
  • Isostasy means mountains have low-density roots; their gravity anomalies are smaller than their visible mass suggests. From “Earth's gravitational field”
  • The geodynamo isn't a permanent magnet; Earth's core is far above the Curie temperature of iron. From “Earth's magnetic field”
  • Geothermal heat flow per square metre is tiny (~0.1 W) compared with sunlight (~200 W average); useful only where concentrated or extracted over large volumes. From “Geothermal energy”

Atmospheric Physics and Climate

  • The thermosphere's high temperature means fast molecules, not much heat: you'd freeze there because there's too little gas to warm you. From “Atmospheric structure”
  • Pressure differences, not absolute pressure, drive winds; a "high" is only a few per cent above average. From “Atmospheric pressure and temperature”
  • Humid air is lighter, not heavier, than dry air at the same temperature and pressure. From “Gas laws in the atmosphere”
  • An atmosphere opaque at a wavelength still emits at it; what escapes comes from the upper layers. From “Radiative transfer”

Biophysics

  • Life doesn't violate the second law; it decreases entropy locally by increasing it more in the environment. From “Physical principles of living systems”
  • Muscles are weak in mechanical advantage but fast; the body trades force for speed and range of motion. From “Biomechanics”
  • Blood flow in large arteries is pulsatile and not exactly Poiseuille; the r4 rule is still a good guide for small vessels. From “Biological fluid dynamics”
  • Diffusion doesn't "push" molecules in a direction; net flux arises statistically from concentration differences. From “Diffusion and transport”

Medical Physics

  • A sharper image is not always a better one. If noise rises with resolution, the CNR can fall and lesions become harder to see. From “Physics of diagnostic imaging”
  • Raising kV improves penetration but reduces contrast, because the photoelectric effect falls steeply with energy. From “X-ray production and imaging”
  • HU values depend on beam energy and calibration; they are not universal constants of the tissue. From “Computed tomography”
  • T2 is always shorter than or equal to T1; T2* is shorter still. From “Magnetic resonance imaging”

Optoelectronics and Photonics

  • A two-level system cannot reach steady-state inversion by optical pumping; at best it saturates to equal populations. From “Light-matter interactions”
  • Silicon is a poor light emitter because its gap is indirect, not because its gap is the wrong size. From “Semiconductor light emission”
  • LED efficiency is often limited by light extraction, not internal recombination. From “LEDs and laser diodes”
  • More gain does not always mean better SNR: APD excess noise grows with gain. From “Photodetectors”

Quantum Information and Quantum Computing

  • You cannot read out all 2n amplitudes; measurement gives only n bits. Algorithms must use interference to make the right answer likely. From “Qubits and quantum states”
  • Orthogonal states are opposite on the sphere, at 180°, not at 90° as in ordinary vector space. From “Bloch sphere”
  • Clifford circuits alone, however large, offer no quantum speed-up; non-Clifford gates like T are needed. From “Quantum gates”
  • Mathematically, a circuit A then B is the operator BA, written in reverse order. From “Quantum circuits”

Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos

  • A good small-amplitude linear model can give badly wrong predictions at large amplitude. From “Linear and nonlinear systems”
  • Nonlinear equations can blow up in finite time, which never happens in linear ones with constant coefficients. From “Nonlinear differential equations”
  • A linear centre may become a spiral when nonlinear terms are included; check further. From “Fixed points and stability”
  • 2D autonomous flows cannot be chaotic; you need three dimensions or a driven (time-dependent) system. From “Phase-space analysis”

Computational Physics

  • Never test floating-point equality with ==; use a tolerance such as abs(a − b) < 1e-12 × scale. From “Scientific programming”
  • High-order rules can perform worse on non-smooth or noisy integrands; check convergence by refining h. From “Numerical integration”
  • Making h ever smaller does not keep improving accuracy; below the optimum, round-off error explodes. From “Numerical differentiation”
  • Newton's method needs a good initial guess; plot the function first. From “Root-finding algorithms”

Mathematical and Theoretical Physics

  • L = T − V, not T + V; the total energy is a different quantity. From “Lagrangian mechanics”
  • H equals total energy only when coordinates are time-independent and the potential doesn't depend on velocity. From “Hamiltonian mechanics”
  • "Least" action is a misnomer; the action is stationary and may be a saddle point. From “Principle of least action”
  • Rolling without slipping on a plane is non-holonomic; you cannot simply eliminate a coordinate. From “Generalised coordinates”

Classical Field Theory

  • The magnetic field is a pseudovector: under a mirror reflection it does not flip like velocity does. From “Scalar, vector and tensor fields”
  • Gauss's law is a constraint, not an evolution equation; initial data must satisfy it. From “Field equations”
  • ℒ is a density; the Lagrangian is its integral over space. From “Lagrangian field theory”
  • In gauge theories not every field component has a conjugate momentum; constraints must be handled carefully. From “Hamiltonian field theory”

Quantum Field Theory

  • The particle concept is observer-dependent in general; the field is the more basic object. From “Quantum fields and particles”
  • Using commutators for fermion fields leads to inconsistencies (negative energies or acausality); spin and statistics are tied. From “Creation and annihilation operators”
  • The infinite zero-point energy is removed by normal ordering; only differences in vacuum energy are observable (except perhaps in gravity). From “Canonical quantisation”
  • Gauge theories need gauge fixing; without it, the path integral overcounts and diverges. From “Path-integral quantisation”

Nuclear and Particle Detection Methods

  • Ion chamber signals are tiny (pA–nA); electrometer leakage and environmental corrections matter. From “Ionisation chambers”
  • GM counters saturate in very high fields and can read low or even zero ("fold-back") at extreme dose rates. From “Geiger–Müller counters”
  • The Compton continuum can hide weak photopeaks; larger or denser crystals improve peak-to-Compton ratios. From “Scintillation detectors”
  • Germanium must be cooled; at room temperature its small band gap gives huge leakage currents. From “Semiconductor radiation detectors”

Experimental and Applied Physics

  • High resolution means nothing without accuracy; a precise sensor can still be badly calibrated. From “Instrumentation and sensor design”
  • Averaging doesn't reduce systematic errors; past a point, more data only makes a wrong answer more precise. From “Precision measurement”
  • A big pump behind a narrow tube is wasted; conductance often limits the effective pumping speed. From “Vacuum technology”
  • Liquid helium boil-off expands ~750× in volume; confined spaces can become asphyxiation hazards. From “Cryogenic systems”

Materials Physics

  • The same composition can give wildly different properties depending on processing and microstructure. From “Structure-property relationships”
  • Close-packed structures (FCC, HCP) have the same packing fraction but different ductility because of slip-system geometry. From “Atomic bonding and crystal structure”
  • A perfect, dislocation-free crystal (like a whisker) can be enormously strong; real metals are weak because of dislocations. From “Defects and dislocations”
  • Doubling the diffusion distance needs four times the time. From “Diffusion in solids”

Renewable Energy and Energy Physics

  • Don't confuse power (kW) with energy (kWh); a 1 kW device running for 3 hours uses 3 kWh. From “Energy conversion and conservation”
  • Solar panels lose efficiency when hot; sunny but hot days don't always give peak output. From “Solar photovoltaic physics”
  • Concentrating systems need direct sunlight; cloudy, diffuse conditions give little output. From “Solar thermal systems”
  • Doubling wind speed gives eight times the power; small errors in wind speed estimates give big errors in yield. From “Wind energy”

Acoustics, Vibrations and Engineering Physics

  • Light damping makes resonance peaks sharp and dangerous; small changes in speed can produce large vibrations. From “Mechanical vibration analysis”
  • A structure strong enough statically can still fail dynamically through resonance or flutter. From “Structural dynamics”
  • Adding damping reduces peak response but also reduces the efficiency of resonant devices like sensors and clocks. From “Resonance and damping”
  • Accelerometer mass and poor boundary conditions can shift measured frequencies; light sensors and realistic supports matter. From “Modal analysis”

Space Physics and Planetary Science

  • The corona is far hotter than the photosphere; heat doesn't simply flow outward from hot to cold here. From “Solar physics”
  • The solar wind is not just fast particles; it carries magnetic field, which controls how it interacts with planets. From “Solar wind”
  • The heliopause isn't the edge of the solar system gravitationally; the Oort cloud extends tens of thousands of AU further. From “Heliosphere”
  • The magnetosphere doesn't block all solar particles; reconnection lets energy and plasma in, especially with southward IMF. From “Magnetospheres”

Historical Development of Physics

  • Ancient atomism was philosophical speculation, not an experimentally grounded theory like modern atomic physics. From “Ancient Greek natural philosophy”
  • A model can predict accurately yet describe reality wrongly; predictive success isn't proof of physical truth. From “Early astronomy and mechanics”
  • Calling the Middle Ages scientifically empty ignores major work in the Islamic world and medieval Europe. From “Medieval scientific traditions”
  • The revolution wasn't instant or purely rational; it involved gradual change, institutions and debates. From “Scientific Revolution”

Philosophy and Foundations of Physics

  • Predictive success doesn't automatically prove truth; history shows successful theories can rest on false entities. From “Scientific realism and instrumentalism”
  • A model that predicts well, such as a neural network, may provide little physical understanding. From “Explanation and prediction in physics”
  • Many textbook "laws" are approximate empirical relations valid only in limited regimes. From “Laws of nature”
  • Determinism and predictability are different: chaotic systems are deterministic but unpredictable. From “Causality and determinism”

Physics Education and Communication

  • Simplifying is good; saying something false is not. Avoid explanations that create misconceptions to be unlearned later. From “Explaining physical concepts clearly”
  • Don't include forces that don't exist (like a separate "centripetal force" alongside tension); centripetal force is the net inward force. From “Scientific diagrams and representations”
  • Every derived formula has assumptions; using it outside them gives wrong answers. From “Deriving equations from first principles”
  • A model is only as good as its assumptions; always check whether neglected effects really are small. From “Translating physical situations into mathematical models”

Advanced Problem-Solving Skills

  • Surface similarity can mislead; two inclined-plane problems may need completely different principles. From “Identifying relevant physical principles”
  • Mixing systems mid-problem (counting a force as both internal and external) leads to double-counting. From “Defining systems and boundaries”
  • Mixing sign conventions between steps (e.g. up positive, then down positive) is a classic error. From “Choosing coordinate systems”
  • "ma" is not a force; it's the result of the net force. From “Drawing free-body and field diagrams”

Interdisciplinary Physics

  • Chemical intuition (octet rule, hybridisation) is a useful model, not fundamental physics; it breaks down for many molecules. From “Physics and chemistry”
  • Thermodynamics tells you if a reaction can occur, not how fast; kinetics determines the rate. From “Physical chemistry”
  • The Born–Oppenheimer approximation fails near conical intersections, where electronic and nuclear motions couple strongly. From “Chemical physics”
  • Intuition from everyday inertia fails for microorganisms; at low Reynolds number, motion stops instantly when propulsion stops. From “Physics and biology”

Research and Professional Physics Skills

  • A published paper isn't automatically correct; independent confirmation matters. From “Reading and evaluating scientific papers”
  • Searching only one database or recent papers can miss key work; document your search so others can repeat it. From “Literature searches and systematic reviews”
  • Questions that are too broad lead to unfocused projects; too narrow ones may lack significance. From “Formulating research questions”
  • Too many free parameters can fit anything; a good model predicts beyond the data it was tuned on. From “Developing theoretical models”
