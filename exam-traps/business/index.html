Business and Management: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Business and Management lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

The Business Environment

  • Confusing added value with profit. Added value is selling price minus bought-in inputs; profit is what remains after all costs, including wages and overheads, have also been paid. From “What is a business?”
  • Treating a mission statement as an objective. "To delight our customers" is an aim or mission; without a measure and a deadline it can't guide decisions or show whether the business succeeded. From “Business purpose and objectives”
  • Assuming stakeholder positions are fixed. Interest and power change with the decision and over time; a matrix is a snapshot to revisit. From “Stakeholders”
  • Mixing up the public sector with public limited companies. A plc such as BP is in the private sector: "public" refers to its shares being offered to the public, not to state ownership. From “The private, public and third sectors”

Management and Organisational Behaviour

  • Treating management and leadership as the same thing. Kotter's distinction is useful: management copes with complexity (plans, budgets, control); leadership copes with change (direction, alignment, inspiration). Organisations need both. From “What is management?”
  • Saying classical theory is obsolete. Its ideas live on in fast-food production lines, call-centre scripts, lean timings and warehouse picking targets, where work is still measured and standardised. From “Classical management theory: Taylor and Fayol”
  • Thinking the roles are separate jobs done one after another. They form an integrated whole: one meeting can involve several roles at once. From “Mintzberg's managerial roles”
  • Overstating the evidence. The studies had tiny samples, changed several things at once and were partly reinterpreted to fit Mayo's ideas. Re-analyses of the original lighting data find the "Hawthorne effect" much weaker than the legend. From “The human relations movement”

Strategy

  • Confusing strategy with being efficient. Doing things better than rivals (operational effectiveness) matters, but rivals can copy it; strategy requires a distinct position and deliberate trade-offs. From “What is strategy?”
  • Assuming a published mission statement shows what an organisation actually does. Statements are often generic; judge them against decisions, resource allocation and behaviour. From “Mission, vision and objectives”
  • Treating emergent strategy as having no strategy. Emergent strategy is a consistent pattern that develops through action and learning, often recognised and then formalised by managers. From “Planned and emergent strategy”
  • Confusing substitutes with competitors. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (trains for domestic flights); a competitor offers a similar product (another airline). From “Porter's five forces”

Marketing

  • Equating marketing with advertising or selling. Promotion is one part of marketing, which begins with understanding customers and shapes products, prices and distribution. From “What is marketing?”
  • Assuming a large sample is always reliable. A large convenience sample can be more biased than a small random one. From “Market research”
  • Choosing segments only because they are large. A segment must also be reachable and respond to a distinct offer the firm can deliver profitably. From “Segmentation, targeting and positioning”
  • Assuming consumers always act rationally. Habits, emotions and mental shortcuts shape many purchases. From “Consumer behaviour”

Operations and Supply Chain Management

  • Thinking operations only applies to factories. Hospitals, banks, schools and online platforms all have operations functions. From “What is operations management?”
  • Ignoring customers as an input. In services, customers are often transformed resources, which makes operations more visible and variable. From “The transformation model and the four Vs”
  • Choosing an assembly line for a highly varied, low-volume product. Line layouts suit standardised, high-volume work. From “Process types and layouts”
  • Aiming for 100% utilisation in services. Near full utilisation, queues and waiting times grow sharply with any variation in demand. From “Capacity management”

People Management

  • Treating HR as only an administrative function. Its strategic role in capability and culture is central to modern HRM. From “What is human resource management?”
  • Relying on turnover alone. A stability index shows whether experienced staff stay or whether turnover is concentrated among new starters. From “Workforce planning”
  • Relying on unstructured interviews and "gut feeling". They predict performance poorly and are prone to bias. From “Recruitment and selection”
  • Labelling someone self-employed to avoid obligations. Courts look at the reality of the relationship, as in Uber v Aslam. From “Employment law essentials”

Finance for Managers

  • Treating profit as money in the bank. Profit measures performance; cash measures the ability to pay bills now. From “Why managers need finance”
  • Including the purchase price of a new machine as an expense. Only that year's depreciation appears in the income statement; the machine is an asset. From “The income statement”
  • Thinking equity is cash the business holds. Equity is a claim on the net assets, which may be tied up in buildings, stock and receivables. From “The statement of financial position”
  • Adding an increase in receivables. More receivables means cash not yet collected, so it is deducted. From “The statement of cash flows”

Economics for Business

  • Saying "demand fell" when price rose. A price rise causes a fall in quantity demanded, a movement along the curve. From “Demand”
  • Thinking a rise in price always shifts supply. A price change moves along the supply curve; only other factors shift it. From “Supply and market equilibrium”
  • Assuming higher prices always raise revenue. If demand is elastic, revenue falls. From “Price elasticity of demand”
  • Assuming government intervention always corrects market failure. Poorly designed policy can create new inefficiencies. From “Market failure”

Entrepreneurship and Innovation

  • Equating entrepreneurship with owning any small business. The defining features are opportunity, innovation and value creation under uncertainty. From “What is entrepreneurship?”
  • Confusing an idea with an opportunity. An idea becomes an opportunity only if customers will pay and the venture can deliver profitably. From “Opportunity recognition and the entrepreneurial mindset”
  • Thinking an MVP means a poor-quality product. It is the smallest test of a key assumption, and must still deliver enough value to measure real behaviour. From “Effectuation and the lean start-up”
  • Filling in the canvas once and treating it as fact. Each block is a hypothesis to be tested and revised. From “The business model canvas”

Digital Business and Information Systems

  • Treating information systems as just computers. Procedures and people are equally part of the system. From “Information systems in organisations”
  • Thinking transformation is mainly about technology. Culture, skills and processes are usually harder to change than systems. From “Digital transformation”
  • Assuming B2C retail is the largest form of e-commerce. By value, trade between businesses is much larger. From “E-commerce business models”
  • Assuming network effects always grow forever. Congestion, poor quality and multi-homing can weaken them. From “Platforms and network effects”

Ethics, ESG and Corporate Governance

  • Assuming legal means ethical. Law sets a minimum; many unethical practices are lawful and ethics asks what a business should do, not only what it must do. From “Ethical theories in business”
  • Blaming unethical behaviour only on a few "bad apples". Incentives, culture and leadership (the "barrel") strongly shape what ordinary people do. From “Ethical decision-making”
  • Equating CSR with charity donations. Philanthropy is only the top layer of Carroll's pyramid; how a firm makes its money matters more than how it gives some away. From “Corporate social responsibility”
  • Thinking section 172 makes directors accountable to all stakeholders equally. It requires them to have regard to other stakeholders while acting for the benefit of members. From “Shareholder and stakeholder theories of the firm”
