Psychology: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Psychology lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

Foundations of Psychology

  • Thinking psychology is mainly therapy. Clinical work is one part; most psychologists research or apply knowledge about perception, learning, development, social behaviour and more. From “What is psychology?”
  • Treating dates such as 1879 as the moment psychology "began". They are useful markers, but ideas developed gradually and in many countries at once. From “The history of psychology”
  • Equating "science" with laboratories and white coats. Science is defined by its method, and observational studies, surveys and case studies can all be conducted scientifically. From “Psychology as a science”
  • Assuming one discipline's explanation must be the "real" one. Explanations at different levels can all be true and can complement each other. From “Psychology and its neighbouring disciplines”

Biological Psychology and Neuroscience

  • Thinking every response needs the brain. Spinal reflexes act before conscious awareness. From “Organisation of the nervous system”
  • Thinking the brain is mostly neurones. Glial cells are about as numerous and do vital work. From “The central nervous system”
  • Assuming "involuntary" means impossible to influence. Breathing and, through biofeedback, some autonomic responses can be partly controlled. From “The peripheral nervous system: somatic and autonomic”
  • Thinking the two branches switch on and off like a light. Both are active continuously; their balance shifts. From “Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions”

Cognitive Psychology

  • Thinking box-and-arrow models describe where things happen in the brain. They describe functions, not anatomy. From “The information-processing approach”
  • Assuming unattended information is never processed. Breakthrough of personally relevant words shows some processing occurs. From “Selective attention”
  • Believing people can truly do two demanding tasks at once. Most "multitasking" is rapid switching with costs. From “Divided attention”
  • Assuming vigilance tasks are easy because nothing much happens. They are effortful and stressful. From “Sustained attention”

Learning and Behaviour

  • Mixing up UCS and CS. Ask: did this stimulus trigger the response before any learning? If yes, it is unconditioned. From “Classical conditioning”
  • Thinking extinction means the association has been unlearned. The evidence shows it is suppressed by new learning, which is why relapse is common after exposure therapy. From “Acquisition and extinction”
  • Treating spontaneous recovery as a failure of the experiment or therapy. It is a predictable property of extinction learning. From “Spontaneous recovery”
  • Confusing generalisation with spontaneous recovery. Generalisation is about similar stimuli; spontaneous recovery is about time after extinction. From “Generalisation and discrimination”

Developmental Psychology

  • Assuming a fetus is unaffected until late pregnancy. The embryonic weeks, when organs form, are the most sensitive to many teratogens. From “Prenatal development”
  • Treating milestone ages as deadlines. Normal development varies widely, and many differences even out. From “Infancy”
  • Overstating the marshmallow test's predictive power. Replications with larger, more diverse samples found much smaller links. From “Childhood”
  • Describing adolescents as having "broken" brains. Their brains are developing, adapted to learning and becoming independent. From “Adolescence”

Social Psychology

  • Assuming famous priming findings are solid. Several high-profile social priming effects haven't replicated. From “Social cognition”
  • Treating fast impressions as accurate. Confidence in snap judgements often exceeds their accuracy. From “Social perception and impressions”
  • Thinking people always use covariation information systematically. People often have incomplete information and use shortcuts. From “Attribution”
  • Calling the FAE universal. It is weaker in some cultures and situations, which is one reason "fundamental" has been questioned. From “The fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias”

Personality and Individual Differences

  • Expecting a trait to predict a single act. Traits predict patterns of behaviour across many situations. From “Trait theories of personality”
  • Treating Big Five scores as types. People vary continuously on each dimension, and most are near the middle. From “The Big Five”
  • Assuming introverts dislike people. Eysenck's introverts prefer less stimulation, not necessarily less company. From “Eysenck's theory”
  • Concluding that "heritable" means "fixed". Heritable traits still change with age and experience. From “Biological approaches to personality”

Psychopathology

  • Equating "rare" with "disordered". Many rare traits are positive or neutral. From “Defining abnormality”
  • Assuming current approaches are free of cultural influence. Every era's ideas reflect its context. From “A history of psychopathology”
  • Assuming a brain difference proves a biological cause. Direction and confounds must be considered. From “The biological model”
  • Assuming negative thoughts are always irrational. Some reflect genuinely difficult circumstances. From “The cognitive model”

Clinical Psychology and Interventions

  • Assuming assessment is a one-off event. Understanding develops throughout care. From “Clinical assessment”
  • Assuming structured interviews are always best. They increase reliability but can miss the person's priorities. From “Clinical interviews”
  • Treating an MSE as a complete assessment. It is a snapshot to be read with the history. From “Mental state assessment”
  • Treating a questionnaire score as a diagnosis. Scores support clinical judgement. From “Psychological testing in clinical practice”

Research Methods

  • Saying a study "proves" a theory. Evidence supports or fails to support; a later study may overturn it. From “Scientific reasoning in psychology”
  • Writing vague hypotheses such as "caffeine affects people". A hypothesis must name the operationalised variables and the predicted relationship. From “Research questions and hypotheses”
  • Treating the measure as if it were the concept. A memory test score measures performance on that test, not "memory" in every sense. From “Operationalisation”
  • Calling a measured characteristic like gender an IV in a true experiment. It can be a grouping variable, but it isn't manipulated, so the study is a quasi-experiment. From “Independent and dependent variables”

Statistics

  • Calculating a mean for nominal data, such as an "average diagnosis". Categories without order can only be counted. From “Levels of measurement”
  • Reporting only the mean for heavily skewed data. Always look at the distribution first. From “Measures of central tendency”
  • Reporting a mean without a measure of spread. Without the SD or IQR, readers can't judge how typical the mean is. From “Measures of dispersion: range, variance and standard deviation”
  • Assuming all psychological data are normal. Reaction times, counts and many clinical measures are skewed. From “Distributions: normal, skew and kurtosis”

Evolutionary Psychology and Behavioural Ecology

  • Assuming every human trait is an adaptation. Many are by-products, exaptations or culturally learned. From “Adaptation”
  • Thinking "survival of the fittest" means the strongest win. Fitness is about reproductive success. From “Natural selection”
  • Assuming sexual selection predicts fixed differences in all individuals. It describes average tendencies with large overlap. From “Sexual selection”
  • Assuming evolved means fixed or present at birth. Evolved mechanisms can develop through experience. From “Evolution of cognition”

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

  • Assuming rewards always increase motivation. For enjoyable tasks, expected tangible rewards can reduce interest. From “Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation”
  • Assuming all behaviour reduces tension. People often seek stimulation, novelty and challenge. From “Drive theories”
  • Thinking dopamine is the brain's "pleasure chemical". It is more closely linked to wanting and motivation. From “Incentive theories”
  • Equating autonomy with independence. In SDT, autonomy means acting from self-endorsed values, which can include following others' guidance. From “Self-determination theory”

Applied Psychology

  • Assuming every popular teaching idea is evidence-based. Learning styles, for example, lack supporting evidence. From “Educational psychology”
  • Assuming lack of motivation is a fixed trait. Motivation responds to classroom conditions and beliefs. From “Achievement and motivation in education”
  • Assuming one behaviour strategy suits all pupils. Needs and context differ. From “Behaviour in schools”
  • Assuming exam grades are perfectly precise. Marking reliability varies by subject and question type. From “Assessment in education”

Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology

  • Treating cultural differences as fixed national traits. They are average tendencies that vary within cultures. From “Culture and cognition”
  • Assuming perception is identical everywhere. Experience can shape how cues are interpreted. From “Culture and perception”
  • Assuming universal recognition means emotions are identical everywhere. Display and meaning vary. From “Culture and emotion”
  • Assuming everyone in a country shares one cultural orientation. Variation within countries is large. From “Individualism and collectivism”

Contemporary Psychology

  • Treating brain activity as direct proof of a mental state. Most regions serve many functions. From “Cognitive neuroscience today”
  • Assuming a model that fits data must be correct. Other models may fit equally well. From “Computational psychology”
  • Assuming computational models can already diagnose or choose treatments. Clinical use is not yet established. From “Computational psychiatry”
  • Assuming good performance on a task means an AI understands it as humans do. Mechanisms may differ. From “Artificial intelligence and psychology”
