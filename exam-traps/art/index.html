Art and Design: common exam mistakes

These are the mistakes our Art and Design lessons warn about most, grouped by topic. Read each trap, then cover it and explain the right idea from memory.

The Visual Language of Art and Design

  • Treating "art" as meaning only painting and sculpture. Exam specifications and galleries include photography, film, installation, performance, textiles, ceramics and digital work. From “What is art and design?”
  • Thinking AO3 means only drawing. Recording includes photography, written annotation, digital capture, maquettes and notes, so long as they record observations and ideas relevant to your intentions. From “How art and design are assessed: the four assessment objectives”
  • Jumping straight to meaning ("it's about loneliness") without describing and analysing first. Examiners reward interpretations that are built on visual evidence. From “Looking closely: an approach to visual analysis”
  • Thinking line only means outline. Most expressive drawing uses varied, broken and internal contour lines; a single even outline flattens a form. From “Line”

Drawing

  • Thinking drawing must be realistic to count. Diagrams, gestural sketches, maps, rubbings and annotated thumbnails are all drawing, and often evidence thinking better than a polished copy. From “Drawing as thinking and recording”
  • Using one pencil for everything. A single HB cannot give both fine construction lines and deep darks; a range of grades widens the tonal scale. From “Drawing materials: graphite, charcoal, ink and pastel”
  • Using the same mark everywhere, for example even outlines filled with uniform shading. Differentiated marks separate materials, planes and distances. From “Mark-making”
  • Bending the elbow or moving closer between measurements. The unit changes size, so proportions drift; also, measuring with the pencil tilted towards you distorts lengths. From “Measuring and sighting”

Colour Theory

  • Thinking an object "has" one true colour. A painter must observe the colour actually seen under the light and next to its neighbours, not the remembered local colour. From “Light and colour perception”
  • Telling students that the "real" primaries are red, yellow and blue in every context. RYB is a traditional painter's convention that works roughly with pigments; it is not the primary set for light (RGB) or for process printing (CMY). From “Additive and subtractive colour”
  • Darkening every colour with black. Black can deaden colours and shift yellows towards green; darker neighbouring hues or complements often give richer shadows. From “Hue, value and saturation”
  • Expecting perfectly saturated secondaries from any red, yellow and blue. Real pigments are impure: a warm cadmium red and ultramarine make a dull violet; a cool quinacridone magenta and ultramarine make a clean one. Paint makers' "split primary" palettes (a warm and a cool of each primary) address this. From “The colour wheel”

Painting Media and Techniques

  • Calling all paint "acrylic" or mixing media without knowing their chemistry. Incompatible layers fail: acrylic over oil, or thick oil over thin oil without more oil in the upper layer. From “What paint is: pigment and binder”
  • Expecting soft, blended transitions like oil. Tempera's character is linear and precise; smooth effects come from very fine hatching, not wet blending. From “Egg tempera”
  • Assuming every mural is fresco. Leonardo's Last Supper (c.1495–98) was painted in tempera and oil on dry plaster, an experiment that began deteriorating within his lifetime. From “Fresco”
  • Repeating that van Eyck "invented" oil paint. He perfected and popularised a technique; oil binders were already in use. From “Oil paint”

Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D

  • Confusing an original print with a reproduction. An original print is made by the artist (or under their supervision) from a matrix they created; a giclée or offset reproduction of a painting is a copy, even if signed. From “What a print is: editions and proofs”
  • Forgetting that the print is reversed. Lettering and asymmetrical compositions must be drawn backwards on the block. From “Relief printing: woodcut and linocut”
  • Handling acid without proper safety measures. Etching uses corrosive mordants; in schools it is done only with supervision, ventilation, gloves and eye protection, often with safer ferric chloride or non-toxic alternatives. From “Intaglio: etching and engraving”
  • Thinking lithography involves cutting the surface. It is planographic: the image is chemical, not physical, which is why it reproduces drawn marks so freely. From “Lithography”

Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art

  • Thinking the daguerreotype could be copied like a modern print. Each daguerreotype was a unique plate with no negative; Talbot's calotype introduced the negative. From “The camera obscura and the invention of photography”
  • Believing that a telephoto lens itself changes perspective. Perspective depends on where the camera stands; a longer lens simply crops a narrower view from that position, which makes space look compressed. From “The camera and the lens”
  • Thinking a larger f-number means a larger opening. The f-number is a ratio, so f/16 is a much smaller opening than f/2. From “Exposure: aperture, shutter speed and ISO”
  • Treating the rule of thirds as a rule that must always be followed. It is one useful guide; centred, symmetrical compositions can be just as powerful when they suit the idea. From “Composition in photography”

Graphic and Communication Design

  • Confusing kerning with tracking. Kerning adjusts one pair of letters; tracking changes spacing evenly across a group of letters. From “The anatomy of type”
  • Assuming sans serif is always more modern or more readable. Readability depends on design, size and context; many humanist sans serifs and old style serifs read well in different situations. From “Type classification”
  • Making everything bold or large to seem important. If every element shouts, nothing stands out; hierarchy needs contrast between levels. From “Hierarchy and layout”
  • Thinking a grid makes every page look the same. A good grid gives many possible layouts while keeping a consistent underlying order. From “Grids”

Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance

  • Describing prehistoric artists as crude or primitive. Works such as Chauvet show skilled observation, confident line and use of the rock's form. From “Prehistoric art”
  • Thinking Egyptian artists could not draw realistically. The composite view was a deliberate convention showing each part clearly, and works such as the Nefertiti bust and detailed animals show great observational skill. From “Ancient Egyptian art”
  • Assuming Greek statues were meant to be pure white. Traces of pigment show they were painted, and the white ideal came later. From “Ancient Greek art”
  • Dismissing Roman art as merely copying the Greeks. Romans developed new forms, including veristic portraits, narrative columns and concrete architecture. From “Roman art”

Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism

  • Thinking the Baroque was only about decoration and excess. Its dramatic effects had a purpose: to persuade, move and involve viewers in religious and political messages. From “The Baroque”
  • Assuming Dutch genre scenes are simple snapshots of life. Many contain moral lessons, jokes or symbolism, and were carefully composed in the studio. From “The Dutch Golden Age”
  • Treating Rococo as simply late Baroque. It shares some features, but Rococo is lighter, more intimate and less concerned with grand religious or political drama. From “Rococo”
  • Thinking Neoclassical art was purely about copying old statues. It used ancient models to make new statements about virtue, patriotism and revolution. From “Neoclassicism”

Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing

  • Assuming Minimalist works required no thought or skill. They involved careful decisions about proportion, material, placement and space. From “Minimalism”
  • Assuming conceptual art has no physical form. Many conceptual works include objects, texts or photographs, but these serve the idea. From “Conceptual art”
  • Treating photographs of a performance as the same as the performance. Documentation records the work but cannot fully reproduce the live experience. From “Performance art”
  • Assuming feminist art is a single style. It includes painting, photography, installation, craft, text and activism. From “Feminist art”

The Psychology of Creativity

  • Thinking creativity is only for geniuses or artists. Research treats creativity as a normal capacity found at every level, from mini-c to Big-C. From “What is creativity?”
  • Studying only the person. Treating creativity as an individual gift ignores the methods and environments that research shows make a large difference. From “The four Ps of creativity”
  • Treating a divergent thinking score as "your creativity". It measures one component, idea generation, not the knowledge, judgement and persistence that creative achievement also needs. From “Divergent thinking and its measurement”
  • Waiting for inspiration. The model shows illumination follows preparation; doing nothing without first working on the problem rarely produces insight. From “Stages of the creative process”

Art in Society and Professional Practice

  • Confusing commercial galleries with public museums. Commercial galleries sell art; public museums hold collections for the public. From “Museums and galleries”
  • Assuming exhibitions simply show art neutrally. Every choice of selection, order and text shapes meaning. From “Curating and exhibitions”
  • Assuming record auction prices reflect how most artists earn. Most artists earn modest and irregular incomes. From “The art market”
  • Assuming patronage is only historical. Public funding, sponsors and collectors are modern patrons. From “Public art and patronage”
