Free Dentistry course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

DentalGradify's free Dentistry course runs from Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy to Professionalism, Ethics and Law: 200 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for dental students and dentists.

Education, not dental advice. DentalGradify teaches dentistry for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a registered dentist, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, the GDC and current guidance (SDCEP, NICE, Delivering Better Oral Health). Nothing here diagnoses a condition, recommends treatment or a dose for a real patient, or authorises anyone to practise. If you have a dental problem, see your dentist; for urgent dental problems call NHS 111; call 999 for swelling that affects breathing or swallowing.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy

16 lessons

Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The skull and facial skeleton: The skull protects the brain and carries the teeth, the airway and the special senses.
  2. The maxilla and palate: The paired maxillae carry the upper teeth, form most of the hard palate and enclose the largest paranasal sinus.
  3. The mandible: The mandible is the largest and strongest bone of the face and the only one that moves.
  4. The temporomandibular joint: The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a synovial joint that both hinges and slides, with an articular disc dividing it into two compartments.
  5. Muscles of mastication: Four paired muscles move the mandible in chewing: masseter, temporalis and the medial and lateral pterygoids.
  6. Mandibular movements: The mandible opens, closes, protrudes, retrudes and moves from side to side, and the teeth guide it as they meet.
  7. Muscles of facial expression: The muscles of facial expression lie in the superficial fascia and move the skin of the face.
  8. The tongue and floor of the mouth: The tongue is a muscular organ of taste, speech, chewing and swallowing, with a complex nerve supply that reflects its development.
  9. The trigeminal nerve: The trigeminal nerve (V) is the great sensory nerve of the face, mouth and teeth and the motor nerve of mastication.
  10. Nerve supply of the teeth and gingivae: Each tooth and each area of gingiva has its own named nerve, and the choice of local anaesthetic technique follows directly from this map.
  11. The facial nerve and other cranial nerves in dentistry: Beyond the trigeminal, several cranial nerves matter in the dental chair: the facial nerve for expression, taste and saliva, the glossopharyngeal and vagus for the gag reflex and swallowing, and the…
  12. Blood supply and lymphatic drainage of the head and neck: The external carotid artery and its maxillary branch supply the jaws and teeth, the pterygoid venous plexus links the face to the cavernous sinus, and lymph from the mouth drains through predictable node groups.
  13. The oral cavity: anatomy and landmarks: A dentist examines the whole mouth at every visit, so the normal landmarks and the common normal variants must be known well enough that anything unusual stands out.
  14. The salivary glands: Three paired major salivary glands and hundreds of minor glands produce saliva.
  15. The pharynx and larynx: The pharynx is shared by the airway and the food passage, and the larynx guards the lower airway.
  16. Fascial spaces of the head and neck: Fascial layers divide the soft tissues of the head and neck into potential spaces, and pus from a dental infection spreads along them.

School 2: Teeth: Morphology, Development and Oral Histology

16 lessons

Teeth: Morphology, Development and Oral Histology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Dental notation systems: Every tooth must be named unambiguously in records, referrals and laboratory prescriptions.
  2. The primary and permanent dentitions: Humans have two dentitions: 20 primary teeth and 32 permanent teeth, with a mixed dentition in between.
  3. Permanent incisors and canines: The anterior teeth cut and tear food and shape the smile.
  4. Permanent premolars: The premolars bridge the incisors and molars, with two cusps (sometimes three) that both tear and grind.
  5. Permanent molars: The molars are the main grinding teeth, with broad occlusal tables and two or three roots.
  6. Primary teeth: Primary teeth are not small permanent teeth.
  7. Odontogenesis: from bud to bell: Teeth develop from an interaction between oral epithelium and neural crest-derived mesenchyme.
  8. Amelogenesis and enamel structure: Enamel is the hardest tissue in the body and the only one made by epithelial cells that are lost once the tooth erupts, so it cannot repair itself biologically.
  9. Dentinogenesis and dentine: Dentine forms the bulk of the tooth.
  10. The dental pulp: The pulp is the soft connective tissue in the centre of the tooth, enclosed in rigid dentine.
  11. Dentine sensitivity and the hydrodynamic theory: Dentine hypersensitivity is short, sharp pain from exposed dentine in response to cold, air, touch or sweet.
  12. Root formation and cementum: Once the crown is complete, Hertwig's epithelial root sheath maps out the root, and the dental follicle forms the cementum that anchors the periodontal ligament fibres.
  13. Eruption and the chronology of the dentition: Teeth move from their crypts into the mouth over months to years, in a predictable order.
  14. The periodontal ligament and alveolar bone: The periodontal ligament (PDL) suspends each tooth in its socket, absorbs chewing forces and senses pressure, while the alveolar bone that holds it remodels constantly.
  15. The oral mucosa: The oral mucosa lines the mouth and varies with the job each region does: tough and keratinised where food is chewed, flexible where the cheeks and floor must stretch, and specialised for taste on the tongue.
  16. Developmental anomalies of the teeth: Teeth can vary in number, size, shape and structure because of genetic and environmental influences during development.

School 3: Foundations of Medical Science

17 lessons

Foundations of Medical Science: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cell structure and function in health: Every disease is ultimately a disorder of cells.
  2. Membranes, transport and the resting potential: Cells keep a different ionic composition from the fluid around them, and that difference powers nerve, muscle and heart.
  3. Homeostasis and negative feedback: The body holds temperature, glucose, blood pressure and hormone levels within narrow limits by negative feedback.
  4. Body fluids, osmolality and electrolytes: Where water and salt sit in the body explains dehydration, oedema, hyponatraemia and how intravenous fluids behave.
  5. Acid–base physiology: Blood pH is held between 7.35 and 7.45 by buffers, the lungs and the kidneys.
  6. Cell injury, adaptation and death: Cells under stress first adapt, then suffer reversible injury, and finally die.
  7. Acute inflammation: Acute inflammation is the body's rapid, stereotyped response to infection and injury.
  8. Chronic inflammation and repair: When injury persists, inflammation becomes chronic and repair runs alongside destruction.
  9. Thrombosis, embolism and infarction: A thrombus is a clot in a living vessel; an embolus is anything that travels in the blood and lodges; infarction is the tissue death that follows.
  10. Neoplasia: benign and malignant tumours: A neoplasm is an abnormal mass of tissue whose growth is uncoordinated and persists after the stimulus stops.
  11. The hallmarks and genetics of cancer: Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells, built up mutation by mutation.
  12. Innate immunity: Innate immunity is the fast, inborn defence that responds within minutes to hours and recognises broad classes of danger.
  13. Adaptive immunity: Adaptive immunity recognises specific antigens with enormous diversity and remembers them.
  14. Hypersensitivity and autoimmunity: The immune system can harm the body it protects, by over-reacting to harmless antigens or attacking self.
  15. Patterns of inheritance in clinical genetics: Many diseases run in families in predictable ways.
  16. Pharmacodynamics: receptors and dose–response: Pharmacodynamics is what a drug does to the body.
  17. Pharmacokinetics: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion: Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to a drug.

School 4: Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health

18 lessons

Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What dental caries is: Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, dynamic disease in which the dental hard tissues lose and regain mineral many times a day.
  2. The ecological plaque hypothesis: Which bacteria cause caries?
  3. Demineralisation and remineralisation: Every time sugar is eaten, the pH of plaque falls and the tooth surface loses mineral; between intakes, saliva helps it regain mineral.
  4. Diet, sugars and caries: Free sugars are the essential dietary cause of caries.
  5. Caries detection and diagnosis: Detecting a caries lesion, judging its extent and deciding whether it is active are three separate steps, and all three guide treatment.
  6. Caries risk assessment: Caries risk assessment predicts who is likely to develop new lesions, so that prevention, recall intervals and radiographs can be matched to need.
  7. Root caries: Root caries affects exposed root surfaces, usually in older adults with gingival recession, and is becoming more common as people keep their teeth for longer.
  8. How fluoride works: Fluoride is the cornerstone of caries prevention.
  9. Fluoride toothpaste, varnish and other vehicles: Fluoride reaches teeth through toothpaste, varnish, mouthrinses, water, milk and salt.
  10. Fissure sealants: Pits and fissures on molars trap plaque and are where most caries occurs in children's permanent teeth.
  11. Delivering Better Oral Health: Delivering Better Oral Health (DBOH) is the evidence-based toolkit for prevention used by dental teams in England.
  12. Minimal intervention dentistry: Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) manages caries by detecting it early, controlling its causes, remineralising early lesions and, when restoration is needed, removing as little tooth as possible.
  13. Epidemiology of oral disease: Epidemiology describes how common oral diseases are, who has them and why, and it underpins planning of services and prevention.
  14. Measuring oral disease: indices: An index turns oral disease into a number that can be compared between people, places and times.
  15. Oral health inequalities: Oral disease follows a social gradient: at every step down the social ladder, oral health gets worse.
  16. Community water fluoridation: Community water fluoridation adjusts the fluoride in public water supplies to a level that helps prevent caries.
  17. Oral health promotion: Oral health promotion enables people to increase control over their oral health.
  18. NHS dental services: Most dental care in the UK is delivered through the NHS by general dental practitioners under contract, alongside community and hospital services and private care.

School 5: Periodontology

16 lessons

Periodontology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The periodontium in health: The periodontium is the set of tissues that invest and support the teeth: gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone.
  2. Gingivitis: Gingivitis is inflammation of the gingiva without loss of attachment.
  3. Pathogenesis of periodontitis: Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease in which a dysbiotic biofilm triggers a host response that destroys the tooth's attachment and bone.
  4. Risk factors for periodontitis: Not everyone with plaque develops periodontitis.
  5. Periodontitis and systemic health: Periodontitis is associated with several systemic diseases, notably diabetes, cardiovascular disease and adverse pregnancy outcomes.
  6. Periodontal examination: BPE and charting: The Basic Periodontal Examination (BPE) is the screening tool used in UK practice to decide who needs further periodontal assessment.
  7. The 2017 classification of periodontal diseases: The 2017 World Workshop, run by the American Academy of Periodontology and the European Federation of Periodontology, produced a new classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases.
  8. Staging and grading periodontitis: Staging describes how severe and complex a patient's periodontitis is; grading describes how fast it has progressed and how much risk the patient carries.
  9. Necrotising periodontal diseases and periodontal abscess: Some periodontal conditions present acutely, with pain and sometimes systemic upset.
  10. Oral hygiene instruction and behaviour change: The patient's own daily plaque removal decides the long-term success of periodontal care.
  11. Professional mechanical plaque removal: Professional mechanical plaque removal (PMPR) is the removal of supragingival and gingival plaque and calculus by the dental team, together with correction of plaque-retentive factors.
  12. Non-surgical periodontal therapy: Non-surgical periodontal therapy is the core treatment for periodontitis.
  13. Adjuncts to periodontal therapy: Adjuncts are treatments added to mechanical therapy in the hope of better results: antiseptics, local and systemic antimicrobials and host-modulating agents.
  14. Periodontal surgery: an overview: When pockets persist after good non-surgical therapy, or when tissues need reshaping or regenerating, periodontal surgery may help.
  15. Supportive periodontal care: Periodontitis is a chronic disease: once treated, it needs lifelong supportive care to keep it stable.
  16. Peri-implant health and disease: Dental implants can develop inflammatory diseases similar to gingivitis and periodontitis: peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis.

School 6: Clinical Method and Reasoning

17 lessons

Clinical Method and Reasoning: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The medical history: Most diagnoses are made, or strongly suggested, by the history.
  2. Consultation models and patient-centred care: Consultation models turn communication into teachable, observable skills.
  3. Communicating risk and shared decision-making: Patients can only decide well if risks are explained in ways they understand.
  4. Breaking bad news: How bad news is given affects patients for years.
  5. Principles of physical examination: Examination is a skill of observation, technique and interpretation, built on consent and dignity.
  6. Vital signs and the early warning score (NEWS2): Deterioration is usually preceded by hours of abnormal observations.
  7. The cardiovascular examination: The cardiovascular examination looks for the signs of heart failure, valve disease, endocarditis and vascular disease, from the hands to the heart.
  8. The respiratory examination: The respiratory examination combines inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation to locate disease in the airways, lung tissue or pleura.
  9. The abdominal examination: The abdominal examination detects organ enlargement, masses, fluid, tenderness and the signs of chronic liver disease.
  10. The neurological examination: The neurological examination localises a lesion, which narrows the causes dramatically.
  11. Clinical reasoning and diagnostic error: Diagnosis is a reasoning process that can be studied and improved.
  12. Interpreting tests: sensitivity, specificity and predictive values: No test is perfect, and the same result means different things in different patients.
  13. Interpreting a full blood count and electrolytes: The full blood count and urea and electrolytes are the most requested tests in hospital.
  14. Arterial blood gas interpretation: An arterial blood gas tells you about oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base status within minutes.
  15. The 12-lead ECG: a systematic approach: The ECG is cheap, fast and diagnostic for arrhythmias, ischaemia and electrolyte disturbance.
  16. Chest X-ray interpretation: The chest X-ray is the most common imaging test in medicine.
  17. Presenting a patient and documenting care: Clinical information only helps patients if it is communicated clearly.

School 7: Dental Materials

15 lessons

Dental Materials: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Properties of dental materials: Every restorative choice depends on how a material behaves under load, heat, moisture and time in the mouth.
  2. Biocompatibility of dental materials: Dental materials sit in contact with pulp, mucosa and bone for many years.
  3. Adhesion to enamel and dentine: Modern minimally invasive dentistry depends on bonding restorations to tooth tissue.
  4. Dental amalgam: Dental amalgam has been used for over 150 years and remains a durable posterior material, though its use is now restricted.
  5. Resin composites: Resin composites are now the most widely used direct restorative material.
  6. Glass ionomer cements: Glass ionomer cements bond chemically to tooth and release fluoride, which makes them valuable in children, root caries and high-risk patients.
  7. Resin-modified glass ionomers and compomers: Hybrid materials try to combine the benefits of glass ionomers and composites.
  8. Dental cements and liners: Cements hold indirect restorations in place, and liners and bases protect the pulp under restorations.
  9. Impression materials: Accurate impressions are the basis of crowns, bridges, dentures and orthodontic appliances.
  10. Gypsum products: Gypsum products are used to make models, casts and dies from impressions.
  11. Dental ceramics: Ceramics give the most natural-looking and durable indirect restorations.
  12. Dental alloys: Metal alloys are used for crowns, bridges, partial denture frameworks and orthodontic wires.
  13. Denture base polymers: Most complete and partial dentures have a base made of acrylic resin.
  14. Endodontic materials: Root canal treatment uses materials to disinfect, fill and seal the canal system and to protect the pulp.
  15. Dental implant materials: Dental implants replace missing teeth by anchoring into bone.

School 8: Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection

17 lessons

Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Production of X-rays: Dental radiographs depend on X-rays generated in a small tube head.
  2. Interaction of X-rays with matter: An X-ray image is a map of how much radiation passes through each part of the patient.
  3. Biological effects of ionising radiation: Ionising radiation can damage DNA and living cells.
  4. Radiation doses in dentistry: Comparing doses helps dentists explain risk and choose the right examination.
  5. IRR17 and IR(ME)R in dentistry: Two sets of UK regulations govern the use of X-rays in dental practice.
  6. Justification and optimisation: Justification and optimisation are the core principles of patient radiation protection.
  7. Radiation protection in the dental surgery: Good radiation protection keeps staff and public doses tiny and patient doses as low as reasonably practicable.
  8. Quality assurance in dental radiography: Quality assurance makes sure radiographs are diagnostic, doses are low and equipment works safely.
  9. Image receptors: film and digital: The receptor captures the X-ray pattern and turns it into an image.
  10. Intraoral techniques: bitewing and periapical: Bitewings and periapicals are the most common dental radiographs.
  11. Occlusal radiographs: Occlusal radiographs show larger areas of the jaws than periapicals.
  12. Panoramic radiography: A panoramic radiograph shows both jaws and all teeth in one image.
  13. Cone beam CT: Cone beam computed tomography gives three-dimensional images of the teeth and jaws.
  14. Selection criteria for dental radiographs: Selection criteria help dentists decide which radiograph to take and when.
  15. Radiographic interpretation: caries and periodontal bone: Reading bitewings and periapicals systematically finds caries and bone loss that are hidden clinically.
  16. Radiographic interpretation: periapical and jaw lesions: Periapical and jaw lesions range from common inflammatory changes to rare tumours.
  17. Radiographic faults and artefacts: Faulty radiographs give extra dose without benefit.

School 9: Restorative Dentistry: Operative, Endodontics and Prosthodontics

21 lessons

Restorative Dentistry: Operative, Endodontics and Prosthodontics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. History, examination and treatment planning: Restorative care starts with a thorough assessment and a logical, phased plan.
  2. Consent and shared decision-making in treatment: Valid consent is a legal and ethical requirement for every dental treatment.
  3. Occlusion in restorative dentistry: Occlusion is the way the teeth meet, and every restoration changes it a little.
  4. Principles of cavity preparation: Cavity design has moved from extension for prevention to minimal, adhesive preparation.
  5. Moisture control and rubber dam: Good isolation protects the patient and makes adhesive and endodontic work reliable.
  6. Direct posterior restorations: Most restorations in posterior teeth are placed directly in one visit.
  7. Direct anterior restorations: Anterior composites restore appearance and function with little tooth removal.
  8. Managing deep caries and the pulp: Deep carious lesions threaten the pulp, and the choice of treatment decides whether it survives.
  9. Managing tooth wear: Tooth wear is increasingly common as people keep their teeth longer.
  10. Pulp and periapical diagnosis: Accurate diagnosis of the pulp and periapical tissues decides whether a tooth needs restoration, root canal treatment or extraction.
  11. Access and canal preparation: Successful root canal treatment depends on good access and careful shaping of the canals.
  12. Irrigation and disinfection: Bacteria in the canal system are the cause of apical periodontitis, and instruments alone cannot remove them.
  13. Root canal obturation: Obturation fills the cleaned canal to prevent reinfection and seal off remaining bacteria.
  14. Endodontic outcomes and retreatment: Most root canal treatments heal, but some fail and need further care.
  15. Dental trauma in adults: Dental injuries in adults range from enamel chips to avulsion and are often linked to sport, falls and assaults.
  16. Crowns and onlays: Indirect restorations protect and restore heavily damaged teeth.
  17. Fixed bridges: Fixed bridges replace missing teeth using adjacent teeth for support.
  18. Complete dentures: Complete dentures restore function and appearance for edentulous patients.
  19. Removable partial dentures: Removable partial dentures replace some missing teeth while relying on remaining teeth and tissues for support.
  20. Dental implants: Implants are a valuable but complex way to replace missing teeth.
  21. Restoring the endodontically treated tooth: Root-filled teeth are prone to fracture and leakage, so the final restoration is as important as the root filling.

School 10: Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics

17 lessons

Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Child development and behaviour management: Successful dental care for children depends on understanding how they develop and communicate.
  2. Caries in children: Caries remains the most common chronic disease of childhood in the UK.
  3. Pulp therapy in primary teeth: Primary teeth with pulp involvement can often be kept until they exfoliate.
  4. Dental trauma in children: Dental injuries are common in children and may affect both primary teeth and their developing successors.
  5. Molar–incisor hypomineralisation: Molar–incisor hypomineralisation is a common developmental enamel defect that causes sensitivity, breakdown and caries.
  6. Safeguarding children in dentistry: Dental professionals see children regularly and are well placed to recognise abuse and neglect.
  7. Children with additional needs: Children with medical, physical, learning or sensory needs often have higher oral disease risk and need adapted care.
  8. Growth of the face and jaws: Facial growth shapes the occlusion and affects the timing of orthodontic treatment.
  9. Development of occlusion: The occlusion develops from the gum pads of infancy to the permanent dentition.
  10. Classification of malocclusion: Malocclusions are described with standard classifications so clinicians can communicate and plan care.
  11. The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need: The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need ranks malocclusions by their effect on dental health and appearance.
  12. Orthodontic assessment: A structured orthodontic assessment gathers the information needed for diagnosis and treatment planning.
  13. Removable and functional appliances: Removable appliances tip teeth and hold space, while functional appliances use muscle forces to correct jaw relationships.
  14. Fixed appliances and tooth movement: Fixed appliances give precise, three-dimensional control of tooth movement.
  15. Retention and stability: Teeth tend to move back after orthodontic treatment, so retention is part of every plan.
  16. Interceptive orthodontics: Some developing malocclusions can be prevented or reduced by simple, timely treatment.
  17. Cleft lip and palate: Cleft lip and palate is the most common craniofacial birth anomaly and needs coordinated care from birth to adulthood.

School 11: Oral Surgery, Pain and Anxiety Control

15 lessons

Oral Surgery, Pain and Anxiety Control: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Local anaesthetics: pharmacology: Local anaesthetics make most dental treatment painless.
  2. Local anaesthetic techniques: Different techniques are used to anaesthetise teeth in the upper and lower jaws.
  3. Complications of local anaesthesia: Local anaesthesia is very safe but can cause local and systemic complications.
  4. Dental anxiety: Dental anxiety is common and can stop people getting the care they need.
  5. Conscious sedation: Conscious sedation helps anxious patients receive dental treatment while remaining able to respond.
  6. General anaesthesia in dentistry: General anaesthesia is sometimes needed for dental treatment, but it carries risks and must be used carefully.
  7. Principles of exodontia: Tooth extraction is one of the most common oral surgery procedures.
  8. Surgical removal of teeth: Some teeth and roots cannot be removed with forceps alone and need a surgical approach.
  9. Third molar surgery: Wisdom teeth often become impacted.
  10. Complications of extractions: Most extractions heal without problems, but some complications need prompt recognition and management.
  11. Odontogenic infections and their management: Dental infections are common and usually localised, but can spread and become life-threatening.
  12. Antibiotic prescribing in dentistry: Dentists prescribe a significant share of antibiotics, and inappropriate use drives antimicrobial resistance.
  13. Analgesia for dental pain: Effective pain relief is essential after dental treatment and while patients wait for care.
  14. Maxillofacial trauma: an introduction: Injuries to the face and jaws may involve teeth, bones and the airway.
  15. Referral pathways and urgent dental care: Dentists must know when and how to refer patients, and patients need to know where to get urgent care.

School 12: Professionalism, Ethics and Law

15 lessons

Professionalism, Ethics and Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The GDC and its Standards for the Dental Team: The General Dental Council regulates the dental team in the UK.
  2. The dental team and scope of practice: The dental team includes several registered professions, each with defined roles.
  3. Communication and consent: Valid consent is a legal and ethical requirement before any dental treatment.
  4. Mental capacity and consent: Some patients may lack the capacity to make decisions about their dental care.
  5. Raising concerns and the duty of candour: Dental professionals must speak up when patients are at risk and be honest when things go wrong.
  6. Fitness to practise: When concerns are raised about a dental professional, the GDC may investigate their fitness to practise.
  7. Record keeping and data protection: Good records support patient care and protect both patients and professionals.
  8. Complaints: Handling complaints well can resolve problems and improve care.
  9. Clinical governance and audit: Clinical governance is the framework through which dental services maintain and improve quality.
  10. Evidence-based dentistry: Evidence-based dentistry combines research evidence with clinical expertise and patients' values.
  11. Safeguarding adults: Dental teams may be the first to notice signs that an adult is being abused or neglected.
  12. Equality, diversity and inclusion: Dental teams must treat everyone fairly and make care accessible.
  13. Health and safety in dental practice: Dental practices contain many hazards, from sharps to radiation.
  14. Continuing professional development: Dental professionals must keep their knowledge and skills up to date.
  15. Ethics in dental practice: Dental professionals face ethical dilemmas in everyday practice.
