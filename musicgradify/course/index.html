Music: a free course on MusicGradify

MusicGradify's free Music course runs from Notation and Rhythm to Composition and Analysis: 201 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for musicians and music students.

Study material, not an exam board or a teacher. MusicGradify teaches music theory, history and musicianship for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a music lecturer or examiner, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, the current ABRSM syllabus or your exam board's specification. It isn't an ABRSM, Trinity or exam board product, doesn't award grades or qualifications, and can't replace lessons with a qualified instrumental, vocal or theory teacher.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Notation and Rhythm

18 lessons

Notation and Rhythm: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Pitch, the stave and clefs: Western notation shows pitch by position on a five-line stave, and a clef fixes which line stands for which note.
  2. Note names, ledger lines and octave registers: Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in every octave.
  3. Accidentals: sharps, flats and naturals: An accidental raises or lowers a note by a semitone or two.
  4. Tones, semitones and enharmonic equivalents: The semitone is the smallest step in Western tonal music, and two semitones make a tone.
  5. The alto and tenor clefs: The C clefs place middle C on a chosen line so that instruments with a middle range can avoid ledger lines.
  6. Note values and rests: Note values show how long each note lasts relative to the beat, and every note value has a matching rest for silence.
  7. Dotted notes and ties: A dot after a note adds half its value, and a tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one sound.
  8. Pulse, beat and metre: Most music has a regular pulse, and metre organises those beats into repeating patterns of strong and weak.
  9. Simple time signatures: A time signature tells the performer how many beats are in each bar and what kind of note is the beat.
  10. Compound time signatures: In compound time each beat is a dotted note that divides into three.
  11. Grouping notes and rests: Beams and rests are grouped so that the beats of the bar are always visible.
  12. Triplets, duplets and irregular divisions: Irregular divisions borrow a grouping from another metre: a triplet fits three notes into the time of two, a duplet two into the time of three.
  13. Syncopation, anacrusis and cross-rhythm: Rhythm becomes interesting when it pulls against the metre.
  14. Irregular and changing metres: Not all music fits two, three or four equal beats.
  15. Tempo and metronome marks: Tempo is the speed of the beat, shown by words, usually Italian, and by metronome marks.
  16. Dynamics and articulation: Dynamics show how loud music is, and articulation shows how each note is begun, held and connected.
  17. Ornaments: Ornaments are signs that tell a performer to decorate a note with extra notes.
  18. Repeats, signs and score navigation: Scores save space with repeat signs and directions that send the performer back or forward.

School 2: Scales, Keys and Intervals

18 lessons

Scales, Keys and Intervals: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The major scale: The major scale is the basis of most Western tonal music.
  2. Key signatures and the circle of fifths: A key signature gathers a key's sharps or flats at the start of each stave.
  3. Scale degrees and their names: Each note of a scale has a number and a technical name that describe its function in the key.
  4. Natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales: Minor scales come in three forms that share a lower half but differ in their sixth and seventh degrees.
  5. Relative and tonic minor keys: Every major key has a relative minor that shares its key signature, and a tonic minor that shares its tonic.
  6. Minor key signatures: A minor key uses the key signature of its relative major.
  7. Finding the key of a melody: Working out the key of a melody is a core skill in analysis and aural work.
  8. The chromatic scale: The chromatic scale moves entirely by semitones through all twelve pitches in the octave.
  9. Pentatonic and blues scales: Pentatonic scales have five notes and are among the most widespread scales in the world's music.
  10. Whole-tone and octatonic scales: Whole-tone and octatonic scales are symmetrical: they divide the octave into equal or alternating steps.
  11. The modes: The modes are seven-note scales built on each degree of the major scale.
  12. Interval number and quality: An interval is the distance between two pitches.
  13. Major, minor and perfect intervals: Every interval from the tonic up to a note of the major scale is either major or perfect.
  14. Augmented and diminished intervals: Augmented and diminished intervals are a semitone larger or smaller than major, minor or perfect intervals.
  15. Compound intervals: A compound interval is larger than an octave.
  16. Inverting intervals: Inverting an interval means moving the lower note up an octave, or the upper note down.
  17. Consonance and dissonance: Consonance and dissonance describe how stable or tense an interval or chord sounds.
  18. Transposition: Transposing means writing or playing music at a different pitch while keeping its intervals.

School 3: Chords and Harmony

19 lessons

Chords and Harmony: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Triads: root, third and fifth: A triad is a three-note chord built from a root, a third above it and a fifth above it.
  2. Major, minor, diminished and augmented triads: Triads come in four qualities, depending on the size of their thirds and fifth.
  3. Inversions and figured bass symbols: A triad can be arranged with its root, third or fifth in the bass.
  4. Diatonic triads and Roman numerals: Each degree of a scale can carry a triad built from the notes of the key.
  5. Chord symbols in popular music: Popular and jazz music uses chord symbols written above the melody instead of Roman numerals.
  6. The dominant seventh: The dominant seventh chord, V7, adds a minor seventh to the dominant triad and is the strongest chord leading to the tonic.
  7. Types of seventh chord: Adding a seventh to a triad creates a seventh chord.
  8. Diatonic seventh chords: Adding a seventh to each diatonic triad gives the diatonic seventh chords of a key.
  9. Tonic, predominant and dominant function: Chords in a key fall into functional groups: tonic chords give rest, predominant chords prepare the dominant, and dominant chords drive back to the tonic.
  10. Progressions by fifth and the circle of fifths sequence: Root movement by falling fifth is the strongest and most common progression in tonal music.
  11. Harmonic rhythm: Harmonic rhythm is the rate at which chords change.
  12. Harmonising a melody: Harmonising a melody means choosing chords that fit its notes and support its phrases.
  13. Secondary dominants: A secondary dominant is the dominant of a chord other than the tonic, borrowed from another key.
  14. Modulation to related keys: Modulation is a change of key confirmed by a cadence.
  15. Modal mixture: Modal mixture (borrowed chords) means using chords from the parallel minor in a major key, or vice versa.
  16. The Neapolitan sixth: The Neapolitan sixth is a major chord built on the flattened second degree, usually in first inversion, used as a dramatic predominant.
  17. Augmented sixth chords: Augmented sixth chords are chromatic predominants containing an augmented sixth that expands outwards to an octave on the dominant.
  18. The diminished seventh chord: The diminished seventh is a four-note chord of stacked minor thirds, built on the leading note in minor keys.
  19. Extended chords: ninths, elevenths and thirteenths: Extended chords add notes beyond the seventh by continuing to stack thirds.

School 4: Voice Leading and Cadences

17 lessons

Voice Leading and Cadences: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. SATB layout and vocal ranges: Four-part harmony is written for soprano, alto, tenor and bass voices.
  2. Doubling and spacing: With four voices and three-note triads, one note must be doubled.
  3. Parallel fifths and octaves: Consecutive perfect fifths and octaves are the most famous prohibitions in harmony.
  4. Principles of voice leading: Good voice leading makes each part a singable melody while the parts together form clear harmony.
  5. Connecting root-position chords: Root-position chords can be joined in three basic ways depending on the interval between their roots.
  6. Using first-inversion chords: First-inversion chords give a smoother bass line and lighter harmonic weight.
  7. The cadential six-four: The cadential six-four is a tonic chord in second inversion that decorates the dominant at a cadence.
  8. The perfect cadence: A cadence is a harmonic punctuation mark at the end of a phrase.
  9. The imperfect cadence: An imperfect cadence ends on the dominant, creating a pause like a comma or question mark.
  10. The plagal cadence: The plagal cadence moves from IV to I.
  11. The interrupted cadence: The interrupted cadence sets up a perfect cadence then swerves to another chord, usually vi.
  12. Cadences in minor keys and the tierce de Picardie: Minor keys need extra care at cadences because of the raised leading note.
  13. Passing notes and auxiliary notes: Non-chord notes add movement and colour to harmony.
  14. Suspensions: A suspension holds a note over a change of harmony, creating a dissonance that then resolves down by step.
  15. Appoggiaturas, anticipations and pedal points: Beyond passing notes and suspensions, composers use appoggiaturas, anticipations and pedal points for expressive and structural effect.
  16. Realising a figured bass: Figured bass is a Baroque shorthand in which numbers under a bass line show the harmony.
  17. Harmonising a chorale melody: Harmonising a chorale melody in the style of Bach brings together everything in this school.

School 5: Melody and Form

16 lessons

Melody and Form: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Melodic contour and range: A melody's shape and span give it character before harmony or rhythm are considered.
  2. Phrases and periods: Melodies are organised into phrases, which combine into larger units such as periods and sentences.
  3. Motif and sequence: Short musical ideas, or motifs, give music unity, and sequences repeat them at different pitches.
  4. Melodic development: Composers transform musical ideas to sustain interest while maintaining unity.
  5. Writing a melody: Writing a good melody combines craft and imagination.
  6. Binary form: Binary form divides a piece into two sections, each usually repeated.
  7. Ternary form: Ternary form, ABA, presents a section, a contrast and a return.
  8. Strophic and through-composed forms: Songs can repeat the same music for each verse or set the text with continuously new music.
  9. Rondo form: Rondo form alternates a recurring main theme with contrasting episodes.
  10. Theme and variations: In theme and variations, a theme is stated and then transformed in a series of variations.
  11. Minuet and trio, scherzo and trio: The minuet and trio, and later the scherzo and trio, provided the dance-like third movement of many Classical works.
  12. Ground bass, ostinato and passacaglia: Some music is built over a repeating bass or pattern.
  13. Sonata form: Sonata form is the most important instrumental form of the Classical and Romantic periods.
  14. The concerto: The concerto sets a soloist or group of soloists against an orchestra.
  15. The symphony: The symphony became the most prestigious orchestral genre.
  16. Cyclic form and leitmotif: Nineteenth-century composers sought unity across large works by bringing back themes.

School 6: Ear Training and Musicianship

15 lessons

Ear Training and Musicianship: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Hearing pulse and metre: Every aural skill rests on feeling the beat.
  2. Rhythmic dictation: Rhythmic dictation means hearing a rhythm and writing it down.
  3. Sight-singing and pitch matching: Sight-singing turns notation into sound without an instrument.
  4. Solfège and movable do: Solfège gives each scale degree a syllable, so singers hear notes by their function in the key.
  5. Recognising intervals by ear: Interval recognition is the core of hearing melody and harmony.
  6. Melodic dictation: Melodic dictation combines rhythm, pitch and memory.
  7. Hearing chord quality: Chords have characteristic colours.
  8. Hearing cadences: Cadences mark the ends of phrases, and graded aural tests often ask you to name them.
  9. Hearing bass lines and inversions: The bass line carries the harmony.
  10. Harmonic dictation: Harmonic dictation means writing down the chords of a progression.
  11. Hearing instruments and texture: Listening questions often ask what instruments are playing and how the parts fit together.
  12. Recognising style and period by ear: Aural tests and listening papers often ask when a piece was written.
  13. Hearing modulation: Modulation is a change of key.
  14. Sight-reading strategies: Sight-reading on an instrument is a skill that improves with method and regular practice.
  15. Aural tests in graded exams: Graded music exams from boards such as ABRSM and Trinity include aural tests that draw on all the skills in this school.

School 7: Counterpoint and Texture

15 lessons

Counterpoint and Texture: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Monophony, homophony and polyphony: Texture describes how the musical lines in a piece relate to each other.
  2. Heterophony and other textures: Not all music fits the three standard textures.
  3. Describing texture: Exam questions often ask you to describe texture in detail.
  4. Principles of species counterpoint: Species counterpoint is a step-by-step method for learning to write independent lines that sound well together.
  5. First species: First species places one note against each note of the cantus firmus.
  6. Second species: Second species places two notes against each note of the cantus firmus.
  7. Third species: Third species places four notes against each note of the cantus firmus.
  8. Fourth species: Fourth species uses syncopation to create suspensions, the most expressive dissonance of Renaissance and Baroque music.
  9. Fifth species: Fifth species, or florid counterpoint, combines the rhythms of the earlier species into a free, varied line.
  10. Imitation and canon: Imitation is the repetition of a melodic idea in another voice.
  11. The two-part invention: Bach's fifteen two-part Inventions are miniature masterpieces of counterpoint written for teaching.
  12. Fugue: A fugue is a contrapuntal composition built on a subject that enters in each voice in turn.
  13. Invertible counterpoint: Invertible counterpoint lets two lines swap places, upper becoming lower, and still sound correct.
  14. Augmentation, diminution, inversion and retrograde: Composers transform themes by changing their rhythm, direction or order.
  15. The chorale prelude: The chorale prelude is an organ piece based on a Lutheran hymn tune.

School 8: Sound, Instruments and Orchestration

21 lessons

Sound, Instruments and Orchestration: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Sound waves: frequency, amplitude and pitch: Musical sound is a pressure wave travelling through the air.
  2. The harmonic series and timbre: A single musical note contains many frequencies sounding together.
  3. Standing waves in strings and pipes: Strings and air columns produce musical notes by forming standing waves.
  4. Beats, tuning and temperament: When two nearly matching notes sound together they produce beats, which musicians use to tune.
  5. Loudness, decibels and hearing: Our ears respond to an enormous range of sound intensities, so loudness is measured on a logarithmic scale.
  6. Room acoustics: The room is part of the instrument: its size, shape and surfaces shape how music sounds.
  7. The string family: Bowed strings form the core of the orchestra.
  8. The woodwind family: Woodwind instruments make sound by setting an air column vibrating with an edge or a reed.
  9. The brass family: Brass players make sound by buzzing their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece.
  10. Percussion: Percussion instruments are struck, shaken or scraped and range from tuned timpani to unpitched cymbals.
  11. Keyboard instruments: Keyboard instruments let one player control many notes at once.
  12. The voice: The voice is the oldest instrument and the only one carried inside the body.
  13. Electronic instruments and music technology: Electronic instruments generate and shape sound with circuits and software.
  14. Transposing instruments: For some instruments, the written note is not the note that sounds.
  15. Ranges and registers: Every instrument has a range of possible notes and distinct registers with different colours and strengths.
  16. The orchestra and its layout: The symphony orchestra grew from a small Baroque ensemble into the large Romantic orchestra.
  17. Doubling and balance: Doubling a line on more than one instrument changes its weight, colour and clarity.
  18. Writing for strings: The string section is the most flexible part of the orchestra.
  19. Orchestral colour: Orchestral colour is the art of combining instruments to create distinctive timbres.
  20. Reading a full score: A full score shows every instrument's part, arranged in a standard order.
  21. Chamber ensembles: Chamber music is played by small groups with one player to a part and usually no conductor.

School 9: Music History: Medieval to Classical

15 lessons

Music History: Medieval to Classical: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Plainchant: Plainchant is the unaccompanied, single-line sung music of the medieval Latin church, and the foundation of Western music.
  2. Organum and early polyphony: Polyphony, music in several independent parts, grew from adding voices to chant.
  3. Ars nova and medieval song: In fourteenth-century France a "new art" brought new rhythms and forms to music.
  4. Renaissance sacred polyphony: Renaissance church music developed a smooth, balanced polyphony built on imitation between voices.
  5. The madrigal: The madrigal was the leading secular vocal form of the sixteenth century, setting poetry with vivid expression.
  6. Renaissance instrumental music: Instrumental music grew in importance during the Renaissance, from dance music to keyboard variations and the brass music of Venice.
  7. Baroque style and the basso continuo: Baroque music, from about 1600 to 1750, is built on a bass line with chords filled in above it.
  8. Baroque opera and oratorio: Opera was born around 1600 and quickly spread across Europe, while oratorio offered sacred drama without staging.
  9. The concerto grosso and the suite: The concerto and the suite were the leading instrumental forms of the Baroque.
  10. Bach and Handel: Johann Sebastian Bach and George Frideric Handel, both born in Germany in 1685, are the towering figures of the late Baroque.
  11. The Classical style: The Classical period, from about 1750 to 1820, valued clarity, balance and contrast.
  12. Haydn, Mozart and the Classical symphony: The symphony became the central orchestral genre of the Classical period.
  13. The string quartet: The string quartet became the most prestigious chamber genre of the Classical period.
  14. Classical opera: Classical opera reformed the conventions of Baroque opera and developed comic opera into a vehicle for drama and character.
  15. Beethoven: Ludwig van Beethoven transformed the Classical style and opened the way to Romanticism.

School 10: Music History: Romantic to the Present

14 lessons

Music History: Romantic to the Present: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Romanticism in music: Romanticism dominated music for most of the nineteenth century.
  2. The Lied and art song: The Lied, a German art song for voice and piano, was one of the great Romantic genres.
  3. Programme music and the symphonic poem: Programme music tells a story or depicts a scene without words.
  4. Romantic piano music: The piano was the central instrument of Romantic music-making.
  5. Romantic opera: Verdi and Wagner: Verdi and Wagner, both born in 1813, dominated nineteenth-century opera in very different ways.
  6. Nationalism in music: In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many composers sought to express national identity through music.
  7. Impressionism: Impressionism in music, led by Claude Debussy, moved away from German Romantic rhetoric towards colour, atmosphere and suggestion.
  8. Expressionism and atonality: In the early twentieth century, some composers abandoned tonality altogether.
  9. Serialism: Serialism gave composers a systematic way to organise atonal music.
  10. Neoclassicism: Between the world wars, many composers turned back to the forms and clarity of earlier music.
  11. Minimalism: Minimalism emerged in the United States in the 1960s as a radical simplification of musical language.
  12. Electronic and experimental music: Technology and new ideas about sound transformed music in the twentieth century.
  13. Film music: Film music shapes how audiences feel and understand what they see.
  14. Music since 1975: Since the 1970s, classical composition has become highly diverse, with no single dominant style.

School 11: Popular Music, Jazz and World Music

18 lessons

Popular Music, Jazz and World Music: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The blues: The blues grew out of African American communities in the southern United States around the end of the nineteenth century and became the foundation of jazz, rhythm and blues and rock.
  2. The origins of jazz: Jazz developed in New Orleans in the first two decades of the twentieth century from a mix of blues, ragtime, brass band music and Caribbean and Creole traditions.
  3. Swing and the big band: In the 1930s and early 1940s jazz became America's popular dance music through the big bands of the swing era.
  4. Bebop and modern jazz: In the 1940s bebop turned jazz from dance music into a demanding art music for listening, and later styles from cool jazz to free jazz grew from it.
  5. Jazz harmony and the ii–V–I: The ii–V–I progression is the most important chord pattern in jazz.
  6. Improvisation: Improvisation is the creation of music in the moment, and it is central to jazz but also found in many other traditions.
  7. Rock and roll and the birth of pop: Rock and roll emerged in the United States in the mid-1950s from rhythm and blues, country music and gospel, and created a new teenage popular music.
  8. Popular song structure: Popular songs are built from a small set of sections arranged in recognisable forms.
  9. Rock, soul and funk: In the 1960s and 1970s rock, soul and funk developed from rock and roll and rhythm and blues in different directions.
  10. Hip hop and electronic dance music: Hip hop and electronic dance music both grew from DJ culture in the 1970s and 1980s and changed how popular music is made.
  11. Musical theatre: Musical theatre combines songs, spoken dialogue and dance to tell a story on stage.
  12. Indian classical music: raga and tala: Indian classical music is built on raga, a framework for melody, and tala, a cycle of beats.
  13. West African drumming: West African drumming traditions build music from layered, interlocking rhythmic parts guided by a timeline and led by a master drummer.
  14. Indonesian gamelan: Gamelan is the ensemble music of Java and Bali, played mainly on bronze gongs and metallophones.
  15. Latin American music: Latin American music combines Indigenous, European and especially African traditions in a wide range of styles.
  16. Arabic music and maqam: Arabic classical music is based on the maqam, a modal system that includes intervals not found in Western equal temperament.
  17. Folk music of the British Isles: The folk music of England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland was passed on orally and has been collected, revived and reinvented.
  18. Ethnomusicology: Ethnomusicology is the study of music in its cultural context, across all the world's traditions, including Western ones.

School 12: Composition and Analysis

15 lessons

Composition and Analysis: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Approaches to analysis: Musical analysis explains how a piece works by examining its materials and how they relate.
  2. Harmonic analysis: Harmonic analysis identifies the chords, keys and cadences of a passage and explains how they create direction.
  3. Analysing melody and motif: Melodies are built from motifs grouped into phrases, and analysis can show how a whole movement grows from a few ideas.
  4. Analysing form: Formal analysis maps how a piece is divided into sections and how those sections relate in theme, key and function.
  5. Schenkerian analysis: Schenkerian analysis, developed by Heinrich Schenker, explains tonal music as the elaboration of a simple underlying structure.
  6. Post-tonal analysis and pitch-class sets: Atonal and serial music needs different analytical tools from tonal music.
  7. Writing about music: Writing about music turns listening and analysis into clear argument supported by evidence.
  8. Composing to a brief: Composing to a brief means writing music that meets a set of requirements, as in examinations and professional commissions.
  9. Developing musical ideas: A composition grows by developing a few ideas rather than piling up new ones.
  10. Writing for voices: Writing for voices requires attention to range, breath and especially the setting of words.
  11. Writing for instruments: Effective instrumental writing depends on knowing each instrument's range, registers, techniques and practical limits.
  12. Composing in a style: Composing in a style, or pastiche, means writing music that follows the conventions of a composer, period or genre.
  13. Contemporary compositional techniques: Since the early twentieth century composers have developed many new ways of organising sound beyond tonality.
  14. Composing with music technology: Music technology lets composers create, edit and produce music in a digital audio workstation.
  15. Presenting a score: A clear score lets performers play a composition as intended and examiners understand it.
