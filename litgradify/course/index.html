Free English Literature course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

LitGradify's free English Literature course runs from Foundations of Literary Study to Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing: 166 lessons in 10 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for literature and English students.

Study material, not a qualification or an exam board resource. LitGradify teaches English literature for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against your set texts, your course materials and your exam board's current specification. It is not endorsed by AQA, OCR or Pearson Edexcel, it does not replace reading the texts themselves, and its model answers are illustrations, not mark schemes. Quotations are short and taken only from public-domain texts; modern works are described rather than quoted.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Literary Study

18 lessons

Foundations of Literary Study: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is literature?: Literature is hard to define, but every definition tries to say what makes some writing worth reading closely and repeatedly.
  2. Genre and form: Genre is the kind of text something is; form is the shape it takes.
  3. Author, narrator and reader: Every text involves someone who wrote it, a voice that speaks it and someone who reads it.
  4. Text and context: Context is the set of circumstances in which a text was written and has been read.
  5. The literary canon: The canon is the set of works treated as central to a literature, taught in schools and universities and set in exams.
  6. Close reading: a method: Close reading is the careful, detailed analysis of a short passage to show how its language and form produce meaning.
  7. Diction and register: Diction is a writer's choice of words; register is the level of formality and the kind of language those choices create.
  8. Imagery: Imagery is language that appeals to the senses.
  9. Metaphor, simile and personification: Figures of speech say one thing in terms of another.
  10. Symbol and motif: A symbol is a concrete thing that stands for something beyond itself; a motif is an element that recurs through a text.
  11. Tone and mood: Tone is the attitude a text's voice takes towards its subject and reader; mood is the atmosphere and feeling the text creates.
  12. Syntax and sentence structure: Syntax is the arrangement of words into sentences.
  13. The sound of language: Writers use the sounds of words as well as their meanings.
  14. Ambiguity and multiple meanings: Literary language often means more than one thing at once.
  15. Irony: Irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what happens.
  16. Intertextuality and allusion: No text is written in isolation: works echo, quote, rewrite and argue with earlier ones.
  17. Using quotations as evidence: Quotations are the evidence of a literature essay.
  18. From annotation to argument: Annotation is where analysis begins, but an essay needs an argument.

School 2: Poetry and Poetics

20 lessons

Poetry and Poetics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What makes a poem?: Poetry is language organised by the line as well as the sentence, using sound, rhythm and compression to make meaning.
  2. The speaker and the poetic voice: Every poem has a speaker, the voice that says the words, who isn't necessarily the poet.
  3. Imagery in poetry: Imagery is language that appeals to the senses.
  4. Rhyme and sound patterning: Poets organise sound as carefully as meaning.
  5. Stress and the metrical foot: English metre is built from patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables.
  6. Iambic pentameter: Iambic pentameter, five iambs per line, has been the dominant metre of English poetry since Chaucer.
  7. Other metres and metrical variation: English poetry uses many metres besides iambic pentameter.
  8. Free verse: Free verse has no regular metre or rhyme scheme, but it isn't formless: it builds rhythm through lines, repetition and syntax.
  9. The sonnet: The sonnet is a fourteen-line poem with a strict rhyme scheme and a turn in thought.
  10. The ballad: The ballad is a narrative poem or song, often in quatrains, rooted in oral tradition.
  11. The ode: The ode is a lyric poem of praise or meditation in an elevated style, usually addressed to someone or something.
  12. The elegy: An elegy is a poem of mourning.
  13. The dramatic monologue: A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by a character to a silent listener at a dramatic moment, revealing more than the speaker intends.
  14. The lyric: The lyric is a short poem expressing a single speaker's thoughts or feelings.
  15. Blank verse: Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter, the metre of Shakespeare's plays, Milton's Paradise Lost and Wordsworth's Prelude.
  16. Narrative and epic poetry: Narrative poems tell stories; epics tell them on a heroic scale.
  17. Stanza forms: A stanza is a group of lines forming a unit in a poem.
  18. Enjambment, caesura and the line: The relationship between the line and the sentence is one of poetry's main resources.
  19. Analysing a poem: a method: Close reading a poem means looking carefully at how its language, form and structure create meaning.
  20. Comparing poems: Comparing poems sharpens understanding of each by showing what they share and how they differ.

School 3: Drama and Shakespeare

18 lessons

Drama and Shakespeare: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Drama as performance: A play is written to be performed, so the text is only part of the work.
  2. Tragedy: Tragedy shows the fall of a significant figure through a combination of character and circumstance.
  3. Comedy: Comedy aims to amuse and usually ends happily, often in marriage or reconciliation.
  4. Dramatic structure: Plays are shaped by structure: how action is divided and how tension builds and resolves.
  5. Character and dialogue in drama: In drama, character is revealed mainly through dialogue and action.
  6. Stagecraft and stage directions: Stagecraft is everything that turns a script into a performance: set, lighting, sound, costume, props and movement.
  7. Shakespeare's theatre: Shakespeare wrote for particular theatres, companies and audiences.
  8. Shakespeare's language: Shakespeare moves between blank verse, rhyme and prose for dramatic effect.
  9. Soliloquy and aside: Soliloquies and asides let characters share their thoughts with the audience.
  10. Shakespearean tragedy: Shakespeare's great tragedies, including Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth, centre on a hero whose choices and flaws lead to catastrophe.
  11. Shakespearean comedy: Shakespeare's comedies blend romance, wit, disguise and festivity.
  12. The history plays and the late romances: Beyond tragedy and comedy, Shakespeare wrote English history plays and, late in his career, romances.
  13. Marlowe and Shakespeare's contemporaries: Shakespeare worked alongside other major playwrights.
  14. Restoration and eighteenth-century drama: After the theatres reopened in 1660, English drama changed: women appeared on stage, indoor theatres dominated, and the witty comedy of manners flourished.
  15. Wilde, Shaw and the Victorian stage: The late Victorian and Edwardian stage saw a revival of serious and witty drama.
  16. Ibsen, Chekhov and modern realism: In the late nineteenth century, Ibsen and Chekhov transformed drama with realism: ordinary settings, everyday speech and serious social and psychological themes.
  17. Modernist and absurdist drama: Twentieth-century dramatists broke with realism.
  18. Post-war and contemporary British drama: British drama since 1945 has included the "angry young men", Pinter's comedy of menace, political and feminist theatre, and the in-yer-face drama of the 1990s.

School 4: Prose Fiction and the Novel

17 lessons

Prose Fiction and the Novel: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Story, plot and narrative: Every novel tells a sequence of events, but it can arrange and present them in many ways.
  2. Narrators and point of view: Who tells a story, and from where, shapes everything the reader learns.
  3. Free indirect discourse: Free indirect discourse blends a narrator's voice with a character's thoughts.
  4. Characterisation: Novelists create characters through description, action, speech and thought.
  5. Setting and atmosphere: Setting is where and when a story takes place, but in good fiction it also shapes mood, theme and character.
  6. Time and structure in narrative: Novels manipulate time by reordering events, speeding up and slowing down, and framing stories within stories.
  7. Dialogue in fiction: Dialogue brings characters to life, advances plot and reveals relationships.
  8. The rise of the novel: The novel became a dominant literary form in eighteenth-century Britain.
  9. The realist novel: The realist novel aimed to represent ordinary life faithfully, especially in nineteenth-century Britain and France.
  10. The Gothic novel: The Gothic novel explores fear, the supernatural and transgression in haunted settings.
  11. The Bildungsroman: The Bildungsroman, or novel of formation, follows a young person's growth to maturity.
  12. The modernist novel: Modernist novelists of the early twentieth century broke with realist conventions to capture consciousness, time and fragmentation.
  13. The postmodern novel: Postmodern novels question the conventions of fiction and the grand narratives of history and truth.
  14. The short story: The short story is a compact form that achieves its effects through compression, suggestion and a single focus.
  15. The novella: The novella sits between the short story and the novel.
  16. Genre fiction: Genre fiction, such as detective, science fiction, romance and horror, follows recognisable conventions that readers expect.
  17. Reading a novel: a method: Studying a novel well means reading actively, noting key features and building an argument.

School 5: Medieval to Eighteenth-Century Literature

13 lessons

Medieval to Eighteenth-Century Literature: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Old English literature: Old English literature, written between about 650 and 1100, is the earliest literature in English.
  2. Chaucer and Middle English: Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–1400) helped establish English as a literary language.
  3. Medieval romance and drama: Romance and drama were among the most popular medieval forms.
  4. The English Renaissance: The English Renaissance, roughly 1500 to 1660, brought new learning, printing and religious change that transformed literature.
  5. Renaissance poetry: Wyatt, Sidney and Spenser: Sixteenth-century poets adapted European forms to create a new English poetry.
  6. Metaphysical poetry: The metaphysical poets used wit, argument and startling comparisons to write about love and faith.
  7. Milton and Paradise Lost: John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) is the great English epic.
  8. The English Bible and Renaissance prose: English prose was transformed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries by Bible translation, the Prayer Book and new forms such as the essay.
  9. Restoration literature: The restoration of Charles II in 1660 reopened the theatres and changed literary culture.
  10. Augustan satire: Dryden, Pope and Swift: Writers of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries used satire to attack folly and vice.
  11. The eighteenth-century novel: The eighteenth century saw rapid experiment with the novel.
  12. Sensibility and the Gothic beginnings: In the mid-eighteenth century, writers turned from reason towards feeling, melancholy and the sublime.
  13. Women writers of the long eighteenth century: Women wrote across every genre between the Restoration and the 1790s, though many were later forgotten.

School 6: Romantic to Contemporary Literature

14 lessons

Romantic to Contemporary Literature: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Romanticism: an overview: Romanticism was a movement in literature, art and thought from the late eighteenth to the mid-nineteenth century.
  2. Wordsworth, Coleridge and Lyrical Ballads: Lyrical Ballads (1798), by William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, is often seen as the start of English Romantic poetry.
  3. The second-generation Romantics: Byron, Shelley and Keats, the younger Romantics, wrote in the decades after Waterloo.
  4. Women writers of the Romantic period: Women were central to Romantic-era literature, as poets, novelists and thinkers, though many were later neglected.
  5. The Victorian age: The Victorian age, from 1837 to 1901, was a time of rapid industrial, scientific and social change.
  6. Victorian poetry: Victorian poets experimented with forms and voices while confronting faith, love and society.
  7. The Victorian novel: The novel became the dominant literary form in Victorian Britain.
  8. Late Victorian literature and the fin de siècle: The last decades of the nineteenth century brought pessimism, aestheticism and new anxieties about gender, science and empire.
  9. Modernism: Modernism transformed literature in the early twentieth century through experiment with form and language.
  10. Literature of the First World War: The First World War produced some of the most powerful writing in English.
  11. Literature between the wars: The years between 1918 and 1939 saw experiment, social criticism and political engagement.
  12. Post-war literature: After 1945, British literature responded to the Second World War, the Cold War, the welfare state, the end of empire and social change.
  13. Postcolonial literature: Postcolonial literature responds to colonialism and its aftermath.
  14. Contemporary literature: Contemporary literature, from the late twentieth century to today, is diverse in voices, forms and themes.

School 7: Literary Theory and Criticism

16 lessons

Literary Theory and Criticism: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is literary theory?: Literary theory asks what literature is, how meaning is made and what reading is for.
  2. Liberal humanism and practical criticism: Before "theory", most English criticism rested on liberal humanist assumptions.
  3. New Criticism: New Criticism dominated American criticism from the 1940s to the 1960s.
  4. Formalism and structuralism: Russian Formalists and later structuralists asked what makes literature literary and what structures underlie texts.
  5. Poststructuralism and deconstruction: Poststructuralism questioned whether meaning can ever be fixed.
  6. Reader-response theory: Reader-response theories argue that meaning arises in the act of reading.
  7. Psychoanalytic criticism: Psychoanalytic criticism reads literature in the light of the unconscious.
  8. Narratology: Narratology is the systematic study of how stories are told.
  9. Marxist criticism: Marxist criticism reads literature in relation to class, economics and ideology.
  10. Feminist criticism: Feminist criticism examines how literature represents women, how gender shapes writing and reading, and how women writers have been treated.
  11. Gender and queer theory: Gender and queer theory question fixed ideas of gender and sexuality.
  12. Postcolonial criticism: Postcolonial criticism examines literature in relation to empire, colonialism and their aftermath.
  13. New historicism and cultural materialism: New historicism and cultural materialism read literary texts alongside other texts of their time to explore power and ideology.
  14. Ecocriticism: Ecocriticism studies the relationship between literature and the physical environment.
  15. Critical views and debate: Advanced literary study requires using critics' views, comparing interpretations and building an argument.
  16. Literary value and evaluation: What makes a work of literature good, and who decides?

School 8: Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose

18 lessons

Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Macbeth: context: Macbeth was written around 1606 for a new king who was fascinated by witchcraft and who traced his ancestry to Banquo.
  2. Macbeth: ambition and power: Macbeth traces how ambition, once unleashed, drives a brave soldier to murder and tyranny.
  3. Macbeth: the supernatural: The witches, the dagger, Banquo's ghost and the apparitions create an atmosphere of evil and uncertainty.
  4. Macbeth: gender and Lady Macbeth: Macbeth repeatedly connects violence with masculinity and questions what it means to be a man or a woman.
  5. Macbeth: language and imagery: Shakespeare builds the meaning of Macbeth through recurring images of blood, darkness, sleep and ill-fitting clothes.
  6. Frankenstein: context and form: Frankenstein grew out of a ghost-story contest in 1816 and the scientific and political ferment of the Romantic period.
  7. Frankenstein: creation and responsibility: Victor Frankenstein creates life and then abandons it.
  8. Frankenstein: the creature and sympathy: The creature begins benevolent but is turned to violence by rejection.
  9. Frankenstein: nature, science and the sublime: Shelley sets her story among Alpine glaciers, Arctic ice and the Scottish islands.
  10. A Christmas Carol: context: A Christmas Carol was written in six weeks in 1843 as a response to poverty and child labour in industrial Britain.
  11. A Christmas Carol: Scrooge's transformation: Over five staves, Scrooge changes from a cold miser to a generous, joyful man.
  12. A Christmas Carol: social responsibility: Dickens uses A Christmas Carol to argue that everyone is responsible for the welfare of others.
  13. Jane Eyre: context: Jane Eyre, published in 1847 under the pseudonym Currer Bell, drew on Charlotte Brontë's own experience of harsh schooling and work as a governess.
  14. Jane Eyre: independence and identity: Jane Eyre is driven by a struggle for independence, equality and self-respect against social, religious and romantic pressures.
  15. Jane Eyre: Bertha Mason and critical readings: Bertha Mason, Rochester's first wife, is hidden in the attic of Thornfield.
  16. Jekyll and Hyde: context: Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886) reflects late-Victorian anxieties about respectability, science, evolution and the city.
  17. Jekyll and Hyde: duality: Stevenson explores the idea that every person contains good and evil, and the dangers of trying to separate them.
  18. Jekyll and Hyde: narrative and setting: Stevenson withholds the truth by telling most of the story through a cautious lawyer and by setting it in a foggy, divided London.

School 9: Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry

15 lessons

Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience: William Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794) pairs poems showing "the two contrary states of the human soul".
  2. Wordsworth: nature and memory: William Wordsworth made nature and memory central to English poetry.
  3. Coleridge: The Rime of the Ancient Mariner: Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner is a supernatural ballad of crime, punishment and redemption.
  4. Shelley: Ozymandias and the political poems: Percy Bysshe Shelley was a radical poet who attacked tyranny and believed in the power of poetry to change the world.
  5. Keats: the odes: In 1819, John Keats wrote a series of odes that explore beauty, art, mortality and the desire to escape suffering.
  6. Tennyson: The Charge of the Light Brigade and In Memoriam: Alfred, Lord Tennyson was Poet Laureate and the most popular poet of the Victorian age.
  7. Browning: My Last Duchess: Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess" is the most famous example of the dramatic monologue.
  8. Christina Rossetti: Christina Rossetti was one of the most important Victorian poets, combining religious devotion, intense feeling and technical mastery.
  9. Thomas Hardy's poetry: Thomas Hardy turned from novels to poetry and wrote about loss, time, chance and war in a distinctive voice.
  10. First World War poetry: an overview: The First World War produced a body of poetry that shaped how later generations remember the war.
  11. Wilfred Owen: Wilfred Owen wrote some of the most powerful poems of the First World War, focused on "the pity of War".
  12. Siegfried Sassoon: Siegfried Sassoon was a decorated officer who became one of the war's fiercest critics.
  13. Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas: Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas both died in 1917 to 1918 and wrote war poetry very different from Owen's and Sassoon's.
  14. Women's poetry of the First World War: Women wrote a large body of poetry about the First World War, often from the home front, hospitals and ambulance units.
  15. War poetry after 1918: Poets have continued to write about war, from the Second World War to modern conflicts, often in dialogue with the First World War poets.

School 10: Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing

17 lessons

Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Approaching an unseen poem: Unseen poetry questions test whether you can read a poem you have never met and write about it with insight under time pressure.
  2. Approaching unseen prose: Unseen prose extracts test your ability to analyse narrative, character and style quickly.
  3. Approaching unseen drama: Drama is written to be performed, so an unseen scene must be read with the stage in mind.
  4. Comparative analysis: principles: Comparing texts reveals more about each than reading them separately.
  5. Structuring a comparison: A clear structure helps a comparative essay stay balanced and focused.
  6. Comparing across periods: Comparing texts from different periods shows how contexts shape writing and how writers respond to one another across time.
  7. Reading the question: Many essays lose marks by answering the question the writer wanted rather than the one set.
  8. Building an argument: A literature essay is an argument, not a list of observations.
  9. Paragraphing: point, evidence, analysis: Well-built paragraphs carry an essay's argument.
  10. Embedding quotations: Quotations work best when they are short and woven into your own sentences.
  11. Writing about context: Context can deepen interpretation when it is linked to the text.
  12. Writing about writers' methods: The highest marks go to essays that explain how writers create meaning.
  13. Introductions and conclusions: Introductions and conclusions frame an essay's argument.
  14. Academic style and register: Clear, precise academic style helps your argument persuade.
  15. Referencing and academic integrity: Referencing gives credit to others' ideas and lets readers check your sources.
  16. The coursework essay: Coursework, or non-exam assessment, gives time to research and write an extended, independent essay.
  17. Revising for literature exams: Effective revision for literature exams combines knowing texts deeply with practising timed analytical writing.
