Free Nursing course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

NurseGradify's free Nursing course runs from Foundations of Nursing and Professional Practice to Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding: 497 lessons in 17 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Clinical reasoning). It's written for student nurses and midwives.

Education, not clinical authorisation. NurseGradify doesn't give NMC registration, replace approved practice placements or assess your competence. Practise clinical skills and give medicines only within your scope, under supervision where required, and following your local policies. NMC standards apply UK-wide; laws, policies and services that differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland are labelled.

Course overview

  • 17 schools, 497 lessons
  • Study paths: Adult nursing, Children's nursing, Mental health nursing, Learning disability nursing, Pre-registration year 1 (all fields), Recognising and responding to deterioration, Medicines management, Newly qualified nurse, Internationally educated nurses: UK practice, Nursing associates, CPD and revalidation
  • 5 practice exams to test yourself under time
  • Curriculum: NMC Future nurse: standards of proficiency (2018) (mapped, not yet verified); Undergraduate degree (aligned). What this means

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Nursing and Professional Practice

30 lessons

Foundations of Nursing and Professional Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to nursing: Nursing is a profession that protects, promotes and restores health, prevents illness, and cares for people of all ages through illness, disability and dying.
  2. History and development of nursing: Modern nursing grew from religious and military caring traditions into a regulated, graduate profession.
  3. The nursing profession: Nursing is the largest professional group in UK health care.
  4. Professional identity: Professional identity is how nurses understand themselves as nurses: their values, knowledge, behaviour and sense of belonging to the profession.
  5. The NMC and professional regulation: The Nursing and Midwifery Council regulates nurses and midwives in the UK and nursing associates in England.
  6. The NMC Code: The Code: Professional standards of practice and behaviour for nurses, midwives and nursing associates (2015, updated 2018) sets out what the public can expect of every NMC registrant.
  7. The seven NMC platforms: The NMC's Future nurse: standards of proficiency for registered nurses (2018) describe what every newly qualified nurse must know and be able to do.
  8. Professional accountability: Accountability means being answerable for your actions and omissions, and being able to justify them.
  9. Duty of care: A duty of care is the legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that you can reasonably foresee would harm others.
  10. Duty of candour: The duty of candour requires health professionals and organisations to be open and honest with people when something goes wrong with their care that causes, or could cause, harm or distress.
  11. Consent: Valid consent is a person's voluntary, informed agreement, given with capacity, to examination, care or treatment.
  12. Capacity: Mental capacity is the ability to make a particular decision at the time it needs to be made.
  13. Confidentiality: Confidentiality is the duty to protect information people share with health professionals and to use it only for their care, unless there is a lawful reason to share it.
  14. Data protection: Data protection law governs how personal information, including health information, is collected, used, stored and shared.
  15. Documentation: Documentation is the written or electronic record of assessment, care, decisions and communication.
  16. Record keeping: Record keeping covers how records are created, stored, shared, retained and disposed of.
  17. Professional boundaries: Professional boundaries are the limits that keep the relationship between a nurse and the people they care for safe, therapeutic and focused on the person's needs.
  18. Delegation: Delegation is transferring to another person the authority to carry out a task, while the delegating registrant keeps accountability for the decision to delegate.
  19. Scope of practice: Scope of practice is the range of activities a nurse is educated, competent and authorised to perform.
  20. Clinical supervision: Clinical supervision is a formal process of professional support and learning that helps practitioners develop knowledge and skills, reflect on practice and look after their wellbeing.
  21. Reflective practice: Reflective practice is thinking critically about your experiences to learn from them and improve future practice.
  22. Evidence-based practice: Evidence-based practice integrates the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values and preferences to make decisions about care.
  23. Equality, diversity and inclusion: Equality means fair treatment and opportunity; diversity recognises and values differences; inclusion means everyone can take part and feels they belong.
  24. Human rights: Human rights are the basic rights and freedoms everyone has.
  25. Safeguarding: Safeguarding means protecting people's health, wellbeing and human rights, enabling them to live free from harm, abuse and neglect.
  26. Professional resilience: Professional resilience is the ability to maintain wellbeing and effective practice in the face of stress and adversity.
  27. Patient advocacy: Advocacy is acting on behalf of a person to make sure their views, wishes and rights are heard and respected, especially when they cannot speak up for themselves.
  28. Ethical decision-making: Ethical decision-making is working out what you ought to do when values, duties and interests conflict.
  29. Multidisciplinary working: Multidisciplinary (or interprofessional) working brings together professionals with different skills to plan and deliver coordinated care.
  30. Continuing professional development: Continuing professional development (CPD) is the ongoing learning that keeps a nurse's knowledge and skills up to date and develops their practice.

School 2: Human Anatomy and Physiology

30 lessons

Human Anatomy and Physiology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Anatomical terminology: Anatomical terminology is the shared, precise language health professionals use to describe where structures are and how they relate to each other.
  2. Cells and tissues: Cells are the basic units of life, and tissues are groups of similar cells working together.
  3. Homeostasis: Homeostasis is the body's ability to keep its internal environment within narrow limits despite changes outside and inside.
  4. The integumentary system: The integumentary system is the skin and its appendages: hair, nails and glands.
  5. Skeletal system: The skeletal system is the bones, joints, cartilage and ligaments that support the body, protect organs and allow movement.
  6. Muscular system: Muscle tissue moves the body, pumps blood, moves food along the gut and generates heat.
  7. Nervous system: The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates the body's responses through electrical and chemical signals.
  8. Brain and spinal cord: The brain and spinal cord make up the central nervous system.
  9. Autonomic nervous system: The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, digestion and bladder emptying.
  10. Cardiovascular system: The cardiovascular system is the heart and blood vessels, which deliver oxygen and nutrients to every cell and remove waste.
  11. Blood and circulation: Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones, heat and immune cells around the body through a network of arteries, capillaries and veins.
  12. Respiratory system: The respiratory system brings air into the lungs so oxygen can enter the blood and carbon dioxide can leave.
  13. Gas exchange: Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli.
  14. Digestive system: The digestive system breaks food down into molecules the body can absorb, and eliminates what it can't use.
  15. Liver and pancreas: The liver and pancreas are accessory organs of digestion with major metabolic roles.
  16. Renal system: The kidneys filter the blood, remove waste and control the body's water, electrolytes, acid-base balance and blood pressure.
  17. Urinary system: The urinary system stores and eliminates urine through the ureters, bladder and urethra.
  18. Endocrine system: The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood to regulate growth, metabolism, fluid balance, stress responses and reproduction.
  19. Reproductive system: The reproductive system produces sex cells and hormones and, in females, supports pregnancy.
  20. Immune system: The immune system defends the body against micro-organisms, toxins and abnormal cells.
  21. Lymphatic system: The lymphatic system returns excess tissue fluid to the blood, absorbs fats from the gut and carries immune cells.
  22. Fluid balance: Water makes up about 60% of an adult's body weight, divided between the cells, the tissues and the blood.
  23. Electrolytes: Electrolytes are charged particles dissolved in body fluids, such as sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium.
  24. Acid-base balance: Acid-base balance keeps the blood pH within a narrow range, about 7.35–7.45.
  25. Temperature regulation: The body keeps its core temperature close to 37°C by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  26. Pain physiology: Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage.
  27. Age-related physiological changes: The body changes across the lifespan, from infancy to older age.
  28. Cellular pathology: Cellular pathology is the study of how cells respond to injury and disease.
  29. Microbiology: Microbiology is the study of micro-organisms: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa and parasites, and agents such as prions.
  30. Genetics and genomics: Genetics is the study of individual genes and how traits are inherited; genomics studies the whole genome and how genes interact with each other and the environment.

School 3: Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning

30 lessons

Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to clinical assessment: Clinical assessment is the systematic gathering and interpretation of information about a person's health to identify needs, problems and risks, and to plan care.
  2. Person-centred assessment: Person-centred assessment puts the person's own experiences, values, preferences and goals at the centre.
  3. History taking: History taking is a structured conversation to understand a person's presenting problem and its context.
  4. Subjective and objective data: Assessment uses two kinds of information: subjective data (what the person tells you) and objective data (what you observe, measure or test).
  5. Physical assessment: Physical assessment is the systematic examination of the body to gather objective data.
  6. General inspection: General inspection is the first impression you form when you approach a person: how they look, behave and breathe.
  7. Vital signs: Vital signs (observations) are measurements of the body's most basic functions: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse, consciousness level and temperature.
  8. Temperature: Body temperature reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss.
  9. Pulse: The pulse is the wave of pressure felt as the heart ejects blood into the arteries.
  10. Respiratory rate: Respiratory rate is the number of breaths per minute.
  11. Blood pressure: Blood pressure is the pressure of blood against arterial walls: systolic (when the heart contracts) over diastolic (when it relaxes).
  12. Oxygen saturation: Oxygen saturation (SpO2) measured by pulse oximetry estimates the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen.
  13. Pain assessment: Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience.
  14. Neurological observations: Neurological observations assess consciousness, pupils, limb power and vital signs to detect changes in brain function.
  15. Cardiovascular assessment: Cardiovascular assessment examines the heart and circulation to identify problems with cardiac output, perfusion and fluid status.
  16. Respiratory assessment: Respiratory assessment looks at how well a person is breathing and oxygenating.
  17. Abdominal assessment: Abdominal assessment explores symptoms and signs from the digestive, urinary and reproductive systems.
  18. Skin assessment: Skin assessment examines the skin, hair and nails for colour, integrity, temperature, moisture and lesions.
  19. Hydration assessment: Hydration assessment identifies dehydration and fluid overload by combining history, observation, examination, fluid balance and test results.
  20. Nutritional assessment: Nutritional assessment identifies people who are malnourished or at risk, so that they get the right support.
  21. Mental state examination: The mental state examination (MSE) is a structured description of a person's mental state at a point in time, based on observation and conversation.
  22. Mobility assessment: Mobility assessment considers how a person moves, transfers and walks, and what support they need.
  23. Falls assessment: Falls are a leading cause of injury in older people and are common in hospitals and care homes.
  24. Pressure ulcer risk assessment: Pressure ulcers are localised damage to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, caused by pressure or pressure combined with shear.
  25. Clinical reasoning: Clinical reasoning is the thinking process nurses use to collect and interpret information, identify problems, decide on actions, and evaluate their effect.
  26. Differential considerations: Differential considerations are the possible explanations for a person's symptoms and signs.
  27. Recognising deterioration: Recognising deterioration means noticing early signs that a person is becoming more unwell and acting promptly.
  28. Escalation of concerns: Escalation is communicating concerns about a person's condition to someone who can provide the right level of help, in time.
  29. Structured handover: Handover transfers responsibility and information about a person's care from one professional or team to another.
  30. Documentation and reassessment: Documentation of assessment and reassessment shows what was found, what was done and whether it worked.

School 4: Pharmacology and Medicines Management

37 lessons

Pharmacology and Medicines Management: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Principles of pharmacology: Pharmacology is the study of how medicines interact with the body.
  2. Pharmacokinetics: Pharmacokinetics describes how a drug moves through the body over time: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME).
  3. Pharmacodynamics: Pharmacodynamics is how drugs produce their effects: the targets they act on, how strongly they act and how the response relates to the dose.
  4. Absorption: Absorption is the movement of a drug from where it is given into the bloodstream.
  5. Distribution: Distribution is the movement of a drug from the bloodstream into tissues.
  6. Metabolism: Metabolism (biotransformation) chemically changes drugs, mainly in the liver, usually making them easier to excrete.
  7. Excretion: Excretion removes drugs and their metabolites from the body, mainly through the kidneys, but also in bile, faeces, breath, sweat and breast milk.
  8. Drug interactions: A drug interaction occurs when one substance changes the effect of another.
  9. Adverse drug reactions: An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful and unintended response to a medicine.
  10. Contraindications: A contraindication is a reason not to use a medicine because the risk of harm outweighs the benefit.
  11. Allergies: A drug allergy is an immune-mediated reaction to a medicine.
  12. Medication reconciliation: Medicines reconciliation is the process of obtaining an accurate, complete list of a person's current medicines and comparing it with what is prescribed at transitions of care: on admission, transfer and discharge.
  13. Medicines legislation: Medicines law in the UK controls how medicines are classified, prescribed, supplied, stored and administered.
  14. Prescribing and administration responsibilities: Prescribers decide which medicine a person should have; those administering medicines are responsible for giving them correctly and safely.
  15. The medicines administration process: Safe administration follows a consistent process every time: preparation, checking, administration, documentation and monitoring.
  16. The rights of medicines administration: The "rights" of medicines administration are a checklist to prevent errors.
  17. Oral medication: Oral medicines are the most common route.
  18. Topical medication: Topical medicines are applied to the skin or mucous membranes for local or, sometimes, systemic effect.
  19. Inhaled medication: Inhaled medicines deliver drugs straight to the airways for asthma and COPD.
  20. Injectable medication: Injections deliver medicines into the skin (intradermal), fat (subcutaneous) or muscle (intramuscular).
  21. Intravenous medication principles: Intravenous (IV) medicines act immediately and can't be retrieved once given, so IV administration carries high risk.
  22. Controlled drugs: Controlled drugs (CDs) are medicines with potential for misuse and harm, controlled under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 and its regulations.
  23. Antimicrobials: Antimicrobials (antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitic drugs) treat infections.
  24. Analgesics: Analgesics relieve pain.
  25. Cardiovascular medicines: Cardiovascular medicines treat hypertension, heart failure, angina, arrhythmias and high cholesterol, and prevent heart attacks and strokes.
  26. Respiratory medicines: Respiratory medicines treat asthma, COPD and other lung conditions, mostly by opening the airways, reducing inflammation, or treating infection.
  27. Endocrine medicines: Endocrine medicines replace or modify hormones.
  28. Gastrointestinal medicines: Gastrointestinal medicines treat acid-related disease, nausea and vomiting, constipation, diarrhoea and inflammatory bowel disease.
  29. Neurological and psychiatric medicines: Medicines for epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, dementia, depression, psychosis, bipolar disorder and anxiety are often long term and some have serious risks.
  30. Anticoagulants: Anticoagulants reduce blood clotting to prevent and treat venous thromboembolism and to prevent stroke in atrial fibrillation.
  31. Insulin and diabetes medicines: Diabetes medicines lower blood glucose.
  32. Polypharmacy: Polypharmacy is the use of multiple medicines.
  33. Medicines safety: Medicines safety is about designing systems and habits that prevent medication harm.
  34. Medication errors: A medication error is any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medicine use or harm.
  35. Patient education: Educating people about their medicines helps them take them safely and effectively, recognise side effects and make informed choices.
  36. Medicines optimisation: Medicines optimisation is a person-centred approach to safe and effective medicine use, so that people get the best possible outcomes from their medicines.
  37. Antimicrobial stewardship: Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated approach to using antimicrobials responsibly, so they stay effective.

School 5: Pathophysiology and Clinical Conditions

37 lessons

Pathophysiology and Clinical Conditions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to disease processes: Pathophysiology is the study of how normal body functions are disrupted by disease.
  2. Inflammation: Inflammation is the body's protective response to injury or infection.
  3. Infection: Infection is the invasion and multiplication of micro-organisms in the body, causing harm.
  4. Immune dysfunction: Immune dysfunction occurs when the immune system is underactive (immunodeficiency), overactive (hypersensitivity), or misdirected against the body (autoimmunity).
  5. Cardiovascular disease: Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a group of conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels, mostly caused by atherosclerosis.
  6. Hypertension: Hypertension is persistently raised arterial blood pressure.
  7. Heart failure: Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can only do so at high filling pressures.
  8. Ischaemic heart disease: Ischaemic heart disease (coronary heart disease) occurs when the coronary arteries are narrowed by atherosclerosis, reducing blood supply to the heart muscle.
  9. Myocardial infarction: A myocardial infarction (MI, heart attack) is death of heart muscle caused by prolonged ischaemia, usually from a blood clot on a ruptured coronary plaque.
  10. Arrhythmias: An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm.
  11. Stroke: A stroke is a sudden loss of brain function caused by an interruption of blood supply: either a blocked artery (ischaemic stroke) or bleeding (haemorrhagic stroke).
  12. Respiratory disease: Respiratory diseases affect the airways, lung tissue, blood vessels or breathing muscles.
  13. Asthma: Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways causing variable, reversible airflow obstruction, with symptoms of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
  14. COPD: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable lung condition with persistent breathlessness and airflow obstruction that is not fully reversible, usually caused by smoking.
  15. Pneumonia: Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue, causing inflammation and consolidation of the alveoli.
  16. Pulmonary embolism: A pulmonary embolism (PE) is a blockage of the pulmonary arteries, usually by a blood clot from a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the leg.
  17. Diabetes: Diabetes mellitus is a group of conditions in which blood glucose is persistently raised because the body produces too little insulin, responds poorly to it, or both.
  18. Thyroid disease: Thyroid disease is common, especially in women.
  19. Renal disease: The kidneys filter blood, remove waste, balance fluid and electrolytes, regulate acid-base and blood pressure, make erythropoietin and activate vitamin D. Renal disease disrupts all of these, and is…
  20. Acute kidney injury: Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function over hours to days.
  21. Chronic kidney disease: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is long-term reduced kidney function or kidney damage lasting more than 3 months.
  22. Liver disease: Liver disease is a growing cause of death in the UK, largely preventable and often diagnosed late.
  23. Gastrointestinal disorders: Gastrointestinal (GI) disorders range from common, mild problems such as reflux and constipation to life-threatening bleeding, obstruction and cancer.
  24. Neurological disorders: Neurological disorders affect the brain, spinal cord, nerves or muscles.
  25. Epilepsy: Epilepsy is a condition of recurrent, unprovoked seizures caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain.
  26. Dementia: Dementia is a syndrome of progressive decline in memory, thinking, behaviour and the ability to perform everyday activities, caused by brain disease.
  27. Parkinson's disease: Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurological condition caused by loss of dopamine-producing neurones in the brain.
  28. Cancer: Cancer is uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells that can invade nearby tissue and spread (metastasise).
  29. Haematological disorders: Haematological disorders affect the blood and blood-forming organs: red cells, white cells, platelets, clotting and the bone marrow.
  30. Musculoskeletal disease: Musculoskeletal (MSK) conditions affect bones, joints, muscles and connective tissue.
  31. Rheumatological conditions: Rheumatological conditions are inflammatory and autoimmune diseases of joints, muscles and connective tissue.
  32. Skin conditions: The skin is the largest organ, and skin conditions are common causes of discomfort, distress and infection.
  33. Sepsis: Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection in which the body's response damages its own tissues and organs.
  34. Shock: Shock is a life-threatening state of circulatory failure in which tissues don't receive enough oxygen.
  35. Multimorbidity: Multimorbidity is the presence of two or more long-term health conditions in one person.
  36. Frailty: Frailty is a long-term condition in which reduced reserve across many body systems makes a person vulnerable to sudden, dramatic changes in health after minor events such as an infection or a new medicine.
  37. Palliative and end-of-life conditions: Palliative care improves quality of life for people with life-limiting illness and their families, through relief of pain and other symptoms and attention to psychological, social and spiritual needs.

School 6: Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills

35 lessons

Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Hand hygiene: Hand hygiene is the single most effective way to prevent the spread of infection in health and social care.
  2. Standard infection precautions: Standard infection control precautions (SICPs) are the basic practices used by all staff, in all settings, with every person, whether or not an infection is known.
  3. Personal protective equipment: Personal protective equipment (PPE) protects staff and patients from exposure to infection and hazardous substances.
  4. Aseptic technique: Aseptic technique prevents micro-organisms being introduced into susceptible sites such as wounds, the bloodstream and the urinary tract during clinical procedures.
  5. Wound assessment: Wound assessment is the systematic evaluation of a wound and the person who has it, so that the right care can be planned, progress monitored and complications spotted early.
  6. Wound dressing principles: Dressings protect wounds and create conditions for healing.
  7. Pressure area care: Pressure area care prevents pressure ulcers by reducing pressure and shear, managing moisture, and supporting nutrition and mobility.
  8. Catheter care: Catheter care keeps an indwelling urinary catheter working and reduces the risk of infection and other harm.
  9. Urinary catheterisation principles: Urinary catheterisation is an invasive procedure with real risks.
  10. Fluid balance monitoring: Fluid balance monitoring tracks the fluid a person takes in and loses, alongside clinical signs and weight, so that dehydration and overload are recognised early.
  11. Intake and output recording: Accurate intake and output recording is the practical skill underneath fluid balance monitoring.
  12. Nutrition support: Nutrition support helps people who are malnourished, or at risk, to meet their needs: from help with eating and food fortification to oral nutritional supplements, tube feeding and parenteral nutrition.
  13. Enteral feeding principles: Enteral feeding gives nutrition through a tube into the stomach or small intestine when someone can't eat enough safely by mouth.
  14. Oxygen therapy: Oxygen is a medicine.
  15. Nebuliser administration principles: A nebuliser turns a liquid medicine into a fine mist inhaled through a mask or mouthpiece.
  16. Blood glucose monitoring: Capillary blood glucose monitoring guides the care of people with diabetes and others at risk of high or low glucose.
  17. Insulin administration principles: Insulin is a high-alert medicine: small errors in type, dose, timing or device can cause serious harm.
  18. Blood sampling: Blood samples are only useful if they are taken from the right person, into the right tubes, labelled correctly, at the right time and sent promptly.
  19. Venepuncture principles: Venepuncture is the puncture of a vein to obtain blood.
  20. Cannulation principles: Peripheral intravenous cannulation provides venous access for fluids and medicines.
  21. Intravenous therapy principles: Intravenous (IV) therapy delivers fluids, medicines and blood products directly into the circulation.
  22. Medication administration: This lesson brings together the practical skill of giving medicines in a clinical setting: preparation, checks, administration by different routes, documentation and monitoring, within the law and your competence.
  23. Specimen collection: Specimens such as urine, stool, sputum and swabs guide diagnosis and treatment.
  24. ECG recording: A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart's electrical activity from different angles.
  25. Resuscitation principles: Resuscitation aims to restore breathing and circulation after cardiac or respiratory arrest.
  26. Basic life support: Basic life support (BLS) keeps oxygenated blood flowing to the brain and heart until defibrillation and advanced care are available.
  27. Patient positioning: How a person is positioned affects their breathing, circulation, comfort, skin, swallowing and recovery.
  28. Safe moving and handling: Moving and handling people safely protects both the person and staff from injury.
  29. Personal care: Personal care, including washing, dressing, toileting and grooming, is central to dignity, comfort and wellbeing.
  30. Oral care: Good oral care prevents pain, infection and malnutrition, supports dignity and communication, and reduces the risk of pneumonia in people who are unwell.
  31. Continence care: Continence care helps people maintain continence where possible and manages incontinence with dignity when it occurs.
  32. Stoma care: A stoma is a surgically created opening that brings part of the bowel or urinary tract to the abdominal surface.
  33. Drain management: Surgical and other drains remove fluid, blood or air from a body cavity or wound.
  34. Observations and escalation: Observations are only valuable if they are accurate, timely and acted on.
  35. Documentation of procedures: Documenting a procedure records what was done, why, how it went and what happens next.

School 7: Clinical Practice and Care Planning

27 lessons

Clinical Practice and Care Planning: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Nursing process: The nursing process is a systematic, problem-solving approach to planning and delivering individualised care.
  2. Assessment: Assessment is the systematic gathering and interpretation of information about a person's physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs.
  3. Nursing diagnoses and problem identification: After assessment, nurses decide what the person's nursing problems or needs are.
  4. Care planning: A care plan sets out the agreed goals for a person's care and the interventions to achieve them.
  5. SMART goals: A goal is the outcome the person and the team are aiming for.
  6. Nursing interventions: Interventions are the actions nurses take, or coordinate, to achieve the goals in the care plan.
  7. Evidence-based care: Evidence-based care combines the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values and preferences.
  8. Evaluation of outcomes: Evaluation judges whether the care given has achieved the agreed goals, and what should change.
  9. Person-centred care: Person-centred care puts the person, not the condition or the system, at the centre of care.
  10. Holistic care: Holistic care considers the whole person: physical, psychological, social, spiritual and cultural needs, and how these affect each other.
  11. Individualised care: Individualised care means tailoring care to the specific person: their needs, preferences, abilities, background and circumstances, rather than applying the same care to everyone with the same condition.
  12. Shared decision-making: Shared decision-making is a collaborative process in which a person and a professional decide together about care, based on the best evidence and what matters to the person.
  13. Patient education: Patient education helps people understand their health, manage conditions and make informed choices.
  14. Health promotion: Health promotion enables people to increase control over and improve their health.
  15. Preventative care: Preventative care aims to stop illness from happening, detect it early, or limit its effects.
  16. Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation helps people regain, maintain or adapt function after illness, injury or surgery, so they can live as independently and fully as possible.
  17. Discharge planning: Discharge planning prepares a person to leave a care setting safely, with the right support in place.
  18. Continuity of care: Continuity of care means care that is coherent and connected over time and between people, teams and settings.
  19. Complex care: Complex care involves people whose needs span several conditions, services or domains of life, such as multimorbidity, frailty, disability, or the overlap of health and social problems.
  20. Multidisciplinary care: Multidisciplinary care brings together professionals from different disciplines to plan and deliver care.
  21. Care coordination: Care coordination is the deliberate organisation of a person's care across professionals, services and settings, so that it is safe, efficient and makes sense to the person.
  22. Risk assessment: Risk assessment identifies hazards to a person's safety or wellbeing, judges how likely and serious harm would be, and plans how to reduce it.
  23. Clinical prioritisation: Clinical prioritisation means deciding what needs doing first when several people or tasks compete for time.
  24. Delegation: Delegation is transferring responsibility for a task to another person while keeping overall accountability for the decision to delegate.
  25. Safe escalation: Escalation is raising a concern to the right person, at the right time, in a way that gets the right response.
  26. Patient advocacy: Advocacy means acting to protect and promote a person's rights, interests and wishes, and helping them to have their voice heard.
  27. End-of-life care: End-of-life care supports people in the last months, weeks and days of life, and those close to them, with dignity, comfort and respect for their wishes.

School 8: Infection Prevention and Control

28 lessons

Infection Prevention and Control: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Microorganisms: Microorganisms are living things too small to see without a microscope.
  2. Bacteria: Bacteria are single-celled organisms responsible for many healthcare-associated infections.
  3. Viruses: Viruses cause many common and serious infections, from colds and norovirus to influenza, hepatitis and HIV.
  4. Fungi: Fungi include yeasts and moulds.
  5. Parasites: Parasites live on or in a host and benefit at the host's expense.
  6. Transmission routes: Microorganisms spread from one place to another by specific routes.
  7. Chain of infection: The chain of infection is a model showing the six links needed for infection to spread.
  8. Standard precautions: Standard infection control precautions (SICPs) are the basic measures used by all staff, for all people, at all times, whether or not infection is known.
  9. Hand hygiene: Hand hygiene is the most important measure for preventing healthcare-associated infection.
  10. PPE: Personal protective equipment protects staff and patients from exposure to infectious organisms and hazardous substances.
  11. Isolation precautions: Isolation and cohorting separate people with infections from others to stop spread.
  12. Aseptic technique: Aseptic technique prevents microorganisms being introduced into susceptible sites during clinical procedures.
  13. Sterilisation: Sterilisation destroys all microorganisms, including bacterial spores.
  14. Disinfection: Disinfection reduces the number of viable microorganisms to a level that isn't harmful, but doesn't necessarily kill spores.
  15. Cleaning: Cleaning removes dirt, organic matter and many microorganisms from surfaces and equipment.
  16. Healthcare-associated infections: Healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) are infections acquired as a result of healthcare, in hospital or in the community.
  17. MRSA: Meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a strain of a common bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics.
  18. Clostridioides difficile: Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is a spore-forming bacterium that causes diarrhoea and colitis, mainly after antibiotics disrupt the gut flora.
  19. Bloodborne viruses: Bloodborne viruses (BBVs), mainly hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV, are carried in blood and some body fluids.
  20. Respiratory infections: Respiratory infections, including influenza, COVID-19, RSV and tuberculosis, spread readily in healthcare settings and cause serious illness in vulnerable people.
  21. Sepsis recognition: Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection that damages the body's own tissues and organs.
  22. Antimicrobial resistance: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) happens when microorganisms change so that medicines no longer work against them.
  23. Antimicrobial stewardship: Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated approach to using antimicrobials responsibly, so that people get effective treatment while resistance is slowed.
  24. Outbreak management: An outbreak is two or more linked cases of the same infection, or a rise in cases above what is expected.
  25. Waste management: Healthcare waste must be segregated, stored and disposed of safely to protect people and the environment and to comply with the law.
  26. Sharps safety: Sharps injuries expose healthcare workers to bloodborne viruses and cause anxiety and harm.
  27. Occupational exposure: Occupational exposure is contact with blood or body fluids at work that could transmit infection, such as a needlestick injury, a splash to the eyes or mouth, or a bite that breaks the skin.
  28. Infection surveillance: Surveillance is the systematic collection, analysis and sharing of infection data to guide action.

School 9: Acute, Emergency and Critical Care Nursing

25 lessons

Acute, Emergency and Critical Care Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Acute nursing principles: Acute nursing is the care of people whose condition is unstable or could become unstable quickly.
  2. Recognition of deterioration: Patients usually show signs of deterioration hours before a cardiac arrest or critical care admission.
  3. ABCDE assessment: The ABCDE approach is a systematic way to assess and treat an acutely unwell patient, dealing with the most life-threatening problems first.
  4. Early warning scores: Early warning scores turn routine observations into a score that signals when a patient needs more attention.
  5. NEWS2: NEWS2 is the national early warning score for adults in the UK.
  6. Sepsis: Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection and a leading cause of avoidable death.
  7. Shock: Shock is a life-threatening failure of the circulation to deliver enough oxygen to the tissues.
  8. Acute respiratory failure: Acute respiratory failure occurs when the lungs can't maintain adequate oxygen or remove enough carbon dioxide.
  9. Acute cardiac conditions: Acute cardiac conditions such as acute coronary syndromes, acute heart failure and dangerous arrhythmias can be rapidly fatal.
  10. Acute neurological conditions: Acute neurological emergencies, such as status epilepticus, raised intracranial pressure, meningitis and spinal cord compression, can cause permanent harm or death within hours.
  11. Chest pain: Chest pain is one of the most common reasons for emergency attendance.
  12. Stroke recognition: Stroke is a medical emergency in which time lost is brain lost.
  13. Acute kidney injury: Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function, common in acutely ill patients and associated with serious complications and death.
  14. Fluid resuscitation principles: Fluid resuscitation restores circulating volume in patients who are hypovolaemic or in shock.
  15. Oxygenation and ventilation: Oxygen is a medicine that must be prescribed and given to a target.
  16. Emergency escalation: Emergency escalation is calling for urgent help when a patient is critically ill or arrests.
  17. Cardiac arrest: Cardiac arrest is the sudden loss of effective heart function.
  18. Resuscitation: Resuscitation includes the whole process around a cardiac arrest: preparation, decision-making, care after the return of circulation, support for families and staff, and learning from events.
  19. Critical care fundamentals: Critical care provides close monitoring and organ support for people with life-threatening illness.
  20. Intensive care: Intensive care nursing provides continuous, highly skilled care to critically ill patients.
  21. Postoperative monitoring: Higher-risk patients after surgery often need enhanced monitoring in recovery, post-anaesthesia care, high dependency or enhanced care units.
  22. Trauma: Major trauma is a leading cause of death and disability, especially in younger adults, and increasingly in older people after falls.
  23. Emergency triage: Triage sorts patients by clinical urgency so that the sickest are seen first.
  24. Major incident response: A major incident is an event that overwhelms the normal capacity of emergency services and hospitals, such as a terrorist attack, transport crash or chemical release.
  25. Deteriorating patient scenarios: Bringing together recognition, assessment, escalation and treatment, these scenarios show how acute care principles apply in practice.

School 10: Adult Nursing

28 lessons

Adult Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Adult health assessment: Adult health assessment brings together history, observation, examination and validated tools to build a full picture of an adult's health and needs.
  2. Medical nursing: Medical nursing cares for adults with acute and chronic medical conditions, such as heart failure, COPD exacerbations, infections, stroke and diabetes.
  3. Surgical nursing: Surgical nursing cares for adults before, during and after surgery.
  4. Cardiovascular nursing: Cardiovascular nursing cares for adults with heart and circulatory conditions, from acute coronary syndromes to heart failure and arrhythmias.
  5. Respiratory nursing: Respiratory nursing supports adults with acute and long-term lung conditions such as COPD, asthma, pneumonia and lung cancer.
  6. Neurological nursing: Neurological nursing cares for adults with conditions of the brain, spinal cord and nerves, such as stroke, head injury, epilepsy, Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis.
  7. Gastrointestinal nursing: Gastrointestinal nursing cares for adults with conditions of the gut, liver and pancreas, such as GI bleeding, inflammatory bowel disease, liver disease and pancreatitis.
  8. Renal nursing: Renal nursing cares for adults with acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease, including those on dialysis or with a kidney transplant.
  9. Endocrine nursing: Endocrine nursing cares for adults with hormone disorders such as thyroid disease, adrenal disorders and pituitary conditions.
  10. Diabetes care: Diabetes affects millions of adults in the UK, and people with diabetes are frequent users of every health service.
  11. Oncology nursing: Oncology nursing supports adults through cancer diagnosis, treatment and beyond.
  12. Haematology nursing: Haematology nursing cares for adults with blood disorders such as anaemia, sickle cell disease, bleeding and clotting disorders, and blood cancers.
  13. Musculoskeletal nursing: Musculoskeletal nursing cares for adults with bone, joint and muscle conditions, including fractures, joint replacements and arthritis.
  14. Dermatology nursing: Dermatology nursing cares for adults with skin conditions, from eczema and psoriasis to leg ulcers, cellulitis and serious drug reactions.
  15. Infection and infectious disease: Adult nurses care for people with a wide range of infections, from urinary and chest infections to HIV, tuberculosis and imported infections.
  16. Older adult care: Older adults make up most hospital inpatients and many community caseloads.
  17. Frailty: Frailty is a state of reduced physiological reserve that makes people vulnerable to sudden, disproportionate declines after minor events.
  18. Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation in adult nursing helps people regain function after acute illness, surgery or injury, and adapt to long-term conditions.
  19. Long-term conditions: Long-term conditions are health problems that can't currently be cured but can be managed, such as diabetes, COPD, heart failure, arthritis and chronic kidney disease.
  20. Multimorbidity: Multimorbidity is living with two or more long-term conditions.
  21. Palliative care: Palliative care improves the quality of life of people with life-limiting illness and their families, by relieving symptoms and supporting physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs.
  22. End-of-life care: End-of-life care in adult nursing covers the last weeks and days of life, the moment of death and care afterwards.
  23. Postoperative care: Postoperative care spans the recovery room to discharge and beyond.
  24. Discharge planning: Discharge planning for adults coordinates the move from hospital to home or another setting, so the person leaves at the right time with the right support.
  25. Community adult nursing: Community adult nurses care for people in their own homes, care homes and clinics.
  26. Law and professional considerations in adult nursing: Adult nurses regularly face legal and professional questions, especially when caring for older people, people who lack capacity and people at risk of harm.
  27. Preparing for an adult nursing placement: Practice placements are where student nurses learn to apply knowledge, develop skills and demonstrate proficiency.
  28. Reflective learning and case studies in adult nursing: Reflection turns experience into learning.

School 11: Children's and Young People's Nursing

27 lessons

Children's and Young People's Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to paediatric nursing: Children's nursing cares for babies, children and young people from birth to adulthood, together with their families.
  2. Child development: Child development describes how children grow in their physical, cognitive, language, social and emotional abilities.
  3. Growth and development: Monitoring growth is one of the most important ways of checking a child's health.
  4. Developmental assessment: Developmental assessment checks whether a child's skills are progressing as expected, so that problems are identified early and support starts sooner.
  5. Family-centred care: Family-centred care recognises that children are part of families and that families are central to children's health and wellbeing.
  6. Communication with children: Communicating well with children and young people builds trust, reduces fear and helps them take part in their care.
  7. Consent and parental responsibility: Children and young people's consent depends on their age, competence and the law of each UK nation, and on who holds parental responsibility.
  8. Safeguarding children: Children's nurses have a central role in safeguarding: recognising when a child may be at risk, offering early help, and working with other agencies.
  9. Paediatric assessment: Assessing an unwell child needs a structured, rapid approach that recognises how children compensate and how quickly they can deteriorate.
  10. Paediatric vital signs: Taking vital signs accurately in children requires the right equipment, technique and age-specific charts.
  11. Neonatal fundamentals: The first weeks of life bring major physiological changes and specific risks.
  12. Prematurity: Babies born before 37 weeks of pregnancy are premature.
  13. Congenital conditions: Congenital conditions are present from birth.
  14. Paediatric respiratory disease: Respiratory illness is the most common reason children are admitted to hospital.
  15. Paediatric cardiovascular conditions: Children's cardiovascular conditions range from congenital heart disease to acquired conditions such as Kawasaki disease and arrhythmias.
  16. Paediatric neurological conditions: Neurological conditions in children include febrile seizures, epilepsy, meningitis, head injury, cerebral palsy and hydrocephalus.
  17. Paediatric infections: Infections are the most common illnesses of childhood.
  18. Paediatric emergencies: Paediatric emergencies are rare but frightening, and children can deteriorate rapidly.
  19. Paediatric medication safety: Children are at higher risk of medication errors because doses are calculated individually, preparations vary and small mistakes can cause large harm.
  20. Childhood diabetes: Type 1 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes in children and young people.
  21. Childhood cancer: Cancer in children is rare, but it is the most common cause of death from disease in children over 1 year in the UK.
  22. Adolescent health: Adolescence is a period of rapid physical, emotional and social change.
  23. Mental health in children: Mental health problems often start in childhood and adolescence.
  24. Child protection: Child protection is the part of safeguarding that responds to children who are suffering, or likely to suffer, significant harm.
  25. Transition to adult services: Young people with long-term conditions move from children's to adult services as they grow up.
  26. Preparing for a children's nursing placement: Placements in children's nursing take students into hospitals, community teams, schools, neonatal units and other settings.
  27. Reflective learning and case studies in children's nursing: Reflection and case studies help children's nursing students and registrants learn from experience, link theory to practice, and process the emotional demands of caring for children and families.

School 12: Mental Health Nursing

32 lessons

Mental Health Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Foundations of mental health nursing: Mental health nursing supports people experiencing mental distress and illness to recover, stay safe and live meaningful lives.
  2. Mental health assessment: Mental health assessment gathers information about a person's mental state, history, risks, strengths and circumstances to understand their difficulties and plan care with them.
  3. Mental state examination: The mental state examination (MSE) is a structured description of a person's mental state at the time of assessment.
  4. Therapeutic relationships: The therapeutic relationship is the foundation of mental health nursing.
  5. Communication: Communication in mental health nursing is a therapeutic intervention in itself.
  6. Trauma-informed care: Many people who use mental health services have experienced trauma, such as abuse, violence or neglect.
  7. Depression: Depression is one of the most common mental health conditions.
  8. Bipolar disorder: Bipolar disorder involves episodes of depression and of mania or hypomania, with periods of stability between.
  9. Anxiety disorders: Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions.
  10. OCD: Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) involves intrusive, distressing obsessions and repetitive compulsions performed to reduce anxiety.
  11. PTSD: Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can develop after experiencing or witnessing traumatic events.
  12. Psychosis: Psychosis is a state in which a person's perception of reality is altered, with hallucinations, delusions or disorganised thinking.
  13. Schizophrenia: Schizophrenia is a long-term mental illness involving episodes of psychosis and often ongoing negative and cognitive symptoms.
  14. Personality disorders: Personality disorders describe long-standing patterns of thinking, feeling and relating that cause significant distress and difficulty.
  15. Eating disorders: Eating disorders are serious mental illnesses with high physical risk; anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates of any mental illness.
  16. Substance misuse: Substance misuse involves harmful use of alcohol, drugs or medicines.
  17. Addiction: Addiction is a chronic, relapsing condition in which a person continues to use a substance or behaviour despite harm.
  18. Self-harm: Self-harm is any act of self-poisoning or self-injury, whatever the motivation.
  19. Suicide risk assessment: Suicide is a leading cause of death, especially among men and young people.
  20. Crisis intervention: A mental health crisis is when a person feels they can't cope or are at risk, and needs urgent help.
  21. Psychiatric medication: Medicines play an important role in mental health care, alongside psychological and social approaches.
  22. Mental Health Act: The Mental Health Act 1983 (as amended) shapes daily practice for mental health nurses in England and Wales.
  23. Mental Capacity Act: The Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies to mental health care as it does elsewhere, but its interaction with the Mental Health Act and with fluctuating capacity raises particular questions.
  24. Safeguarding: People who use mental health services are at higher risk of abuse, exploitation and neglect, and may also pose risks to others, including children in their family.
  25. De-escalation: De-escalation uses communication and other approaches to reduce agitation and aggression and prevent harm.
  26. Recovery-oriented practice: Recovery in mental health means living a satisfying, hopeful and contributing life, even with ongoing symptoms.
  27. Community mental health: Most mental health care happens in the community.
  28. Inpatient mental health care: Inpatient mental health wards care for people who need more intensive support and safety than the community can provide.
  29. Forensic mental health: Forensic mental health services care for people with mental illness who have been in contact with the criminal justice system or who pose a risk of serious harm to others.
  30. Older adult mental health: Older people experience mental health problems such as depression, anxiety, dementia and delirium, often alongside physical illness.
  31. Preparing for a mental health nursing placement: Mental health placements offer rich learning in therapeutic relationships, assessment and recovery-focused care.
  32. Reflective learning and case studies in mental health nursing: Reflection is especially important in mental health nursing, where relationships, emotions and ethical tensions are central.

School 13: Learning Disability Nursing

26 lessons

Learning Disability Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Foundations of learning disability nursing: Learning disability nursing supports people with learning disabilities and autistic people across their lives to be healthy, safe and included.
  2. Intellectual disability: Intellectual (learning) disability is a lifelong condition that affects how a person learns, understands and copes independently.
  3. Neurodevelopmental conditions: Neurodevelopmental conditions affect the development of the brain and nervous system, influencing learning, attention, communication, movement and behaviour.
  4. Autism: Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference in how people communicate, interact and experience the world.
  5. Down syndrome: Down syndrome is the most common chromosomal cause of learning disability.
  6. Communication needs: Many people with learning disabilities communicate in ways other than speech, or understand less than they appear to.
  7. Accessible healthcare: People with learning disabilities often face barriers to healthcare: inaccessible information, difficult environments, communication problems and attitudes.
  8. Reasonable adjustments: Reasonable adjustments are changes to how services are provided so that disabled people, including people with learning disabilities and autistic people, can use them as effectively as others.
  9. Mental capacity: Supporting people with learning disabilities to make their own decisions is central to learning disability nursing.
  10. Consent: Valid consent applies to people with learning disabilities just as it does to anyone else.
  11. Advocacy: Advocacy helps people speak up, be heard and have their rights respected.
  12. Positive behavioural support: Positive behaviour support (PBS) is a person-centred, evidence-based approach to understanding and reducing behaviour that challenges by improving quality of life and meeting needs.
  13. Challenging behaviour: "Behaviour that challenges" describes behaviour that puts the person or others at risk or limits their quality of life.
  14. Physical health inequalities: People with learning disabilities experience more physical health problems and die younger than the general population, often from treatable conditions.
  15. Epilepsy: Epilepsy is much more common in people with learning disabilities than in the general population, and more so in people with more severe disabilities.
  16. Complex needs: Some people with learning disabilities have complex health needs, such as profound and multiple learning disabilities, dysphagia, postural difficulties and sensory impairments.
  17. Safeguarding: People with learning disabilities are at higher risk of abuse, exploitation and neglect, and may find it harder to report it.
  18. Family and carer support: Families and carers provide much of the support for people with learning disabilities, often for a lifetime.
  19. Community learning disability services: Community learning disability teams support people to stay healthy, safe and included in their local communities.
  20. Hospital care: Hospital stays can be frightening and risky for people with learning disabilities.
  21. Health promotion: Health promotion for people with learning disabilities aims to reduce health inequalities by making healthy living, screening and prevention accessible.
  22. Independent living: Independent living means people with learning disabilities having choice and control over where and how they live, who supports them and how they spend their time.
  23. Multidisciplinary care: People with learning disabilities often need support from many professionals.
  24. Rights and inclusion: People with learning disabilities have the same human rights as everyone else.
  25. Preparing for a learning disability nursing placement: Learning disability placements take students into homes, communities, hospitals and specialist services.
  26. Reflective learning and case studies in learning disability nursing: Reflection and case studies help learning disability nursing students and registrants understand people's lives, link theory and law to practice, and develop values-based, person-centred care.

School 14: Community, Public Health and Health Promotion

25 lessons

Community, Public Health and Health Promotion: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Public health foundations: Public health is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society.
  2. Social determinants of health: The social determinants of health are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.
  3. Health inequalities: Health inequalities are unfair and avoidable differences in health between groups of people.
  4. Epidemiology: Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health and disease in populations.
  5. Disease prevention: Disease prevention at population level aims to reduce the burden of illness through action on causes and early detection.
  6. Health promotion: Health promotion at community and population level goes beyond individual advice to building healthy public policy, supportive environments and community action.
  7. Screening: Screening identifies people who appear healthy but may be at increased risk of a disease, so they can be offered further tests or treatment.
  8. Vaccination: Vaccination is giving a vaccine to stimulate immunity against a disease.
  9. Immunisation: Immunisation is the process of becoming immune to a disease.
  10. Sexual health: Sexual health is a state of physical, emotional, mental and social wellbeing in relation to sexuality.
  11. Reproductive health: Reproductive health covers the ability to have a satisfying and safe sex life and the freedom to decide whether, when and how often to have children.
  12. Maternal health: Maternal health covers the health of women and birthing people during pregnancy, birth and the postnatal period.
  13. Smoking cessation: Smoking remains the leading preventable cause of death and illness in the UK and a major driver of health inequalities.
  14. Alcohol and substance use: Alcohol and drug use cause substantial harm to individuals, families and communities.
  15. Nutrition: Diet is one of the biggest influences on health across the life course.
  16. Physical activity: Physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases, supports mental health and helps people stay independent as they age.
  17. Community nursing: Community nursing covers the wide range of nurses who care for people outside hospital: in homes, clinics, schools, workplaces, prisons and communities.
  18. District nursing: District nurses lead teams providing complex care to housebound people in their homes and care homes.
  19. School nursing: School nurses are specialist community public health nurses who promote the health and wellbeing of school-aged children and young people.
  20. Health visiting: Health visitors are specialist community public health nurses who work with families from pregnancy until children start school.
  21. Population health: Population health is an approach that aims to improve the physical and mental health outcomes and wellbeing of whole populations while reducing inequalities.
  22. Infection control in communities: Infection prevention and control in community settings, such as homes, care homes, schools and prisons, protects people who are often vulnerable and helps prevent outbreaks spreading.
  23. Health education: Health education gives people knowledge and skills to make informed decisions about their health.
  24. Behaviour change: Changing health behaviours is complex.
  25. Community assessment: Community assessment identifies the health needs, assets and priorities of a defined population, so services and interventions can be targeted where they will make the most difference.

School 15: Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing

22 lessons

Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Ageing physiology: Ageing brings gradual changes in every body system that reduce physiological reserve.
  2. Frailty: Frailty is a distinct clinical state of vulnerability.
  3. Falls: Falls are common in older people and can cause injury, fear, loss of independence and death.
  4. Dementia: Dementia is a progressive syndrome affecting memory, thinking, behaviour and daily function.
  5. Delirium: Delirium is a sudden, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually caused by physical illness.
  6. Polypharmacy: Many older people take several medicines, often for multiple conditions.
  7. Continence: Bladder and bowel problems are common in older people but aren't an inevitable part of ageing.
  8. Nutrition and hydration: Malnutrition and dehydration are common in older people, in hospital, in care homes and at home.
  9. Pressure injuries: Pressure injuries (pressure ulcers) are localised damage to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, caused by pressure or pressure with shear.
  10. Mobility: Mobility underpins independence, dignity and health in older age.
  11. Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation and intermediate care help older people recover function after illness, injury or surgery, often outside hospital.
  12. Care homes: Several hundred thousand older people in the UK live in care homes.
  13. Safeguarding older people: Older people can experience abuse and neglect at home, in care homes and in hospitals, often from people they trust.
  14. Advanced care planning: Advance care planning is a voluntary process of discussion between a person, those close to them and professionals about future wishes and priorities for care.
  15. Palliative care: Palliative care for older people often involves non-cancer conditions such as frailty, dementia, heart failure and COPD, with uncertain trajectories.
  16. Symptom management: Good symptom management relieves suffering and improves quality of life at the end of life.
  17. Pain management: Pain is one of the most feared symptoms at the end of life.
  18. End-of-life communication: Conversations about serious illness, dying and death are among the most important nurses have.
  19. Last days of life: The last days of life are a time when the focus of care shifts to comfort, dignity and the wishes of the dying person and those close to them.
  20. Bereavement support: Bereavement is the experience of losing someone close; grief is the response.
  21. Family support: Families and those close to older and dying people are often carers, decision-makers and people in their own distress.
  22. Ethical decision-making: Caring for older and dying people raises difficult ethical questions about treatment, autonomy, burden and benefit.

School 16: Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership

30 lessons

Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Research literacy: Research literacy is the ability to find, understand, judge and use research.
  2. Quantitative research: Quantitative research uses numerical data to measure, compare and test relationships.
  3. Qualitative research: Qualitative research explores people's experiences, meanings and perspectives in depth, using words rather than numbers.
  4. Mixed methods: Mixed methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches in one study, so that each answers questions the other can't.
  5. Study design: The design of a study determines what questions it can answer and how much its results can be trusted.
  6. Clinical trials: Clinical trials test whether interventions work and are safe.
  7. Cohort studies: Cohort studies follow groups of people over time to see who develops an outcome, comparing those with and without an exposure.
  8. Case-control studies: Case-control studies compare people who have a condition (cases) with people who don't (controls), looking back at their past exposures.
  9. Systematic reviews: A systematic review uses explicit, reproducible methods to find, appraise and synthesise all relevant studies on a question.
  10. Meta-analysis: Meta-analysis statistically combines the results of several studies to give a more precise overall estimate of effect.
  11. Critical appraisal: Critical appraisal is the systematic assessment of a study's trustworthiness, results and relevance.
  12. Research ethics: Research ethics protects the rights, safety, dignity and wellbeing of research participants.
  13. Informed consent in research: Informed consent is the foundation of ethical research.
  14. Evidence-based practice: Evidence-based practice integrates research evidence, clinical expertise and the person's preferences.
  15. Clinical guidelines: Clinical guidelines are systematically developed recommendations that help professionals and patients make decisions about care.
  16. Quality improvement: Quality improvement (QI) uses systematic methods to make care safer, more effective, more person-centred and more efficient.
  17. Clinical audit: Clinical audit is a quality improvement process that measures care against explicit standards and makes changes to close the gap.
  18. Patient safety: Patient safety is the avoidance of unintended or unexpected harm to people during healthcare.
  19. Human factors: Human factors (ergonomics) is the science of designing systems, equipment and tasks around how people actually work.
  20. Leadership: Leadership is about influencing and supporting others towards shared goals.
  21. Management: Management is about planning, organising and controlling resources to deliver safe, effective care.
  22. Teamwork: Healthcare is delivered by teams, and how well they work together affects safety and outcomes.
  23. Delegation: Delegation transfers responsibility for a task to another person while the delegator stays accountable for the decision to delegate.
  24. Supervision: Clinical supervision and preceptorship support practitioners to reflect, learn and stay well.
  25. Service improvement: Service improvement means redesigning how care is delivered so it works better for patients and staff.
  26. Clinical governance: Clinical governance is the framework through which healthcare organisations are accountable for continuously improving quality and safeguarding high standards of care.
  27. Risk management: Risk management is the systematic identification, assessment and control of risks to patients, staff and organisations.
  28. Healthcare policy: Health policy shapes what services exist, how they are funded and organised, and what nurses are expected to do.
  29. Digital health: Digital health uses technology such as electronic records, apps, remote monitoring and artificial intelligence to improve care.
  30. Health informatics: Health informatics is the use of information, data and technology to improve health and care.

School 17: Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding

28 lessons

Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Professional regulation: Professional regulation protects the public by setting standards for education and practice, keeping a register of who may practise, and acting when a professional's fitness to practise is in question.
  2. The NMC Code: The NMC Code sets out the professional standards that nurses, midwives and nursing associates must uphold.
  3. Duty of care: A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to others.
  4. Consent: Valid consent is required before examination, treatment or care.
  5. Mental Capacity Act 2005: The Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA) provides the legal framework in England and Wales for making decisions for, and supporting, people who may lack capacity.
  6. Mental Health Act: The Mental Health Act 1983 (as amended) allows people in England and Wales with a mental disorder to be assessed and treated in hospital without their consent in defined circumstances, with safeguards.
  7. Safeguarding adults: Safeguarding adults means protecting an adult's right to live in safety, free from abuse and neglect, while respecting their wishes.
  8. Safeguarding children: Safeguarding children means protecting them from maltreatment, preventing impairment of their health and development, and ensuring they grow up with safe and effective care.
  9. Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards and relevant reforms: The Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS) protect people who lack capacity to consent to arrangements for their care that amount to a deprivation of liberty.
  10. Human rights: Human rights are basic rights and freedoms belonging to everyone.
  11. Equality law: Equality law protects people from discrimination, harassment and victimisation, and requires public bodies to advance equality.
  12. Data protection: Data protection law governs how personal information is collected, used, stored and shared.
  13. Confidentiality: Confidentiality is a legal, ethical and professional duty to protect information people share in confidence.
  14. Information governance: Information governance is the framework of law, policy and practice that ensures information is handled legally, securely, efficiently and effectively.
  15. Record keeping: Health records are legal documents that support safe care, communication and accountability.
  16. Duty of candour: The duty of candour requires openness and honesty with people when things go wrong in their care.
  17. Negligence: Clinical negligence is a civil claim that a healthcare professional or organisation breached their duty of care and caused harm.
  18. Professional accountability: Accountability means being answerable for your decisions and actions.
  19. Clinical incidents: A clinical or patient safety incident is any unintended or unexpected event that could have harmed, or did harm, a person receiving care.
  20. Raising concerns: Raising concerns about care that puts people at risk is a professional duty under the NMC Code.
  21. Whistleblowing: Whistleblowing is making a disclosure in the public interest about wrongdoing, such as risks to patient safety, in a way that is protected by law.
  22. Professional boundaries: Professional boundaries are the limits that keep the relationship between a nurse and the people in their care safe, therapeutic and focused on the person's needs.
  23. Ethical dilemmas: Ethical dilemmas arise when values, duties or principles conflict and there is no clearly right answer.
  24. End-of-life law: The law at the end of life covers decisions to withhold or withdraw treatment, the limits on hastening death, and what must happen after death.
  25. Advance decisions: Advance decisions let people refuse specified medical treatment in advance, for a time when they lack capacity.
  26. Best interests: When a person lacks capacity to make a decision, any decision made for them must be in their best interests.
  27. Professional misconduct: Misconduct is a serious departure from the standards expected of a registered professional.
  28. Regulatory investigations: When a concern about a nurse is raised with the NMC, it follows a defined fitness to practise process.
