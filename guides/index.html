Study guides

How to revise in less time, from the research on memory. Start with the complete guide to active recall.

Active recall and revision

  • The complete guide to active recall for examsActive recall means testing yourself by pulling information out of memory, instead of reading it again.
  • Active recall vs re-reading: which actually works?Active recall works better.
  • How to use active recall for science subjectsIn science, use flashcards for definitions and facts, draw processes and diagrams from memory on a blank page, and practise calculations as unseen problems.
  • The best active recall schedule before finalsSpend the first four weeks covering every topic with active recall and spaced reviews, then use the last two weeks for timed past papers and your weakest topics.
  • How to make good flashcards: 9 rulesGood flashcards test one idea each, ask a precise question with one right answer, and come from your own course material.
  • How to revise in two weeksTriage first: list every topic, rank them by how likely they are to come up and how weak you are, and cut what you can't cover.
  • The 7 best study tools for university students in 2026Anki is best for serious long-term memorisation, Quizlet for quick shared vocabulary sets, Notion for organising a whole degree, and Gradify for turning your own lecture notes into checked flashcards and a revision plan.

Exam technique

  • Exam command words: what each one wantsThe command word tells you what kind of answer earns the marks: 'state' wants a fact, 'describe' wants what happens, 'explain' wants why or how, and 'evaluate' wants a judgement backed by evidence on both sides.
  • Exam question stems and the answer skeleton for eachUnfamiliar exam questions are usually familiar stems in disguise: 'account for this anomaly', 'suggest why', 'compare these two', 'design an investigation'.
  • Exam technique: how to get the marks you already knowGood exam technique turns what you know into marks: spend about 1 to 1.5 minutes per mark, read every question's command word and constraints before writing, answer the questions you're surest of first, and keep five minutes to check units, names and missed parts..
  • How to answer 6-mark and 8-mark questionsLong-answer questions are usually marked by levels: examiners look for a logical chain of correct points, not a list.
  • Definitions vs explanations: give the depth the marks ask forA definition is one precise sentence in technical terms; an explanation is a chain of steps showing how or why; an evaluation weighs it up.
  • Multiple-choice traps: how distractors are builtWrong options in a good multiple-choice question are built to catch specific mistakes.
  • Reading graphs and data: the 4-check scanBefore you describe any graph, scan four things: the axes and their units, the scale (is it linear or logarithmic, does it start at zero?), the overall trend versus any anomaly, and the caveats (error bars, sample size, control group).
  • Variables and controls: naming vs controllingThe independent variable is what you change, the dependent variable is what you measure, and control variables are everything you keep the same.
  • Unit conversion traps in science examsMost unit mistakes are factor-of-1,000 slips: cm³ to dm³, mg to g, mmol to mol, J to kJ.
  • Open-book and take-home exams: how to prepareOpen-book exams test understanding and application, not memory, and the clock is the real enemy: students who haven't revised lose time flipping through PDFs.

Seasonal plans and recovery

  • A winter break revision plan for January examsRest properly first, then keep the term's material alive with 20 minutes of flashcards a day, and save the intensive revision (two 50-minute blocks plus past questions a day) for the last two weeks.
  • A first-year study system: set it up in freshers' weekA simple system beats a perfect one: one folder per module, notes filed the same day, one weekly review session per module, and flashcards made as you go rather than in May.
  • Failed an exam? A resit recovery planA failed exam is information, not a verdict.
  • Resits, capped marks and compensation, explainedAt most UK universities, a failed module can be resat, but the resit mark is often capped at the pass mark (usually 40% for undergraduates).

Study methods

  • Seven study myths that waste revision timeMany of the most popular study habits (highlighting, re-reading, studying to your 'learning style', multitasking, and trusting AI summaries without checking) feel productive but do little for exam performance.
  • Study methods compared: which fits your workload?The two methods with the strongest evidence are active recall (testing yourself) and spaced repetition (spreading reviews out).
  • The blurting method: how to do it properlyBlurting is writing down everything you can remember about a topic on a blank page, without notes, then checking it against your notes in a different colour and filling the gaps.
  • Free alternatives to paid study appsMost of what study subscriptions charge for has a free option: Anki for spaced repetition, Gradify for flashcards and mind maps made from your notes (no account needed to try it), the free calculators and planners on this site, and your phone's built-in focus mode.

Degree survival guides

  • Surviving first-year medicinal chemistry and pharmacology labsLabs reward preparation: read the script and do the calculations before you arrive, keep a lab book anyone could follow, and write up within 48 hours while you remember.
  • How to revise organic mechanisms without rote learningMost organic mechanisms are combinations of a few patterns: a nucleophile attacks an electrophile, a leaving group leaves, a proton moves.
  • How to write a history source essayJudge each source on three things: its provenance (who made it, when, why and for whom), its content (what it says and what it leaves out) and its context (what you know about the period).
