Multiple-choice traps: how distractors are built

By the Gradify team · Updated for the 2026–27 academic year

Wrong options in a good multiple-choice question are built to catch specific mistakes. The four most common traps are a true statement that doesn't answer the question, reversed cause and effect, extreme absolutes like 'always' or 'never', and a unit or scale slip. Name the trap and the wrong option loses its pull.

Core rule: True isn't enough: it has to answer this question.

Trap 1: a true statement that doesn't answer the question

The option is correct science but answers a different question. Re-read the stem after choosing: does your option answer this question?

Key takeaway: True isn't enough: it has to answer this question.

Trap 2: reversed cause and effect

"Increased heart rate causes exercise" sounds familiar because the right words are there in the wrong order. Check the direction of every 'causes', 'leads to' or 'because'.

Trap 3: always, never, only, all

Absolute words are rarely true in biology, medicine or the social sciences. They're not automatically wrong, but they need one counter-example to fail.

Trap 4: unit and scale slips

Options that differ by a factor of 10 or 1,000 test whether you converted mg to g or cm³ to dm³. Do the unit check before looking at the options. The unit converter shows the factors.

Test yourself: Two options are 0.25 and 250. What's the trap likely to be? Reveal

A unit conversion by a factor of 1,000, such as cm³ to dm³ or mg to g.

How should I approach a multiple-choice question?

  1. Cover the options and answer from memory.
  2. Find your answer among the options.
  3. Check each remaining option for one of the four traps.
  4. If you guessed, mark it and log it: a lucky guess is still a gap.

Common pitfalls

  • Picking a true statement that answers a different question.
  • Trusting a right guess as knowledge.

Questions

Is it better to guess on multiple choice?

If there's no negative marking, never leave one blank: eliminate what you can and guess. Then treat every guess as a gap to revise, even if it was right.
