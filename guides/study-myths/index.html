Seven study myths that waste revision time

By the Gradify team · Updated for the 2026–27 academic year

Many of the most popular study habits (highlighting, re-reading, studying to your 'learning style', multitasking, and trusting AI summaries without checking) feel productive but do little for exam performance. The swaps are simple: test yourself, space your reviews, and check what you study against the source.

Myth 1: highlighting helps you remember

A 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated highlighting as low utility. It feels like work, but it doesn't make you retrieve anything. Swap: turn each highlight into a question and answer it later.

Key takeaway: Turn highlights into questions.

Myth 2: re-reading is revising

Re-reading builds familiarity, which feels like knowing. Testing yourself shows what you can actually recall. See active recall vs re-reading.

Myth 3: you have a learning style

Matching teaching to visual, auditory or kinaesthetic 'styles' hasn't held up in controlled studies. What helps everyone is combining words and pictures (dual coding).

Myth 4: multitasking while revising works

Switching between revision and messages costs attention every time. Use a focus timer and keep your phone out of the room.

Myth 5: cramming works

It can get you through tomorrow, but most of it is gone in a week. Spacing the same hours across days gives far better long-term memory (the spacing effect).

Myth 6: AI notes don't need checking

AI tools can state wrong facts fluently. If you revise from AI-made material, check it against your lecture notes or textbook. Gradify links every card to the line it came from and marks mismatches to check for this reason.

Key takeaway: Check AI-made notes against your source before you learn them.

Myth 7: more hours means better grades

An hour of testing yourself beats three hours of re-reading. Count recall, not hours.

Test yourself: Which two techniques did the 2013 Dunlosky review rate as high utility? Reveal

Practice testing (retrieval practice) and distributed practice (spacing).

Questions

Is highlighting a good study technique?

On its own, no: research rates it low utility. It's fine as a first step if you then turn the highlighted points into questions and test yourself.
