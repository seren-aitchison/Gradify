The complete guide to active recall for exams

By the Gradify team · Based on the learning research named in the text

Active recall means testing yourself by pulling information out of memory, instead of reading it again. It's one of the two best-supported revision methods in learning research (the other is spacing), and it works because the effort of retrieving a fact is what makes it stick.

What is active recall?

Every time you close your notes and try to answer a question, explain a process from memory or write out a definition on a blank page, you're using active recall. Researchers call it retrieval practice or the testing effect. The opposite is passive review: re-reading, highlighting and copying notes out neatly, which feel productive but leave little behind.

Why does active recall work?

Retrieval strengthens the route back to a memory. In a well-known 2006 study by Roediger and Karpicke, students who read a passage once and then practised recalling it remembered far more a week later than students who spent the same time re-reading it, even though the re-readers felt more confident. A large 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as one of only two "high utility" techniques, alongside distributed practice, and rated highlighting and re-reading as low utility.

Recall also shows you what you don't know. Re-reading makes everything look familiar; a blank page tells you the truth, so you spend your time on the gaps.

How to do active recall: a step-by-step method

  1. Learn it once, properly. Attend the lecture or read the chapter with the aim of understanding, not memorising.
  2. Turn it into questions. Make flashcards or a list of questions from your notes: definitions, processes, causes, comparisons, worked examples. Gradify can do this from a PDF and check each card against your notes.
  3. Answer from memory first. Say or write the full answer before you look. A half-answer in your head doesn't count.
  4. Check and mark honestly. Compare with the source. Anything wrong or vague comes back sooner.
  5. Space the reviews. Come back after 1, 3, 7 and 14 days, longer each time you get it right. The schedule generator gives you the dates.
  6. Finish with exam questions. In the last week or two, past papers under timed conditions are active recall in the exam's own format.

Five active recall techniques

  • Flashcards: best for definitions, facts and short processes. Keep one idea per card (how to make good flashcards).
  • The blank page: write everything you know about a topic, then check against your notes and fill the gaps in another colour.
  • Teach it: explain the topic aloud as if to a first-year (the Feynman technique).
  • Question lists: turn each heading of your notes into a question and answer it without looking (the left column of Cornell notes does this).
  • Past papers: the closest practice to the real thing.

Common mistakes

  • Peeking at the answer before committing to one.
  • Making cards so big that you can't tell whether you got them right.
  • Only reviewing cards you already know because it feels good.
  • Cramming every card the night before instead of spacing them.
  • Making cards from material you never understood: recall needs something to recall.

Common pitfalls

  • Peeking before you commit to an answer.
  • Making cards too big to mark right or wrong.

Questions

Is active recall better than re-reading?

Yes. Studies consistently find that testing yourself produces better long-term memory than spending the same time re-reading, even though re-reading feels more effective at the time.

How long should an active recall session be?

Twenty-five to fifty minutes is plenty. Stop when your answers become careless, and come back to the same material on a later day.

Can I use active recall for essay subjects?

Yes: recall the argument, the key evidence and the counter-arguments for each essay plan, then write timed answers. History, law and English students use question lists and blank-page plans this way.
