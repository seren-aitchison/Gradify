Exam technique: how to get the marks you already know

By the Gradify team · Updated for the 2026–27 academic year

Good exam technique turns what you know into marks: spend about 1 to 1.5 minutes per mark, read every question's command word and constraints before writing, answer the questions you're surest of first, and keep five minutes to check units, names and missed parts.

Core rule: Minutes per mark = exam minutes ÷ total marks. Stick to it.

Tools for this step: Question deconstructor (plan by marks) · Mistake log (find your pattern)

How long should I spend on each question?

Divide the exam length by the total marks. A 2-hour paper with 100 marks gives 1.2 minutes a mark, so a 6-mark question gets about 7 minutes. Write the finish time for each section on the paper at the start. If a question is taking much longer than its marks justify, move on and come back.

Key takeaway: Minutes per mark = exam minutes ÷ total marks. Stick to it.

How do I break down a multi-part question?

Use four steps: read the constraints (command word, marks, "using the data"), identify the concept being tested, map one point per mark, then check units and relevance. The question deconstructor walks you through it for any question you paste.

Test yourself: A 4-mark 'explain' question: how many linked points should you plan? Reveal

About four creditable points, each a cause linked to its effect.

What order should I answer questions in?

Skim the paper, then start with a question you're confident on: early marks calm nerves and bank time. Don't leave the long, high-mark questions until the last ten minutes.

What should I check at the end?

  • Every part answered (look for questions on the back of pages).
  • Units and significant figures on every calculation.
  • Names, candidate numbers and question numbers.
  • Command words: did you explain where it said explain?

Common pitfalls

  • Spending 15 minutes on a 4-mark question.
  • Not leaving time to check units and missed parts.

Questions

How can I improve my exam technique quickly?

Do one timed past paper, mark it with the mark scheme, and log why you dropped each mark. Most students find a pattern (misreading, timing, missing units) that's quick to fix.
