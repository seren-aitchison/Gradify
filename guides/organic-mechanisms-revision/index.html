How to revise organic mechanisms without rote learning

By the Gradify team · Updated for the 2026–27 academic year

Most organic mechanisms are combinations of a few patterns: a nucleophile attacks an electrophile, a leaving group leaves, a proton moves. Learn to spot the nucleophile and electrophile and draw curly arrows from electron-rich to electron-poor, then practise drawing each mechanism from memory on a blank page.

Core rule: Arrows go from electron-rich to electron-poor, always.

Tools for this step: Mistake log (log arrow mistakes)

What's the key to organic mechanisms?

Curly arrows show electron pairs moving, always from a lone pair or bond (electron-rich) to an atom that can accept them (electron-poor). If you can say why each arrow goes where it goes, you don't need to memorise the mechanism.

Key takeaway: Arrows go from electron-rich to electron-poor, always.

Which patterns cover most mechanisms?

  • Nucleophilic substitution (SN1, SN2)
  • Elimination (E1, E2)
  • Electrophilic addition to alkenes
  • Nucleophilic addition to carbonyls
  • Electrophilic substitution of arenes

See chemistry topics for sample cards and quizzes.

How do I practise mechanisms?

Blank page, name the reaction, draw the mechanism with every arrow and charge, then check. Do it again in two days. That's blurting for chemistry.

Test yourself: In SN2, why does the nucleophile attack from the side opposite the leaving group? Reveal

The C–X σ* orbital is on the far side, and the leaving group blocks the front: backside attack gives inversion of configuration.

Common pitfalls

  • Memorising arrows without the electron logic.
  • Forgetting charges and lone pairs.

Questions

How do you remember organic chemistry reactions?

Understand the electron flow rather than memorising: identify nucleophile and electrophile, draw arrows from electron-rich to electron-poor, and practise from a blank page with spaced reviews.
