Free Spanish course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

LanguageGradify's free Spanish course runs from First Steps in Spanish to Skills: Listening, Reading, Speaking, Writing and Translation: 145 lessons in 10 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for language learners.

Study material, not a qualification or an exam board. LanguageGradify teaches Spanish grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation and culture for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a Spanish teacher or lecturer, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, your exam board's specification and a reference grammar. It isn't an Instituto Cervantes (DELE), AQA, Edexcel or Eduqas product, doesn't award a CEFR level or any grade, and can't replace speaking practice with a teacher and with native speakers.

Schools and lessons

School 1: First Steps in Spanish

16 lessons

First Steps in Spanish: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The Spanish alphabet and its sounds: Spanish is spelt almost exactly as it sounds, so learning how each letter is pronounced lets you read aloud any word you meet.
  2. Vowels and diphthongs: Spanish has five pure vowel sounds, each always pronounced the same way, which is the single biggest step towards sounding Spanish.
  3. Stress and written accents: Every Spanish word of more than one syllable has one stressed syllable, and two short rules predict where it falls.
  4. Consonants that differ from English: Most Spanish consonants sound close to English, but a handful are pronounced differently and give away an English accent.
  5. Greetings and goodbyes: Greetings are the first Spanish you will use and the first impression you will make.
  6. Introducing yourself: Telling someone your name, age, where you are from and where you live is the core of the first conversation in any language.
  7. Tú and usted: Spanish has more than one word for "you", and choosing between them signals friendliness or respect.
  8. Numbers 0 to 100: Numbers come up in every exam paper and every conversation about age, prices, phone numbers and times.
  9. Days, months and dates: Days, months and dates let you talk about birthdays, timetables and plans.
  10. Telling the time: Times are tested in every listening paper and needed for every arrangement.
  11. Asking questions: Asking questions keeps a conversation going and is required in role plays at every level.
  12. Nouns: gender and number: Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine, and that gender controls the articles and adjectives around it.
  13. Definite and indefinite articles: Spanish articles agree with their nouns in gender and number, and they are used in places where English leaves them out.
  14. Subject pronouns: Subject pronouns say who does the action, but in Spanish the verb ending already does that job, so the pronouns are often left out.
  15. Ser: identity, origin and nationality: Ser is one of the two Spanish verbs for "to be", and the one that describes what someone or something is.
  16. Adjective agreement: Spanish adjectives change their endings to agree with the noun they describe, and they usually come after it.

School 2: The Present Tense, Family and Friends

15 lessons

The Present Tense, Family and Friends: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Regular -ar verbs in the present: Most Spanish verbs end in -ar, and nearly all of them follow one pattern in the present tense.
  2. Regular -er and -ir verbs in the present: The second and third groups of Spanish verbs, ending in -er and -ir, share almost all their present-tense endings.
  3. Using the present tense: The Spanish present tense covers more ground than the English simple present, translating "I do", "I am doing", "I have been doing" and sometimes "I will do".
  4. Tener and expressions with tener: Tener (to have) is irregular and appears in dozens of everyday expressions where English uses "to be".
  5. Ir, ir a and the near future: Ir (to go) is completely irregular and doubles as the easiest way to talk about the future.
  6. Irregular yo forms: hacer, poner, salir and others: A group of very common verbs is regular in the present except for the yo form, which often ends in -go or -zco.
  7. Stem-changing verbs: e to ie: In many common verbs the stressed e of the stem becomes ie in four of the six present-tense forms.
  8. Stem-changing verbs: o to ue and e to i: Two more groups of stem-changing verbs follow the same boot pattern as e to ie: stressed o becomes ue, and in some -ir verbs stressed e becomes i.
  9. Estar: location and states: Estar is the second verb for "to be", used for where things are and how people and things are at a particular moment.
  10. Ser or estar?: Choosing between ser and estar is one of the first real challenges of Spanish, and some adjectives change meaning depending on which verb you use.
  11. Hay and estar compared: "There is" and "there are" are both hay in Spanish, and choosing between hay and estar depends on whether you are introducing something new or locating something already known.
  12. Family and relationships: Family is a core GCSE theme and the subject of countless conversations.
  13. Describing people: appearance and personality: Describing what people look like and what they are like combines ser, tener and llevar with agreeing adjectives.
  14. Possessive adjectives: Possessive adjectives say whose something is: my, your, his, our, their.
  15. Gustar and expressing likes: Gustar doesn't work like English "to like": the thing liked is the subject and the person who likes it is the indirect object, as in "it pleases me".

School 3: Daily Life, School and Food

14 lessons

Daily Life, School and Food: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Reflexive verbs: Reflexive verbs describe actions people do to themselves, and Spanish uses many more of them than English.
  2. Daily routine: Describing a typical day is a staple of GCSE speaking and writing.
  3. Frequency and time expressions: Saying how often you do something adds detail to every answer and is rewarded in GCSE marking.
  4. School subjects and opinions: School is a GCSE theme in its own right, and giving and justifying opinions about subjects is the easiest way to show a range of language.
  5. Obligation: tener que, hay que and deber: Rules, duties and advice are expressed with three structures, each followed by an infinitive.
  6. Comparatives: Comparing people, places and things is essential for giving opinions.
  7. Superlatives: Superlatives single out the most or least of a group, and the -ísimo ending intensifies an adjective.
  8. Food and meals: Food is a favourite exam topic because it combines vocabulary, opinions and culture.
  9. At the restaurant: Ordering food and drink is a classic GCSE role play.
  10. Shopping for food: quantities and large numbers: Buying food needs quantities, containers and prices, and prices need numbers above 100.
  11. Demonstratives: Demonstratives point things out: this, that, these, those.
  12. My home and my room: Describing where you live combines vocabulary for types of home, rooms and furniture with hay, tener and estar.
  13. Prepositions of place: Prepositions of place say where things are in relation to each other, and most are two- or three-word phrases ending in de.
  14. Household chores and helping at home: Talking about chores combines obligation, frequency and opinion, which is why it is a favourite exam topic.

School 4: Town, Travel and Directions

14 lessons

Town, Travel and Directions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Places in town: Describing your town and saying what there is to do there is a core GCSE topic.
  2. Asking for and giving directions: Asking the way and understanding the answer is a classic role play and a listening favourite.
  3. Tú commands: Commands (the imperative) give instructions, advice and invitations.
  4. Usted and ustedes commands: Formal commands are used to strangers, customers and in written instructions, and they are formed from the yo form of the present.
  5. Transport and travel: Getting around involves choosing transport, buying tickets and understanding announcements.
  6. Weather and seasons: Describing the weather uses three different structures, which learners often mix up.
  7. Holiday plans: Talking about holidays lets you combine destinations, accommodation, activities and future time.
  8. At the hotel: Booking a room and sorting out problems at reception is a standard role play.
  9. Shopping for clothes: Buying clothes needs vocabulary for garments, colours, sizes and materials, and a few key phrases for trying things on and returning them.
  10. Direct object pronouns: Direct object pronouns replace the thing or person that receives the action, so you don't repeat nouns.
  11. Indirect object pronouns: Indirect object pronouns show to whom or for whom something is done.
  12. Double object pronouns: When a sentence has both an indirect and a direct object pronoun, Spanish has a fixed order and one important change: le and les become se.
  13. Invitations and arrangements: Inviting someone out, accepting or turning it down politely, and agreeing when and where to meet is a staple of everyday Spanish and of exam role plays.
  14. The present continuous: The present continuous, estar + gerund, describes what is happening at this moment.

School 5: Talking about the Past

14 lessons

Talking about the Past: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The regular preterite: The preterite is the past tense for completed actions: what happened, once or a set number of times, at a definite point.
  2. Spelling changes in the preterite: Some regular verbs change their spelling in the preterite, not because they are irregular but to keep the same sound.
  3. Irregular preterites: ser, ir, hacer and tener: The commonest verbs are irregular in the preterite, and four of them, ser, ir, hacer and tener, appear in almost every account of the past.
  4. More irregular preterites: estar, poder, poner, querer, venir and decir: A second group of common verbs has an irregular stem in the preterite with the same unstressed endings.
  5. Stem-changing -ir verbs in the preterite: Verbs ending in -ir that change their stem in the present also change in the preterite, but only in the third persons and in a different way.
  6. The imperfect tense: The imperfect describes the past as ongoing: what used to happen, what was going on, and what things were like.
  7. Describing childhood and the past: Describing what life was like when you were younger, and contrasting it with now, is a high-scoring exam task.
  8. Preterite or imperfect?: Choosing between the preterite and the imperfect is the central skill of talking about the past in Spanish.
  9. Narrating a story in the past: Telling a story combines the preterite and imperfect with connectives that move the action forward.
  10. The past continuous: The past continuous, estaba + gerund, stresses that an action was in progress at a moment in the past.
  11. The perfect tense: The perfect tense, he hablado, links the past to the present: what you have done, in a period that includes now.
  12. Preterite or perfect: Spain and Latin America: English speakers often find the choice between comí and he comido confusing, and native speakers themselves differ by region.
  13. The pluperfect: The pluperfect, había hablado, describes what had happened before another past event.
  14. Acabar de and hace with time: Two short structures handle time very precisely: acabar de + infinitive for something that has just happened, and hace for "ago" and for how long something has been going on.

School 6: The Future, the Conditional and Work

13 lessons

The Future, the Conditional and Work: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The future tense: The simple future tense says what will happen.
  2. Irregular futures: A small group of common verbs have irregular stems in the future.
  3. The future of probability: Spanish can use the future tense to guess or wonder about the present.
  4. The conditional: The conditional says what would happen.
  5. Polite requests and advice: The conditional and other structures help you make polite requests and give advice.
  6. Si clauses: possible conditions: Si clauses say what will happen if something occurs.
  7. Jobs and workplaces: Talking about jobs is a key GCSE topic.
  8. Future plans and careers: GCSE students often talk about their plans after school.
  9. Applying for a job: formal letters and emails: Formal writing in Spanish follows set conventions.
  10. Part-time jobs and money: Many young people have part-time jobs and earn pocket money.
  11. Relative pronouns: que, quien and donde: Relative pronouns join ideas and make sentences longer and more complex.
  12. Lo que and lo with adjectives: The neuter word lo helps express abstract ideas.
  13. Adverbs: Adverbs describe how, when, where and how often something happens.

School 7: The Subjunctive

15 lessons

The Subjunctive: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Forming the present subjunctive: The present subjunctive is a set of verb forms used after certain triggers such as wishes, emotions and doubt.
  2. Irregular present subjunctives: A handful of very common verbs do not follow the yo-form rule in the present subjunctive.
  3. The subjunctive after wishes and influence: When one person wants, asks or advises someone else to do something, Spanish uses que plus the subjunctive.
  4. The subjunctive after emotion: Expressions of feeling about someone else's actions or a situation take que plus the subjunctive.
  5. The subjunctive after doubt and denial: Spanish uses the subjunctive when the speaker doubts or denies that something is true.
  6. Impersonal expressions and the subjunctive: Phrases such as es importante que and es posible que take the subjunctive, but phrases of certainty such as es verdad que do not.
  7. The subjunctive after cuando and time conjunctions: Cuando and other time words take the subjunctive when they refer to the future.
  8. The subjunctive after para que and other conjunctions: Some conjunctions always take the subjunctive because they introduce purposes, conditions or events that have not happened.
  9. The subjunctive in relative clauses: A relative clause describing something unknown, hypothetical or non-existent takes the subjunctive.
  10. Negative commands: Negative commands in Spanish are formed with no plus the present subjunctive for every person.
  11. Ojalá, quizás and tal vez: Ojalá expresses hopes and wishes and always takes the subjunctive, while quizás and tal vez can take either mood.
  12. The imperfect subjunctive: The imperfect subjunctive is the past form of the subjunctive, used in si clauses, after past triggers and in polite requests.
  13. Si clauses: hypothetical conditions: To talk about unlikely or imaginary situations, Spanish uses si plus the imperfect subjunctive with the conditional.
  14. Sequence of tenses: The tense of the main verb decides whether a following subjunctive is present or imperfect.
  15. Como si and the pluperfect subjunctive: Como si always takes a past subjunctive, and the pluperfect subjunctive expresses unreal past situations.

School 8: Advanced Grammar and Accuracy

13 lessons

Advanced Grammar and Accuracy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Por and para: Por and para can both translate "for", but they are not interchangeable.
  2. Personal a: Spanish puts a small word, a, before a direct object that is a specific person or a pet.
  3. Verbs with prepositions: Many Spanish verbs need a particular preposition before a noun or infinitive, and some English verbs with prepositions have none in Spanish.
  4. The passive with ser: The true passive in Spanish uses ser plus a past participle that agrees with the subject.
  5. Passive and impersonal se: Spanish often uses se instead of the ser passive or a vague subject such as "people" or "you".
  6. Se for unplanned events: Spanish has a special structure for accidents and things that happen unintentionally, such as se me olvidó.
  7. Gerunds and infinitives: English uses the -ing form in many places where Spanish uses the infinitive.
  8. Verbs of becoming: Spanish has no single verb for "to become".
  9. The future perfect and conditional perfect: The future perfect says what will have happened and the conditional perfect says what would have happened.
  10. Diminutives and augmentatives: Spanish endings such as -ito and -ón change the size or tone of a word.
  11. Connectives and discourse markers: Connectives link ideas and help a text flow logically.
  12. Register: formal and informal Spanish: Register is the level of formality chosen to suit the reader and situation.
  13. Common errors for English speakers: Many mistakes made by English-speaking learners come from translating word for word.

School 9: The Spanish-Speaking World

16 lessons

The Spanish-Speaking World: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Spain's regions and languages: Spain is divided into autonomous communities, several of which have their own co-official language alongside Spanish.
  2. Festivals and traditions in Spain: Spain is famous for its festivals, from religious processions to tomato fights.
  3. The Civil War, Franco and the transition to democracy: The twentieth century shaped modern Spain through civil war, dictatorship and a peaceful move to democracy.
  4. Mexico: history and culture: Mexico has the largest Spanish-speaking population in the world and a rich mix of Indigenous and Spanish heritage.
  5. The Andes and Indigenous cultures: The Andes run through countries such as Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador, where Indigenous cultures and languages remain strong.
  6. The Southern Cone: The Southern Cone of South America includes Argentina, Chile and Uruguay.
  7. Central America and the Caribbean: Six Central American countries and three Caribbean territories have Spanish as a main language.
  8. Spanish in the United States: The United States has one of the largest Spanish-speaking populations in the world.
  9. Family and society today: Family life in Spain and Latin America has changed a great deal in recent decades.
  10. Technology and cyberspace: Technology and social media are central GCSE topics.
  11. Equality, migration and diversity: Spain and Latin America have long histories of migration and growing debates about equality.
  12. Art and architecture: The Spanish-speaking world has produced some of the most famous artists and buildings in history.
  13. Literature and film in Spanish: Spanish-language writers and directors are celebrated around the world.
  14. Music and dance: Music and dance are central to culture across the Spanish-speaking world.
  15. Regional variation: vocabulary and pronunciation: Spanish is spoken by more than 500 million people, and it varies between countries and regions.
  16. Voseo: In several Latin American countries, people say vos instead of tú, with different verb forms.

School 10: Skills: Listening, Reading, Speaking, Writing and Translation

15 lessons

Skills: Listening, Reading, Speaking, Writing and Translation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Listening strategies: Listening exams test whether you can pick out key information from spoken Spanish at natural speed.
  2. Understanding numbers, dates and details: Many listening and reading questions ask for precise details such as prices, times, dates and quantities.
  3. Listening for opinions and attitudes: Higher tier questions often ask what a speaker thinks or feels rather than what they say.
  4. Reading strategies: You do not need to know every word to understand a Spanish text.
  5. Reading literary texts: GCSE reading papers include short, adapted literary texts.
  6. Cognates and false friends: Thousands of Spanish words look like English words, which makes reading easier, but some are misleading.
  7. Pronunciation and intonation in practice: Clear pronunciation matters in the speaking exam, especially in the reading aloud task.
  8. Role plays: In the role play you complete a short transactional conversation using prompts.
  9. Describing a photo: The speaking exam includes a task based on one or more photos, followed by a conversation.
  10. Conversation and spontaneity: The conversation rewards developed, spontaneous answers.
  11. Writing structured paragraphs: Good GCSE writing is organised in clear paragraphs that cover every bullet point.
  12. Writing an extended essay: Higher tier writing includes an extended response that rewards range, complexity and accuracy.
  13. Translation into English: The reading paper includes a translation from Spanish into English.
  14. Translation into Spanish: The writing paper includes a translation from English into Spanish, which tests grammatical accuracy.
  15. Dictation: The listening paper includes a dictation in which you write down short Spanish sentences that you hear.
