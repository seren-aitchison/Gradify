Japanese: a free course on LanguageGradify

LanguageGradify's free Japanese course runs from First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana to Exam Skills: GCSE and JLPT: 182 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for language learners.

Study material, not a qualification or an exam board. LanguageGradify teaches Japanese writing, grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation and culture for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a Japanese teacher or lecturer, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, your exam board's specification and a reference grammar. It isn't a Japan Foundation, JEES (JLPT) or Pearson Edexcel product, doesn't award a JLPT level, a CEFR level or any grade, and can't replace speaking practice with a teacher and with native speakers.

Schools and lessons

School 1: First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana

15 lessons

First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The sounds of Japanese and the mora: Japanese has a small, regular sound system built from about a hundred short units called morae, and almost every sound will already feel familiar to an English speaker.
  2. Vowels, long vowels and devoicing: Japanese vowels are short, pure and few, but each can also be held for a second beat, and that length changes meaning.
  3. Pitch accent basics: Standard Japanese marks words with pitch, high and low, rather than the loud stressed syllables of English.
  4. Hiragana: the vowel, k and s rows: Hiragana is the basic Japanese script, a set of 46 letters each standing for one mora, and every learner starts here.
  5. Hiragana: the t, n and h rows: The t, n and h rows add fifteen more hiragana and bring in the first irregular sounds, chi, tsu and fu, where the letter doesn't simply combine a consonant with a vowel.
  6. Hiragana: the m, y, r and w rows and n: The last rows of the chart complete the 46 basic hiragana: the m, y and r rows, the two letters of the w row, and ん, the only kana that stands for a consonant on its own.
  7. Voiced sounds: dakuten and handakuten: Two small marks turn twenty hiragana into twenty-five new sounds: the dakuten makes a consonant voiced, and the handakuten turns h into p.
  8. Combined sounds: small ya, yu and yo: By writing a small や, ゆ or よ after a kana of the i column, Japanese spells sounds like kya, sho and ryu in a single mora.
  9. The small tsu and double consonants: A small っ before a consonant doubles it, adding a beat of held silence or hiss that changes meaning as much as any letter.
  10. Long vowels in hiragana and particle spellings: Hiragana spells long vowels by adding a vowel kana, but long e and long o have their own rules, and three particles are written with letters that are no longer pronounced as they look.
  11. Greetings through the day: Japanese greetings change with the time of day and with how close you are to the person, and they come with a bow rather than a handshake.
  12. Hajimemashite: introducing yourself: A Japanese self-introduction (jikoshōkai) follows a fixed and polite shape: it opens with hajimemashite and closes with yoroshiku onegaishimasu.
  13. Set phrases for home, school and the table: Japanese daily life runs on fixed phrases (aisatsu) that are said at set moments: before eating, leaving home, coming back, entering a room.
  14. Numbers 0 to 100: Japanese numbers are among the most regular in any language: learn one to ten and you can build every number to ninety-nine.
  15. Thank you, sorry and excuse me: Japanese thanks and apologies come in several strengths, and one word, sumimasen, does the work of "sorry", "excuse me" and even "thank you".

School 2: Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence

16 lessons

Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Katakana: the basic chart: Katakana is the second Japanese syllabary, with the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana but sharper, more angular shapes.
  2. Katakana: voiced sounds, combinations and the long vowel mark: Katakana uses the same dakuten, handakuten, small ya, yu and yo, and small tsu as hiragana, plus one device of its own: the long vowel bar ー.
  3. Loanwords and extended katakana: Japanese borrows thousands of words, mostly from English, and fits them into its own sound system with extra vowels and some special katakana combinations.
  4. What kanji are: on and kun readings: Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese that carry meaning as well as sound, and most have two kinds of reading: a Chinese-derived on reading and a native Japanese kun reading.
  5. Strokes, stroke order and radicals: Every kanji is built from a small set of stroke types written in a standard order, and many share recurring components called radicals.
  6. First kanji: numbers, days and time: Numbers, days of the week and time words are the first kanji most learners meet, because they appear on every calendar, timetable and price tag.
  7. First kanji: people, places and nature: Many early kanji began as pictures of what they mean, and others combine pictures to make new meanings.
  8. Okurigana and furigana: Japanese writing uses kana alongside kanji in two ways: okurigana spell the changing endings of verbs and adjectives, and furigana are small kana printed beside a kanji to show its reading.
  9. X wa Y desu: The pattern X は Y です (X wa Y desu, "X is Y") is the first Japanese sentence most learners build, and it shows three key features of the language: the topic particle, the polite copula and the verb at the end.
  10. Questions with ka and question words: Japanese turns a statement into a question simply by adding か at the end, and question words stay in the same place as the answer would.
  11. Negative and past of desu: です has four basic polite forms: present and past, affirmative and negative.
  12. The particle no: The particle の (no) links two nouns, with the first describing the second, and it covers English "'s", "of" and many noun-plus-noun phrases.
  13. Kore, sore, are and dore: Japanese points at things with a set of words built on ko-, so-, a- and do-, which divide space according to where the speaker and the listener are.
  14. Kono, sono, ano and koko, soko, asoko: The ko-so-a-do pattern extends to words that come before a noun (この, その, あの, どの) and to place words (ここ, そこ, あそこ, どこ).
  15. Nationalities, languages and jobs: Nationalities and languages in Japanese are built regularly from country names with the suffixes 人 (jin) and 語 (go), and jobs are simple nouns used with です.
  16. Mo: also and too: The particle も (mo) means "also" or "too", and in negative sentences "not either".

School 3: Verbs, Particles and Daily Routine

15 lessons

Verbs, Particles and Daily Routine: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Verb groups: ru-verbs, u-verbs and irregular verbs: Every Japanese verb belongs to one of three groups, and the group decides how the verb changes.
  2. The masu form: present and future: The masu form is the polite verb form used with people you do not know well, in class and at work.
  3. Negative and past masu forms: Once you know the masu form, its negative and past forms follow one pattern for every verb.
  4. Mashou and masen ka: suggestions and invitations: Japanese uses verb endings to make suggestions and invitations politely.
  5. The object particle o: Particles are short words that show the role of each noun in a sentence.
  6. Ni and e: direction and destination: To say where you are going, Japanese uses the particles に or へ with verbs of movement.
  7. De: place of action and means: The particle で has several uses.
  8. Ni for time and to for together: The particle に can mark a specific time, and と can mean "with" a person.
  9. Arimasu and imasu: existence and location: Japanese has two verbs for "there is" or "to be in a place": one for things and one for living beings.
  10. Wa and ga: a first look: The particles は and が both appear before the subject, but they do different jobs.
  11. Telling the time: Telling the time in Japanese uses numbers with counters for hours and minutes, with some irregular readings.
  12. Days of the week and dates: Days, months and dates in Japanese follow patterns with some irregular readings.
  13. Daily routine: Describing a daily routine brings together verbs, particles and time expressions.
  14. Frequency adverbs: Frequency adverbs say how often you do something.
  15. Hoshii and tai: wants: Japanese has two main ways to say what you want: ほしい for things and ～たい for actions.

School 4: Adjectives, Counters and Description

14 lessons

Adjectives, Counters and Description: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. I-adjectives: present and negative: Japanese adjectives come in two types, and each changes in its own way.
  2. Na-adjectives: present and negative: な-adjectives behave more like nouns than い-adjectives do.
  3. Past tense of adjectives: Adjectives in Japanese have past forms, so "it was cold" changes the adjective itself or the following です.
  4. Adjectives before nouns and joining adjectives: Adjectives can describe nouns directly, and two adjectives can be joined in one sentence.
  5. Suki, kirai, jouzu and heta: Likes, dislikes and skills are expressed with special な-adjectives that take が.
  6. Native numbers and the tsu counter: Japanese has two sets of numbers.
  7. Counters: nin, mai, hon, hiki and others: Japanese uses different counters depending on the type of thing being counted.
  8. Sound changes in counters: Many counters change sound after certain numbers.
  9. Large numbers, prices and shopping: Shopping in Japanese needs numbers into the thousands and tens of thousands, plus key shopping phrases.
  10. Ages, years and duration: Talking about age, years and how long something lasts uses special counters.
  11. Comparisons: yori and no hou ga: Comparing two things is common in conversation.
  12. Superlatives with ichiban: To say something is the most or best in a group, Japanese uses いちばん.
  13. Position words: ue, shita, naka and more: Position words describe exactly where things are.
  14. Describing a room and a town: Describing your room, home and town brings together adjectives, existence verbs and position words.

School 5: The Te-form and Plain Forms

16 lessons

The Te-form and Plain Forms: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Making the te-form: The て-form is one of the most useful verb forms in Japanese, used for requests, ongoing actions, permission and linking.
  2. Te kudasai: requests: The て-form plus ください makes a polite request.
  3. Te imasu: ongoing actions and resulting states: The て-form plus います has two main meanings: an action in progress and a state that results from a past change.
  4. Te mo ii desu and te wa ikemasen: Japanese uses the て-form to give and ask permission and to forbid actions.
  5. Linking actions and states with the te-form: The て-form links verbs, adjectives and nouns into longer sentences.
  6. Te kara: after doing: ～てから means "after doing" and makes the order of actions explicit.
  7. The dictionary form: The dictionary form is the plain non-past form of a verb.
  8. The nai form: The ない-form is the plain negative of a verb.
  9. The plain past: the ta form: The た-form is the plain past of a verb.
  10. Plain past negative: nakatta: The plain past negative says "didn't" in casual speech.
  11. Casual speech between friends: Friends and family speak casually using plain forms and other features.
  12. Ta koto ga aru: experiences: ～たことが あります means "have done" in the sense of experience.
  13. Tari tari: listing actions: ～たり～たり します lists examples of activities without giving a complete list or order.
  14. To omoimasu and to iimasu: と おもいます and と いいます let you give opinions and report what people say.
  15. Noun-modifying clauses: Japanese describes nouns by putting a whole clause before them, like English relative clauses but without "who" or "which".
  16. Kara and node: giving reasons: から and ので both mean "because" or "so".

School 6: Everyday Life: GCSE Themes

16 lessons

Everyday Life: GCSE Themes: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Family and relationships: Japanese has two sets of family words: humble ones for your own family and respectful ones for other people's.
  2. Describing people: appearance and personality: Describing people combines adjectives, the ～て います form and noun-modifying clauses.
  3. Hobbies, sport and free time: Talking about hobbies is a core GCSE topic.
  4. Health, the body and illness: Being able to describe symptoms and understand advice is important when living or travelling in Japan.
  5. School life in Japan: Japanese schools differ from UK schools in several ways.
  6. Part-time work and future plans: Talking about work and the future is a GCSE Higher topic.
  7. Technology and social media: Technology and social media are part of daily life and a common GCSE topic.
  8. Home and housing: Japanese homes have distinctive features, from the genkan to tatami rooms.
  9. Town, directions and transport: Getting around a Japanese town needs vocabulary for places, directions and transport.
  10. Holidays and travel in Japan: Travel in Japan offers hot springs, traditional inns and famous sights.
  11. Weather and seasons: Japan has four distinct seasons plus a rainy season and typhoons.
  12. Japanese food and eating out: Japanese food and table manners are an important part of the culture.
  13. Shopping and clothes: Japanese uses different verbs for wearing different items of clothing.
  14. The environment and recycling: Japan has detailed rules for sorting rubbish and strong recycling habits.
  15. Volunteering and global issues: GCSE Higher tasks include global issues and helping others.
  16. Festivals and celebrations: describing an event: Japanese festivals and celebrations mark the year.

School 7: N4 Grammar: Ability, Intention and Conditions

17 lessons

N4 Grammar: Ability, Intention and Conditions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The potential form: The potential form says what someone can do.
  2. The volitional form and to omotte imasu: The volitional form is the casual way to say "let's" and, with と おもって います, expresses what you are thinking of doing.
  3. Tsumori desu: plans: つもりです expresses a firm intention.
  4. Koto ga dekiru: ～ことが できる is another way to say "can".
  5. You ni naru and you ni suru: ～ように なる describes gradual change in ability or habit, while ～ように する describes making an effort.
  6. Hazu desu: expectation: はずです expresses what the speaker expects to be true based on information or logic.
  7. The to conditional: The と conditional expresses natural or automatic consequences: whenever A happens, B follows.
  8. The ba conditional: The ば conditional expresses hypothetical conditions, often focusing on what is needed for a result.
  9. The tara conditional: The たら conditional is the most flexible Japanese conditional, meaning "if" or "when".
  10. Nara: if that is the case: なら means "if that is the case" or "if it's about…".
  11. Choosing a conditional: Japanese has four main conditionals: と, ば, たら and なら.
  12. Ageru, kureru and morau: Japanese has three verbs for giving and receiving, chosen by who gives to whom.
  13. Te ageru, te kureru and te morau: The giving verbs combine with the て-form to show who does a favour for whom.
  14. The passive form: The passive form shows that something is done to the subject.
  15. The suffering passive: Japanese has an indirect passive that shows the subject was negatively affected by something.
  16. The causative form: The causative form means "make" or "let" someone do something.
  17. The causative-passive: The causative-passive means "be made to do" something, usually unwillingly.

School 8: Keigo and Social Register

14 lessons

Keigo and Social Register: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Plain, polite and honorific speech: Japanese speakers adjust their language to the situation and the listener.
  2. Uchi and soto: in-group and out-group: Keigo depends on whether people are inside or outside your group.
  3. Beautifying o and go: The prefixes お and ご make words sound more polite or refined.
  4. Sonkeigo: respectful verbs: Respectful language uses special verbs to describe the actions of superiors, customers and guests.
  5. O ni naru and the honorific passive: Verbs without special respectful forms can be made respectful with お～に なる or with the passive form.
  6. Kenjougo: humble verbs: Humble language lowers your own actions to show respect for the listener.
  7. O shimasu: humble patterns: Verbs without special humble forms can be made humble with お～します or ご～します.
  8. Choosing sonkeigo or kenjougo: Choosing between respectful and humble language depends on whose action you describe.
  9. Keigo in shops and restaurants: Shop and restaurant staff in Japan use a set of fixed keigo phrases.
  10. Telephone Japanese: Telephone calls use set phrases, especially in business.
  11. Formal emails and letters: Formal written Japanese follows set structures.
  12. Requests and refusals: indirectness: Japanese communication often prefers indirectness, especially in requests and refusals.
  13. Apologies and gratitude in formal settings: Japanese has many expressions for apologising and thanking, chosen by formality and situation.
  14. Aizuchi and conversational etiquette: Japanese conversation relies on frequent listener responses called aizuchi.

School 9: N3 Grammar: Nuance and Viewpoint

17 lessons

N3 Grammar: Nuance and Viewpoint: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Te shimau: completion and regret: ～て しまう adds the meaning of completing something fully or doing something regrettable.
  2. Te oku: doing in advance: ～て おく means doing something in advance or leaving something in a certain state.
  3. Te aru: deliberate results: ～て ある describes a state that someone deliberately created.
  4. Te iku and te kuru: ～て いく and ～て くる add direction in space or time.
  5. Te miru: trying: ～て みる means trying something to see what it is like.
  6. Sou desu: hearsay: Plain form + そうです reports information heard from others.
  7. Sou desu: it looks like: Stem + そうです describes how something looks or what seems about to happen, based on what you can see.
  8. Rashii: らしい has two meanings: "it seems" based on indirect evidence, and "typical of" a type.
  9. You da and mitai da: ようだ and みたいだ express that something seems so, based on the speaker's judgement, and make comparisons.
  10. Kamoshirenai and darou: かもしれない and だろう express possibility and probability.
  11. Noni: despite and in order to: のに has two main uses: "despite" with a feeling of surprise or frustration, and "in order to" for purpose.
  12. Tame ni and you ni: purpose: ために and ように both express purpose, but they are used with different kinds of verbs.
  13. Bakari and tokoro: ばかり and ところ add precise timing and emphasis.
  14. Wake and hazu: reasoning: わけ and はず help express reasoning, conclusions and explanations.
  15. Nagara and aida ni: ながら and あいだ express actions happening at the same time.
  16. Shika and dake: しか and だけ both mean "only", but しか needs a negative verb and adds a feeling of "not enough".
  17. Sentence-ending particles ne, yo and yo ne: Sentence-ending particles such as ね, よ and よね shape how a statement relates to the listener.

School 10: Kanji and Reading to N3

15 lessons

Kanji and Reading to N3: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The Joyo kanji list and the JLPT levels: Japan has an official list of kanji for everyday use, and learners plan their study around it and the JLPT levels.
  2. Radicals and semantic-phonetic kanji: Most kanji are built from smaller parts called radicals and components.
  3. Kanji compounds: on and kun readings together: Most Japanese words written in kanji are compounds of two or more characters.
  4. Rendaku and sound changes in compounds: When words combine in Japanese, sounds often change.
  5. Homophones and choosing the right kanji: Japanese has many words that sound the same but are written with different kanji.
  6. Native, Sino-Japanese and loan vocabulary: Japanese vocabulary comes from three main sources, and each tends to have a different feel.
  7. Four-character compounds and set phrases: Four-character compounds, or yojijukugo, express ideas concisely, often with origins in Chinese classics.
  8. Onomatopoeia: giongo and gitaigo: Japanese has a rich system of sound-symbolic words that describe sounds, states and feelings.
  9. Using a Japanese dictionary: Finding an unknown kanji or word is an important skill.
  10. Reading signs and notices: Signs and notices in Japan use short, formal kanji expressions.
  11. Reading emails and messages: Emails and messages range from formal business mail to casual chats.
  12. Reading news and articles: News articles use a formal written style with dense kango vocabulary.
  13. Reading stories: Stories in Japanese use narrative techniques such as plain past narration, dialogue and explanatory のだ.
  14. Vertical writing and Japanese punctuation: Japanese can be written vertically or horizontally, and it uses its own punctuation marks.
  15. Strategies for unknown kanji: Readers at every level meet unknown kanji.

School 11: Japanese Culture and Society

14 lessons

Japanese Culture and Society: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. A short history of Japan: Knowing the main periods of Japanese history helps you understand culture, place names and everyday references.
  2. Shinto and Buddhism in everyday life: Shinto and Buddhism both shape Japanese customs, often side by side.
  3. The history of the writing system: The Japanese writing system developed over many centuries by adapting Chinese characters.
  4. Festivals and the Japanese calendar: The Japanese calendar combines the Western calendar, era names and seasonal customs.
  5. New Year in Japan: New Year (お正月) is the most important holiday in Japan, a time for family, food and fresh beginnings.
  6. The tea ceremony and traditional arts: Traditional arts such as the tea ceremony, flower arranging, calligraphy and theatre express key Japanese aesthetic values.
  7. Gift-giving, bowing and etiquette: Etiquette shapes everyday interactions in Japan, from giving gifts to exchanging business cards.
  8. Anime, manga and popular culture: Anime, manga, games and music have made Japanese popular culture famous worldwide.
  9. Work culture and society: Japanese work culture has distinctive traditions and is changing quickly.
  10. An ageing society: Japan has one of the oldest populations in the world and a falling birth rate.
  11. Japanese cities and regions: Japan's regions differ in climate, food and character.
  12. Dialects: Kansai-ben and standard Japanese: Japanese has many regional dialects, and Kansai-ben is the most widely heard after standard Japanese.
  13. Washoku: Japanese food culture: Washoku, traditional Japanese food culture, is recognised by UNESCO for its respect for nature and seasons.
  14. Japan and the world: Japan plays a major role in the world through its economy, culture, diplomacy and tourism.

Exam Skills: GCSE and JLPT: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The JLPT: levels and format: The Japanese-Language Proficiency Test is the most widely taken Japanese exam in the world.
  2. GCSE Japanese: the four skills: GCSE Japanese assesses listening, speaking, reading and writing.
  3. Listening strategies: Listening exams test understanding at natural speed.
  4. Reading strategies for exams: Exam reading requires understanding texts accurately within a time limit.
  5. JLPT vocabulary and grammar questions: The JLPT language knowledge section uses several question types for kanji, vocabulary and grammar.

  9. Writing: longer texts and genkou youshi: Longer writing tasks require organised paragraphs, a range of grammar and correct layout.
  10. Translation from Japanese: Translating from Japanese into English tests precise understanding.
  11. Translation into Japanese: Translating from English into Japanese tests accurate grammar, word order and script.
  12. Pronunciation and pitch in speaking exams: Clear pronunciation helps examiners understand you.
  13. Common errors and how to avoid them: Many learners make the same mistakes in Japanese.
