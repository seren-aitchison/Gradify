Free Research and the Dissertation course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

PostGradify's free Research and the Dissertation course runs from The Research Landscape and Research Degrees to The Viva and Beyond: 197 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for master's and PhD students, researchers.

General guidance, not your institution's rules. PostGradify teaches research skills for Master's and doctoral study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by research supervisors, so they may contain errors. Regulations on progression, word limits, ethics review, data protection and examination differ between universities and funders and change over time: always follow your own institution's regulations, your ethics committee and your supervisor. Nothing here is legal advice on data protection or a substitute for formal ethical approval.

Schools and lessons

School 1: The Research Landscape and Research Degrees

17 lessons

The Research Landscape and Research Degrees: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is research?: Research is systematic, original investigation that produces new knowledge and shares it so others can check and use it.
  2. An original contribution to knowledge: A doctorate must make an original contribution to knowledge; a Master's dissertation usually needs to show advanced understanding and some independent analysis.
  3. Disciplines and interdisciplinarity: Every researcher works within a discipline's questions, methods and standards of evidence.
  4. The UK research system and its funders: UK university research is funded through a "dual support" system: block funding allocated by research assessment, and competitive project grants from UK Research and Innovation, charities, government and industry.
  5. Research degrees: Master's, MPhil and PhD: UK postgraduate study includes taught Master's degrees with a dissertation, research Master's (MRes, MPhil) and doctorates.
  6. Professional doctorates and the PhD by publication: Not every doctorate is a traditional PhD by thesis.
  7. Doctoral training: DTPs, CDTs and studentships: Much UK doctoral study is funded through studentships, many awarded by UKRI's councils through Doctoral Training Partnerships and Centres for Doctoral Training.
  8. Progression: upgrade and confirmation: Doctoral students are formally reviewed during their degree, most importantly at the upgrade (also called transfer or confirmation), when an independent panel decides whether the project is on track for a PhD.
  9. Researcher development and the Vitae RDF: The Vitae Researcher Development Framework (RDF) describes the knowledge, behaviours and attributes of effective researchers in four domains.
  10. The supervisory relationship: The relationship with your supervisors is the strongest single influence on a research degree experience.
  11. Managing your supervisor: "Managing your supervisor" means taking responsibility for making supervision work: preparing meetings, asking for the feedback you need and keeping a record.
  12. Regulations, responsibilities and expectations: Every research degree is governed by the awarding university's regulations and code of practice, which set out registration periods, supervision, progression, examination and the responsibilities of…
  13. Research culture and community: Research culture is the behaviours, values, expectations and norms of research communities, and it shapes how research is done and who thrives.
  14. Research project management: A research degree is a long, uncertain project with a fixed deadline, so project management skills matter as much as subject knowledge.
  15. Writing a research proposal: A research proposal persuades a reader (an admissions panel, a funder or an upgrade panel) that a question matters, can be answered and can be answered by you in the time available.
  16. Planning time: milestones and Gantt charts: A research degree has a fixed end date and many dependent tasks.
  17. Wellbeing and resilience in research: Research degrees can be intellectually rewarding and personally demanding.

School 2: Research Questions and the Literature Review

17 lessons

Research Questions and the Literature Review: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Choosing a research topic: A good research topic is interesting, significant and feasible.
  2. Formulating research questions: Research questions define what a study is trying to find out.
  3. Aims, objectives and hypotheses: Aims, objectives and hypotheses translate research questions into a plan.
  4. Finding the gap: gap-spotting and problematisation: A research contribution responds to something missing or questionable in existing knowledge.
  5. Searching databases: Academic databases are the main route to published research.
  6. Search strategies: Boolean operators and subject headings: A systematic search strategy finds relevant literature efficiently and reproducibly.
  7. Grey literature: Much valuable evidence is not published in academic journals.
  8. Reference management: Postgraduate research involves hundreds of sources, so references must be managed systematically.
  9. Critical reading: Postgraduate reading means questioning, not just summarising.
  10. Evaluating sources: Not all sources are equally trustworthy.
  11. Critical appraisal tools: Critical appraisal tools provide structured ways to judge the quality and risk of bias in studies.
  12. Types of literature review: "Literature review" covers many different approaches.
  13. Systematic reviews and PRISMA: Systematic reviews follow explicit, reproducible methods to answer a focused question.
  14. Scoping reviews: Scoping reviews map the breadth of research on a topic.
  15. Synthesising the literature: Synthesis means bringing studies together to say something new, not listing them one by one.
  16. Structuring a literature review: A literature review needs a clear structure that leads the reader to your research questions.
  17. Theoretical and conceptual frameworks: Frameworks give a study its lens and logic.

School 3: Philosophy of Research

15 lessons

Philosophy of Research: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Ontology: Ontology is the study of what exists and what reality is like.
  2. Epistemology: Epistemology is the study of knowledge: what counts as knowledge and how we can know things.
  3. Research paradigms: A research paradigm is a shared set of beliefs about how research should be done.
  4. Positivism: Positivism holds that knowledge should be based on observable, measurable facts.
  5. Post-positivism: Post-positivism keeps the goal of studying an objective reality but accepts that knowledge is fallible and imperfect.
  6. Interpretivism: Interpretivism holds that social research must understand the meanings people give to their actions.
  7. Social constructionism: Social constructionism holds that much of what we take as natural or obvious is created through social processes.
  8. Critical realism: Critical realism combines a realist ontology with the recognition that knowledge is fallible and socially produced.
  9. Pragmatism: Pragmatism judges ideas by their practical consequences.
  10. Critical and transformative paradigms: Some researchers see research as a way to challenge injustice.
  11. Deduction, induction and abduction: Researchers reason from theory to data, from data to theory, or to the best explanation.
  12. The role of theory in research: Theory helps researchers explain, predict and interpret.
  13. Popper and falsification: Karl Popper argued that science advances by trying to prove theories wrong.
  14. Kuhn and paradigm shifts: Thomas Kuhn argued that science develops through periods of normal science interrupted by revolutions.
  15. Reflexivity and positionality: Researchers bring their identities, values and experiences to their work.

School 4: Research Design

16 lessons

Research Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is research design?: Research design is the overall plan that connects a research question to the evidence needed to answer it.
  2. Aligning question, philosophy and method: A coherent study aligns its research question, philosophical assumptions, methodology and methods.
  3. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed approaches: Research approaches are often grouped as quantitative, qualitative or mixed.
  4. Experimental designs: Experiments manipulate a variable and use comparison to test causal claims.
  5. Quasi-experimental designs: Quasi-experiments test interventions without random allocation, often in real-world settings.
  6. Survey research design: Surveys collect standardised data from a sample to describe a population or examine relationships.
  7. Cross-sectional and longitudinal designs: Cross-sectional designs collect data at one point in time, while longitudinal designs follow cases over time.
  8. Case study research: Case study research examines one or a small number of cases in depth within their real-life context.
  9. Ethnography: Ethnography studies people's everyday lives and cultures through sustained immersion in a setting.
  10. Action research: Action research combines inquiry with deliberate action to improve practice, usually with participants as co-researchers.
  11. Evaluation research: Evaluation research judges the design, implementation and effects of programmes, policies and interventions.
  12. Sampling strategies: Sampling is how researchers select cases from a wider population or setting.
  13. Sample size and saturation: Deciding how many participants or cases to include depends on the design.
  14. Validity and reliability: Validity and reliability are central criteria for judging the quality of research, especially quantitative research.
  15. Trustworthiness in qualitative research: Qualitative researchers often use trustworthiness criteria rather than validity and reliability.
  16. Pilot studies: Pilot and feasibility studies test whether a study can be carried out as planned before the main study.

School 5: Quantitative Methods and Statistics

24 lessons

Quantitative Methods and Statistics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Variables and levels of measurement: Quantitative research measures variables, and the level of measurement determines which statistics are appropriate.
  2. Questionnaire design: A good questionnaire turns concepts into clear, unbiased questions that respondents can answer accurately.
  3. Measurement scales and psychometrics: Psychometrics is the science of measuring psychological and social constructs.
  4. Data screening and cleaning: Before analysis, data must be checked for errors, outliers and violations of assumptions.
  5. Missing data: Almost every dataset has missing values, and how they are handled can bias results.
  6. Descriptive statistics: Descriptive statistics summarise the main features of a dataset.
  7. Distributions and the normal curve: Probability distributions describe how values of a variable are spread.
  8. Visualising data: Good graphs reveal patterns, outliers and uncertainty that tables can hide.
  9. Sampling distributions and confidence intervals: A statistic calculated from a sample varies from sample to sample, and its sampling distribution describes that variation.
  10. Null hypothesis significance testing: Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is the most widely used framework for statistical inference.
  11. Effect sizes: Effect sizes quantify how large an effect or relationship is, independent of sample size.
  12. Statistical power and sample size: Underpowered studies are common and can mislead even when results are significant.
  13. Type I and Type II errors: Every significance test risks two kinds of wrong decision.
  14. t-tests: The t-test compares means between two groups or conditions.
  15. Analysis of variance (ANOVA): ANOVA compares means across three or more groups or conditions in a single test.
  16. Chi-square tests: Chi-square tests analyse categorical data by comparing observed and expected frequencies.
  17. Correlation: Correlation measures the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables.
  18. Linear regression: Simple linear regression predicts an outcome from one predictor using a straight line.
  19. Multiple regression: Multiple regression predicts an outcome from several predictors at once.
  20. Logistic regression: Logistic regression models binary outcomes such as pass or fail, using odds and odds ratios.
  21. Non-parametric tests: Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about distributions, often by analysing ranks.
  22. Bayesian statistics: Bayesian statistics updates beliefs about parameters in the light of data using Bayes' theorem.
  23. Statistical software: SPSS, R and Stata: Postgraduate researchers can choose among several statistical packages.
  24. Reporting statistics: Clear, complete statistical reporting allows readers to judge and reproduce findings.

School 6: Qualitative Methods

21 lessons

Qualitative Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is qualitative research?: Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and social processes, usually through words, images and observation.
  2. Qualitative research designs: Qualitative research includes several established methodological traditions, each with its own aims and procedures.
  3. Purposive and theoretical sampling: Qualitative studies select participants and settings deliberately for the information they can provide.
  4. Semi-structured interviews: Semi-structured interviews are the most widely used qualitative data collection method.
  5. Interviewing skills: Good interview data depend on the interviewer's skills in listening, questioning and managing the encounter.
  6. Focus groups: Focus groups gather data through facilitated group discussion.
  7. Participant observation: Participant observation collects data by taking part in and watching everyday activities in a setting.
  8. Documents and visual methods: Documents and images are rich sources of qualitative data.
  9. Online and remote qualitative research: Qualitative data are increasingly collected online, through video calls, messaging and digital platforms.
  10. Transcription: Transcription turns recorded talk into text for analysis and involves many interpretive choices.
  11. Coding qualitative data: Coding is the process of labelling segments of qualitative data to organise and interpret them.
  12. Thematic analysis: Braun and Clarke: Thematic analysis is a widely used method for identifying and interpreting patterns of meaning across qualitative data.
  13. Reflexive thematic analysis: Braun and Clarke have developed their approach into reflexive thematic analysis, which emphasises researcher subjectivity and interpretation.
  14. Grounded theory: Grounded theory develops theory from data through iterative collection and analysis.
  15. Interpretative phenomenological analysis: Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) explores how individuals make sense of significant life experiences.
  16. Discourse analysis: Discourse analysis studies how language constructs social reality, identities and power.
  17. Narrative analysis: Narrative analysis studies the stories people tell and how they tell them.
  18. Framework analysis: Framework analysis is a structured, matrix-based method widely used in applied policy and health research.
  19. Qualitative data analysis software: Computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software (CAQDAS) helps researchers organise, code and retrieve qualitative data.
  20. Rigour in qualitative research: Qualitative research must show that it is rigorous, credible and ethically sound.
  21. Writing up qualitative findings: Writing is part of qualitative analysis, and findings must be presented in a way that is persuasive and true to the data.

School 7: Mixed Methods and Evidence Synthesis

12 lessons

Mixed Methods and Evidence Synthesis: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is mixed methods research?: Mixed methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches within one study or programme.
  2. Convergent designs: In a convergent design, quantitative and qualitative data are collected and analysed separately and then merged.
  3. Explanatory sequential designs: An explanatory sequential design starts with quantitative research and follows it with qualitative research to explain the results.
  4. Exploratory sequential designs: An exploratory sequential design starts with qualitative research and uses the findings to build a quantitative phase.
  5. Integration and joint displays: Integration is the defining feature of mixed methods research.
  6. Quality in mixed methods research: Mixed methods studies must meet quality standards for each strand and for their integration.
  7. Meta-analysis: Meta-analysis statistically combines the results of several studies to estimate an overall effect.
  8. Qualitative evidence synthesis: Qualitative evidence synthesis brings together findings from multiple qualitative studies.
  9. Realist reviews: Realist reviews synthesise evidence to explain how and why complex interventions work in different contexts.
  10. Secondary data analysis: Secondary data analysis uses data collected by others to answer new questions.
  11. Big data and computational methods: Big data and computational methods allow researchers to analyse very large, often digital, datasets.
  12. Using AI tools in research: Generative AI tools are increasingly used in research, from searching literature to drafting text.

School 8: Research Ethics and Integrity

19 lessons

Research Ethics and Integrity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Principles of research ethics: Research ethics protects participants, researchers and the trust society places in research.
  2. The history of research ethics: Modern research ethics grew out of serious abuses.
  3. Informed consent: Informed consent means people agree to take part in research freely and with enough understanding.
  4. Confidentiality and anonymity: Participants share information trusting it will be protected.
  5. Avoiding harm: Researchers must minimise the risk of harm to participants, themselves and others.
  6. Vulnerable participants: Some people face greater risks or barriers in research.
  7. Deception and covert research: Some studies withhold information or observe people without their knowledge.
  8. Research ethics committees: Research ethics committees review proposed studies independently before they begin.
  9. Applying for ethical approval: A clear, well-prepared ethics application speeds approval and improves the research.
  10. Health research: HRA approval: Health and social care research in the UK has its own approval system.
  11. Ethics in online research: Online research raises new ethical questions about privacy, consent and identification.
  12. The Concordat to Support Research Integrity: The Concordat to Support Research Integrity sets out the UK's national framework for good research conduct.
  13. The UKRIO Code of Practice for Research: The UK Research Integrity Office provides practical guidance on good research practice.
  14. Research misconduct: Research misconduct undermines trust in research and can harm people.
  15. Questionable research practices: Questionable research practices fall short of misconduct but still distort the research record.
  16. Authorship and plagiarism: Authorship gives credit and responsibility for research.
  17. Conflicts of interest: Conflicts of interest can bias research or create the appearance of bias.
  18. UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018: Research using personal data must comply with data protection law.
  19. Special category data and research: Some personal data are especially sensitive and need extra protection.

School 9: Research Data Management and Open Research

14 lessons

Research Data Management and Open Research: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Research data management: Research data management covers how data are organised, stored, documented, shared and preserved.
  2. Data management plans: A data management plan sets out how data will be handled during and after a project.
  3. File organisation and version control: Clear file organisation and version control prevent confusion and lost work.
  4. Secure storage and backup: Research data must be protected against loss, theft and unauthorised access.
  5. Anonymising and pseudonymising data: Removing or masking identifiers protects participants and can allow data to be shared.
  6. Archiving and data sharing: Sharing and archiving data allows others to verify, reuse and build on research.
  7. The FAIR principles: The FAIR principles describe how to make data findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.
  8. Open access publishing: Open access makes research publications free to read.
  9. Preregistration and registered reports: Preregistration and registered reports commit researchers to their plans before data are collected.
  10. Reproducibility and the replication crisis: Many published findings have failed to replicate.
  11. Open data and open code: Sharing data and code allows others to check, reproduce and build on research.
  12. Reproducible workflows: A reproducible workflow lets you or others regenerate results from raw data.
  13. Persistent identifiers: ORCID and DOIs: Persistent identifiers give researchers and outputs permanent, unique references.
  14. Copyright and licensing: Copyright and licences determine how research materials can be used and shared.

School 10: Academic Writing and the Thesis

16 lessons

Academic Writing and the Thesis: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Academic writing style: Academic writing at postgraduate level is precise, evidence-based and clear.
  2. Writing habits and productivity: Writing a thesis is a long project that depends on steady habits.
  3. Building an argument: A thesis must make a coherent argument, not just report information.
  4. Paragraphs and signposting: Well-structured paragraphs and signposting guide readers through complex arguments.
  5. The structure of a thesis: Theses follow recognisable structures that vary by discipline and approach.
  6. Writing the introduction: The introduction sets up the whole thesis.
  7. Writing the methodology chapter: The methodology chapter explains and justifies how the research was done.
  8. Writing results and findings: The results or findings chapter presents what the research found.
  9. Writing the discussion: The discussion interprets findings and shows what they mean for the field.
  10. Writing the conclusion: The conclusion draws the thesis together and leaves a lasting impression.
  11. Writing the abstract: The abstract is often the first and only part of a thesis people read.
  12. Referencing and citation styles: Accurate referencing gives credit, allows readers to find sources and supports your argument.
  13. Using sources and avoiding plagiarism: Integrating sources well shows critical engagement and avoids plagiarism.
  14. Editing and revising: Good writing comes from revision.
  15. Working with feedback: Feedback from supervisors and peers is essential to improving a thesis.
  16. Writing in English as an additional language: Many doctoral researchers write in English as an additional language.

School 11: Publishing, Presenting and Impact

14 lessons

Publishing, Presenting and Impact: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The academic publishing system: Academic publishing shares research findings and builds careers.
  2. Choosing a journal: Choosing the right journal increases the chance of acceptance and reaching the right readers.
  3. Writing a journal article: A journal article is shorter and more focused than a thesis chapter.
  4. Peer review: Peer review is how experts evaluate research before publication.
  5. Responding to reviewers: Responding well to reviewers greatly increases the chance of acceptance.
  6. Predatory journals: Predatory journals exploit researchers by charging fees without proper peer review.
  7. Research metrics: Metrics such as citations and impact factors are widely used but often misused.
  8. Conference presentations: Presenting at conferences shares research, builds networks and develops communication skills.
  9. Academic posters: Posters present research visually and invite conversation.
  10. Public engagement: Public engagement involves sharing research with and learning from people outside academia.
  11. Research impact: Research impact is the difference research makes beyond academia.
  12. The Research Excellence Framework: The Research Excellence Framework assesses the quality of research in UK universities.
  13. Knowledge exchange and policy engagement: Knowledge exchange connects research with organisations, practitioners and policymakers.
  14. Communicating research online: Online communication helps researchers share work, find collaborators and reach wider audiences.

School 12: The Viva and Beyond

12 lessons

The Viva and Beyond: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Preparing to submit: Submitting a thesis is the culmination of years of work.
  2. Examiners and the viva: The viva is an oral examination of the thesis.
  3. Preparing for the viva: Preparing well for the viva builds confidence and helps candidates show their expertise.
  4. In the viva: How candidates handle the viva can make a real difference.
  5. Viva outcomes and corrections: The viva ends with a recommendation about the thesis.
  6. Academic careers: Many doctoral graduates consider academic careers.
  7. Postdoctoral research: Postdoctoral posts are often the first job after a doctorate.
  8. Fellowships and grant writing: Winning research funding is central to many research careers.
  9. Careers beyond academia: Most doctoral graduates build careers outside academia.
  10. Transferable skills and the RDF: The Researcher Development Framework describes the knowledge, behaviours and attributes of successful researchers.
  11. Teaching in higher education: Many doctoral researchers teach while studying, and teaching is central to most academic roles.
  12. Networking and your research profile: Networks and a visible research profile open doors to collaborations, jobs and opportunities.
