Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does organic chemistry study?

Compounds based on carbon. From “Introduction to organic chemistry” →

In plain English: Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Key idea: Organic chemistry studies carbon compounds, usually with hydrogen and often other elements; carbon's bonding allows millions of compounds used as fuels, medicines and materials and found in living things, and Wöhler's urea synthesis began the end of vitalism.

What is the test for an alkene?

Bromine water is decolourised. From “Alkenes” →

In plain English: Alkenes contain a C=C double bond. Key idea: Alkenes, CₙH₂ₙ, contain a σ and an electron-rich π bond and undergo electrophilic addition with hydrogen, halogens, hydrogen halides and steam; bromine water tests for unsaturation and Markovnikov's rule predicts major products.

By what unit do members of a homologous series differ?

CH₂. From “Functional groups” →

In plain English: Functional groups determine how organic molecules react. Key idea: Functional groups such as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, esters, amines and amides determine reactivity; a homologous series shares a functional group and general formula, differing by CH₂ with similar chemistry and gradually changing physical properties.

Which conformation of ethane is most stable?

Staggered. From “Conformational analysis” →

In plain English: Rotation about single bonds produces conformations. Key idea: Conformations arise from rotation about single bonds; staggered and anti forms are lowest in energy, cyclohexane adopts a strain-free chair with axial and equatorial positions and large substituents prefer equatorial positions.

What is the enantiomeric excess of a racemate?

0%. From “Optical activity” →

In plain English: Chiral compounds rotate plane-polarised light. Key idea: Chiral compounds rotate plane-polarised light, measured with a polarimeter; specific rotation corrects for path length and concentration, enantiomeric excess compares observed rotation with that of the pure enantiomer and the sign of rotation does not give R or S.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Organic Chemistry Foundations

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is an organic compound? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ethanol, C₂H₅OH. Ethanol is built on a chain of carbon atoms bonded to hydrogen.

  2. What is the bond angle around an sp² carbon? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    120°. Three electron regions in a plane spread out to 120° apart.

  3. Which compound is a hydrocarbon? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Propane, C₃H₈. Propane contains only carbon and hydrogen atoms.

  4. Why was Wöhler's urea synthesis important? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It showed organic compounds can be made without living things. Making urea from an inorganic salt challenged the idea of a vital force.

  5. Why does carbon form many more chain compounds than silicon? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    C–C bonds are strong and carbon compounds resist oxidation. Weak Si–Si bonds and reactive Si–H bonds limit silicon chains.

  6. Why do crude oil fractions separate in a fractionating column? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Fractions with different boiling points condense at different heights. The column is hotter at the bottom, so fractions condense by boiling point.

  7. Why is CO₂ usually treated as inorganic although it contains carbon? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is a simple compound with no C–H bonds and behaves like inorganic oxides. Convention groups simple carbon oxides and carbonates with inorganic chemistry.

  8. In propene, why do the carbons have different shapes? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Two carbons share a double bond and are sp², while the third is sp³. Each carbon's geometry depends on its own pattern of bonds.

  9. Why is incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons dangerous? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It produces toxic carbon monoxide and soot. Too little oxygen gives CO, which blocks oxygen transport in blood.

Key takeaways

  • Organic chemistry studies carbon compounds, usually with hydrogen and often other elements; carbon's bonding allows millions of compounds used as fuels, medicines and materials and found in living things, and Wöhler's urea synthesis began the end of vitalism.
  • Carbon forms four strong covalent bonds and catenates into chains and rings with single, double and triple bonds; sp³ carbons are tetrahedral, sp² trigonal planar and sp linear, giving organic molecules their shapes.
  • Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen and include alkanes, alkenes, alkynes and arenes; crude oil is separated by fractional distillation and cracked into useful products, and combustion gives CO₂ and water or, when incomplete, CO and soot.
  • Alkanes are saturated sp³ hydrocarbons, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, whose boiling points rise with chain length and fall with branching; they are fairly unreactive but burn and undergo free-radical substitution with halogens in UV light.
  • Alkenes, CₙH₂ₙ, contain a σ and an electron-rich π bond and undergo electrophilic addition with hydrogen, halogens, hydrogen halides and steam; bromine water tests for unsaturation and Markovnikov's rule predicts major products.
  • Alkynes, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, contain a linear sp C≡C with one σ and two π bonds; they add hydrogen, halogens and hydrogen halides, Lindlar's catalyst gives cis-alkenes and terminal alkynes are weakly acidic, forming acetylides.

Common mistakes in Organic Chemistry Foundations

  • Thinking "organic" means natural or chemical-free. In chemistry, it simply means compounds based on carbon.
  • Thinking all carbon atoms in a molecule have the same shape. Each carbon's geometry depends on its own bonds.
  • Thinking any compound with carbon and hydrogen is a hydrocarbon. Hydrocarbons contain only those two elements.
  • Thinking free-radical substitution gives a single clean product. It gives a mixture of substituted products.
  • Thinking alkenes undergo substitution like alkanes. Their typical reaction is addition across the double bond.
  • Thinking hydrogenating an alkyne always gives an alkane. Lindlar's catalyst stops at the alkene.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to organic chemistry
    Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds.
  2. Carbon bonding
    Carbon's bonding explains the variety of organic compounds.
  3. Hydrocarbons
    Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen.
  4. Alkanes
    Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons.
  5. Alkenes
    Alkenes contain a C=C double bond.
  6. Alkynes
    Alkynes contain a C≡C triple bond.
  7. Aromatic compounds
    Aromatic compounds contain stable rings of delocalised electrons.
  8. Benzene structure
    Benzene's structure puzzled chemists for decades.
  9. Functional groups
    Functional groups determine how organic molecules react.
  10. IUPAC nomenclature
    IUPAC rules give every organic compound a systematic name.
  11. Structural isomerism
    Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures.
  12. Stereoisomerism
    Stereoisomers have the same bonds but different arrangements in space.
  13. Constitutional isomers
    Constitutional isomers differ in how atoms are connected.
  14. Conformational analysis
    Rotation about single bonds produces conformations.
  15. Chirality
    Chiral molecules are not superimposable on their mirror images.
  16. Enantiomers
    Enantiomers are pairs of non-superimposable mirror-image molecules.
  17. Diastereomers
    Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images.
  18. Optical activity
    Chiral compounds rotate plane-polarised light.
  19. R and S nomenclature
    The Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules assign R or S to stereocentres.
  20. E and Z nomenclature
    E/Z nomenclature describes geometric isomers around double bonds.
  21. Functional group interconversion
    Functional groups can be converted into one another.
  22. Organic reaction classification
    Organic reactions can be classified by what happens and by the species involved.
