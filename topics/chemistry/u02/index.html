Key terms and formulas at a glance

Nucleus
The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons
Atomic number
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom (Z)
Isotope
An atom of an element with a different number of neutrons from other atoms of that element
Nuclide
An atom with a specific number of protons and neutrons, written with its mass and atomic numbers
Electron shell
A group of electron energy levels with the same principal quantum number n
Atomic orbital
A region around the nucleus where there is a high probability of finding an electron of a particular energy
Principal quantum number
n, the quantum number for an orbital's shell, size and main energy
Electron configuration
The arrangement of an atom's electrons in subshells, written as 1s² 2s² and so on
Aufbau principle
Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first
Pauli exclusion principle
No two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers
Hund's rule
Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing
First ionisation energy
The energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why did most alpha particles pass straight through the gold foil?

The atom is mostly empty space; the nucleus is tiny, so most particles never come close to one. From “Structure of the atom” →

In plain English: An atom is a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by a much larger cloud of electrons. Key idea: An atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus holding nearly all its mass, surrounded by electrons in a space about 100 000 times wider; chemistry happens in the electrons.

What is the maximum number of electrons in the third shell?

18 (2n² with n = 3). From “Electron shells” →

In plain English: Electrons in atoms occupy shells at increasing distances and energies from the nucleus. Key idea: Electrons fill shells of increasing energy, each holding up to 2n² electrons; the number of outer electrons decides an element's group and much of its chemistry.

State the aufbau principle.

Electrons occupy the lowest-energy orbitals available first. From “Aufbau principle” →

In plain English: The aufbau ("building up") principle says electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first. Key idea: Electrons occupy the lowest-energy available orbitals; subshells fill in order of n + l, which puts 4s before 3d.

Write the equation for the first electron affinity of oxygen.

O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g). From “Electron affinity” →

In plain English: Electron affinity describes the energy change when atoms gain electrons. Key idea: First electron affinities are usually exothermic and most so for the halogens; second electron affinities are endothermic because of repulsion.

What does the square of the wavefunction tell you?

The probability density: how likely the electron is to be found at each point. From “Quantum mechanical model” →

In plain English: The quantum mechanical model treats the electron in an atom as a wave described by the Schrödinger equation. Key idea: In the quantum mechanical model the electron is a wave; the Schrödinger equation gives its allowed energies and orbitals, and ψ² gives the probability of finding it.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What did the large-angle deflection of a few alpha particles show? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Only a small, dense, positive centre could turn back a fast alpha particle; most of the atom must be empty.

  2. Which particle has the smallest mass? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Electron. The electron's mass is about 1/1836 of a proton's.

  3. What do isotopes of the same element have in common? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The same number of protons. Isotopes share the atomic number (protons) but differ in neutrons, and so in mass.

  4. Why do heavy stable nuclei such as lead-208 have more neutrons (126) than protons (82)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrical repulsion. As nuclei get bigger, proton–proton repulsion grows; additional neutrons provide binding without charge.

  5. What determines which element an atom is? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The number of protons. The atomic number, the proton count, defines the element.

  6. A particle has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A chloride ion, ³⁵Cl⁻. 17 protons make it chlorine; mass number 35; one extra electron gives a 1− charge.

  7. If Thomson's plum pudding model had been correct, what would Geiger and Marsden have seen? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Almost all alpha particles passing through with only small deflections. Spread-out positive charge produces only weak forces, so no large deflections; the backscattering was the surprise.

  8. Why is the radius of Na⁺ (about 102 pm) much smaller than that of a sodium atom (about 186 pm)? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Losing the single outer electron removes the outermost shell, and the remaining electrons are pulled in more strongly by the same nuclear charge. The cation keeps 11 protons but has only 10 electrons, all in inner shells, so it is much smaller.

  9. Heavy water (D₂O) can be separated from ordinary water by repeated electrolysis, but no ordinary chemical reaction separates them cleanly. Why? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their chemistry is the same, but the small mass difference slightly changes rates, which repeated processing exploits. Isotopes react the same way, but mass affects reaction rates a little (the kinetic isotope effect), enough for gradual enrichment.

Key takeaways

  • An atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus holding nearly all its mass, surrounded by electrons in a space about 100 000 times wider; chemistry happens in the electrons.
  • Protons (+1) and neutrons (0) have mass about 1 and sit in the nucleus; electrons (−1) are about 1/1836 of that mass; ions differ from atoms only in their electrons.
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons and electrons but different numbers of neutrons, so they react alike but differ in mass.
  • The atomic number defines the element and orders the periodic table; the mass number identifies the isotope; both are conserved in nuclear equations.
  • Electrons fill shells of increasing energy, each holding up to 2n² electrons; the number of outer electrons decides an element's group and much of its chemistry.
  • An orbital is a region where an electron is likely to be found and holds up to two electrons; s, p, d and f subshells have 1, 3, 5 and 7 orbitals.

Common mistakes in Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

  • Picturing electrons as tiny planets in fixed orbits close to the nucleus. Electrons occupy a large region around it, described by probabilities, as later lessons explain.
  • Changing the number of protons to make an ion. Ions form by electron transfer; changing the protons would make a different element.
  • Saying isotopes are different elements. They are the same element, because the number of protons is the same.
  • Using relative atomic mass as the mass number. Mass number is always a whole number for one nucleus.
  • Saying the third shell can only hold 8 electrons. It holds up to 18; the 2,8,8 pattern only describes the first 20 elements.
  • Using "orbit" and "orbital" interchangeably. An orbit is a definite path, which electrons don't follow; an orbital is a probability region.

Lessons in this school

  1. Structure of the atom
    An atom is a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by a much larger cloud of electrons.
  2. Protons, neutrons and electrons
    Atoms are built from three particles with very different masses and charges.
  3. Isotopes
    Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
  4. Atomic number and mass number
    Two numbers identify any nucleus: the atomic number, which fixes the element, and the mass number, which identifies the isotope.
  5. Electron shells
    Electrons in atoms occupy shells at increasing distances and energies from the nucleus.
  6. Atomic orbitals
    Within each shell, electrons occupy orbitals: regions where an electron is likely to be found.
  7. Quantum numbers
    Four quantum numbers describe each electron in an atom: which shell, which type of orbital, which orientation and which spin.
  8. Electron configurations
    An electron configuration lists the subshells an atom's electrons occupy and how many are in each, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ for sodium.
  9. Aufbau principle
    The aufbau ("building up") principle says electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first.
  10. Pauli exclusion principle
    The Pauli exclusion principle says no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers.
  11. Hund's rule
    Hund's rule says that when electrons fill a set of orbitals of equal energy, they occupy them singly first, with parallel spins, before pairing up.
  12. Ionisation energy
    Ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove electrons from atoms.
  13. Electron affinity
    Electron affinity describes the energy change when atoms gain electrons.
  14. Atomic spectra
    When atoms are given energy, they emit light at only certain wavelengths, producing a line spectrum unique to each element.
  15. Emission and absorption
    Atoms emit light at particular wavelengths when excited electrons fall back, and absorb the same wavelengths when electrons are lifted up.
  16. Wave-particle duality
    Light and electrons both behave sometimes like waves and sometimes like particles.
  17. Quantum mechanical model
    The quantum mechanical model treats the electron in an atom as a wave described by the Schrödinger equation.
  18. Atomic orbitals and shapes
    Each type of orbital has a characteristic shape: s orbitals are spherical, p orbitals have two lobes, and d orbitals mostly have four.
  19. Periodic trends
    Atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity change in regular ways across periods and down groups.
  20. Electronic structure and chemical properties
    An element's chemistry follows from its electron structure.

Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry: questions students ask

How many electrons does Mg²⁺ have (Z = 12)?

10: it has lost two of its 12 electrons.

Why do isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties?

They have the same number and arrangement of electrons, which decide chemical behaviour.

What does the atomic number tell you?

The number of protons, which defines the element and its place in the periodic table.

How many orbitals are in a d subshell, and how many electrons can it hold?

5 orbitals, holding up to 10 electrons.
