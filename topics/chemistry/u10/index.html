Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does OIL RIG stand for?

Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons. From “Oxidation and reduction” →

In plain English: Oxidation and reduction always happen together in redox reactions. Key idea: Oxidation is loss of electrons or increase in oxidation state and reduction is gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation state (OIL RIG); they always occur together in redox reactions, which include combustion, respiration and corrosion.

What is an oxidising agent?

A species that accepts electrons and is reduced. From “Oxidising agents” →

In plain English: Oxidising agents cause oxidation by accepting electrons. Key idea: Oxidising agents accept electrons and are reduced; common ones include oxygen, halogens, hydrogen peroxide, manganate(VII), which goes purple to colourless, and dichromate(VI), which goes orange to green, used in tests and titrations.

Which electrode is negative in an electrolytic cell?

The cathode. From “Electrolytic cells” →

In plain English: Electrolytic cells use electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions. Key idea: Electrolytic cells use a power supply to drive non-spontaneous reactions: cations are reduced at the negative cathode and anions oxidised at the positive anode in molten or dissolved electrolytes, reversing the polarity of galvanic cells.

State the Nernst equation.

E = E⦵ − (RT/nF) ln Q. From “Nernst equation” →

In plain English: The Nernst equation shows how electrode potentials change with concentration. Key idea: The Nernst equation E = E⦵ − (RT/nF) ln Q gives electrode potentials under non-standard conditions; lower ion concentrations make metal electrode potentials more negative, explaining concentration cells, pH meters and falling battery voltages.

What is the only product of a hydrogen fuel cell?

Water. From “Fuel cells” →

In plain English: Fuel cells generate electricity continuously from a fuel and oxygen. Key idea: Fuel cells continuously oxidise a fuel such as hydrogen at the anode and reduce oxygen at the cathode, producing electricity and water; they are efficient and clean at the point of use but face hydrogen storage, production and cost challenges.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Redox and Electrochemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, which species is oxidised? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Zinc. Zinc loses two electrons to become Zn²⁺, so zinc is oxidised.

  2. What is the oxidation state of iron in [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    +3. Six CN⁻ give −6, so Fe must be +3 for an overall −3.

  3. Which is a reduction half-equation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Gaining electrons on the left shows reduction.

  4. Why is sodium burning in chlorine a redox reaction? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Sodium loses electrons and chlorine gains them. Electron transfer defines redox even without oxygen.

  5. Which reaction is a disproportionation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    2Cu⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺. Copper(I) is both reduced to 0 and oxidised to +2.

  6. Why are half-equations multiplied before adding? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    So electrons lost equal electrons gained. Electron numbers must match before cancelling.

  7. Why is the electron-transfer definition preferred? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It covers redox reactions that involve no oxygen. Many redox reactions involve electrons but not oxygen.

  8. Why does oxidising ethanol to ethanoic acid count as oxidation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The carbon's oxidation state increases as it gains oxygen. Gaining bonds to oxygen raises carbon's oxidation state.

  9. Why don't spectator ions appear in ionic redox equations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are not oxidised or reduced and appear unchanged on both sides. Ions that are not oxidised or reduced appear unchanged on both sides and cancel.

Key takeaways

  • Oxidation is loss of electrons or increase in oxidation state and reduction is gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation state (OIL RIG); they always occur together in redox reactions, which include combustion, respiration and corrosion.
  • Increases in oxidation state show oxidation and decreases show reduction, with totals balancing; disproportionation oxidises and reduces the same element, and oxidation states apply to organic and complex species.
  • Half-equations show electrons lost in oxidation (on the right) and gained in reduction (on the left); they are balanced for atoms and charge, multiplied so electrons match and added with electrons cancelled to give the overall equation.
  • Redox half-equations in acid are balanced by atoms, then H₂O for oxygen, H⁺ for hydrogen and electrons for charge; in alkali, OH⁻ is added to neutralise H⁺, and half-equations are then combined with electrons cancelled.
  • Oxidising agents accept electrons and are reduced; common ones include oxygen, halogens, hydrogen peroxide, manganate(VII), which goes purple to colourless, and dichromate(VI), which goes orange to green, used in tests and titrations.
  • Reducing agents donate electrons and are oxidised; examples include reactive metals, hydrogen, carbon, CO, iodide and thiosulfate, used in metal extraction, titrations, organic reductions and thermite welding.

Common mistakes in Redox and Electrochemistry

  • Thinking oxidation always involves oxygen. Oxidation is fundamentally loss of electrons.
  • Thinking every element changes oxidation state in a redox reaction. Spectator elements stay the same.
  • Leaving electrons in the overall equation. Electrons must cancel when half-equations are combined.
  • Adding O₂ or O atoms to balance oxygen. Use water molecules instead.
  • Thinking an oxidising agent is oxidised. It is reduced while oxidising something else.
  • Thinking a reducing agent is reduced. It is oxidised while reducing something else.

    Redox reactions in acidic or alkaline solution need water and H⁺ or OH⁻ to balance.

    Oxidising agents cause oxidation by accepting electrons.
  6. Reducing agents
    Reducing agents cause reduction by donating electrons.
  7. Electrochemical cells
    Electrochemical cells interconvert chemical and electrical energy.

    Galvanic cells generate electricity from spontaneous redox reactions.
  9. Electrolytic cells
    Electrolytic cells use electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions.
  10. Standard electrode potentials
    Standard electrode potentials measure the tendency of half-cells to be reduced.
  11. Cell potentials
    The cell potential shows how strongly a cell drives electrons.
  12. Electrochemical series
    The electrochemical series ranks half-cells by their standard electrode potentials.
  13. Nernst equation
    The Nernst equation shows how electrode potentials change with concentration.

    Electrolysis uses electricity to decompose compounds.
  15. Faraday's laws
    Faraday's laws relate the amount of product in electrolysis to the charge passed.
  16. Batteries
    Batteries are galvanic cells that store chemical energy for later use.
  17. Fuel cells
    Fuel cells generate electricity continuously from a fuel and oxygen.
  18. Corrosion
    Corrosion is the electrochemical deterioration of metals.
  19. Corrosion prevention
    Corrosion can be slowed or prevented by several methods.
