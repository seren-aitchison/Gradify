Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his table?

For elements not yet discovered. From “Development of the periodic table” →

In plain English: The periodic table grew from attempts to organise the elements by their properties. Key idea: Early classifications included Döbereiner's triads and Newlands' octaves; Mendeleev's 1869 table grouped elements by properties, left gaps and swapped orders where needed, and his successful predictions confirmed it.

What is the electron configuration of chromium?

[Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. From “d-block elements” →

In plain English: The d-block contains the transition elements of groups 3–12. Key idea: d-block elements fill d orbitals, lose 4s electrons first when ionising, show anomalous configurations for Cr and Cu and typically have variable oxidation states, coloured compounds and catalytic activity; Sc and Zn are not true transition elements.

What is catenation?

Bonding of atoms of the same element into chains or rings. From “Group 14” →

In plain English: Group 14 shows the clearest change from non-metal to metal. Key idea: Group 14 changes from non-metallic carbon through metalloids to metallic tin and lead; carbon catenates and has allotropes, CO₂ is molecular while SiO₂ is giant covalent, and the +2 state becomes more stable down the group.

What is a ligand?

A molecule or ion that donates a lone pair to a metal ion. From “Transition metals” →

In plain English: Transition metals show characteristic properties linked to their d electrons. Key idea: Transition metals form complex ions with ligands donating lone pairs; ligands split d orbitals, so electrons absorb visible light and compounds appear coloured, and variable oxidation states and adsorption make transition metals good catalysts.

Why does aluminium have a lower first ionisation energy than magnesium?

Its outer electron is in a higher-energy 3p orbital. From “Ionisation energy trends” →

In plain English: Ionisation energies reveal electron structure and follow clear patterns. Key idea: First ionisation energy increases across periods and decreases down groups, with dips at groups 2–13 and 15–16 explained by p-orbital energy and electron pairing; jumps in successive ionisation energies reveal the number of outer electrons.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Periodic Table and Periodicity

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who published a periodic table with gaps for undiscovered elements in 1869? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Dmitri Mendeleev. Mendeleev's gaps let him predict new elements.

  2. How is the modern periodic table ordered? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    By atomic number. Atomic number counts protons and defines each element.

  3. Which block contains the transition elements? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The d-block. Groups 3–12 fill d orbitals, so they form the d-block.

  4. Why did Mendeleev place tellurium before iodine despite its greater mass? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their properties matched the groups better in that order. Grouping by properties was more important than strict mass order.

  5. Why does argon come before potassium in the table? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Argon has atomic number 18 and potassium 19. Atomic number, not mass, sets the order in the modern table.

  6. Why do elements in the same group react similarly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They have the same number of outer electrons. Outer electrons govern chemical reactions.

  7. Why did the discovery of germanium support Mendeleev's table? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its properties closely matched his predictions for eka-silicon. Successful predictions are strong evidence for a theory.

  8. Why is mass order occasionally wrong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Average atomic mass depends on isotope proportions. Isotope mixtures can make a lighter element follow a heavier one.

  9. An element has configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. Where is it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Period 3, group 16, p-block. Three shells and six outer electrons place it in group 16.

Key takeaways

  • Early classifications included Döbereiner's triads and Newlands' octaves; Mendeleev's 1869 table grouped elements by properties, left gaps and swapped orders where needed, and his successful predictions confirmed it.
  • Moseley's X-ray measurements showed elements have whole-number atomic numbers, and the modern periodic law orders elements by atomic number, which resolves mass-order anomalies because atomic number determines electron structure.
  • Periods are rows whose number gives the number of occupied shells; groups are columns of elements with similar outer electrons; and the table divides into s, p, d and f blocks by the subshell being filled.
  • s-block elements have outer s electrons, are reactive metals forming +1 or +2 ions in mostly ionic compounds, act as reducing agents and become more reactive down each group, with group 1 more reactive than group 2.
  • The p-block, with configurations ns²np¹ to ns²np⁶, includes metals, metalloids and non-metals, with metallic character increasing down groups; heavier elements show lower oxidation states through the inert pair effect.
  • d-block elements fill d orbitals, lose 4s electrons first when ionising, show anomalous configurations for Cr and Cu and typically have variable oxidation states, coloured compounds and catalytic activity; Sc and Zn are not true transition elements.

Common mistakes in Periodic Table and Periodicity

  • Thinking Mendeleev ordered elements strictly by atomic mass. He broke mass order when properties required it.
  • Thinking the table is ordered by mass. It is ordered by atomic number, the number of protons.
  • Thinking elements in the same period have similar chemistry. Similar chemistry comes from being in the same group.
  • Thinking s-block metals are found as pure metals in nature. They are too reactive and occur as compounds.
  • Thinking all p-block elements are non-metals. The block includes metals such as aluminium, tin and lead.
  • Thinking 3d electrons are removed before 4s. The 4s electrons are lost first when transition metals form ions.

Lessons in this school

  1. Development of the periodic table
    The periodic table grew from attempts to organise the elements by their properties.
  2. Modern periodic law
    The modern periodic table is ordered by atomic number.
  3. Periods and groups
    The periodic table is arranged in rows called periods and columns called groups.
  4. s-block elements
    The s-block contains the reactive metals of groups 1 and 2.
  5. p-block elements
    The p-block contains metals, metalloids and non-metals.
  6. d-block elements
    The d-block contains the transition elements of groups 3–12.
  7. f-block elements
    The f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides.
  8. Group 1
    Group 1, the alkali metals, are soft and very reactive.
  9. Group 2
    Group 2, the alkaline earth metals, are reactive metals forming 2+ ions.
  10. Group 13
    Group 13 runs from the metalloid boron to metals such as aluminium.
  11. Group 14
    Group 14 shows the clearest change from non-metal to metal.
  12. Group 15
    Group 15 includes nitrogen and phosphorus, essential for life.
  13. Group 16
    Group 16, the chalcogens, includes oxygen and sulfur.
  14. Group 17
    Group 17, the halogens, are reactive non-metals.
  15. Group 18
    Group 18, the noble gases, are very unreactive.
  16. Transition metals
    Transition metals show characteristic properties linked to their d electrons.
  17. Lanthanides
    The lanthanides are fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium.
  18. Actinides
    The actinides are radioactive elements from actinium to lawrencium.
  19. Atomic radius
    Atomic radius describes the size of atoms.
  20. Ionic radius
    Ions differ in size from their atoms.
  21. Ionisation energy trends
    Ionisation energies reveal electron structure and follow clear patterns.
  22. Electronegativity
    Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts bonding electrons.
  23. Reactivity trends
    Reactivity varies predictably across the periodic table.
  24. Oxidation states
    Oxidation states track electrons in compounds.
  25. Periodic relationships
    The periodic table reveals relationships beyond simple group and period trends.
