Five sample flashcards

What is the analyte?

The substance being identified or measured. From “Foundations of analytical chemistry” →

In plain English: Analytical chemistry finds out what substances are present and how much. Key idea: Analytical chemistry identifies what is present and how much, following a process of defining the problem, sampling, preparation, measurement, data evaluation and reporting, and it supports medicine, safety, environment and industry.

What is used to measure water hardness by titration?

EDTA. From “Titrimetry” →

In plain English: Titrimetry determines amounts by reacting the analyte with a solution of known concentration. Key idea: Titrimetry uses acid-base, redox, complexometric and precipitation reactions, with primary standards, careful technique and concordant titres; concentrations are calculated from moles and stoichiometry.

What percentage of carbon is ¹³C?

About 1.1%. From “Carbon-13 NMR” →

In plain English: Carbon-13 NMR reveals carbon environments. Key idea: ¹³C NMR is weak because only 1.1% of carbon is ¹³C; proton-decoupled spectra give one line per carbon environment, shifts identify alkyl, C–O, alkene/aromatic and C=O carbons and symmetry reduces signal numbers.

Why do CO and N₂ differ in accurate mass?

Isotopic masses are not exact whole numbers. From “High-resolution mass spectrometry” →

In plain English: High-resolution mass spectrometry measures masses very precisely. Key idea: High-resolution mass spectrometry measures accurate masses to four or more decimal places, using non-integer isotopic masses to distinguish formulas with the same nominal mass, with uses in drug discovery, sport, proteomics and environmental analysis.

What is the mobile phase in GC?

An inert carrier gas such as helium. From “Gas chromatography” →

In plain English: Gas chromatography separates volatile compounds. Key idea: GC uses an inert carrier gas and a heated column with a liquid stationary phase to separate volatile compounds by boiling point and interaction; retention times identify components and peak areas quantify them.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the matrix in an analysis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Everything in the sample except the analyte. Matrix components can interfere with measurement.

  2. Which halide gives a yellow precipitate with acidified silver nitrate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Iodide. Silver iodide is pale yellow and insoluble in ammonia.

  3. Which method is best for measuring trace pesticide residues? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    LC-MS, a sensitive instrumental method. Trace levels need a highly sensitive and selective method.

  4. Why is representative sampling so important? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An unrepresentative sample gives misleading results however precise the instrument. The sample must reflect the composition of the whole.

  5. Why is the sample acidified before adding barium chloride? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To remove carbonate, which would also precipitate with barium. Carbonate gives a false positive unless destroyed.

  6. Why do most instrumental methods need calibration? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The signal must be related to concentration using standards. Standards of known concentration convert signal to amount.

  7. Why must results be reported with uncertainty? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Users need to know how reliable the value is when comparing with limits. A value near a legal limit needs its uncertainty to be judged.

  8. Why are tests performed in a particular order? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Earlier reagents can interfere with later tests. Planning the order avoids false positives from earlier reagents.

  9. Why might a classical titration be chosen over an instrument? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can be very accurate without calibration when the analyte is abundant. For major components, classical methods are accurate and cheap.

Key takeaways

  • Analytical chemistry identifies what is present and how much, following a process of defining the problem, sampling, preparation, measurement, data evaluation and reporting, and it supports medicine, safety, environment and industry.
  • Qualitative analysis uses flame tests, precipitation and gas tests for inorganic ions and characteristic reagents for organic groups, applied in a logical order to avoid interference.
  • Quantitative analysis uses classical methods such as titration and gravimetry or instrumental methods that relate signal to concentration through calibration, judged by sensitivity, detection and quantification limits and selectivity.
  • Accuracy is closeness to the true value and precision is closeness of repeats; systematic errors reduce accuracy and are found with reference materials, while random errors reduce precision, measured by standard deviation, and average out over repeats.
  • Calibration uses standards to relate signal to concentration, with blanks to correct baselines, standard addition to correct matrix effects and internal standards to correct variable losses, and unknowns should fall within the linear calibrated range.
  • Titrimetry uses acid-base, redox, complexometric and precipitation reactions, with primary standards, careful technique and concordant titres; concentrations are calculated from moles and stoichiometry.

Common mistakes in Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy

  • Thinking the instrument is the most important step. Poor sampling or preparation ruins even precise measurements.
  • Adding barium chloride without acid first. Carbonate would also give a white precipitate.
  • Thinking a detected substance can always be quantified. Between the LOD and LOQ it is detected but not reliably measured.
  • Thinking precise results must be accurate. Results can be tightly grouped but all wrong.
  • Extrapolating beyond the highest standard. The response may not stay linear.
  • Ignoring the reaction ratio. Always use the balanced equation's mole ratio.
