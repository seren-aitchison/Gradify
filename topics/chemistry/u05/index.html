Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What causes gas pressure?

Particles colliding with the walls of the container. From “Gases” →

In plain English: Gases have properties that differ sharply from liquids and solids. Key idea: Gases fill their containers, are compressible and mix readily; pressure arises from particle collisions, is measured in pascals and temperatures for gas calculations must be in kelvin.

What is average kinetic energy proportional to?

Kelvin temperature. From “Kinetic molecular theory” →

In plain English: Kinetic molecular theory explains gas behaviour from the motion of particles. Key idea: Kinetic molecular theory treats gases as randomly moving particles with elastic collisions, whose average kinetic energy is proportional to kelvin temperature; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shows speeds spread out more at higher temperature, and lighter particles move faster.

Why does dry ice sublime at 1 atm?

CO₂'s triple point is above 1 atm, so the liquid is not stable there. From “Phase diagrams” →

In plain English: Phase diagrams show which state a substance is in at any temperature and pressure. Key idea: Phase diagrams show stable phases at each temperature and pressure, with lines of coexistence, a triple point and a critical point; water's negative solid-liquid slope reflects less dense ice, while CO₂ sublimes at 1 atm because its triple point is above 1 atm.

What is a saturated solution?

A solution holding the maximum amount of dissolved solute at that temperature. From “Solubility” →

In plain English: Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent. Key idea: Solubility is the maximum solute dissolving in a given solvent at a set temperature; like dissolves like, solids usually become more soluble when hot, gases less soluble when hot and more soluble at higher pressure, as Henry's law states.

Why is diffusion faster in gases than in liquids?

Gas particles move faster and collide less often. From “Diffusion” →

In plain English: Diffusion is the spreading of particles from high to low concentration. Key idea: Diffusion is net movement from high to low concentration by random motion, faster in gases, at higher temperature and with steeper gradients; Graham's law states effusion rate is inversely proportional to the square root of molar mass.

Quick quiz: test yourself on States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the SI unit of pressure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The pascal. One pascal is one newton per square metre of area.

  2. Which law relates pressure and volume at constant temperature? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Boyle's law. Boyle showed pV stays constant when temperature is fixed.

  3. What is the approximate molar volume of a gas at room temperature and pressure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    24 dm³. One mole occupies about 24 dm³ at 298 K and 101 kPa.

  4. Why are gases easily compressed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their particles are far apart with much empty space. Large gaps between particles let the volume shrink.

  5. Why does pressure rise when a sealed gas is heated? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Particles move faster and hit the walls harder and more often. Faster particles exert more force on the walls.

  6. Which rearrangement gives the amount of gas? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    n = pV ÷ RT. Dividing both sides of pV = nRT by RT isolates n.

  7. Why must temperature be in kelvin for gas calculations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Gas laws are proportional relationships starting from absolute zero. Proportionality only works on an absolute scale without negatives.

  8. Why does a weather balloon expand as it rises? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Atmospheric pressure outside falls, so the gas expands. Lower external pressure lets the gas occupy more volume.

  9. Why must volume be in m³ when using pressure in Pa? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    SI units must be consistent with R in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Mixing units gives answers wrong by factors of a thousand.

Key takeaways

  • Gases fill their containers, are compressible and mix readily; pressure arises from particle collisions, is measured in pascals and temperatures for gas calculations must be in kelvin.
  • Boyle's law links pressure and volume, Charles's law volume and temperature, Gay-Lussac's law pressure and temperature, and Avogadro's law volume and amount; the combined gas law p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂ applies to a fixed amount of gas with kelvin temperatures.
  • The ideal gas equation pV = nRT uses SI units with R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, allowing calculation of amount, volume, pressure and molar mass, with one mole occupying about 24 dm³ at room conditions.
  • The ideal model assumes negligible particle volume, no intermolecular forces and elastic collisions; real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, measured by the compressibility factor and modelled by the van der Waals equation.
  • Kinetic molecular theory treats gases as randomly moving particles with elastic collisions, whose average kinetic energy is proportional to kelvin temperature; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution shows speeds spread out more at higher temperature, and lighter particles move faster.
  • Liquid particles are close but mobile, so liquids flow with fixed volume; stronger intermolecular forces raise boiling point, viscosity and surface tension and lower volatility, and evaporation cools a liquid.

Common mistakes in States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

  • Using degrees Celsius in gas law calculations. Temperature must be converted to kelvin.
  • Doubling the Celsius temperature and expecting volume to double. Only doubling the kelvin temperature doubles volume.
  • Using volume in dm³ with pressure in pascals. Volume must be in m³ when pressure is in pascals.
  • Thinking real gases always behave ideally. Deviations are significant at high pressure and low temperature.
  • Thinking all particles in a gas move at the same speed. They have a distribution of speeds.
  • Thinking evaporation only happens at the boiling point. Evaporation occurs from the surface at any temperature.

Lessons in this school

  1. Gases
    Gases have properties that differ sharply from liquids and solids.
  2. Gas laws
    The gas laws describe how pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are related.
  3. Ideal gas equation
    The ideal gas equation links pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.
  4. Real gases
    Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour under some conditions.
  5. Kinetic molecular theory
    Kinetic molecular theory explains gas behaviour from the motion of particles.
  6. Liquids
    Liquids have particles close together but free to move.
  7. Vapour pressure
    Vapour pressure is the pressure of a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid.
  8. Boiling and melting
    Melting and boiling are changes of state that require energy.
  9. Phase diagrams
    Phase diagrams show which state a substance is in at any temperature and pressure.
  10. Solids
    Solids have fixed shapes because their particles are held in place.
  11. Crystalline solids
    Crystalline solids have long-range order that gives them sharp melting points and flat faces.
  12. Amorphous solids
    Amorphous solids lack long-range order.
  13. Solutions
    A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute in a solvent.
  14. Solubility
    Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent.
  15. Concentration
    This lesson extends concentration beyond mol dm⁻³ to other units used in physical chemistry and everyday life.
  16. Colligative properties
    Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their identity.
  17. Osmosis
    Osmosis is the movement of solvent through a semipermeable membrane.
  18. Diffusion
    Diffusion is the spreading of particles from high to low concentration.
  19. Surface tension
    Surface tension makes liquid surfaces behave like a stretched skin.
  20. Viscosity
    Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flow.
  21. Intermolecular interactions
    This lesson compares all the interactions between particles and uses them to predict and explain physical properties.
  22. Materials properties
    The properties of materials come from their structure and bonding.

States of Matter and Physical Chemistry: questions students ask

Under what conditions do real gases deviate most from ideal behaviour?

High pressure and low temperature.

What does the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution show?

The spread of particle speeds or energies in a gas.

Why can liquids flow?

Their particles can move past one another.

When does a liquid boil?

When its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.
