Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does a full-headed curly arrow represent?

The movement of a pair of electrons. From “Curly arrow notation” →

In plain English: Curly arrows are the language of organic mechanisms. Key idea: Full-headed curly arrows show electron-pair movement and half-headed arrows show single electrons; arrows start at lone pairs or bonds and end at atoms or bonding positions, and mechanisms must conserve charge and valence.

What characterises a radical?

An unpaired electron. From “Radical intermediates” →

In plain English: Radicals are species with unpaired electrons. Key idea: Carbon radicals have an unpaired electron, are roughly planar and are stabilised by alkyl groups and resonance; they react in chain reactions, bromination is more selective than chlorination, and radicals matter in polymerisation, ozone depletion and biology.

What does Zaitsev's rule predict?

The more substituted alkene is the major product. From “Elimination reactions” →

In plain English: Elimination reactions remove atoms to form multiple bonds. Key idea: Elimination removes a leaving group and a β-hydrogen to form an alkene; Zaitsev's rule favours the more substituted alkene, and hot ethanolic conditions, bulky bases and tertiary substrates favour elimination over substitution.

What is the intermediate in electrophilic aromatic substitution?

An arenium ion. From “Electrophilic aromatic substitution” →

In plain English: This lesson details the mechanism of electrophilic aromatic substitution and how electrophiles are generated. Key idea: Electrophilic aromatic substitution forms an arenium ion that loses H⁺ to restore aromaticity; catalysts generate electrophiles such as NO₂⁺, Br⁺ equivalents and acylium ions and are regenerated, and the first step is rate-determining.

What does nitrile hydrolysis produce?

A carboxylic acid. From “Hydrolysis” →

In plain English: Hydrolysis splits molecules using water. Key idea: Hydrolysis splits molecules with water; acid hydrolysis of esters is reversible while base hydrolysis gives carboxylates irreversibly, as in soap-making, and amides, haloalkanes, nitriles, proteins and polysaccharides also hydrolyse, with amides being slow because of delocalisation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Organic Reaction Mechanisms

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does a fishhook arrow show? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The movement of a single electron. Half-headed arrows are used in radical mechanisms.

  2. Which carbon in bromoethane is electrophilic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The carbon bonded to bromine. Bromine pulls electron density, leaving its carbon δ+.

  3. What does heterolytic cleavage produce? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A cation and an anion. One atom takes both bonding electrons, so a cation and an anion form.

  4. Why must an arrow never start at a positive charge? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A positive charge has no electrons to donate. Arrows track electrons, so they must begin where electrons are.

  5. Why does a polar aprotic solvent speed up reactions of anionic nucleophiles? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It does not hydrogen bond to the anion, leaving it more reactive. Protic solvents cage anions in hydrogen bonds.

  6. Why do polar solvents favour heterolytic cleavage? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They stabilise the ions formed by solvation. Solvation lowers the energy of charged products.

  7. In hydroxide attacking chloromethane, why are two arrows needed? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    One forms the C–O bond and one breaks the C–Cl bond so carbon keeps four bonds. Without the second arrow carbon would have five bonds.

  8. Why is a carbonyl carbon electrophilic? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Oxygen pulls electron density from the C=O, leaving carbon δ+. Polarisation of C=O makes carbon open to attack.

  9. Why is ion formation in the gas phase so costly? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No solvent is present to stabilise the separated charges. Separating charges without solvation needs much more energy.

Key takeaways

  • Full-headed curly arrows show electron-pair movement and half-headed arrows show single electrons; arrows start at lone pairs or bonds and end at atoms or bonding positions, and mechanisms must conserve charge and valence.
  • Nucleophiles donate lone pairs or π electrons and are stronger with negative charge and less electronegative donor atoms; electrophiles have empty orbitals or δ+ atoms and are stronger with positive charge, and polar reactions pair the most nucleophilic and electrophilic sites.
  • Homolytic cleavage gives two radicals and is favoured by light, heat and non-polar bonds, while heterolytic cleavage gives ions and is favoured by polar bonds and solvents, with the more electronegative atom taking both electrons.
  • Carbocations are sp² planar intermediates with an empty p orbital; stability increases from methyl to tertiary through inductive effects and hyperconjugation and with resonance, and stability controls products, rearrangements and stereochemistry.
  • Carbanions carry a lone pair and negative charge, are stabilised by electron-withdrawing groups and resonance as in enolates, and act as strong nucleophiles and bases, with Grignard reagents, organolithiums and enolates used to form C–C bonds.
  • Carbon radicals have an unpaired electron, are roughly planar and are stabilised by alkyl groups and resonance; they react in chain reactions, bromination is more selective than chlorination, and radicals matter in polymerisation, ozone depletion and biology.

Common mistakes in Organic Reaction Mechanisms

  • Drawing an arrow from a positive charge. Positive charges have no electrons to give.
  • Thinking nucleophiles must be negatively charged. Neutral molecules such as ammonia and water are nucleophiles too.
  • Thinking heterolysis puts the electrons on either atom at random. The more electronegative atom usually takes them.
  • Thinking primary carbocations are the most stable because they are least crowded. Alkyl groups stabilise the charge.
  • Thinking alkyl groups stabilise carbanions as they do carbocations. They destabilise negative charge.
  • Thinking all radicals react equally unselectively. Bromine radicals are much more selective than chlorine radicals.

Organic Reaction Mechanisms: questions students ask

Why is OH⁻ a stronger nucleophile than H₂O?

Its negative charge makes its lone pairs more available.

Why are peroxides good radical initiators?

Their O–O bond is weak and breaks easily.

What is an enolate?

A resonance-stabilised anion formed next to a carbonyl group.

Where do intermediates appear on an energy profile?

In energy minima between steps.
