Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What symbol shows a reversible reaction?

⇌. From “Reversible reactions” →

In plain English: Many reactions can go in both directions. Key idea: Reversible reactions proceed in both directions, shown by ⇌, and do not go to completion in closed systems; the energy change of the reverse reaction is equal and opposite to the forward reaction.

What does ICE stand for?

Initial, Change, Equilibrium. From “Kc” →

In plain English: Kc is the equilibrium constant in terms of concentrations. Key idea: Kc uses equilibrium concentrations in mol dm⁻³ with products over reactants raised to coefficients; ICE tables find equilibrium amounts using mole ratios, and units of Kc depend on the expression.

State Le Chatelier's principle.

A system at equilibrium shifts to oppose a change applied to it. From “Le Chatelier's principle” →

In plain English: Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to change. Key idea: Le Chatelier's principle states that a disturbed equilibrium shifts to oppose the change: adding reactant or removing product shifts right, heating favours the endothermic direction, raising pressure favours fewer gas molecules, and catalysts cause no shift.

What happens to the equilibrium when product is removed?

It shifts right, forming more product. From “Concentration and equilibrium” →

In plain English: Changing concentrations shifts the position of equilibrium. Key idea: Adding reactant or removing product shifts equilibrium right and adding product shifts it left, with K unchanged; excess reactants and product removal increase yields, as in esterification and metabolic pathways.

What catalyst is used in the Contact process?

Vanadium(V) oxide. From “Industrial equilibrium” →

In plain English: Industry chooses conditions to make equilibrium reactions economical. Key idea: The Haber process uses about 450 °C, 200 atm and iron with recycling, and the Contact process about 450 °C, low pressure and V₂O₅; conditions are compromises between yield, rate, cost and safety, and recycling raises overall conversion.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chemical Equilibrium

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which colour is anhydrous copper(II) sulfate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    White. Removing water of crystallisation leaves a white powder.

  2. What is true of concentrations at equilibrium? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They remain constant. Equal rates mean no net change in concentration.

  3. Which substances are left out of equilibrium expressions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pure solids and liquids. Their concentrations are effectively constant.

  4. If a forward reaction has ΔH = −50 kJ mol⁻¹, what is ΔH for the reverse? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    +50 kJ mol⁻¹. Reversing a reaction reverses the sign of its enthalpy change.

  5. How does isotope labelling show equilibrium is dynamic? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Labelled atoms spread into all species though concentrations stay constant. Exchange of labelled atoms shows reactions keep happening both ways.

  6. How does K for a reversed reaction relate to the original K? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is the reciprocal, 1/K. Swapping products and reactants inverts the ratio.

  7. Why don't reversible reactions go to completion in a closed system? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Products keep reacting to re-form reactants. Forward and reverse reactions both continue.

  8. Why does starting with pure HI give the same equilibrium as starting with H₂ and I₂? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Equilibrium can be approached from either direction. The final position depends on conditions, not the starting side.

  9. Why does adding a catalyst not change K? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It speeds forward and reverse reactions equally. Equal acceleration leaves the equilibrium ratio unchanged.

Key takeaways

  • Reversible reactions proceed in both directions, shown by ⇌, and do not go to completion in closed systems; the energy change of the reverse reaction is equal and opposite to the forward reaction.
  • At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal and concentrations are constant but not necessarily equal; reactions continue at molecular level in a closed system and the same equilibrium can be reached from either direction.
  • The equilibrium constant K = [products]^coefficients ÷ [reactants]^coefficients uses equilibrium concentrations and omits pure solids and liquids; large K favours products, small K reactants, and K changes only with temperature.
  • Kc uses equilibrium concentrations in mol dm⁻³ with products over reactants raised to coefficients; ICE tables find equilibrium amounts using mole ratios, and units of Kc depend on the expression.
  • Kp uses partial pressures, found from mole fraction times total pressure, with products over reactants raised to coefficients; units depend on the expression and Kp changes only with temperature.
  • The reaction quotient Q uses current concentrations in the K expression; comparing Q with K predicts the direction: Q < K forward, Q > K backward, Q = K at equilibrium, and ΔG = ΔG⦵ + RT ln Q.

Common mistakes in Chemical Equilibrium

  • Thinking reversible reactions stop when products form. Both directions continue to occur.
  • Thinking concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant, not necessarily equal.
  • Thinking adding more reactant changes K. Only temperature changes K; the position shifts but K stays the same.
  • Using initial concentrations in Kc. Only equilibrium concentrations are used.
  • Using moles directly instead of partial pressures in Kp. Convert moles to mole fractions then partial pressures.
  • Thinking Q and K are always the same. Q equals K only at equilibrium.

    Many reactions can go in both directions.
  2. Dynamic equilibrium
    At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.
  3. Equilibrium constants
    The equilibrium constant expresses the position of equilibrium as a number.
  4. Kc
    Kc is the equilibrium constant in terms of concentrations.
  5. Kp
    Kp is the equilibrium constant for gases in terms of partial pressures.
  6. Reaction quotients
    The reaction quotient Q describes a mixture that may not be at equilibrium.
  7. Le Chatelier's principle
    Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to change.
  8. Temperature and equilibrium
    Temperature is the only factor that changes the equilibrium constant.
  9. Pressure and equilibrium
    Pressure changes affect equilibria involving gases.
  10. Concentration and equilibrium
    Changing concentrations shifts the position of equilibrium.
  11. Heterogeneous equilibria
    Heterogeneous equilibria involve more than one phase.
  12. Equilibrium calculations
    Equilibrium calculations find unknown concentrations or constants.
  13. Industrial equilibrium
    Industry chooses conditions to make equilibrium reactions economical.
  14. Coupled equilibria
    Coupled equilibria share species, so a change in one affects the other.
  15. Thermodynamic interpretation
    Thermodynamics explains why equilibrium positions are where they are.
