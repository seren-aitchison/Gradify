Key terms and formulas at a glance

Matter
Anything that has mass and takes up space
Pure substance
A single element or compound with a fixed composition and properties
Element
A substance consisting of atoms that all have the same atomic number
Mobile phase
The solvent that moves through a chromatography system
Physical change
A change in which no new substance is formed
Sublimation
A change directly from solid to gas without melting
Nucleus
The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons
Chemical symbol
The internationally agreed one- or two-letter abbreviation for an element
Molecular formula
The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule
Coefficient
A number in front of a formula in an equation, giving the number of particles or moles
Conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants
Relative atomic mass (Ar)
The weighted mean mass of an atom of an element relative to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is chemistry called the central science?

It connects physics (the behaviour of particles and energy) with biology, medicine, materials and earth science, which all depend on chemical substances and reactions. From “What is chemistry?” →

In plain English: Chemistry is the study of matter: what substances are made of, how their particles are arranged and bonded, and how substances change into new ones in chemical reactions. Key idea: Chemistry explains the properties and changes of matter in terms of particles, and describes them with symbols.

Why does temperature stay constant while ice melts?

The energy supplied is used to overcome the forces holding particles in the solid, not to increase their kinetic energy. From “States of matter” →

In plain English: Substances exist as solids, liquids or gases depending on the balance between the energy of their particles and the forces between them. Key idea: State depends on particle energy versus the strength of forces between particles; temperature is constant during a change of state.

Why must chemical equations be balanced?

Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in reactions, so each element must have the same number of atoms on both sides. From “Balancing equations” →

In plain English: Balancing makes the number of atoms of each element the same on both sides of an equation, reflecting the conservation of mass. Key idea: Balance by adjusting coefficients only, leaving free elements to last and using the smallest whole numbers.

What is the difference between Mr and molar mass?

They have the same number, but Mr has no units and molar mass is in g/mol. From “Molar mass” →

In plain English: Molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol. Key idea: M (g/mol) equals Mr in value; use n = m/M in whichever arrangement you need.

How many moles are in 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid?

n = 0.200 × 0.0250 = 0.00500 mol. From “Concentration” →

In plain English: The concentration of a solution tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume. Key idea: c = n/V with V in dm³; always convert cm³ ÷ 1,000.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which branch of chemistry mainly studies carbon compounds? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Organic chemistry. Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds, including most molecules in living things and medicines.

  2. Which is a compound? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sodium chloride. NaCl contains two elements chemically combined; O₂ is an element, and brass and sea water are mixtures.

  3. Which can't be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An element. Elements are the simplest substances chemically; compounds can be decomposed into elements.

  4. A chemist measures how quickly a medicine breaks down at different temperatures. Which branch is this mostly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Physical chemistry (kinetics). Rates of reaction and their temperature dependence belong to physical chemistry.

  5. A white solid melts between 120 and 128 °C. What does this suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is impure: a mixture melts over a range. Pure substances melt at a sharp, fixed temperature; a melting range indicates impurities.

  6. Two samples of copper(II) oxide, made in different ways, both contain 79.9% copper by mass. This supports… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    the law of definite proportions. A fixed mass composition regardless of preparation is exactly what the law predicts.

  7. Which statement links all three levels of description correctly? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Limewater turns cloudy (macroscopic) because Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions form insoluble calcium carbonate (particulate), Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (symbolic). Only this answer connects what is observed to the particles and to the equation.

  8. Which particle diagram describes a mixture of an element and a compound? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Some pairs of identical atoms and some groups of two different atoms joined together, not linked to each other. Pairs of identical atoms are an element's molecules, groups of different joined atoms are a compound, and the two kinds being separate makes a mixture.

  9. Why is sodium chloride safe to eat when sodium and chlorine are both dangerous? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    In the compound, sodium and chloride are ions held in a lattice and no longer have the reactivity of the free elements. Chemical bonding creates a new substance with new properties; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions don't behave like sodium metal or chlorine gas.

Key takeaways

  • Chemistry explains the properties and changes of matter in terms of particles, and describes them with symbols.
  • Pure substances (elements and compounds) have fixed compositions; mixtures don't, and their parts aren't chemically joined.
  • A compound has a fixed composition and new properties because its elements are chemically bonded.
  • Separation methods work because components differ in a physical property: size, solubility, boiling point or affinity for a phase.
  • A chemical change makes new substances by breaking and forming bonds; a physical change doesn't.
  • State depends on particle energy versus the strength of forces between particles; temperature is constant during a change of state.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Chemistry

  • Thinking "chemicals" are only artificial substances. Everything, including water and the food we eat, is made of chemicals.
  • Calling O₂ a compound because it contains two atoms. Both atoms are oxygen, so it is an element made of molecules.
  • Thinking a compound keeps the properties of its elements, like a mixture does. Sodium chloride isn't reactive or toxic like its elements.
  • Calling dissolving a chemical change. Dissolving salt is a physical process: evaporating the water gets the salt back.
  • Calling boiling water a chemical change because bubbles form. The bubbles are water vapour, the same substance.
  • Thinking particles in a solid don't move. They vibrate constantly about fixed positions.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is chemistry?
    Chemistry is the study of matter: what substances are made of, how their particles are arranged and bonded, and how substances change into new ones in chemical reactions.
  2. Matter and its classification
    All matter can be classified as a pure substance (an element or a compound) or a mixture.
  3. Elements and compounds
    There are 118 known elements, but millions of compounds.
  4. Mixtures
    Mixtures can be separated by physical methods that exploit differences in the properties of their parts: particle size, solubility, boiling point or how strongly they stick to a surface.
  5. Physical and chemical changes
    In a physical change the same substance remains, perhaps in a different state or shape; in a chemical change new substances form.
  6. States of matter
    Substances exist as solids, liquids or gases depending on the balance between the energy of their particles and the forces between them.
  7. Atomic theory
    Everything is made of atoms.
  8. Chemical symbols
    Each element has a one- or two-letter symbol, agreed internationally by IUPAC.
  9. Chemical formulae
    A chemical formula shows which elements are in a substance and in what ratio.
  10. Chemical equations
    A chemical equation summarises a reaction: reactants on the left, products on the right, with formulae, state symbols and the numbers of each particle.
  11. Balancing equations
    Balancing makes the number of atoms of each element the same on both sides of an equation, reflecting the conservation of mass.
  12. Relative atomic mass
    Atoms are far too small to weigh individually in grams, so chemists compare their masses with carbon-12.
  13. Relative molecular mass
    The relative molecular (or formula) mass of a substance is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula.
  14. The mole
    The mole is the chemist's counting unit.
  15. Avogadro's constant
    The Avogadro constant, 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, connects the atomic scale with the laboratory scale.
  16. Molar mass
    Molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol.
  17. Stoichiometry
    Stoichiometry is the calculation of the amounts of substances that react and form, using the mole ratios in a balanced equation.
  18. Limiting reagents
    In most reactions one reactant runs out first: the limiting reagent.
  19. Percentage yield
    The actual amount of product is almost always less than the theoretical maximum.
  20. Atom economy
    Atom economy measures how much of the mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.
  21. Concentration
    The concentration of a solution tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume.
  22. Dilutions
    Diluting a solution adds solvent but doesn't change the amount of solute.
  23. Significant figures
    The number of significant figures in a result shows how precisely it is known.
  24. Scientific notation
    Chemistry spans numbers from 10²³ particles in a mole to 10⁻¹⁰ m across an atom.
  25. Units and measurements
    Every measurement in chemistry needs a number, a unit and an uncertainty.

Foundations of Chemistry: questions students ask

What is the difference between an element and a compound?

An element contains only one type of atom; a compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.

Which method separates ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) from water?

Fractional distillation.

Why is dissolving salt in water a physical change?

No new substance forms: evaporating the water gives back the salt.

What did the gold-foil experiment show?

Most of the atom is empty space and the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.
