Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why do atoms form bonds?

Bonding lowers the energy of the system. From “Introduction to chemical bonding” →

In plain English: Chemical bonds hold atoms together in elements and compounds. Key idea: Atoms bond because it lowers their energy through electrostatic attraction; ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals, covalent bonds between non-metals and metallic bonds between metal atoms.

How many valence electrons are in CO₂?

16. From “Lewis structures” →

In plain English: Lewis structures show how valence electrons are arranged in molecules. Key idea: Lewis structures are drawn by counting valence electrons, choosing a central atom, adding bonds and lone pairs and forming multiple bonds where needed; exceptions include incomplete octets, expanded octets and odd-electron molecules.

What is a bond dipole?

A separation of charge along a polar bond. From “Bond polarity” →

In plain English: Bonds between atoms of different electronegativity are polar. Key idea: Bond polarity arises from unequal electron sharing, giving δ+ and δ− ends and a bond dipole; greater electronegativity difference gives more polarity, partial ionic character and reactive δ+ sites.

What force helps ionic solids dissolve in water?

Ion-dipole forces. From “Dipole-dipole interactions” →

In plain English: Polar molecules attract each other through permanent dipoles. Key idea: Permanent dipole-dipole forces attract δ+ and δ− ends of polar molecules in addition to London forces, raising boiling points for molecules of similar size, though London forces dominate in larger molecules.

Why is O₂ paramagnetic?

It has two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals. From “Molecular orbital theory” →

In plain English: Molecular orbital theory describes electrons spread over whole molecules. Key idea: Molecular orbital theory combines atomic orbitals into lower-energy bonding and higher-energy antibonding orbitals; bond order is half the difference between bonding and antibonding electrons, and unpaired π* electrons explain oxygen's paramagnetism.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which type of bond forms between a metal and a non-metal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ionic bonding. Electrons transfer from the metal to the non-metal, forming ions.

  2. What is the formula of aluminium oxide? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Al₂O₃. Two Al³⁺ (+6) balance three O²⁻ (−6) to give a neutral compound.

  3. How many electron pairs are shared in a double bond? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Two. A double bond consists of two shared pairs of electrons between atoms.

  4. Which molecule breaks the octet rule? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Boron trifluoride, BF₃. Boron in BF₃ has only six outer electrons, an exception to the rule.

  5. Why is magnesium oxide's melting point much higher than sodium chloride's? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Its ions have higher charges, giving stronger attraction. Doubly charged ions attract each other far more strongly than singly charged ions.

  6. Why does diamond have a very high melting point? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds in its network. Every atom is covalently bonded in a giant network.

  7. Why does making bonds release energy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Attraction between charges lowers the potential energy of the system. Moving into an attractive arrangement lowers energy, which is released.

  8. Why are ionic crystals brittle? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Shifting layers brings like charges together, which repel. A small displacement aligns like charges and splits the crystal.

  9. Why do simple molecular substances have low melting points? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Only weak intermolecular forces must be overcome. Melting separates molecules without breaking covalent bonds.

Key takeaways

  • Atoms bond because it lowers their energy through electrostatic attraction; ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals, covalent bonds between non-metals and metallic bonds between metal atoms.
  • Ionic bonds form by electron transfer from metals to non-metals, giving ions held in giant lattices by strong attraction, so ionic compounds have high melting points, conduct when molten or dissolved and are brittle.
  • Covalent bonds are shared electron pairs; single, double and triple bonds share one to three pairs, with multiple bonds shorter and stronger; simple molecules have low melting points while giant covalent structures have very high ones.
  • A coordinate bond forms when one atom donates both electrons of a shared pair, needing a lone pair and an empty orbital; examples include NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, Al₂Cl₆ and complex ions, and once formed it is identical to other covalent bonds.
  • Metallic bonding is the attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons, explaining conductivity, malleability and ductility; it strengthens with more delocalised electrons and smaller ions, and alloys are harder because layers slide less easily.
  • Lewis structures are drawn by counting valence electrons, choosing a central atom, adding bonds and lone pairs and forming multiple bonds where needed; exceptions include incomplete octets, expanded octets and odd-electron molecules.

Common mistakes in Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

  • Thinking atoms "want" a full shell. Bonding happens because it lowers energy, not because atoms have desires.
  • Thinking ionic compounds conduct when solid. Ions are fixed in the solid lattice and cannot move.
  • Thinking covalent bonds break when water boils. Boiling separates molecules; the O–H bonds remain intact.
  • Thinking a dative bond stays different after forming. It becomes indistinguishable from other covalent bonds.
  • Thinking metals conduct because ions move. Delocalised electrons carry the current.
  • Forgetting to adjust the electron count for ion charge. Add electrons for negative charges and remove them for positive charges.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to chemical bonding
    Chemical bonds hold atoms together in elements and compounds.
  2. Ionic bonding
    Ionic bonds form when electrons transfer from metal to non-metal atoms.
  3. Covalent bonding
    Covalent bonds form when atoms share pairs of electrons.
  4. Coordinate bonding
    In a coordinate bond, both shared electrons come from one atom.
  5. Metallic bonding
    Metallic bonding holds metal atoms together with a sea of delocalised electrons.
  6. Lewis structures
    Lewis structures show how valence electrons are arranged in molecules.
  7. Formal charge
    Formal charge helps decide which Lewis structure best represents a molecule.
  8. Resonance
    Some molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure.
  9. VSEPR theory
    VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes from electron pair repulsion.
  10. Molecular geometry
    Molecular shapes follow from the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  11. Bond polarity
    Bonds between atoms of different electronegativity are polar.
  12. Molecular polarity
    A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel.
  13. Electronegativity
    This lesson applies electronegativity to bonding: predicting bond character, comparing scales and explaining the bonding continuum.
  14. Intermolecular forces
    Intermolecular forces act between molecules and determine many physical properties.
  15. London dispersion forces
    London dispersion forces arise from temporary dipoles in all molecules.
  16. Dipole-dipole interactions
    Polar molecules attract each other through permanent dipoles.
  17. Hydrogen bonding
    Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces in many substances.
  18. Bond enthalpy
    Bond enthalpy measures the energy needed to break a bond.
  19. Lattice enthalpy
    Lattice enthalpy measures the strength of ionic bonding.
  20. Born-Haber cycles
    Born-Haber cycles use Hess's law to find lattice enthalpies.
  21. Molecular orbital theory
    Molecular orbital theory describes electrons spread over whole molecules.
  22. Hybridisation
    Hybridisation explains bonding geometry by mixing atomic orbitals.
  23. Sigma and pi bonding
    Covalent bonds can be sigma or pi bonds, formed by different orbital overlaps.
  24. Delocalisation
    Delocalised electrons spread over several atoms, changing structure and properties.
  25. Crystal structures
    Solids are built from repeating arrangements of particles.
  26. Solid-state bonding
    Solids are classified by the bonding between their particles.
