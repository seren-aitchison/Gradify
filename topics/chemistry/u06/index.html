Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is an isolated system?

A system exchanging neither matter nor energy with its surroundings. From “Energy in chemical systems” →

In plain English: Chemical reactions involve energy changes between a system and its surroundings. Key idea: Chemical systems exchange energy with surroundings as heat and work; systems can be open, closed or isolated, chemical energy is stored in bonds and energy is conserved, so energy released by reactions appears in the surroundings.

What is standard pressure for thermochemistry?

100 kPa. From “Standard enthalpy changes” →

In plain English: Standard conditions allow enthalpy changes to be compared. Key idea: Standard enthalpy changes are measured at 100 kPa, a stated temperature (usually 298 K) and 1 mol dm⁻³ solutions, with substances in their standard states; types include formation, combustion, neutralisation and reaction enthalpies.

State Hess's law.

The enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken. From “Hess's law” →

In plain English: Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route. Key idea: Hess's law states that enthalpy change is independent of route, allowing unmeasurable enthalpy changes to be found from cycles using formation data (products minus reactants) or combustion data (reactants minus products).

State the Gibbs equation.

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. From “Gibbs free energy” →

In plain English: Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict whether reactions are feasible. Key idea: Gibbs free energy ΔG = ΔH − TΔS predicts feasibility when negative; setting ΔG = 0 gives the temperature at which feasibility changes, and feasibility depends on the signs of ΔH and ΔS, though kinetics may still make reactions slow.

State the second law of thermodynamics.

In a spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe increases. From “Second law of thermodynamics” →

In plain English: The second law states that the total entropy of the universe increases in spontaneous processes. Key idea: The second law states that total entropy increases in spontaneous processes; ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH ÷ T, and ΔG < 0 is equivalent to ΔS(total) > 0, so local order can increase only if the surroundings gain more entropy.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which type of system exchanges energy but not matter? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A closed system. Closed systems allow heat transfer but keep matter inside.

  2. What happens to the temperature of the surroundings in an exothermic reaction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It increases. Energy released to the surroundings warms them up.

  3. What happens to the temperature of the surroundings in an endothermic reaction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It decreases. The reaction takes energy from the surroundings, cooling them.

  4. Where does the energy released by a burning fuel go? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Into the surroundings as heat and light. Energy is conserved and transferred to the surroundings.

  5. In an exothermic energy profile, where are the products? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Lower in energy than the reactants. Energy has been released, so products are lower.

  6. Why is photosynthesis endothermic? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It absorbs light energy to make glucose. Plants store absorbed light energy in chemical bonds.

  7. How can energy changes in a reaction be detected? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    By measuring temperature changes in the surroundings. Heat transferred changes the surroundings' temperature.

  8. Why do some exothermic reactions need a spark or flame to start? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Activation energy must be supplied before bonds can break. An energy barrier must be crossed even if energy is released overall.

  9. How can an endothermic reaction be spontaneous? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A large increase in entropy can make it favourable. Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy.

Key takeaways

  • Chemical systems exchange energy with surroundings as heat and work; systems can be open, closed or isolated, chemical energy is stored in bonds and energy is conserved, so energy released by reactions appears in the surroundings.
  • Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings, so they warm up and ΔH is negative, with products lower in energy than reactants; examples include combustion, neutralisation, respiration and oxidation.
  • Endothermic reactions absorb energy from the surroundings, so they cool and ΔH is positive, with products higher in energy; examples include thermal decomposition, photosynthesis and some dissolving, used in cold packs.
  • Enthalpy change ΔH is the heat transferred at constant pressure, equal to H(products) − H(reactants) in kJ mol⁻¹; thermochemical equations include states and ΔH, which scales with amount and changes sign on reversal.
  • Standard enthalpy changes are measured at 100 kPa, a stated temperature (usually 298 K) and 1 mol dm⁻³ solutions, with substances in their standard states; types include formation, combustion, neutralisation and reaction enthalpies.
  • Standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change forming one mole of a compound from its elements in standard states, zero for elements; ΔrH⦵ = ΣΔfH⦵(products) − ΣΔfH⦵(reactants), using coefficients.

Common mistakes in Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

  • Thinking energy is "used up" in reactions. It is transferred, not destroyed.
  • Thinking exothermic reactions need no energy to start. Many need activation energy first.
  • Thinking endothermic reactions cannot happen without heating. Some happen spontaneously because entropy increases.
  • Ignoring state symbols in thermochemical equations. Different states give different ΔH values.
  • Thinking standard temperature is always 273 K. Thermochemical data usually use 298 K.
  • Forgetting to multiply ΔfH⦵ by the coefficients. Each value must be multiplied by its number of moles.

Lessons in this school

  1. Energy in chemical systems
    Chemical reactions involve energy changes between a system and its surroundings.
  2. Exothermic reactions
    Exothermic reactions release energy to their surroundings.
  3. Endothermic reactions
    Endothermic reactions take in energy from their surroundings.
  4. Enthalpy
    Enthalpy is the heat content of a system at constant pressure.
  5. Standard enthalpy changes
    Standard conditions allow enthalpy changes to be compared.
  6. Enthalpy of formation
    The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change for making a compound from its elements.
  7. Enthalpy of combustion
    Enthalpy of combustion measures the energy released when a substance burns completely.
  8. Enthalpy of neutralisation
    Neutralisation releases energy as acid and alkali react.
  9. Hess's law
    Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route.
  10. Bond enthalpies
    This lesson compares enthalpy changes calculated from bond enthalpies with those from Hess's law and explains the differences.
  11. Calorimetry
    Calorimetry measures energy changes by recording temperature changes.
  12. Entropy
    Entropy measures the dispersal of energy and matter in a system.
  13. Gibbs free energy
    Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict whether reactions are feasible.
  14. Spontaneity
    A spontaneous process occurs without continuous outside help.
  15. Thermodynamic equilibrium
    At equilibrium, ΔG is zero and there is no driving force for change.
  16. First law of thermodynamics
    The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved.
  17. Second law of thermodynamics
    The second law states that the total entropy of the universe increases in spontaneous processes.
  18. Chemical potential
    Chemical potential describes how Gibbs free energy changes when a substance is added.
  19. Temperature dependence
    Temperature changes the balance between enthalpy and entropy.
  20. Thermodynamic cycles
    Thermodynamic cycles combine energy terms to find quantities that cannot be measured directly.

What is enthalpy change?

Why are spirit burner results less negative than data book values?

Which formula is used with combustion data?
