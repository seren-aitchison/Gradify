Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the usual units of rate?

mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. From “Introduction to reaction rates” →

In plain English: Reaction rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products form. Key idea: Rate is the change in concentration or another measurable quantity per unit time, found from gradients of graphs, with initial rate from the tangent at t = 0; methods include gas volume, mass loss, colour change and precipitate formation.

What is a catalyst?

A substance that increases rate without being permanently changed. From “Catalysts” →

In plain English: Catalysts speed up reactions without being used up. Key idea: Catalysts increase rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, are not used up, do not change ΔH or equilibrium position and allow lower temperatures, saving energy and reducing waste.

How are orders of reaction determined?

By experiment. From “Rate equations” →

In plain English: Rate equations show how rate depends on the concentrations of reactants. Key idea: Rate equations have the form rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, where orders are found experimentally and overall order is their sum; k is the rate constant, and the equation reflects the mechanism rather than the balanced equation.

Does k change with concentration?

No; it changes with temperature. From “Rate constants” →

In plain English: The rate constant links rate to concentration and depends on temperature. Key idea: The rate constant k links rate to concentrations, is fixed at a given temperature, has units depending on overall order and increases with temperature or when a catalyst is added.

Why does refrigeration slow food spoilage?

Lower temperature slows the reactions and microbial processes. From “Temperature effects” →

In plain English: Temperature has a strong effect on reaction rates. Key idea: Higher temperature speeds reactions mainly by increasing the fraction of collisions above Ea, roughly doubling many rates per 10 °C, with consequences for food storage, biology and industrial safety, including thermal runaway.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chemical Kinetics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which method suits a reaction that produces a gas? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Collecting the gas in a gas syringe. Gas volume collected over time shows the rate.

  2. How does increasing concentration affect rate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It increases rate by making collisions more frequent. More particles in the same volume collide more often.

  3. Which particles can react on a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Those with energy above the activation energy. Only energetic enough collisions succeed.

  4. Why does the rate of most reactions decrease over time? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reactants are used up, so collisions become less frequent. Lower concentration means fewer collisions per second.

  5. Why does temperature have a large effect on rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    More collisions have energy above the activation energy. The fraction of energetic collisions rises sharply.

  6. How does a catalyst affect the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It does not change the curve but lowers Ea. More particles exceed a lower activation energy.

  7. Why is a colorimeter useful for measuring rate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It measures concentration changes continuously without disturbing the reaction. Absorbance tracks coloured species as they change.

  8. Why can flour dust explode in a mill? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its huge surface area allows extremely rapid combustion. Fine particles burn almost instantly in air.

  9. Why can a 10 °C rise roughly double the rate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The fraction of particles above Ea increases greatly. The energetic tail grows much more than the average energy.

Key takeaways

  • Rate is the change in concentration or another measurable quantity per unit time, found from gradients of graphs, with initial rate from the tangent at t = 0; methods include gas volume, mass loss, colour change and precipitate formation.
  • Successful collisions require enough energy and correct orientation; higher concentration, pressure and surface area increase collision frequency, higher temperature increases both frequency and energy, and catalysts lower the activation energy.
  • Activation energy is the minimum energy for reaction; on a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, only particles beyond Ea react, so raising temperature or using a catalyst greatly increases the fraction able to react.
  • Reaction profiles plot energy against reaction progress, showing reactants, products, transition states at peaks, intermediates in troughs, Ea and ΔH; catalysts lower peaks without changing ΔH.
  • Catalysts increase rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, are not used up, do not change ΔH or equilibrium position and allow lower temperatures, saving energy and reducing waste.
  • Homogeneous catalysts share the reactants' phase and work by forming intermediates, often changing oxidation state, as Fe²⁺ does with peroxodisulfate and iodide and chlorine radicals do in ozone depletion.

Common mistakes in Chemical Kinetics

  • Thinking rate stays constant throughout a reaction. It usually decreases as reactants are used up.
  • Thinking every collision causes a reaction. Most collisions lack enough energy or the right orientation.
  • Thinking catalysts give particles more energy. Catalysts lower the activation energy instead.
  • Confusing transition states with intermediates. Transition states are peaks; intermediates are troughs.
  • Thinking catalysts increase the yield at equilibrium. They speed up reaching equilibrium but do not shift it.
  • Thinking homogeneous catalysts are unchanged at every step. They change temporarily but are regenerated.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to reaction rates
    Reaction rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products form.
  2. Collision theory
    Collision theory explains why reactions happen and what changes their rates.
  3. Activation energy
    Activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur.
  4. Reaction profiles
    Reaction profiles show how energy changes as a reaction proceeds.
  5. Catalysts
    Catalysts speed up reactions without being used up.
  6. Homogeneous catalysis
    Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants.
  7. Heterogeneous catalysis
    Heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase from the reactants.
  8. Enzyme catalysis
    Enzymes are biological catalysts with remarkable speed and selectivity.
  9. Rate equations
    Rate equations show how rate depends on the concentrations of reactants.
  10. Reaction order
    Reaction orders can be found from initial rates or from graphs.
  11. Zero-order reactions
    In a zero-order reaction, rate does not depend on concentration.
  12. First-order reactions
    In a first-order reaction, rate is proportional to concentration.
  13. Second-order reactions
    In a second-order reaction, rate depends on the square of a concentration or on two concentrations.
  14. Rate constants
    The rate constant links rate to concentration and depends on temperature.
  15. Half-life
    Half-life is the time for a concentration to fall to half its value.
  16. Integrated rate laws
    Integrated rate laws give concentration as a function of time.
  17. Arrhenius equation
    The Arrhenius equation links the rate constant to temperature and activation energy.
  18. Temperature effects
    Temperature has a strong effect on reaction rates.
  19. Reaction mechanisms
    A reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps by which reactants become products.
  20. Rate-determining steps
    The slowest step in a mechanism controls the overall rate.
  21. Catalytic cycles
    Catalytic cycles show how catalysts are used and regenerated in repeating loops.
  22. Experimental kinetics
    Kinetics experiments require careful planning and analysis.
