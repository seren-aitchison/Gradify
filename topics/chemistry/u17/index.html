Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the four main classes of materials.

Metals, ceramics, polymers and composites. From “Materials chemistry” →

In plain English: Materials chemistry designs substances with useful properties. Key idea: Materials are grouped as metals, ceramics, polymers and composites; their bonding, microstructure, defects and processing set their properties, and materials are chosen by matching properties, cost and environmental impact to the application.

Why are ceramics brittle?

Bonds cannot rearrange, so cracks spread rapidly. From “Ceramics” →

In plain English: Ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic solids. Key idea: Ceramics have ionic or covalent giant structures that are hard, heat resistant and chemically inert but brittle; they range from traditional clays and glass to advanced and functional ceramics used in engineering, medicine and electronics.

Name the four environmental compartments.

Atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere. From “Environmental chemistry” →

In plain English: Environmental chemistry studies chemical processes in air, water and soil. Key idea: Environmental chemistry studies chemicals in the atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere, how they move, persist and bioaccumulate and how carbon, nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus cycles are affected by human activity.

Why do N₂ and O₂ not absorb infrared radiation?

Their vibrations do not change the dipole moment. From “Greenhouse gases” →

In plain English: Greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere. Key idea: Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation from Earth's surface because their vibrations change dipole moments; CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and water are important, and GWP compares their warming effects relative to CO₂.

What does steam cracking mainly produce?

Ethene and propene. From “Petroleum chemistry” →

In plain English: Petroleum provides fuels and chemical feedstocks. Key idea: Refining uses fractional distillation, catalytic and steam cracking and reforming to make fuels and feedstocks; ethene, propene and aromatics are converted into plastics, solvents and medicines, while most oil is still burned as fuel.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which class of material is hard, brittle and heat resistant? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ceramics, with ionic or covalent networks. Strong directional bonds resist deformation and heat.

  2. Which alloy contains copper and zinc? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Brass. Brass is a substitutional alloy of copper and zinc.

  3. Which polymer type can be recycled by melting? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Thermoplastics without cross-links. Without cross-links, chains can slide when heated.

  4. Why are aircraft parts often made from composites? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They combine high strength with low weight. Combining materials unites useful properties.

  5. Why are alloys usually harder than pure metals? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Different-sized atoms stop layers sliding over each other. Disrupted layers resist slip, so the metal is harder to deform.

  6. Why does cross-linking make rubber tougher? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Covalent links stop chains sliding past each other. Linked chains spring back rather than flow.

  7. Why does grain size affect a metal's strength? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Grain boundaries block dislocation movement, so finer grains are stronger. More boundaries hinder slip between planes.

  8. Why does too much carbon make steel brittle? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Hard carbide phases form that crack instead of deforming. Excess hard carbide phases reduce toughness, so the steel cracks.

  9. Why does increasing chain length raise a polymer's strength? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Longer chains entangle more and have greater intermolecular forces. More contact and entanglement resist pulling apart.

Key takeaways

  • Materials are grouped as metals, ceramics, polymers and composites; their bonding, microstructure, defects and processing set their properties, and materials are chosen by matching properties, cost and environmental impact to the application.
  • Alloys are substitutional or interstitial; different-sized atoms disrupt layers so they cannot slide easily, making alloys stronger, and important alloys include steels, stainless steel, bronze and shape-memory alloys.
  • Polymers are long chains of repeating units; chain length, branching, cross-linking and crystallinity control properties, thermoplastics soften on heating while thermosets do not and elastomers stretch and recover.
  • Addition polymerisation joins alkene monomers through radical chains with no by-product, while condensation polymerisation joins bifunctional monomers with loss of small molecules, giving polyesters and polyamides; repeat units reveal the monomers.
  • Natural biopolymers include polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids and rubber, while bio-based plastics such as PLA and PHAs come from renewable sources; bio-based and biodegradable are different properties and benefits depend on disposal and life-cycle assessment.
  • Ceramics have ionic or covalent giant structures that are hard, heat resistant and chemically inert but brittle; they range from traditional clays and glass to advanced and functional ceramics used in engineering, medicine and electronics.

Common mistakes in Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry

  • Thinking one material is best for every job. Each application needs a different balance of properties.
  • Thinking an alloy is a chemical compound. It is a mixture with variable composition.
  • Thinking all plastics can be remelted. Thermosets are cross-linked and cannot be melted.
  • Drawing the repeat unit with the C=C still present. The double bond opens during polymerisation.
  • Assuming bio-based means biodegradable. Some bio-based plastics are not biodegradable at all.
  • Thinking ceramics are only pottery. Advanced ceramics are vital in engines, electronics and medicine.

Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry: questions students ask

What is a dopant?

A deliberately added impurity that changes properties.

What type of alloy is steel?

An interstitial alloy of iron and carbon.

What is a thermoset?

A cross-linked polymer that does not melt on heating.

What is lost in condensation polymerisation?

A small molecule such as water or HCl.
