Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is an alkali?

A base that dissolves in water. From “Introduction to acids and bases” →

In plain English: Acids and bases are among the most important classes of substance in chemistry. Key idea: Acids have pH below 7 and release H⁺ in water, bases neutralise acids and alkalis are soluble bases releasing OH⁻; acids react with metals, bases, carbonates and ammonia to form salts.

What is a strong acid?

An acid that ionises completely in water. From “Strong acids and bases” →

In plain English: Strong acids and bases ionise completely in water. Key idea: Strong acids and bases ionise completely, so [H⁺] equals concentration for monoprotic strong acids; strength means degree of ionisation, unlike concentration, and pH of strong bases uses Kw.

What does a smaller pKa indicate?

A stronger acid. From “Acid dissociation constants” →

In plain English: The acid dissociation constant Ka measures the strength of a weak acid. Key idea: Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA] measures weak acid strength, with pKa = −log Ka; for weak acids [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka[HA]) using approximations valid for small dissociation, and larger Ka or smaller pKa means a stronger acid.

What are concordant titres?

Titres within 0.10 cm³ of each other. From “Acid-base titrations” →

In plain English: Titrations measure the concentration of an acid or base accurately. Key idea: Titrations use a pipette for an exact volume and a burette to add titrant to an indicator end point, repeating to concordant results; concentration is found from moles = c × V and the mole ratio, while careful rinsing and reading reduce errors.

When does a precipitate form?

When the ionic product exceeds Ksp. From “Ksp” →

In plain English: The solubility product Ksp quantifies the solubility of sparingly soluble salts. Key idea: Ksp = product of ion concentrations raised to their formula powers in a saturated solution; it gives solubility through expressions such as s² or 4s³, and comparing the ionic product Q with Ksp predicts precipitation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What forms when an acid reacts with a metal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A salt and hydrogen. Reactive metals displace hydrogen from acids to form a salt.

  2. According to Arrhenius, what does an acid produce in water? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    H⁺ ions. Arrhenius acids release hydrogen ions in aqueous solution.

  3. What is the conjugate acid of NH₃? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    NH₄⁺. Adding one proton to ammonia gives the ammonium ion.

  4. Which substance is a base but not an alkali? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Copper(II) oxide. Copper(II) oxide neutralises acids but does not dissolve in water.

  5. Why can't Arrhenius theory easily explain ammonia as a base? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ammonia contains no hydroxide ions. Its basicity comes from accepting a proton.

  6. Why is water described as amphoteric? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It can donate or accept a proton. Water acts as base with HCl and acid with NH₃.

  7. Why does limewater turn cloudy with the gas from acid and carbonate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Carbon dioxide forms insoluble calcium carbonate with limewater. CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ gives a white precipitate of CaCO₃.

  8. What is a major limitation of Arrhenius theory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It only applies to reactions in water. Non-aqueous acid-base reactions are not covered.

  9. Why does a strong acid have a weak conjugate base? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The conjugate base has little tendency to regain a proton. Readily giving up a proton means weak attraction to regain it.

Key takeaways

  • Acids have pH below 7 and release H⁺ in water, bases neutralise acids and alkalis are soluble bases releasing OH⁻; acids react with metals, bases, carbonates and ammonia to form salts.
  • Arrhenius defined acids as producing H⁺ and bases as producing OH⁻ in water, with neutralisation forming water, but the theory is limited to aqueous solutions and cannot explain bases such as ammonia directly.
  • Brønsted-Lowry acids are proton donors and bases proton acceptors; conjugate pairs differ by one proton, stronger acids have weaker conjugate bases and water is amphoteric, acting as acid or base.
  • Lewis acids accept electron pairs and Lewis bases donate them, forming dative bonds; this broadest theory covers reactions without protons, such as BF₃ with NH₃ and metal complexes, and includes all Brønsted-Lowry bases.
  • Strong acids and bases ionise completely, so [H⁺] equals concentration for monoprotic strong acids; strength means degree of ionisation, unlike concentration, and pH of strong bases uses Kw.
  • Weak acids and bases ionise only partially, with equilibrium to the left; at equal concentration weak acids have higher pH, conduct less and react more slowly, but neutralise the same amount of base per mole.

Common mistakes in Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

  • Thinking all bases are alkalis. Only soluble bases are alkalis.
  • Thinking H⁺ ions exist freely in water. They bond to water molecules as H₃O⁺.
  • Thinking a conjugate base must contain OH⁻. It is simply the species left after the acid loses a proton.
  • Thinking Lewis acids must contain hydrogen. BF₃ and metal ions are Lewis acids without hydrogen.
  • Confusing strong with concentrated. Strength is degree of ionisation; concentration is amount per volume.
  • Thinking a weak acid neutralises less alkali than a strong acid. Per mole, both neutralise the same amount.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to acids and bases
    Acids and bases are among the most important classes of substance in chemistry.
  2. Arrhenius theory
    The Arrhenius theory defined acids and bases by the ions they produce in water.
  3. Brønsted-Lowry theory
    The Brønsted-Lowry theory defines acids and bases by proton transfer.
  4. Lewis acid-base theory
    Lewis theory defines acids and bases by electron pairs, covering even more reactions.
  5. Strong acids and bases
    Strong acids and bases ionise completely in water.
  6. Weak acids and bases
    Weak acids and bases only partially ionise in water.
  7. pH
    pH measures the acidity of a solution on a logarithmic scale.
  8. pOH
    pOH and the ionic product of water connect acidity and basicity.
  9. Acid dissociation constants
    The acid dissociation constant Ka measures the strength of a weak acid.
  10. Base dissociation constants
    The base dissociation constant Kb measures the strength of a weak base.
  11. Ka and Kb
    Ka and Kb of a conjugate pair are linked through Kw.
  12. Buffers
    Buffers resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.
  13. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation
    The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation calculates buffer pH.
  14. Acid-base titrations
    Titrations measure the concentration of an acid or base accurately.
  15. Indicators
    Indicators change colour over a pH range.
  16. Equivalence points
    The equivalence point is reached when reactants have reacted in exact stoichiometric amounts.
  17. Solubility equilibria
    Sparingly soluble salts set up equilibria between solid and dissolved ions.
  18. Ksp
    The solubility product Ksp quantifies the solubility of sparingly soluble salts.
  19. Common-ion effect
    Adding an ion already present in an equilibrium reduces solubility or ionisation.
  20. Complex ion equilibria
    Metal ions form complexes with ligands in equilibrium.
  21. Amphoteric substances
    Amphoteric substances can act as acids or bases.
  22. Polyprotic acids
    Polyprotic acids can donate more than one proton.
