Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the lexical hypothesis?

Important personality differences become encoded as words in language. From “Trait theories of personality” →

In plain English: Trait theories describe personality as a set of stable characteristics that vary between people. Key idea: Traits are stable dispositions identified through language and factor analysis; they predict aggregated behaviour, with situations shaping specific acts.

What is sublimation?

Channelling unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities. From “Freud's psychodynamic theory of personality” →

In plain English: Freud's psychodynamic theory explains personality through unconscious processes, internal conflict and early childhood experience. Key idea: Freud explained personality through the id, ego, superego, unconscious conflict, defences and psychosexual stages; the theory is influential but largely untestable.

What is goodness of fit?

The match between a child's temperament and their environment. From “Temperament” →

In plain English: Temperament is the early-appearing, biologically based style of emotional reactivity and self-regulation. Key idea: Temperament is early, partly biological reactivity and regulation; goodness of fit with the environment shapes how it develops into personality.

What does the TAT ask people to do?

Tell stories about ambiguous pictures of people. From “Projective tests” →

In plain English: Projective tests ask people to respond to ambiguous stimuli, on the idea that responses reveal unconscious aspects of personality. Key idea: Projective tests interpret responses to ambiguous stimuli; most indices have weak reliability and validity, though a few show support.

What is the gender similarities hypothesis?

Males and females are similar on most psychological variables, with mostly small differences. From “Cognitive differences” →

In plain English: People differ in cognitive abilities, and psychologists study both individual and group differences. Key idea: Most cognitive sex differences are small and culturally variable; socioeconomic factors shape cognitive development; group differences must be interpreted with care.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Personality and Individual Differences

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statistical method did Cattell use to reduce trait words to 16 factors? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Factor analysis. Factor analysis groups correlated items into factors.

  2. A tendency to feel anxious and moody reflects high… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Neuroticism. Neuroticism is the tendency to experience anxiety, moodiness and other negative emotions.

  3. Eysenck measured personality with the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Eysenck Personality Questionnaire. The Eysenck Personality Questionnaire measured his three dimensions.

  4. A person is outgoing at parties but quiet in lectures. Which view best explains this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Interactionism. Traits and situations together shape behaviour.

  5. A colleague is curious, imaginative and loves new ideas. Which trait is likely high? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Openness to experience. Curiosity and imagination define openness.

  6. According to Eysenck, why might an introvert prefer a quiet library? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They are already highly aroused. Introverts are already highly aroused, so they avoid extra stimulation.

  7. Why does aggregating behaviour strengthen trait correlations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Random situational noise averages out. Averaging many occasions reveals stable tendencies.

  8. What does the HEXACO model add to the Big Five? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    An honesty–humility factor. HEXACO adds honesty–humility as a sixth dimension.

  9. Why is Eysenck's theory of crime criticised? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It underplays social causes. Poverty and environment are important causes of offending.

Key takeaways

  • Traits are stable dispositions identified through language and factor analysis; they predict aggregated behaviour, with situations shaping specific acts.
  • The Big Five (OCEAN) are broad traits measured by questionnaires, broadly replicated across cultures and predictive of important life outcomes.
  • Eysenck described extraversion, neuroticism and psychoticism with biological bases; the theory is testable and partly supported, but arousal and crime links are mixed.
  • Personality is moderately heritable, linked to reward and threat systems in the brain, and may persist because traits have environment-dependent costs and benefits.
  • Freud explained personality through the id, ego, superego, unconscious conflict, defences and psychosexual stages; the theory is influential but largely untestable.
  • Humanistic approaches stress growth, self-concept and needs; they influenced therapy and positive psychology but are hard to test precisely.

Common mistakes in Personality and Individual Differences

  • Expecting a trait to predict a single act. Traits predict patterns of behaviour across many situations.
  • Treating Big Five scores as types. People vary continuously on each dimension, and most are near the middle.
  • Assuming introverts dislike people. Eysenck's introverts prefer less stimulation, not necessarily less company.
  • Concluding that "heritable" means "fixed". Heritable traits still change with age and experience.
  • Treating Freud's specific claims as established. His broad ideas influenced psychology, but most specific claims lack support.
  • Treating Maslow's hierarchy as a rigid ladder. People pursue several needs at once.

Lessons in this school

  1. Trait theories of personality
    Trait theories describe personality as a set of stable characteristics that vary between people.
  2. The Big Five
    The Big Five model describes personality using five broad traits.
  3. Eysenck's theory
    Eysenck proposed a biologically based theory of personality with three dimensions.
  4. Biological approaches to personality
    Biological approaches explain personality through genes, brain systems and evolution.
  5. Freud's psychodynamic theory of personality
    Freud's psychodynamic theory explains personality through unconscious processes, internal conflict and early childhood experience.
  6. Humanistic approaches to personality
    Humanistic psychology sees people as striving towards growth and self-fulfilment.
  7. Self-concept
    The self-concept is the set of beliefs a person holds about who they are.
  8. Self-esteem
    Self-esteem is a person's overall evaluation of their own worth.
  9. Self-efficacy
    Self-efficacy is belief in one's ability to succeed at a particular task.
  10. Temperament
    Temperament is the early-appearing, biologically based style of emotional reactivity and self-regulation.
  11. Personality development
    Personality develops from childhood temperament into adult traits through biology, experience and social roles.
  12. Stability and change in personality
    Personality is both stable and changing.
  13. Personality assessment: psychometric tests
    Psychometric tests measure personality using standardised questionnaires.
  14. Projective tests
    Projective tests ask people to respond to ambiguous stimuli, on the idea that responses reveal unconscious aspects of personality.
  15. General intelligence (g)
    General intelligence, or g, is the common factor underlying performance on diverse cognitive tests.
  16. Fluid and crystallised intelligence
    Fluid intelligence is reasoning with new problems; crystallised intelligence is accumulated knowledge.
  17. Multiple intelligences
    Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences proposes several relatively independent intelligences.
  18. Emotional intelligence
    Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to perceive, understand and manage emotions.
  19. Cognitive differences
    People differ in cognitive abilities, and psychologists study both individual and group differences.
  20. Motivation and individual differences
    People differ in what motivates them and how strongly.
  21. Achievement motivation
    Achievement motivation is the drive to succeed and meet standards of excellence.
  22. Personality, behaviour and mental health
    Personality traits are linked to health behaviours and to the risk of mental health difficulties.
  23. Personality across cultures
    Cross-cultural research asks whether personality structure and average traits are the same everywhere.

Personality and Individual Differences: questions students ask

What does OCEAN stand for?

Openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism.

What are Eysenck's three dimensions?

Extraversion–introversion, neuroticism–stability and psychoticism.

What are the id, ego and superego?

The id seeks gratification, the ego balances demands with reality, and the superego holds moral standards.

What are conditions of worth?

Conditions a person believes they must meet to be loved or valued.
