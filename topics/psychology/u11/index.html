Key terms and formulas at a glance

Nominal data
Categories with no inherent order
Mean
The sum of all values divided by the number of values
Standard deviation
The square root of the variance; the typical distance of scores from the mean
Normal distribution
A symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution in which mean, median and mode are equal
Histogram
A graph showing the distribution of a continuous variable, with touching bars
Probability
A number from 0 to 1 expressing how likely an event is
Sampling distribution
The distribution of a statistic, such as the mean, across all possible samples of a given size
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for a population parameter, calculated from a sample
Null hypothesis
The hypothesis that there is no effect, difference or relationship in the population
p-value
The probability of data at least as extreme as those observed, if the null hypothesis were true
Effect size
A measure of the magnitude of a difference or relationship
Statistical power
The probability of detecting an effect that really exists (1 − β)

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is ordinal data?

Ordered categories or ranks where the gaps between values are not equal or known. From “Levels of measurement” →

In plain English: Data come at different levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio. Key idea: Nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio data carry increasing information, and the level determines which averages and tests are appropriate.

What is the multiplication rule for independent events?

p(A and B) = p(A) × p(B). From “Probability” →

In plain English: Probability is the language of uncertainty that underlies statistical inference. Key idea: Probabilities combine by simple rules; conditional probabilities depend heavily on base rates, which intuition tends to ignore.

How is Cohen's d calculated?

The difference between two means divided by the pooled standard deviation. From “Effect sizes” →

In plain English: An effect size measures how big an effect is, independently of sample size. Key idea: Effect sizes measure how large an effect is, independent of sample size, and should be interpreted in context rather than only by benchmarks.

What does a coefficient in multiple regression mean?

The predicted change in the outcome for a one-unit increase in that predictor, holding the other predictors constant. From “Multiple regression” →

In plain English: Multiple regression predicts an outcome from several predictors at once, estimating each predictor's contribution while holding the others constant. Key idea: Multiple regression estimates each predictor's unique contribution, holding the others constant; R² shows how much variance the model explains.

What is the non-parametric equivalent of the paired t-test?

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test. From “Non-parametric tests” →

In plain English: Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about distributions, working with ranks instead of raw scores. Key idea: Non-parametric tests use ranks and suit ordinal, skewed or outlier-prone data; each has a parametric equivalent.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Statistics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Reaction time in milliseconds is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ratio data. It has equal intervals and a true zero, so ratios (twice as slow) make sense.

  2. Which average is the only one appropriate for nominal data? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The mode. Categories without order can only be described by the most frequent one.

  3. The range of 3, 7, 8, 12, 20 is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    17. Range = highest − lowest = 20 − 3 = 17; it uses only the two extreme values.

  4. A researcher records which of four therapy types each client received. Which average is appropriate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The mode. Therapy type is nominal, so only the most frequent category (mode) makes sense.

  5. Which level of measurement is data on whether people did or didn't help? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Nominal. Categories with no order are nominal, which suits chi-square.

  6. Household incomes in a sample include one billionaire. Which average best describes a typical household? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The median. The median isn't pulled up by the extreme value.

  7. Why is the level of measurement relevant to choosing between a t-test and a Mann–Whitney test? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Parametric tests assume at least interval data. Ordinal data suit non-parametric tests; interval or ratio data allow parametric tests if other assumptions hold.

  8. In a distribution where the mean is much higher than the median, the data are most likely… (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Positively skewed. A long right tail pulls the mean above the median.

  9. Why divide by n − 1 when calculating sample variance? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A sample tends to underestimate population spread. n − 1 corrects the bias of using the sample mean.

Key takeaways

  • Nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio data carry increasing information, and the level determines which averages and tests are appropriate.
  • The mean uses all the data but is pulled by outliers; the median resists outliers; the mode suits nominal data.
  • The standard deviation is the typical distance of scores from the mean; the IQR is a robust alternative for skewed data.
  • The normal distribution is symmetrical with known proportions within each SD; skew pulls the mean towards the tail; z-scores standardise values.
  • Choose graphs that match the data and show distributions, not just means; always check scales and axes.
  • Probabilities combine by simple rules; conditional probabilities depend heavily on base rates, which intuition tends to ignore.

Common mistakes in Statistics

  • Calculating a mean for nominal data, such as an "average diagnosis". Categories without order can only be counted.
  • Reporting only the mean for heavily skewed data. Always look at the distribution first.
  • Reporting a mean without a measure of spread. Without the SD or IQR, readers can't judge how typical the mean is.
  • Assuming all psychological data are normal. Reaction times, counts and many clinical measures are skewed.
  • Starting a bar chart's y-axis well above zero. It makes small differences look large.
  • Confusing p(positive test | condition) with p(condition | positive test). They can differ enormously when the condition is rare.

Lessons in this school

  1. Levels of measurement
    Data come at different levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio.
  2. Measures of central tendency
    Measures of central tendency summarise the typical value of a data set: the mean, median and mode.
  3. Measures of dispersion: range, variance and standard deviation
    Measures of dispersion describe how spread out the data are.
  4. Distributions: normal, skew and kurtosis
    The shape of a distribution matters for which statistics are appropriate.
  5. Data visualisation
    Good graphs show the shape of the data, not just the averages.
  6. Probability
    Probability is the language of uncertainty that underlies statistical inference.
  7. Sampling distributions and standard error
    If you repeated a study many times, the sample means would vary.
  8. Confidence intervals
    A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for a population parameter, showing the precision of an estimate.
  9. Null and alternative hypotheses
    Null hypothesis significance testing compares data against a null hypothesis of no effect.
  10. p-values and statistical significance
    The p-value is the most reported and most misunderstood number in psychology.
  11. Effect sizes
    An effect size measures how big an effect is, independently of sample size.
  12. Statistical power
    Statistical power is the probability that a study detects an effect if it exists.
  13. Type I and Type II errors
    Every significance test risks two kinds of error: a false positive (Type I) or a false negative (Type II).
  14. Correlation: Pearson and Spearman
    Correlation coefficients measure the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables.
  15. Simple linear regression
    Simple linear regression fits a straight line that predicts one variable from another.
  16. Multiple regression
    Multiple regression predicts an outcome from several predictors at once, estimating each predictor's contribution while holding the others constant.
  17. Independent t-tests
    The independent t-test compares the means of two separate groups.
  18. Paired t-tests
    The paired (related) t-test compares two means from the same participants, or matched pairs, by analysing the differences within each pair.
  19. Analysis of variance (ANOVA)
    ANOVA compares the means of three or more groups at once, avoiding the inflated error rate of many t-tests.
  20. Chi-square tests
    Chi-square tests analyse frequencies in categories: whether observed counts differ from those expected, or whether two categorical variables are associated.
  21. Non-parametric tests
    Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about distributions, working with ranks instead of raw scores.
  22. Meta-analysis
    Meta-analysis statistically combines the results of many studies to estimate an overall effect and explain variation between studies.
  23. Reproducible analysis
    A reproducible analysis can be rerun by someone else, from the raw data to the final numbers, and give the same result.
  24. Statistical software
    Psychologists analyse data with software ranging from point-and-click programs to programming languages.
  25. Interpreting and reporting results
    Results must be reported fully and interpreted carefully: what was found, how large and how certain, and what it does and doesn't show.

Statistics: questions students ask

How is the median found for an even number of values?

Order the values and take the mean of the two middle values.

What does the standard deviation tell you?

The typical distance of scores from the mean.

What is the formula for a z-score?

z = (score − mean) ÷ standard deviation.

What does a box plot show?

The median, interquartile range, range and any outliers.
