Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define psychology.

The scientific study of mind and behaviour. From “What is psychology?” →

In plain English: Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour. Key idea: Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour: it infers unobservable mental processes from careful measurement of what people and animals do.

What are the four aims of psychological research?

Description, explanation, prediction and control (or change). From “The aims of psychological research” →

In plain English: Psychological research has four aims: to describe, explain, predict and control or change behaviour. Key idea: The aims build on each other: good description makes explanation possible; explanations allow prediction; reliable prediction makes effective, evidence-based change possible.

Name the three parts of the personality in Freud's theory.

The id, the ego and the superego. From “The psychodynamic approach” →

In plain English: The psychodynamic approach, founded by Sigmund Freud, explains behaviour through unconscious mental processes shaped by early childhood. Key idea: The psychodynamic approach explains behaviour through unconscious conflicts rooted in early experience; its broad ideas have been influential even though many of Freud's specific claims lack support.

What is conformity?

Changing behaviour or beliefs to match those of a group. From “The social approach” →

In plain English: Social psychology studies how people's thoughts, feelings and behaviour are shaped by other people, real or imagined. Key idea: Social psychology shows that behaviour depends heavily on the social situation, and that people underestimate how much.

Give an example of a holistic approach in psychology.

Gestalt psychology (perceiving wholes) or humanistic psychology (the whole person). From “Reductionism and holism” →

In plain English: Reductionism explains complex behaviour by breaking it into simpler parts; holism argues some properties only make sense at the level of the whole. Key idea: Reductionism makes explanations testable and precise but can miss emergent and contextual causes; holism captures complexity but can be vague and hard to test.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the best definition of psychology? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The scientific study of mind and behaviour. Psychology covers all mental processes and behaviour, studied scientifically; therapy, anatomy and dreams are narrower topics within or beside it.

  2. Which event is usually taken as the start of psychology as a separate experimental science? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Wundt's Leipzig laboratory, 1879. The Leipzig laboratory is the conventional marker: the first institution devoted to experimental psychology. The others are later turning points.

  3. Which feature of science means that other researchers can repeat a study and check its findings? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Replicability. Replicability depends on a clearly described procedure; it lets findings be checked and errors corrected.

  4. A newspaper claims that "everyone knows" stress causes stomach ulcers. How would a psychologist respond? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Treat it as a hypothesis and look at controlled evidence. Psychology treats widely held beliefs as claims to test. (In fact most ulcers are caused by Helicobacter pylori infection, though stress may play a role: a good example of why testing matters.)

  5. Cognitive psychologists in the 1960s studied memory using reaction times and recall tests. Why was this acceptable to scientists who had rejected introspection? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It inferred mental processes from objective, repeatable measurements. The key change was method: mental processes became theoretical constructs tested through observable, repeatable data, rather than private reports.

  6. Which of these claims is falsifiable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Students who sleep under six hours will score lower on a memory test than those who sleep eight. Only the first predicts a specific, measurable result that could fail to appear.

  7. Which is the strongest reply to the claim that psychology is "just common sense"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Common sense contains contradictory sayings and is often wrong when tested, as myths show. The key point is that intuition must be tested; it is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, and only evidence tells us which. Jargon or credentials don't make a claim true.

  8. Which is the best criticism of the "great founders" account of psychology's history? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It overlooks contributors excluded from academic posts and the gradual spread of ideas. Historians argue such accounts simplify a gradual, international process and neglect people (often women and minorities) who were denied recognition, such as Calkins.

  9. Which is the best evaluation of psychology's scientific status? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Much of it meets scientific criteria, though human subject matter makes control and measurement harder. A balanced view recognises scientific methods are used widely, while human reactivity, measurement problems and culture make psychology harder; replication work is part of how science corrects itself.

Key takeaways

  • Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour: it infers unobservable mental processes from careful measurement of what people and animals do.
  • Each major approach arose partly as a reaction to the limits of the one before: behaviourism rejected unreliable introspection; cognitive psychology rejected behaviourism's refusal to study the mind.
  • A scientific claim makes a risky prediction: it says what should not happen. Theories that can explain any result are not strengthened by evidence, because no evidence could count against them.
  • Psychology overlaps with its neighbours; what distinguishes it is a focus on individual mind and behaviour, studied with mostly empirical methods.
  • The aims build on each other: good description makes explanation possible; explanations allow prediction; reliable prediction makes effective, evidence-based change possible.
  • Structuralism asked "what is the mind made of?"; functionalism asked "what does the mind do, and why?". Functionalism's broader, applied focus proved more lasting.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Psychology

  • Thinking psychology is mainly therapy. Clinical work is one part; most psychologists research or apply knowledge about perception, learning, development, social behaviour and more.
  • Treating dates such as 1879 as the moment psychology "began". They are useful markers, but ideas developed gradually and in many countries at once.
  • Equating "science" with laboratories and white coats. Science is defined by its method, and observational studies, surveys and case studies can all be conducted scientifically.
  • Assuming one discipline's explanation must be the "real" one. Explanations at different levels can all be true and can complement each other.
  • Assuming a good prediction means we understand the cause. A variable can predict an outcome without causing it (for example, if both depend on a third factor).
  • Confusing functionalism in psychology with functionalism in sociology (Parsons, Merton), which asks how institutions serve society. They share the idea of function but are separate theories.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is psychology?
    Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour.
  2. The history of psychology
    Psychology grew out of philosophy and physiology in the 19th century.
  3. Psychology as a science
    What makes psychology a science is its method: testable hypotheses, systematic observation and openness to being wrong.
  4. Psychology and its neighbouring disciplines
    Psychology sits between the biological and the social sciences.
  5. The aims of psychological research
    Psychological research has four aims: to describe, explain, predict and control or change behaviour.
  6. Structuralism and functionalism
    The first two schools of psychology disagreed about what to study: structuralism asked what consciousness is made of, functionalism what it is for.
  7. Behaviourism
    Behaviourism held that psychology should study only observable behaviour and how it is learned through the environment.
  8. The cognitive approach
    The cognitive approach studies the mental processes between stimulus and response: attention, perception, memory, language and thinking.
  9. The psychodynamic approach
    The psychodynamic approach, founded by Sigmund Freud, explains behaviour through unconscious mental processes shaped by early childhood.
  10. The humanistic approach
    Humanistic psychology emphasises free will, personal growth and the person's own experience.
  11. The biological approach
    The biological approach explains behaviour and experience through the brain, nervous system, hormones and genes.
  12. The evolutionary approach
    The evolutionary approach asks how mental abilities and behaviours may have been shaped by natural and sexual selection.
  13. The social approach
    Social psychology studies how people's thoughts, feelings and behaviour are shaped by other people, real or imagined.
  14. Positive psychology
    Positive psychology studies what makes life go well: wellbeing, strengths, meaning and flourishing.
  15. Contemporary psychology
    Today's psychology is less a set of rival schools than a network of specialisms that share methods and borrow from one another.
  16. Levels of explanation
    The same behaviour can be explained at different levels: molecules, brain systems, mental processes, individuals and societies.
  17. Reductionism and holism
    Reductionism explains complex behaviour by breaking it into simpler parts; holism argues some properties only make sense at the level of the whole.
  18. The nature–nurture debate
    The nature–nurture debate asks how far behaviour is shaped by inherited biology or by experience.
  19. Individual differences
    Individual differences research asks how and why people differ from one another in personality, intelligence, motivation and more.
  20. Psychology in everyday life
    Psychological research shapes daily life in ways people rarely notice, from road signs and school timetables to health campaigns and online design.
  21. Careers in psychology
    Psychology leads to careers in health, education, business, law, research and beyond.

Foundations of Psychology: questions students ask

How do psychologists study the mind if it can't be observed directly?

By measuring behaviour and physiology (responses, reaction times, brain activity) and inferring the mental processes behind them.

Who opened the first laboratory for experimental psychology, and when?

Wilhelm Wundt, in Leipzig, in 1879.

What does it mean for a hypothesis to be falsifiable?

It could be shown to be false: there is some possible observation that would contradict it.

What is the main difference in focus between psychology and sociology?

Psychology focuses on individual mind and behaviour; sociology on social structures, institutions and groups.
