Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the two main divisions of the nervous system?

The central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system. From “Organisation of the nervous system” →

In plain English: The nervous system is the body's network for sending, receiving and processing signals. Key idea: The CNS (brain and spinal cord) processes information; the PNS (somatic and autonomic) links it to the body.

What does the sodium–potassium pump do?

Uses energy to move 3 sodium ions out and 2 potassium ions in. From “The resting potential” →

In plain English: A neurone at rest holds an electrical charge across its membrane. Key idea: The resting potential (about −70 mV) comes from uneven ion distribution and potassium leak, maintained by the sodium–potassium pump.

What are the four lobes of the cortex?

Frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital. From “The cerebral cortex and the four lobes” →

In plain English: The cerebral cortex is divided into four lobes, each linked to broad functions. Key idea: The frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes have broad specialisations; motor and sensory cortex map the body contralaterally.

Name two factors affecting recovery after brain injury.

Age, extent of damage, rehabilitation and cognitive reserve (any two). From “Neuroplasticity” →

In plain English: Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function through experience and after injury. Key idea: The brain changes with experience through synaptic strengthening and reorganisation, which supports learning and recovery.

What is DNA methylation?

Adding methyl groups to DNA, usually reducing gene expression. From “Epigenetics” →

In plain English: Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence. Key idea: Epigenetics regulates gene expression without changing DNA; experience can alter it, but human transgenerational claims are uncertain.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Biological Psychology and Neuroscience

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. The spinal cord is part of the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Central nervous system. The spinal cord and brain together make up the central nervous system.

  2. Roughly how many neurones are in the human brain? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 86 billion. Azevedo et al. counted roughly 86 billion.

  3. Which division of the PNS controls skeletal muscles? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Somatic. Voluntary skeletal movement is controlled by the somatic system.

  4. You pull your hand away from a pin before you feel pain. Why? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A spinal reflex arc acts first. The reflex passes through the spinal cord before the brain registers pain.

  5. A new drug works in the body but has no effect on the brain. What is the likely reason? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It can't cross the blood–brain barrier. The barrier blocks many substances from entering brain tissue.

  6. Your pupils widen in dim light without you deciding. Which system is responsible? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Autonomic nervous system. Pupil size is an involuntary autonomic response.

  7. Why is damage to the medulla often life-threatening? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It controls breathing and heart rate. Vital autonomic functions depend on the medulla.

  8. Why is the fight-or-flight response sometimes called maladaptive today? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Modern stressors are rarely physical. Repeated arousal to psychological stressors may harm health without helping.

  9. Why are myelinated axons faster than unmyelinated ones? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The signal jumps between nodes. Saltatory conduction skips the insulated sections.

Key takeaways

  • The CNS (brain and spinal cord) processes information; the PNS (somatic and autonomic) links it to the body.
  • The CNS integrates information and controls behaviour; it is protected by bone, meninges, fluid and the blood–brain barrier.
  • The PNS has a voluntary somatic division serving skeletal muscle and an involuntary autonomic division regulating internal organs.
  • The sympathetic branch prepares the body for action via adrenaline; the parasympathetic restores rest and digestion.
  • Neurones receive signals at dendrites and send them along axons to terminals; myelin speeds transmission.
  • The resting potential (about −70 mV) comes from uneven ion distribution and potassium leak, maintained by the sodium–potassium pump.

Common mistakes in Biological Psychology and Neuroscience

  • Thinking every response needs the brain. Spinal reflexes act before conscious awareness.
  • Thinking the brain is mostly neurones. Glial cells are about as numerous and do vital work.
  • Assuming "involuntary" means impossible to influence. Breathing and, through biofeedback, some autonomic responses can be partly controlled.
  • Thinking the two branches switch on and off like a light. Both are active continuously; their balance shifts.
  • Thinking signals travel both ways along a neurone. Signals normally pass from dendrites to cell body to axon terminals.
  • Thinking the resting neurone is inactive. It spends energy constantly to stay ready.

Lessons in this school

  1. Organisation of the nervous system
    The nervous system is the body's network for sending, receiving and processing signals.
  2. The central nervous system
    The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord.
  3. The peripheral nervous system: somatic and autonomic
    The peripheral nervous system links the central nervous system with the rest of the body.
  4. Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions
    The autonomic nervous system has two branches that usually work in opposition: the sympathetic prepares the body for action and the parasympathetic restores calm.
  5. Neurone structure and types
    Neurones are the cells that carry information in the nervous system.
  6. The resting potential
    A neurone at rest holds an electrical charge across its membrane.
  7. Action potentials
    An action potential is the brief electrical impulse that travels along an axon.
  8. Synaptic transmission
    Synaptic transmission is how one neurone passes a signal to another, usually by releasing a chemical across a tiny gap.
  9. Neurotransmitters and receptors
    Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers of the nervous system.
  10. Excitatory and inhibitory signalling
    Every neurone receives a mix of excitatory and inhibitory input and fires only when the balance tips past threshold.
  11. Brain anatomy: an overview
    The brain is organised into regions with broadly different roles, working together as networks.
  12. The cerebral cortex and the four lobes
    The cerebral cortex is divided into four lobes, each linked to broad functions.
  13. The limbic system: hippocampus and amygdala
    The limbic system is a set of structures involved in emotion, motivation and memory.
  14. Hypothalamus and thalamus
    The hypothalamus and thalamus are small structures in the middle of the brain with large roles.
  15. Basal ganglia and cerebellum
    The basal ganglia and cerebellum both help control movement, in different ways.
  16. Lateralisation of function
    Some functions are handled more by one cerebral hemisphere than the other.
  17. Neuroplasticity
    Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function through experience and after injury.
  18. Neurogenesis
    Neurogenesis is the creation of new neurones.
  19. Hormones and the endocrine system
    The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood to send slower, longer-lasting signals than the nervous system.
  20. Circadian rhythms
    Circadian rhythms are cycles of about 24 hours in body and behaviour, such as the sleep–wake cycle.
  21. Sleep
    Sleep is a regular state of reduced awareness with distinct stages.
  22. Genetics and behaviour
    Genes influence behaviour, but always in interaction with the environment.
  23. Epigenetics
    Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence.
  24. Evolution and the brain
    Evolutionary psychology explains brain and behaviour in terms of natural selection.
  25. Brain imaging: EEG, MRI, fMRI and PET
    Brain imaging lets researchers study the living brain's structure and activity.
  26. Lesion studies
    Lesion studies examine what happens when part of the brain is damaged or temporarily disrupted.
  27. Psychopharmacology
    Psychopharmacology studies how drugs affect mind and behaviour.
  28. Biological explanations of mental disorders
    Biological explanations link mental disorders to genes, brain structure and neurochemistry.

Biological Psychology and Neuroscience: questions students ask

What protects the CNS?

Bone, the meninges, cerebrospinal fluid and the blood–brain barrier.

How many pairs of cranial and spinal nerves are there?

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves and thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves.

What does the myelin sheath do?

Insulates the axon and speeds signal conduction.

What is the all-or-none law?

If threshold is reached, the neurone fires at full strength; otherwise it doesn't fire.
