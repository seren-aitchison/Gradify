Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is reverse inference?

Inferring a mental state from observed brain activity. From “Cognitive neuroscience today” →

In plain English: Cognitive neuroscience links mental processes to brain activity. Key idea: Cognitive neuroscience uses imaging, stimulation, lesions and models; research has moved from localisation to networks; small samples undermine reliability; reverse inference needs caution.

What is iCBT?

Cognitive behavioural therapy delivered over the internet. From “Digital mental health” →

In plain English: Digital mental health uses apps, online therapy and other technologies to support mental health. Key idea: Digital mental health includes iCBT, apps, peer support and chatbots; trials show small to moderate benefits, stronger with guidance; engagement, quality and privacy are concerns.

What happens in the Ultimatum Game?

One player proposes a split; if the other rejects it, both get nothing. From “Neuroeconomics” →

In plain English: Neuroeconomics combines neuroscience, psychology and economics to study how the brain makes decisions. Key idea: Neuroeconomics studies how the brain computes value; regions such as the vmPFC and ventral striatum represent subjective value; the Ultimatum Game shows fairness concerns linked to the insula.

What did Wagenmakers et al. (2016) find?

A large registered replication found no effect of the pen-in-mouth facial feedback manipulation. From “Embodied cognition” →

In plain English: Embodied cognition proposes that thinking depends on the body and its interaction with the environment, not just on abstract processing in the brain. Key idea: Embodied cognition grounds thinking in bodily experience (Barsalou); neuroimaging supports sensorimotor involvement; some behavioural effects, such as the pen facial feedback study, failed to replicate.

What did Polderman et al. (2015) find?

An average heritability of about 49% across 17,804 traits. From “Behavioural genetics” →

In plain English: Behavioural genetics studies how genetic and environmental differences contribute to differences in behaviour. Key idea: Heritability is population-level variation linked to genes; twin studies suggest average heritability around 49%; polygenic scores predict less than twin studies; heritability varies with environment.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Contemporary Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. The probability that a study detects a real effect is its… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Statistical power. Statistical power is the chance of detecting a real effect and depends heavily on sample size.

  2. Describing decisions as evidence accumulating until a threshold is reached is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Drift diffusion model. Drift diffusion models describe evidence accumulation.

  3. Consistency of a measure over time is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Test–retest reliability. Reliability over time is test–retest reliability.

  4. A news story claims "scientists found the love centre" because one region lit up when people saw partners. This relies on… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reverse inference. Inferring love from activity is reverse inference.

  5. A model with many parameters fits one dataset perfectly but predicts new data poorly. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Overfitting. Fitting noise reduces predictive accuracy.

  6. Researchers model how quickly people update beliefs after rewards and find different patterns among participants with the same diagnosis. This aims at… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    More precise subtyping. Differences in model parameters may reveal subgroups within one diagnosis.

  7. Why has research shifted from brain "centres" to networks? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Functions depend on distributed regions working together. Distributed activity better explains cognition.

  8. Why isn't a good model fit proof that a model is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Other models can fit the same data equally well. Model comparison and new predictions are needed.

  9. Why do critics warn about reducing distress to model parameters? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Distress is complex and socially shaped. Social and personal context matter beyond models.

Key takeaways

  • Cognitive neuroscience uses imaging, stimulation, lesions and models; research has moved from localisation to networks; small samples undermine reliability; reverse inference needs caution.
  • Computational models make theories precise and testable; Marr's three levels guide explanation; reinforcement learning and drift diffusion models explain learning and choice; good fit isn't proof.
  • Computational psychiatry uses models of learning and inference to study mental health; it aims for mechanisms, subtypes and treatment prediction; reliability and clinical use remain challenges.
  • Psychology and AI share roots in cognitive science and connectionism; psychologists test LLMs with cognitive tasks; AI performance differs from human cognition in important ways; anthropomorphism shapes how people treat AI.
  • Digital mental health includes iCBT, apps, peer support and chatbots; trials show small to moderate benefits, stronger with guidance; engagement, quality and privacy are concerns.
  • Social media involve self-presentation, comparison and support; Orben and Przybylski found very small average associations with well-being; correlational designs and self-report limit conclusions.

Common mistakes in Contemporary Psychology

  • Treating brain activity as direct proof of a mental state. Most regions serve many functions.
  • Assuming a model that fits data must be correct. Other models may fit equally well.
  • Assuming computational models can already diagnose or choose treatments. Clinical use is not yet established.
  • Assuming good performance on a task means an AI understands it as humans do. Mechanisms may differ.
  • Assuming any mental health app is evidence-based. Many have not been tested.
  • Assuming small average effects mean no one is harmed. Effects may vary across individuals and uses.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cognitive neuroscience today
    Cognitive neuroscience links mental processes to brain activity.
  2. Computational psychology
    Computational psychology uses mathematical and computer models to explain how the mind works.
  3. Computational psychiatry
    Computational psychiatry uses models of brain and behaviour to understand mental health conditions.
  4. Artificial intelligence and psychology
    Psychology and artificial intelligence (AI) influence each other: psychology inspired AI, and AI is now a tool and a subject for psychological research.
  5. Digital mental health
    Digital mental health uses apps, online therapy and other technologies to support mental health.
  6. Social media and psychology
    Social media are part of daily life for most young people.
  7. The psychology of technology
    Technology changes how we remember, pay attention and relate to others.
  8. Human–computer interaction today
    Human–computer interaction (HCI) applies psychology to the design of technology.
  9. Neuroeconomics
    Neuroeconomics combines neuroscience, psychology and economics to study how the brain makes decisions.
  10. Behavioural economics
    Behavioural economics uses psychology to explain why people's economic decisions depart from traditional models of rational choice.
  11. Decision science
    Decision science studies how people and organisations make decisions and how decisions can be improved.
  12. Predictive processing
    Predictive processing proposes that the brain constantly predicts incoming sensory information and updates its predictions when errors occur.
  13. Embodied cognition
    Embodied cognition proposes that thinking depends on the body and its interaction with the environment, not just on abstract processing in the brain.
  14. Bayesian cognition
    Bayesian models describe the mind as combining prior knowledge with new evidence according to probability theory.
  15. Network approaches to mental health
    Network approaches see mental health conditions as systems of symptoms that influence each other, rather than as hidden underlying diseases.
  16. Psychoneuroimmunology
    Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) studies how psychological processes, the nervous system and the immune system interact.
  17. Behavioural genetics
    Behavioural genetics studies how genetic and environmental differences contribute to differences in behaviour.
  18. Epigenetics and behaviour
    Epigenetics studies changes in how genes are expressed without changes to the DNA sequence.
  19. Open science and the replication crisis
    In the 2010s, psychology faced a replication crisis when many well-known findings failed to replicate.
  20. Current debates in psychology
    Psychology is a lively, contested science.
  21. Future directions
    Psychology is changing through large-scale collaboration, new technology and broader participation.
