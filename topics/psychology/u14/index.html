Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What do educational psychologists do?

Assess learning needs, advise schools and families, contribute to EHC assessments and train staff. From “Educational psychology” →

In plain English: Educational psychology applies psychology to learning and teaching. Key idea: Educational psychology applies learning theories to teaching; practitioners assess and support learners; evidence-informed practice must consider context.

What did Melby-Lervåg and Hulme find about working memory training?

It showed little transfer to other skills such as classroom learning. From “Individual differences in learning” →

In plain English: Pupils differ in prior knowledge, working memory, attention, language and motivation. Key idea: Pupils differ in prior knowledge, working memory and other factors; teaching should adapt while keeping high expectations; brain training shows little transfer.

What are Schein's three levels of culture?

Artefacts, espoused values and basic assumptions. From “Organisational behaviour” →

In plain English: Organisational behaviour studies how people act in organisations: in teams, cultures and during change. Key idea: Effective teams need clear goals, roles and psychological safety; culture operates at levels from artefacts to assumptions; change succeeds with involvement and communication.

What is mental workload?

The demand a task places on a person's limited mental resources. From “Mental workload” →

In plain English: Mental workload is the demand a task places on a person's limited mental resources. Key idea: Mental workload reflects demands relative to resources; it can be measured subjectively, by performance or physiologically; both overload and underload impair safety.

What is the prevention paradox?

Small changes across a population prevent more illness than targeting only high-risk individuals. From “Psychology and public health” →

In plain English: Psychology contributes to public health by understanding behaviour at population level. Key idea: Psychology informs population health through nudges, campaigns and policy; structural measures such as the sugar levy can change environments; interventions must consider inequalities.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Applied Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which body regulates practitioner psychologists, including educational psychologists, in the UK? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Health and Care Professions Council. The HCPC registers practitioner psychologists.

  2. Showing pupils how algebra is used in real jobs raises… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Value (usefulness). Showing real-world usefulness raises the value pupils place on a task.

  3. Giving pupils points for following rules that can be exchanged for rewards is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Token economy. Points exchanged for rewards are tokens that reinforce desired behaviour.

  4. A teacher breaks a complex task into smaller steps with worked examples. This applies… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cognitive load theory. Reducing working memory load supports learning.

  5. A pupil says "I failed because I'm just no good at maths". This is an attribution to… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stable, uncontrollable ability. Ability attributions are stable and uncontrollable.

  6. After a fight, both pupils meet with a teacher to discuss the harm and agree how to make amends. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Restorative practice. Bringing those involved together to repair harm is restorative practice.

  7. Why does the EEF stress implementation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Context changes the effect. Effective use depends on how and where it's applied.

  8. Why can't classroom strategies alone solve low motivation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Wider factors also matter. Poverty, peer culture and assessment pressure also shape motivation beyond the classroom.

  9. Why are zero-tolerance policies debated? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They may increase exclusions for some groups. Strictness may disproportionately affect pupils with SEN.

Key takeaways

  • Educational psychology applies learning theories to teaching; practitioners assess and support learners; evidence-informed practice must consider context.
  • Expectancy, value, autonomy, competence, belonging and attributions shape pupils' motivation; classroom strategies can help but wider factors matter.
  • Behaviour can be shaped by clear routines and reinforcement and supported by relationships and restorative approaches; evidence favours combining structure with support.
  • Formative assessment supports learning; summative assessment certifies it; reliability and validity matter; high-stakes testing has benefits and costs.
  • Pupils differ in prior knowledge, working memory and other factors; teaching should adapt while keeping high expectations; brain training shows little transfer.
  • England's SEND system includes SEN support and EHC plans (538,547 EHC plans in January 2026); the social model highlights barriers; inclusion is valued but debated.

Common mistakes in Applied Psychology

  • Assuming every popular teaching idea is evidence-based. Learning styles, for example, lack supporting evidence.
  • Assuming lack of motivation is a fixed trait. Motivation responds to classroom conditions and beliefs.
  • Assuming one behaviour strategy suits all pupils. Needs and context differ.
  • Assuming exam grades are perfectly precise. Marking reliability varies by subject and question type.
  • Assuming differentiation means giving some pupils easier tasks. Adaptive teaching keeps high expectations with tailored support.
  • Treating disability as only an individual impairment. The social model shows barriers in schools and society also disable.

Lessons in this school

  1. Educational psychology
    Educational psychology applies psychology to learning and teaching.
  2. Achievement and motivation in education
    Pupils' motivation strongly influences their learning.
  3. Behaviour in schools
    Classroom behaviour affects learning and wellbeing.
  4. Assessment in education
    Assessment measures and supports learning.
  5. Individual differences in learning
    Pupils differ in prior knowledge, working memory, attention, language and motivation.
  6. Inclusion and special educational needs
    Inclusion aims to ensure all pupils, including those with special educational needs and disabilities, can learn and participate.
  7. Occupational psychology
    Occupational psychology applies psychology to work and organisations.
  8. Work motivation
    Work motivation is what drives people to put effort into their jobs.
  9. Leadership at work
    Leadership strongly shapes employees' motivation, wellbeing and performance.
  10. Organisational behaviour
    Organisational behaviour studies how people act in organisations: in teams, cultures and during change.
  11. Job satisfaction
    Job satisfaction is how positively people feel about their jobs.
  12. Wellbeing at work
    Work can support or harm wellbeing.
  13. Burnout
    Burnout is a response to chronic workplace stress characterised by exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy.
  14. Human factors and ergonomics
    Human factors and ergonomics design systems, tools and environments to fit human abilities and limits.
  15. Mental workload
    Mental workload is the demand a task places on a person's limited mental resources.
  16. Human–computer interaction
    Human–computer interaction (HCI) studies how people use digital technology and how to design it to be usable, accessible and safe.
  17. Consumer and marketing psychology
    Consumer psychology studies how people choose, buy and use products.
  18. Health psychology
    Health psychology studies how psychological and social factors affect health, illness and healthcare.
  19. Behaviour change
    Behaviour change interventions apply psychology to help people change habits for health, environment or wellbeing.
  20. Psychology and public health
    Psychology contributes to public health by understanding behaviour at population level.
  21. Environmental psychology
    Environmental psychology studies how people and their physical environments affect each other.
  22. Forensic psychology
    Forensic psychology applies psychology to crime and the criminal justice system.
  23. Psychology and the law
    Psychology contributes to the legal system by studying eyewitnesses, interviews, juries and false confessions.
  24. Sport and performance psychology
    Sport and performance psychology applies psychology to improve performance and wellbeing in sport, music and other high-pressure activities.

Applied Psychology: questions students ask

What does expectancy-value theory predict about pupils?

They engage with subjects they expect to succeed in and value.

What is restorative practice?

Bringing together those involved in an incident to repair harm.

What is the difference between formative and summative assessment?

Formative guides learning during teaching; summative measures learning at the end.

What is the expertise reversal effect?

Instruction that helps novices, such as worked examples, becoming less effective for experts.
