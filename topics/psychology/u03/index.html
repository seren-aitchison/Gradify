Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the main stages in information-processing models?

Input, encoding, storage, processing and output. From “The information-processing approach” →

In plain English: The information-processing approach describes the mind as a system that takes in, transforms, stores and retrieves information in stages. Key idea: The information-processing approach models cognition as staged processing of information, inferred from reaction times and errors.

Where is sound transduced?

In the cochlea, by hair cells on the basilar membrane. From “Auditory perception” →

In plain English: Auditory perception turns pressure waves into the experience of pitch, loudness, location and speech. Key idea: Hearing transduces vibrations in the cochlea; pitch depends on place and timing, location on differences between the ears, and speech perception integrates vision.

What did HM's mirror drawing show?

He learned a new skill without remembering practising, showing procedural memory was intact. From “Long-term memory: episodic, semantic and procedural” →

In plain English: Long-term memory isn't one store but several. Key idea: Long-term memory includes explicit episodic and semantic memory and implicit procedural memory, supported by amnesia and brain-imaging evidence.

What is overregularisation?

Applying a grammar rule too widely, such as saying "goed" or "mouses". From “Language acquisition” →

In plain English: Children learn their first language rapidly and in a predictable sequence. Key idea: Children acquire language in predictable stages; nativist, learning and interactionist theories each explain part of the evidence.

What is the conjunction fallacy?

Judging a combination of events more likely than one of them alone. From “Biases and heuristics” →

In plain English: Heuristics are mental shortcuts that usually work but can produce systematic errors called biases. Key idea: Heuristics are useful shortcuts that can produce predictable biases; whether they are flaws or adaptive depends on context.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cognitive Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which measure is most used to infer the duration of mental processes? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reaction time. Differences in reaction time indicate extra processing.

  2. Who proposed the early-selection filter model of attention? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Donald Broadbent. Broadbent's 1958 model filtered information by physical features.

  3. Which theory says tasks interfere most when they use the same type of resource? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Multiple-resource theory. Wickens proposed separate pools for different modalities and responses.

  4. Responding takes 50 ms longer when participants must also decide which hand to use. What does the subtractive method suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The decision stage takes about 50 ms. The extra time is attributed to the added stage.

  5. What does the information-processing approach compare the mind to? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A computer that takes in, processes and outputs information. The approach models cognition as stages of processing.

  6. While shadowing one ear, a participant hears their name in the other ear. Which theory explains this best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Attenuation theory. Attenuation lets important words break through.

  7. Which is the strongest criticism of the computer analogy in information processing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It neglects emotion, context and the body. Embodied and emotional factors shape cognition in ways the analogy misses.

  8. How does load theory reconcile early and late selection? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Selection depends on how demanding the main task is. High load produces early selection; low load allows late processing.

  9. Why do expert typists find it easier to talk while typing than beginners do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Practice has made typing largely automatic. Automatic processes need fewer attentional resources.

Key takeaways

  • The information-processing approach models cognition as staged processing of information, inferred from reaction times and errors.
  • Selective attention filters information; theories differ on how early and how completely unattended information is filtered, and load theory reconciles them.
  • Divided attention is limited by capacity and shared resources; similar, difficult and unpractised tasks interfere most.
  • Sustained attention declines over time on monotonous tasks with rare signals, with important consequences for safety-critical work.
  • Sensation detects energy; perception interprets it, combining bottom-up information with top-down knowledge and expectation.
  • Vision builds a stable 3D world from retinal images using depth cues and constancies, processed in partly separate streams for recognition and action.

Common mistakes in Cognitive Psychology

  • Thinking box-and-arrow models describe where things happen in the brain. They describe functions, not anatomy.
  • Assuming unattended information is never processed. Breakthrough of personally relevant words shows some processing occurs.
  • Believing people can truly do two demanding tasks at once. Most "multitasking" is rapid switching with costs.
  • Assuming vigilance tasks are easy because nothing much happens. They are effortful and stressful.
  • Assuming perception is a passive recording of the world. It actively constructs an interpretation.
  • Assuming we see with our eyes alone. The eyes transduce light; seeing depends on the brain's interpretation.

Lessons in this school

  1. The information-processing approach
    The information-processing approach describes the mind as a system that takes in, transforms, stores and retrieves information in stages.
  2. Selective attention
    Selective attention is the ability to focus on some information while ignoring the rest.
  3. Divided attention
    Divided attention is attending to more than one task at once.
  4. Sustained attention
    Sustained attention (vigilance) is maintaining focus over long periods, especially when watching for rare signals.
  5. Sensation and perception
    Sensation is detecting physical energy with sense organs; perception is organising and interpreting it into meaningful experience.
  6. Visual perception
    Vision turns patterns of light on the retina into a stable, three-dimensional world of objects.
  7. Auditory perception
    Auditory perception turns pressure waves into the experience of pitch, loudness, location and speech.
  8. Gestalt principles
    Gestalt psychologists showed that we perceive organised wholes, not collections of separate parts.
  9. Pattern recognition
    Pattern recognition is identifying objects, letters, words and faces from sensory input.
  10. Sensory memory
    Sensory memory holds a brief, detailed copy of what the senses have just registered.
  11. Short-term memory
    Short-term memory holds a small amount of information for a short time unless it is rehearsed.
  12. Working memory
    Working memory is the system that holds and manipulates information while we think.
  13. Long-term memory: episodic, semantic and procedural
    Long-term memory isn't one store but several.
  14. Encoding, storage and retrieval
    Remembering involves encoding information, storing it and retrieving it.
  15. Forgetting
    Forgetting is the loss of the ability to recall or recognise information.
  16. Interference
    Interference theory holds that we forget because other memories get in the way.
  17. Retrieval failure
    Retrieval failure means information is stored but can't be accessed without the right cues.
  18. Reconstructive memory and false memories
    Remembering is an active reconstruction, not a replay.
  19. Eyewitness testimony
    Eyewitness testimony is a witness's account of a crime or event.
  20. Language acquisition
    Children learn their first language rapidly and in a predictable sequence.
  21. Language processing
    Understanding language means recognising words, building sentence structures and constructing meaning, in a fraction of a second.
  22. Speech production
    Speaking involves turning an idea into words, grammar and sounds in real time.
  23. Problem solving
    Problem solving is finding a way from a current state to a goal when the route isn't obvious.
  24. Reasoning
    Reasoning is drawing conclusions from information.
  25. Decision-making
    Decision-making is choosing between options under uncertainty.
  26. Biases and heuristics
    Heuristics are mental shortcuts that usually work but can produce systematic errors called biases.
  27. Metacognition
    Metacognition is thinking about thinking: knowing what you know and controlling how you learn.
  28. Cognitive development
    Cognitive development is how thinking changes from infancy to adulthood.
  29. Theories of intelligence
    Intelligence is the general ability to reason, learn and solve problems.
  30. Intelligence testing
    Intelligence tests measure cognitive abilities and compare people with others of the same age.
  31. Creativity
    Creativity is producing ideas or products that are both novel and useful.
  32. Cognitive ageing
    Cognitive ageing is how thinking changes in later adulthood.

Cognitive Psychology: questions students ask

What is the cocktail party effect?

Noticing personally relevant information, such as your name, in an unattended conversation.

What is automaticity?

Performing a task with little attention after extensive practice.

What is the vigilance decrement?

The decline in detection performance over time on monotonous watch tasks.

What is transduction?

The conversion of physical energy into neural signals by sensory receptors.
