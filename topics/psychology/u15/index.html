Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is holistic thinking?

Attending to context, relationships and change rather than isolated objects. From “Culture and cognition” →

In plain English: Cultural psychology asks whether basic thinking processes are the same everywhere. Key idea: Holistic and analytic thinking styles differ on average between East Asian and Western samples; Masuda and Nisbett found differences in attention to context; the WEIRD critique challenges assumptions of universality.

What is the difference between an independent and an interdependent self?

Independent selves are defined by internal traits; interdependent selves by relationships and roles. From “Individualism and collectivism” →

In plain English: Individualism and collectivism describe how cultures balance personal goals and group obligations. Key idea: Hofstede identified individualism–collectivism among national culture dimensions; Markus and Kitayama distinguished independent and interdependent selves; the concepts are useful but can oversimplify.

What is culture shock?

Anxiety and disorientation when familiar cues and routines don't work in a new culture. From “Cultural adaptation” →

In plain English: People who move to a new culture must adapt psychologically and socially. Key idea: Culture shock involves disorientation in a new culture; the U-curve doesn't fit everyone; Ward distinguished psychological and sociocultural adaptation; support and skills aid adjustment.

What is interpersonal relatedness?

A Chinese personality factor including harmony and face, not well captured by the Big Five. From “Culture and personality” →

In plain English: Do personality traits have the same structure in every culture? Key idea: The Big Five structure replicates in many cultures (McCrae and Terracciano); emic research finds culture-specific factors such as interpersonal relatedness; national character stereotypes are often inaccurate.

What did the AESOP study find?

Higher incidence of psychosis among Black Caribbean and Black African groups in three English cities. From “Culture and diagnosis” →

In plain English: Psychiatric diagnosis relies on categories that may not fit all cultures equally well. Key idea: The category fallacy warns against exporting diagnoses; DSM-5 moved from culture-bound syndromes to cultural concepts of distress; ethnic differences in psychosis diagnosis in England reflect debated causes.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Focusing on objects, categories and rules rather than context is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Analytic thinking. Analytic thinking isolates objects from their context.

  2. An illusion in which lines of equal length look different because of arrow-like fins is the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Müller-Lyer illusion. The fins create the Müller-Lyer illusion.

  3. Cultural norms about when and how to show emotion are called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Display rules. Display rules govern emotional expression.

  4. Shown an aquarium scene, a participant describes the plants, rocks and how fish interact rather than the biggest fish. This fits… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Holistic attention. Attending to context and relationships is holistic.

  5. People raised in a rural area with round huts and few straight lines are less fooled by the Müller-Lyer illusion. This supports… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The carpentered world hypothesis. Less experience with right angles reduces the illusion.

  6. A student hides her disappointment in front of her teacher but shows it with friends. This illustrates… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Display rules. Showing or hiding emotion depending on the audience reflects cultural display rules.

  7. Why must cross-cultural comparisons of thinking style be interpreted cautiously? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They can stereotype nations and hide variation. National averages hide regional, class and generational variation.

  8. Why might cultural differences in illusions not reflect experience alone? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Test methods and biological factors may also differ. Alternative explanations include methods and biology.

  9. Why do critics question evidence for basic emotions? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Forced-choice methods may inflate agreement. Limited answer options can create apparent agreement.

Key takeaways

  • Holistic and analytic thinking styles differ on average between East Asian and Western samples; Masuda and Nisbett found differences in attention to context; the WEIRD critique challenges assumptions of universality.
  • Segall et al. found people in carpentered environments were more susceptible to the Müller-Lyer illusion; the carpentered world hypothesis explains this through experience; colour research suggests language affects perception.
  • Ekman found evidence of universal recognition of basic emotions; display rules shape expression; recognition shows an in-group advantage; cultures differ in emotional ideals.
  • Hofstede identified individualism–collectivism among national culture dimensions; Markus and Kitayama distinguished independent and interdependent selves; the concepts are useful but can oversimplify.
  • Cultural identity develops through socialisation and exploration (Phinney); bicultural people frame switch; a secure identity is linked to well-being.
  • Berry's model has four acculturation strategies: integration, assimilation, separation and marginalisation; integration is often linked to better adaptation; acculturative stress and host society attitudes shape outcomes.

Common mistakes in Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology

  • Treating cultural differences as fixed national traits. They are average tendencies that vary within cultures.
  • Assuming perception is identical everywhere. Experience can shape how cues are interpreted.
  • Assuming universal recognition means emotions are identical everywhere. Display and meaning vary.
  • Assuming everyone in a country shares one cultural orientation. Variation within countries is large.
  • Assuming people have one fixed cultural identity. Many have multiple, context-dependent identities.
  • Assuming acculturation is only a migrant's choice. The host society's attitudes constrain options.

Lessons in this school

  1. Culture and cognition
    Cultural psychology asks whether basic thinking processes are the same everywhere.
  2. Culture and perception
    Perception seems automatic, but experience can shape how we interpret what we see.
  3. Culture and emotion
    Are emotions universal, or do they vary across cultures?
  4. Individualism and collectivism
    Individualism and collectivism describe how cultures balance personal goals and group obligations.
  5. Cultural identity
    Cultural identity is a person's sense of belonging to cultural groups.
  6. Acculturation
    Acculturation is the process of cultural and psychological change when people from different cultures come into contact.
  7. Cultural adaptation
    People who move to a new culture must adapt psychologically and socially.
  8. Cross-cultural research methods
    Comparing cultures raises special methodological problems.
  9. Ethnocentrism and cultural relativism
    Ethnocentrism means judging other cultures by the standards of one's own.
  10. Culture and personality
    Do personality traits have the same structure in every culture?
  11. Culture and development
    Children develop within cultural contexts that shape childrearing, learning and milestones.
  12. Culture and mental health
    Culture shapes how distress is experienced, expressed and explained, and how people seek help.
  13. Culture and diagnosis
    Psychiatric diagnosis relies on categories that may not fit all cultures equally well.
  14. Language and thought
    Does the language we speak shape how we think?
  15. Indigenous psychologies
    Indigenous psychologies develop psychological knowledge from within particular cultures rather than importing Western theories.
  16. Globalisation and psychology
    Globalisation brings people into contact with many cultures through media, migration and technology.

Cultural and Cross-Cultural Psychology: questions students ask

What is the carpentered world hypothesis?

Growing up with many right angles teaches people to read angled lines as depth cues.

What did Ekman and Friesen find with the Fore people?

They matched facial expressions to emotional stories above chance for most basic emotions.

What are Phinney's stages of ethnic identity development?

Unexamined identity, exploration and achieved identity.

What are Berry's four acculturation strategies?

Integration, assimilation, separation and marginalisation.
