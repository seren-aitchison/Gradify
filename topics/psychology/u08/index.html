Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What did Jahoda propose as criteria for ideal mental health?

Criteria such as self-acceptance, autonomy, personal growth and accurate perception of reality. From “Defining abnormality” →

In plain English: Psychologists have proposed several ways to define abnormality, each with strengths and limits. Key idea: Abnormality can be defined by statistical rarity, norm violation, failure to function or deviation from ideal mental health; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction combines biology and values; no single definition is sufficient.

What are conditions of worth?

Approval and love that depend on meeting others' expectations. From “The humanistic model” →

In plain English: The humanistic model sees psychological difficulties as arising when people's needs for growth and self-acceptance are blocked. Key idea: Rogers linked distress to incongruence and conditions of worth; Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs; the model stresses growth and relationships but its concepts are hard to measure.

What is a safety behaviour?

An action taken to prevent a feared outcome that stops people learning it won't happen. From “Panic disorder” →

In plain English: Panic disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks and worry about further attacks. Key idea: Panic attacks are sudden surges of fear with physical symptoms; Clark's model explains panic as catastrophic misinterpretation of sensations in a vicious cycle; safety behaviours maintain it.

What is the difference between positive and negative symptoms?

Positive symptoms add to experience, such as hallucinations; negative symptoms reduce motivation, emotion or speech. From “Schizophrenia and psychosis” →

In plain English: Psychosis involves losing touch with shared reality, through hallucinations or delusions. Key idea: Psychosis involves positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions and negative symptoms; biological (dopamine, genes) and social (adversity, urbanicity, migration) factors interact; the concept of schizophrenia is debated.

What is obstructive sleep apnoea?

A condition where breathing repeatedly stops during sleep, disrupting it. From “Sleep disorders” →

In plain English: Sleep disorders affect the quality, timing or amount of sleep and have wide effects on health. Key idea: Common sleep disorders include insomnia, sleep apnoea, narcolepsy and parasomnias; worry, conditioning and habits maintain insomnia; CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Psychopathology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Defining abnormality as behaviour that is rare is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Statistical infrequency. Defining abnormality by how rare a behaviour or score is, is statistical infrequency.

  2. The ancient idea that health depends on balanced bodily fluids is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Humoral theory. Hippocrates proposed that illness came from an imbalance of the bodily humours.

  3. Explaining complex behaviour by its simplest parts is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reductionism. Reductionism breaks behaviour into basic components.

  4. A person stops washing, misses work and can't keep relationships going. Which definition best applies? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Failure to function adequately. Being unable to cope with daily life fits this definition.

  5. A 19th-century institution replaces chains with gardening, routine and kindness. This reflects… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Moral treatment. Humane care with routine, activity and respect defined moral treatment.

  6. A study finds a brain difference in people with a condition and concludes the difference caused it. This ignores… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Possible reverse causation and confounds. The condition or medication may cause the difference.

  7. Why is deviation from social norms a risky definition? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Norms vary and have been used to control people. Same-sex relationships were once wrongly labelled disorder.

  8. What does the history of psychopathology suggest about current approaches? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are shaped by culture, science and power. Every era's ideas about mental distress reflect its science, culture and power.

  9. Why might biological explanations increase stigma? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They can make difficulties seem permanent. Perceived permanence can increase social distance.

Key takeaways

  • Abnormality can be defined by statistical rarity, norm violation, failure to function or deviation from ideal mental health; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction combines biology and values; no single definition is sufficient.
  • Explanations moved from supernatural and humoral ideas to biological and psychological models; moral treatment and later deinstitutionalisation reformed care; history shows the influence of culture and power.
  • The biological model links difficulties to genes, neurochemistry and brain structure; it informed medications; simple neurotransmitter theories such as low serotonin have been challenged; correlation isn't causation.
  • Beck's cognitive triad and Ellis's ABC model link distress to thoughts and beliefs; attentional and interpretive biases maintain difficulties; cognitive therapies are effective, but causal direction is debated.
  • The behavioural model explains distress through conditioning; Mowrer's two-process model explains how avoidance maintains phobias; learned helplessness links uncontrollability to depression; preparedness and cognition limit the model.
  • The psychodynamic model links distress to unconscious conflict, defences and early experience; later theorists stressed relationships; key concepts are influential but many claims are hard to test.

Common mistakes in Psychopathology

  • Equating "rare" with "disordered". Many rare traits are positive or neutral.
  • Assuming current approaches are free of cultural influence. Every era's ideas reflect its context.
  • Assuming a brain difference proves a biological cause. Direction and confounds must be considered.
  • Assuming negative thoughts are always irrational. Some reflect genuinely difficult circumstances.
  • Assuming all fears come from a frightening experience. Many arise without one, and preparedness plays a role.
  • Treating all psychodynamic ideas as discredited. Some, such as unconscious processes and attachment, have research support.

Lessons in this school

  1. Defining abnormality
    Psychologists have proposed several ways to define abnormality, each with strengths and limits.
  2. A history of psychopathology
    Ideas about mental distress have changed greatly over time.
  3. The biological model
    The biological model explains psychological difficulties in terms of genes, brain structure and neurochemistry.
  4. The cognitive model
    The cognitive model explains psychological difficulties in terms of thoughts, beliefs and information processing.
  5. The behavioural model
    The behavioural model explains psychological difficulties as learned through conditioning.
  6. The psychodynamic model
    The psychodynamic model explains psychological difficulties in terms of unconscious conflicts and early experiences.
  7. The humanistic model
    The humanistic model sees psychological difficulties as arising when people's needs for growth and self-acceptance are blocked.
  8. The diathesis–stress model
    The diathesis–stress model proposes that psychological difficulties arise when an underlying vulnerability meets stressful circumstances.
  9. The biopsychosocial model
    The biopsychosocial model explains psychological difficulties through the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors.
  10. Classification: DSM and ICD
    Diagnostic classification systems group symptoms into named conditions.
  11. Reliability and validity of diagnosis
    A good diagnosis should be reliable (consistent between clinicians) and valid (meaningful and useful).
  12. Generalised anxiety disorder
    Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) involves persistent, hard-to-control worry about many areas of life.
  13. Panic disorder
    Panic disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks and worry about further attacks.
  14. Specific phobias
    A specific phobia is an intense, persistent fear of a particular object or situation.
  15. Social anxiety disorder
    Social anxiety disorder involves intense fear of being judged or embarrassed in social situations.
  16. Obsessive-compulsive disorder
    Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) involves intrusive, distressing thoughts and repetitive behaviours performed to reduce distress.
  17. Trauma and post-traumatic stress disorder
    Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can develop after exposure to a traumatic event.
  18. Depression
    Depression involves persistent low mood or loss of interest, with effects on thinking, energy and daily life.
  19. Bipolar disorder
    Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania, usually alongside episodes of depression.
  20. Schizophrenia and psychosis
    Psychosis involves losing touch with shared reality, through hallucinations or delusions.
  21. Personality disorders
    Personality disorders describe long-standing patterns of thinking, feeling and relating that cause significant difficulties.
  22. Eating disorders
    Eating disorders involve serious disturbances in eating and in thoughts about weight and shape.
  23. Substance use disorders
    Substance use disorders involve continued use of alcohol or drugs despite significant harm.
  24. Autism
    Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication and how people experience the world.
  25. ADHD
    Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition involving inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity.
  26. Sleep disorders
    Sleep disorders affect the quality, timing or amount of sleep and have wide effects on health.
  27. Dissociative disorders
    Dissociation involves disruptions in the normal integration of memory, identity, perception and awareness.
  28. Somatic symptom disorders
    Somatic symptom disorders involve distressing physical symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours about them.
  29. Risk and protective factors
    Risk factors increase the likelihood of psychological difficulties, while protective factors reduce it.
  30. Comorbidity
    Comorbidity is the occurrence of two or more conditions in the same person.
  31. Stigma
    Mental health stigma involves negative stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination towards people with mental health difficulties.
  32. Culture and psychopathology
    Culture shapes how psychological difficulties are expressed, understood and responded to.
