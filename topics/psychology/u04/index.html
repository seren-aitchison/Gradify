Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is an unconditioned stimulus?

A stimulus that triggers a response without any learning, such as food causing salivation. From “Classical conditioning” →

In plain English: Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response after being paired with a stimulus that triggers it naturally. Key idea: In classical conditioning, repeated pairing of a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus makes it a conditioned stimulus that triggers a conditioned response.

What is the law of effect?

Responses followed by satisfying consequences become more likely; those followed by discomfort become less likely (Thorndike). From “Operant conditioning” →

In plain English: Operant conditioning is learning from consequences: behaviours followed by good outcomes become more frequent, and those followed by bad outcomes less frequent. Key idea: Operant conditioning changes voluntary behaviour through its consequences: reinforcement strengthens it and punishment weakens it.

What is shaping?

Training a new behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it. From “Shaping” →

In plain English: Shaping is training a new behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it. Key idea: Shaping builds new behaviours by reinforcing successive approximations, raising the criterion step by step.

Name three characteristics of models that increase imitation.

Any three of similarity, high status or competence, being rewarded, warmth or importance to the observer. From “Modelling and imitation” →

In plain English: Not all models are equally likely to be imitated. Key idea: Imitation is most likely when observers identify with similar, high-status or rewarded models.

What did Garcia and Koelling (1966) find?

Rats made ill learned to avoid a flavour but not light and noise; rats shocked avoided light and noise but not the flavour. From “Biological constraints on learning” →

In plain English: Not everything is equally easy to learn. Key idea: Biology makes some associations easy and others hard to learn, contradicting the assumption that any stimulus can be linked equally well to any response.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Learning and Behaviour

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In Pavlov's experiment, salivation to the bell after conditioning is the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Conditioned response. It is learned, and triggered by the bell, so it is the conditioned response.

  2. When does acquisition usually happen fastest? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Early in training. Learning curves are negatively accelerated: big gains at first, then levelling off.

  3. Spontaneous recovery happens… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    After a rest following extinction. It is the return of the CR when the CS is presented after time has passed.

  4. A child is stung by a wasp in a garden and afterwards feels anxious in that garden. What is the conditioned stimulus? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The garden. The garden was neutral until paired with the sting (UCS); now it triggers anxiety (CR).

  5. Food arrives after a tone on half of trials, and just as often with no tone. What does Rescorla's research predict? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Little conditioning to the tone. The tone doesn't predict food better than its absence, so little learning occurs.

  6. A dog's salivation to a bell is extinguished on Monday. On Tuesday the bell produces some salivation again. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Spontaneous recovery. The CR has returned after a rest, without new pairings.

  7. Which is the strongest methodological criticism of the Little Albert study? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It was a single, poorly measured case. One infant, informal observations and no follow-up limit what can be concluded.

  8. Which finding best supports the view that extinction is new learning? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A fear returns when the CS is met in a new context. Renewal in a new context shows the original association was suppressed, not lost.

  9. What is the most useful practical implication of spontaneous recovery for therapy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Plan for relapse with booster sessions and varied practice. Because fear can return, maintaining and generalising extinction learning helps prevent relapse.

Key takeaways

  • In classical conditioning, repeated pairing of a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus makes it a conditioned stimulus that triggers a conditioned response.
  • Acquisition builds a CR when the CS reliably predicts the UCS; extinction weakens it when the prediction fails, but the original learning is not erased.
  • Spontaneous recovery shows extinction suppresses rather than erases learning, so learned responses can return after time passes.
  • Generalisation spreads a learned response to similar stimuli; discrimination narrows it to the stimulus that actually predicts the outcome.
  • Operant conditioning changes voluntary behaviour through its consequences: reinforcement strengthens it and punishment weakens it.
  • Reinforcement increases behaviour; positive reinforcement adds something pleasant, negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant.

Common mistakes in Learning and Behaviour

  • Mixing up UCS and CS. Ask: did this stimulus trigger the response before any learning? If yes, it is unconditioned.
  • Thinking extinction means the association has been unlearned. The evidence shows it is suppressed by new learning, which is why relapse is common after exposure therapy.
  • Treating spontaneous recovery as a failure of the experiment or therapy. It is a predictable property of extinction learning.
  • Confusing generalisation with spontaneous recovery. Generalisation is about similar stimuli; spontaneous recovery is about time after extinction.
  • Thinking operant conditioning only applies to animals. Much human behaviour, from studying to social media use, is shaped by its consequences.
  • Confusing negative reinforcement with punishment. Negative reinforcement increases behaviour by removing something unpleasant; punishment decreases behaviour.

Lessons in this school

  1. Classical conditioning
    Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response after being paired with a stimulus that triggers it naturally.
  2. Acquisition and extinction
    Acquisition is the phase in which a conditioned response is learned; extinction is its gradual weakening when the conditioned stimulus is no longer followed by the unconditioned stimulus.
  3. Spontaneous recovery
    Spontaneous recovery is the reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a rest period.
  4. Generalisation and discrimination
    Generalisation is responding to stimuli similar to the conditioned stimulus; discrimination is learning to respond only to the conditioned stimulus.
  5. Operant conditioning
    Operant conditioning is learning from consequences: behaviours followed by good outcomes become more frequent, and those followed by bad outcomes less frequent.
  6. Positive and negative reinforcement
    Reinforcement always increases behaviour.
  7. Punishment
    Punishment is a consequence that decreases behaviour.
  8. Schedules of reinforcement
    Schedules of reinforcement are rules about which responses are reinforced.
  9. Shaping
    Shaping is training a new behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it.
  10. Behaviour modification
    Behaviour modification applies learning principles to change behaviour, in schools, care settings, health and everyday life.
  11. Observational learning
    Observational learning is learning by watching others, without needing to perform the behaviour or be reinforced directly.
  12. Social learning theory
    Bandura's social learning theory explains how people learn through observation, combining behaviourist ideas with cognitive processes.
  13. Modelling and imitation
    Not all models are equally likely to be imitated.
  14. Vicarious reinforcement
    Vicarious reinforcement is learning about the consequences of behaviour by seeing what happens to others.
  15. Latent learning
    Latent learning is learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it.
  16. Insight learning
    Insight learning is the sudden solution of a problem through restructuring it mentally, rather than gradual trial and error.
  17. Biological constraints on learning
    Not everything is equally easy to learn.
  18. Habituation and sensitisation
    Habituation and sensitisation are the simplest forms of learning: responding less to a repeated harmless stimulus, or more to stimuli after something intense.
  19. Habit formation
    Habits are behaviours triggered automatically by context, after being repeated in that context.
  20. Applications of learning theory
    Learning theory has shaped therapy, education, parenting, health and the design of technology.
