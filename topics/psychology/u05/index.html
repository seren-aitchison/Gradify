Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three stages of prenatal development?

Germinal, embryonic and fetal. From “Prenatal development” →

In plain English: Prenatal development runs from conception to birth. Key idea: Prenatal development moves through germinal, embryonic and fetal stages; timing, dose and susceptibility shape teratogen effects.

What does multidirectional mean in lifespan development?

Development involves both gains and losses at every age. From “Lifespan perspectives” →

In plain English: Lifespan perspectives treat development as continuing from conception to death and shaped by many interacting influences. Key idea: Lifespan perspectives see development as lifelong, multidirectional and embedded in nested contexts that interact over time.

What is social referencing?

Using others' emotional reactions to decide how to behave in an uncertain situation. From “Emotional development” →

In plain English: Emotional development is how children come to express, understand and regulate emotions. Key idea: Basic emotions appear in the first year, self-conscious emotions in the second and third; children use others' emotions to guide action and gradually learn to regulate their own.

Name Baumrind's three original parenting styles.

Authoritative, authoritarian and permissive. From “Caregivers and parenting styles” →

In plain English: Caregivers shape development through warmth, responsiveness and control. Key idea: Infants form multiple attachments based on sensitive care; authoritative parenting is often linked to good outcomes, but culture and child effects complicate cause and effect.

What is Gilligan's main criticism?

Kohlberg's justice focus and male sample undervalued an ethic of care. From “Moral development: Kohlberg” →

In plain English: Kohlberg proposed that moral reasoning develops through six stages grouped into three levels. Key idea: Kohlberg described moral reasoning developing from punishment-avoidance to principled ethics; the sequence is supported but culture, care and behaviour are underplayed.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Developmental Psychology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. During which stage do major organs first form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Embryonic. Organs form in weeks 3 to 8, the embryonic stage.

  2. Most infants walk alone at around… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    12 months. Walking alone typically appears around the first birthday, with wide variation.

  3. At about what age do most children pass the Sally–Anne task? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4 years. Most typically developing children pass around age 4.

  4. Newborns prefer a story their mother read aloud during pregnancy. What does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Learning before birth. Prenatal exposure to speech shaped newborn preferences.

  5. An infant looks less at a repeated picture but looks longer when a new one appears. This shows… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The infant can tell the pictures apart. Recovery of looking shows discrimination.

  6. A child says Sally will look in the box, where the marble now is. What does this suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They don't yet understand false belief. Answering with reality, not Sally's belief, shows no false-belief understanding yet.

  7. Why is it hard to show that prenatal stress causes later child problems? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Shared genes and settings. Shared genes and postnatal settings confound the link.

  8. Why do some cultures see earlier sitting or walking? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Infants are given more practice. Experience and practice can speed motor milestones.

  9. Why should the Sally–Anne autism findings be interpreted cautiously? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Autistic people vary greatly. Later research shows wide variation and task limitations.

Key takeaways

  • Prenatal development moves through germinal, embryonic and fetal stages; timing, dose and susceptibility shape teratogen effects.
  • Infants are born with reflexes and perceptual preferences, reach motor milestones in a broad window and are studied through looking and habituation.
  • Childhood brings theory of mind around age 4, steadily improving executive function and an increasingly psychological self-concept.
  • Adolescence brings puberty, synaptic pruning, increasing white matter and a prefrontal cortex still maturing, with heightened sensitivity to peers and rewards.
  • Adulthood involves continued change in identity, personality and goals; many older adults maintain well-being by prioritising meaningful goals.
  • Lifespan perspectives see development as lifelong, multidirectional and embedded in nested contexts that interact over time.

Common mistakes in Developmental Psychology

  • Assuming a fetus is unaffected until late pregnancy. The embryonic weeks, when organs form, are the most sensitive to many teratogens.
  • Treating milestone ages as deadlines. Normal development varies widely, and many differences even out.
  • Overstating the marshmallow test's predictive power. Replications with larger, more diverse samples found much smaller links.
  • Describing adolescents as having "broken" brains. Their brains are developing, adapted to learning and becoming independent.
  • Assuming old age is a time of general decline and unhappiness. Emotional well-being often remains high or improves.
  • Treating development as finished by adulthood. Gains and losses continue throughout life.

Lessons in this school

  1. Prenatal development
    Prenatal development runs from conception to birth.
  2. Infancy
    Infancy is the first two years of life, a time of rapid physical, perceptual and social development.
  3. Childhood
    Childhood, from about 2 to 11 years, brings major growth in thinking, self-control and social understanding.
  4. Adolescence
    Adolescence is the transition from childhood to adulthood, marked by puberty, brain development and social change.
  5. Adulthood and ageing
    Adulthood brings continued development in relationships, work, personality and well-being.
  6. Lifespan perspectives
    Lifespan perspectives treat development as continuing from conception to death and shaped by many interacting influences.
  7. Piaget's theory of cognitive development
    Piaget's theory proposes that children actively construct understanding through four stages.
  8. Vygotsky and sociocultural development
    Vygotsky argued that children's thinking develops through social interaction and cultural tools.
  9. Information-processing approaches to development
    Information-processing approaches explain cognitive development as gradual improvements in speed, capacity, strategies and knowledge.
  10. Language development
    Language development runs from early speech perception to complex grammar and conversation.
  11. Emotional development
    Emotional development is how children come to express, understand and regulate emotions.
  12. Social development
    Social development is how children learn to relate to others.
  13. Bowlby's attachment theory
    Attachment is a deep, lasting emotional bond to a caregiver.
  14. Ainsworth and the Strange Situation
    Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation to assess the quality of infant attachment.
  15. Measuring attachment
    Attachment can be measured at different ages using observation, interviews and questionnaires.
  16. Caregivers and parenting styles
    Caregivers shape development through warmth, responsiveness and control.
  17. Peers and friendship
    Peers become increasingly important through childhood and adolescence.
  18. Family influences
    Families shape development through relationships, conflict, structure and economic circumstances.
  19. Identity development
    Identity is a coherent sense of who you are, what you value and where you are going.
  20. Erikson's psychosocial stages
    Erikson proposed eight stages of psychosocial development across the lifespan, each centred on a conflict.
  21. Moral development: Kohlberg
    Kohlberg proposed that moral reasoning develops through six stages grouped into three levels.
  22. Risk and resilience
    Resilience is positive adaptation despite significant adversity.
  23. Neurodevelopment
    Neurodevelopment is how the brain develops from before birth to adulthood.
  24. Neurodevelopmental conditions
    Neurodevelopmental conditions begin in development and affect how the brain processes information.
  25. Developmental psychopathology
    Developmental psychopathology studies how mental health difficulties emerge and change across development.
  26. Developmental research methods
    Developmental researchers study change over time, which needs special designs.

Developmental Psychology: questions students ask

What is the rooting reflex?

Turning the head towards a touch on the cheek.

What is theory of mind?

Understanding that others have beliefs and desires that may differ from one's own.

What happens to grey and white matter in adolescence?

Grey matter peaks and declines with pruning; white matter increases.

What is emerging adulthood?

A proposed stage from about 18 to 29 of identity exploration and instability.
