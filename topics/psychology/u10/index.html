Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between deduction and induction?

Deduction reasons from a general theory to a specific prediction; induction from specific observations to a general pattern. From “Scientific reasoning in psychology” →

In plain English: Scientific reasoning is the way psychologists move from ideas to evidence and back: making claims testable, collecting data systematically and drawing conclusions that go no further than the data allow. Key idea: Scientific reasoning tests specific predictions deduced from theory, and treats every conclusion as provisional.

What is ecological validity?

The extent to which findings apply to real-life settings. From “Laboratory experiments” →

In plain English: Laboratory experiments manipulate an IV in a controlled setting, allowing strong causal conclusions at a possible cost to realism. Key idea: Laboratory experiments maximise control and causal inference, but their artificiality can limit how far findings apply to real life.

What is the main weakness of naturalistic observation for explaining behaviour?

There is no manipulation or control, so causes can't be established. From “Naturalistic observation” →

In plain English: Naturalistic observation records behaviour as it happens in its everyday setting, without interfering. Key idea: Naturalistic observation captures real behaviour in context, at the cost of control and with risks of observer effects and bias.

What is volunteer bias?

Systematic differences between people who volunteer for research and those who don't. From “Opportunity and volunteer sampling” →

In plain English: Opportunity and volunteer samples are quick and common in psychology, but they are rarely representative. Key idea: Opportunity and volunteer samples are convenient but likely to be unrepresentative, so generalisation must be cautious.

What is preregistration?

A public, time-stamped record of a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan made before data collection. From “Preregistration” →

In plain English: Preregistration means publicly recording a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan before collecting or analysing data. Key idea: Preregistration records hypotheses and analyses in advance, separating confirmatory tests from exploration and reducing p-hacking.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Deriving a specific prediction from a general theory is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Deduction. Deduction moves from the general to the specific.

  2. "There will be a difference in anxiety scores between two groups" is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Non-directional hypothesis. It predicts a difference without saying which group will score higher.

  3. Measuring "hunger" as hours since the last meal is an example of… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Operationalisation. It defines the concept in measurable terms.

  4. A researcher only reports the three participants whose results fit her theory. Which error is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Confirmation bias. Selecting supportive evidence is confirmation bias, here as selective reporting.

  5. Several studies show exercise improves mood. Which hypothesis suits a new study? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Exercisers will report higher mood than non-exercisers. Consistent previous findings justify a directional, operationalised prediction.

  6. What does a directional hypothesis do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Predicts the direction of the effect. A directional, one-tailed hypothesis states which way the difference will go.

  7. Which design feature best protects against confirmation bias in analysis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Preregistering predictions and analyses. Committing to predictions and analyses in advance limits choosing results that fit.

  8. Why is a non-directional hypothesis sometimes preferred? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can detect an effect in either direction. With no clear previous direction, a two-tailed test can detect effects either way.

  9. Why might two studies of "anxiety" reach different conclusions? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They may have operationalised anxiety differently. Different measures capture different aspects of a construct.

Key takeaways

  • Scientific reasoning tests specific predictions deduced from theory, and treats every conclusion as provisional.
  • A hypothesis is a precise, testable prediction naming the variables and, if justified by previous research, the direction of the effect.
  • Operationalisation makes concepts measurable; every operational definition captures only part of the concept, so the choice shapes the conclusions.
  • The IV is manipulated by the researcher; the DV is measured. Changing only the IV, while controlling everything else, is what lets experiments show cause and effect.
  • A confound varies systematically with the IV and provides an alternative explanation; control techniques aim to remove or balance such variables.
  • The choice of design trades off participant variables against order effects and practicality.

Common mistakes in Research Methods

  • Saying a study "proves" a theory. Evidence supports or fails to support; a later study may overturn it.
  • Writing vague hypotheses such as "caffeine affects people". A hypothesis must name the operationalised variables and the predicted relationship.
  • Treating the measure as if it were the concept. A memory test score measures performance on that test, not "memory" in every sense.
  • Calling a measured characteristic like gender an IV in a true experiment. It can be a grouping variable, but it isn't manipulated, so the study is a quasi-experiment.
  • Calling every extraneous variable a confound. It is only a confound if it changes systematically with the IV.
  • Saying repeated measures has "no participant variables". It controls differences between conditions, but participants still differ from one another.

Lessons in this school

  1. Scientific reasoning in psychology
    Scientific reasoning is the way psychologists move from ideas to evidence and back: making claims testable, collecting data systematically and drawing conclusions that go no further than the data allow.
  2. Research questions and hypotheses
    A good study starts with a clear research question and a precise hypothesis.
  3. Operationalisation
    Operationalisation turns an abstract concept, such as stress or aggression, into something that can be measured.
  4. Independent and dependent variables
    In an experiment, the independent variable is what the researcher changes and the dependent variable is what they measure.
  5. Confounding variables
    A confounding variable changes along with the independent variable, so it offers an alternative explanation for the results.
  6. Experimental design
    Experimental design is how participants are allocated to conditions: independent groups, repeated measures or matched pairs.
  7. Laboratory experiments
    Laboratory experiments manipulate an IV in a controlled setting, allowing strong causal conclusions at a possible cost to realism.
  8. Field experiments
    Field experiments manipulate an IV in a natural setting, often without participants knowing they are in a study.
  9. Natural experiments
    In a natural experiment, the independent variable changes naturally, without the researcher manipulating it, and the researcher studies its effects.
  10. Quasi-experiments
    A quasi-experiment compares groups that differ on a pre-existing characteristic, such as age, sex or diagnosis, which the researcher can't assign.
  11. Correlational research
    Correlational research measures two or more variables to see whether they are related, without manipulating anything.
  12. Cross-sectional and longitudinal research
    To study change over time, researchers either compare different age groups at one point (cross-sectional) or follow the same people over years (longitudinal).
  13. Case studies
    A case study is an in-depth investigation of one person, group or event, usually using several methods.
  14. Naturalistic observation
    Naturalistic observation records behaviour as it happens in its everyday setting, without interfering.
  15. Structured observation
    Structured observation uses predefined behavioural categories and sampling methods so that observations are systematic, countable and reliable.
  16. Surveys and questionnaires
    Surveys collect self-report data from many people using questionnaires.
  17. Interviews
    Interviews collect self-report data through conversation, from fully structured to unstructured.
  18. Focus groups
    A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group about a topic, used to explore views and how people talk about an issue together.
  19. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods
    Quantitative research measures and counts; qualitative research explores meanings in words and experiences; mixed methods combine them.
  20. Random and stratified sampling
    Sampling decides who takes part.
  21. Opportunity and volunteer sampling
    Opportunity and volunteer samples are quick and common in psychology, but they are rarely representative.
  22. Sample size
    How many participants a study needs depends on the size of the effect it is looking for and how sure the researchers want to be of detecting it.
  23. Reliability
    Reliability is the consistency of a measure: whether it gives the same results under the same conditions.
  24. Validity
    Validity is whether a study or measure actually captures what it claims to.
  25. Generalisability
    Generalisability is how far findings extend beyond the specific people, place and time studied.
  26. Bias in research
    Bias is systematic error that pushes results in a particular direction.
  27. Replication
    Replication means repeating a study to see whether its findings hold.
  28. Preregistration
    Preregistration means publicly recording a study's hypotheses, design and analysis plan before collecting or analysing data.
  29. Open science
    Open science is a set of practices that make research transparent, accessible and checkable: sharing data, materials, code and papers, and making plans public.
  30. Informed consent
    Informed consent means participants agree to take part knowing what the research involves, its risks and their rights.
  31. Confidentiality
    Confidentiality means protecting participants' personal information and ensuring they can't be identified in reports.
  32. Deception and debriefing
    Deception means misleading participants about a study, and debriefing means explaining the study fully afterwards.
  33. Vulnerable participants
    Some participants need extra protection because their capacity to consent is limited or they face greater risk: children, people with some cognitive impairments or mental health conditions, prisoners
  34. Research governance and ethics committees
    Research governance is the system of rules, review and accountability that oversees research.
