Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the standard of proof in a civil case?

The balance of probabilities. From “The English legal system” →

In plain English: Business law in England and Wales rests on legislation and case law applied through a hierarchy of courts. Key idea: English law comes mainly from legislation and binding case law; civil cases are proved on the balance of probabilities and criminal cases beyond reasonable doubt; lower courts follow the ratio of higher courts.

Can a business exclude liability for death caused by its negligence?

No; section 2(1) of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 prevents it. From “Contract terms and breach” →

In plain English: The terms of a contract set out each party's obligations. Key idea: Terms are express or implied; conditions, warranties and innominate terms give different remedies; exclusion clauses must be incorporated and are controlled by UCTA 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015.

What is constructive dismissal?

Resigning because of the employer's fundamental (repudiatory) breach of contract. From “Employment law” →

In plain English: Employment law governs the relationship between employers and employees. Key idea: Employees have greater rights than contractors; wrongful dismissal is breach of contract, unfair dismissal needs a fair reason and fair procedure, and discrimination is prohibited under the Equality Act 2010.

From what may a company pay dividends?

Accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses (distributable profits). From “Share capital and capital maintenance” →

In plain English: Share capital is the money members invest in a company, and the law protects it for creditors. Key idea: Shares cannot be issued at a discount; existing members have pre-emption rights; dividends may be paid only from accumulated realised profits less losses; capital may be reduced or shares bought back only by statutory procedures.

What percentage of votes does a special resolution need?

At least 75%. From “Company meetings and resolutions” →

In plain English: Members make key decisions at general meetings or by written resolution. Key idea: Public companies must hold an AGM; ordinary resolutions need a simple majority and special resolutions 75%; private companies can use written resolutions except to remove directors or auditors; 5% of members can requisition a meeting.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Business and Company Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a civil matter? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A supplier suing a customer for an unpaid invoice. Contract disputes between parties are civil; the others are criminal prosecutions.

  2. Goods displayed in a shop window with a price are usually what? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An invitation to treat. Fisher v Bell and Boots treat displays as invitations to treat.

  3. What is a condition? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A vital term whose breach allows the contract to be ended. Breach of a condition allows termination and damages.

  4. What part of an earlier judgment binds a lower court? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The ratio decidendi. Only the legal reasoning necessary for the decision is binding.

  5. A buyer replies to an offer to sell at £1,000 by saying "I'll pay £900". What is the effect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is a counter-offer that rejects the original offer. Hyde v Wrench: a counter-offer destroys the original offer.

  6. When was the exclusion notice in Olley v Marlborough Court ineffective? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It was seen only after the contract was made. Terms must be brought to notice before or when the contract is made.

  7. A High Court judge finds that an earlier Court of Appeal case had materially different facts. What may the judge do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Distinguish the earlier case. Distinguishing avoids following a precedent when facts differ materially.

  8. A builder is promised extra pay to finish on time, and the client gains a practical benefit. Is the promise enforceable? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Yes, following Williams v Roffey. A practical benefit to the promisor can amount to consideration.

  9. How is an innominate term treated when breached? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    By looking at how serious the consequences of the breach are. Hongkong Fir: the remedy depends on whether the breach deprives the party of substantially the whole benefit.

Key takeaways

  • English law comes mainly from legislation and binding case law; civil cases are proved on the balance of probabilities and criminal cases beyond reasonable doubt; lower courts follow the ratio of higher courts.
  • A contract needs an offer, unconditional acceptance, consideration, intention to create legal relations and capacity; displays and adverts are usually invitations to treat.
  • Terms are express or implied; conditions, warranties and innominate terms give different remedies; exclusion clauses must be incorporated and are controlled by UCTA 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015.
  • Damages compensate expectation loss, limited by remoteness and mitigation; liquidated damages are enforceable unless out of all proportion to a legitimate interest; equitable remedies are discretionary.
  • Negligence requires duty, breach, causation and remoteness; economic loss from misstatements needs an assumption of responsibility; auditors owe their statutory duty to the company and shareholders as a body, not to individual investors.
  • Employees have greater rights than contractors; wrongful dismissal is breach of contract, unfair dismissal needs a fair reason and fair procedure, and discrimination is prohibited under the Equality Act 2010.

Common mistakes in Business and Company Law

  • Writing "UK law" when only English law is meant. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate legal systems; this school describes the law of England and Wales.
  • Treating a price display as an offer. Displays and most adverts are invitations to treat; the customer makes the offer.
  • Assuming a signed document always binds every term. Signature incorporates terms, but statutory controls can still make exclusion clauses ineffective.
  • Thinking damages punish the party in breach. Contract damages compensate; they are not meant to punish.
  • Assuming auditors are liable to anyone who relies on audited accounts. Caparo limits the duty to the company and members as a body unless special circumstances exist.
  • Employment law is being reformed. Reforms begun with the Employment Rights Bill introduced in 2024 change several rights, including the unfair dismissal qualifying period; check the current position before applying the two-year rule.

Lessons in this school

  1. The English legal system
    Business law in England and Wales rests on legislation and case law applied through a hierarchy of courts.
  2. Contract formation
    A contract is a legally binding agreement.
  3. Contract terms and breach
    The terms of a contract set out each party's obligations.
  4. Contract remedies
    When a contract is broken, the law provides remedies to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in.
  5. Negligence and professional liability
    The tort of negligence imposes liability for careless acts and statements that cause loss.
  6. Employment law
    Employment law governs the relationship between employers and employees.
  7. Agency and partnership law
    Businesses act through agents, and many professional firms are partnerships.
  8. Forming a company
    A registered company is a separate legal person distinct from its members.
  9. Share capital and capital maintenance
    Share capital is the money members invest in a company, and the law protects it for creditors.
  10. Loan capital and charges
    Companies borrow as well as issuing shares, and lenders often take security.
  11. Directors' duties
    Directors manage the company and owe it legal duties.
  12. Company meetings and resolutions
    Members make key decisions at general meetings or by written resolution.
  13. Insolvency
    When a company cannot pay its debts, insolvency law decides whether it can be rescued and how its assets are shared.
  14. Fraudulent behaviour: insider dealing, money laundering and bribery
    Some business misconduct is criminal.

Business and Company Law: questions students ask

What did Hyde v Wrench establish?

A counter-offer rejects the original offer, which cannot later be accepted.

What remedy follows breach of a warranty?

Damages only; the contract continues.

What is the duty to mitigate?

The claimant must take reasonable steps to reduce the loss caused by the breach.

What are the three Caparo questions for a novel duty of care?

Foreseeability of harm, proximity, and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty.
