Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three core decisions of financial management?

Investment, financing and dividend decisions. From “The financial management function” →

In plain English: Financial management makes decisions about raising and using funds to create value. Key idea: Financial management covers investment, financing and dividend decisions, supported by a treasury function managing cash, funding and risk, with a forward-looking focus on cash flow and value.

How is average investment calculated for ARR?

(Initial cost + residual value) ÷ 2. From “Accounting rate of return and payback” →

In plain English: Accounting rate of return (ARR) and payback are simple appraisal methods still widely used. Key idea: Payback measures how quickly cash repays the investment and ARR measures average accounting profit relative to average investment; both are simple but ignore the time value of money, so they support rather than replace NPV.

What is soft capital rationing?

Internal limits on investment set by management. From “Capital rationing and asset replacement” →

In plain English: When capital is limited, firms must choose the best combination of projects. Key idea: Capital rationing is hard or soft; divisible projects are ranked by profitability index and indivisible ones by testing combinations; equivalent annual cost compares replacement cycles of different lengths.

What does the Miller–Orr model set?

A lower limit, an upper limit and a return point for cash balances. From “Managing cash” →

In plain English: Cash management keeps enough cash to meet obligations while investing any surplus. Key idea: Firms hold cash for transactions, precaution and speculation; the Baumol and Miller–Orr models set cash levels, with Miller–Orr suited to random flows; surpluses are invested in short-term, liquid, low-risk instruments.

Why is the after-tax cost of debt lower than the interest rate?

Interest is tax-deductible, giving tax relief. From “The cost of debt and WACC” →

In plain English: The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) combines the costs of equity and debt into one discount rate. Key idea: The after-tax cost of debt reflects tax relief on interest; WACC weights the costs of equity and debt by market values; it suits projects with the firm's usual risk and financing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Financial Management

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which decision concerns how much profit to pay to shareholders? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The dividend decision. Dividend policy decides distribution versus retention.

  2. Who are the principals in the agency relationship between shareholders and managers? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Shareholders. Shareholders appoint managers as their agents.

  3. Which market deals with short-term borrowing of less than a year? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The money market. Applying the standard result: Money markets handle short-term funds.

  4. How is financial management different from financial accounting? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It looks forward at cash flows, risk and value. Financial management supports forward-looking decisions.

  5. Why is maximising earnings per share an imperfect objective? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It ignores risk and the timing of cash flows. Applying the standard result: EPS can rise while value falls.

  6. What is maturity transformation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Banks using short-term deposits to make long-term loans. Intermediaries bridge savers' and borrowers' time horizons.

  7. A firm plans major investment but also wants to raise its dividend sharply. Why might this be a problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Higher dividends reduce retained earnings available for investment, increasing the need for external finance. Applying the standard result: The three decisions are interdependent.

  8. A CEO's bonus depends only on this year's profit. What risk does this create? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Short-term decisions that harm long-term shareholder value. Short-term incentives can cut valuable investment.

  9. Why do insider dealing laws suggest markets are not strong-form efficient? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Insiders could profit from private information not yet in prices. If private information were priced, insider dealing would not pay.

Key takeaways

  • Financial management covers investment, financing and dividend decisions, supported by a treasury function managing cash, funding and risk, with a forward-looking focus on cash flow and value.
  • Companies usually aim to maximise shareholder wealth, but agency problems arise when managers pursue their own interests; governance, incentives and shareholder engagement reduce them, and directors must have regard to wider stakeholders.
  • Money markets handle short-term funds and capital markets long-term funds; intermediaries pool and transform savings; the Bank of England sets Bank Rate; the efficient market hypothesis holds that prices reflect available information to varying degrees.
  • Compounding gives future values and discounting gives present values; annuity factors value level cash flows, and perpetuities are valued as cash flow divided by the discount rate.
  • Payback measures how quickly cash repays the investment and ARR measures average accounting profit relative to average investment; both are simple but ignore the time value of money, so they support rather than replace NPV.
  • NPV discounts all relevant cash flows at the cost of capital; projects with positive NPV should be accepted because they increase shareholder wealth, making NPV the preferred method.

Common mistakes in Financial Management

  • Treating the three decisions separately. Investment plans affect how much finance is needed and what dividends can be paid.
  • Equating profit maximisation with shareholder wealth. Profit ignores risk, timing and cash flows.
  • Assuming efficient markets mean prices are always "right". Efficiency means prices quickly reflect information, not that they never err.
  • Adding cash flows from different years without discounting. A pound in five years is worth less than a pound today.
  • Rejecting a project with a long payback that has large later cash flows. Payback ignores everything after the payback point.
  • Using accounting profit or including sunk costs in NPV. Only relevant future cash flows should be discounted.

Lessons in this school

  1. The financial management function
    Financial management makes decisions about raising and using funds to create value.
  2. Financial objectives and stakeholders
    Companies pursue financial objectives on behalf of shareholders, but managers and other stakeholders have their own interests.
  3. The financial environment
    Companies raise and invest funds through financial markets and institutions.
  4. The time value of money
    Money received today is worth more than the same amount later, because it can be invested.
  5. Accounting rate of return and payback
    Accounting rate of return (ARR) and payback are simple appraisal methods still widely used.
  6. Net present value
    Net present value (NPV) is the theoretically preferred method of investment appraisal.
  7. Internal rate of return
    The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which a project's NPV is zero.
  8. Inflation and taxation in investment appraisal
    Inflation and tax affect project cash flows and discount rates.
  9. Risk and uncertainty in investment appraisal
    Investment cash flows are uncertain, so appraisal must consider risk.
  10. Capital rationing and asset replacement
    When capital is limited, firms must choose the best combination of projects.
  11. Working capital and its financing
    Working capital is the capital tied up in day-to-day operations.
  12. Managing inventory
    Inventory management balances the costs of holding stock against the risks of running out.
  13. Managing receivables and payables
    Credit given to customers and taken from suppliers has a major effect on cash.
  14. Managing cash
    Cash management keeps enough cash to meet obligations while investing any surplus.
  15. Sources of finance
    Businesses can raise finance from many sources, each with different costs and risks.
  16. Islamic finance
    Islamic finance follows Sharia principles, notably the prohibition of interest.
  17. Dividend policy
    Dividend policy decides how much of its profit a company pays to shareholders.
  18. The cost of equity
    The cost of equity is the return shareholders require.
  19. The cost of debt and WACC
    The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) combines the costs of equity and debt into one discount rate.
  20. Capital structure
    Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity finance.
  21. Business valuation
    Businesses are valued for takeovers, share issues and investment decisions.
  22. Foreign exchange risk
    Companies trading internationally face risks from exchange rate movements.
  23. Interest rate risk
    Changes in interest rates affect borrowing costs and the value of investments.

Financial Management: questions students ask

What is the agency problem?

The conflict when managers pursue their own interests rather than those of shareholders.

What does the semi-strong form of market efficiency state?

Prices reflect all publicly available information.

What is the present value of a perpetuity?

The annual cash flow divided by the discount rate.

What is the payback period?

The time taken for a project's cash inflows to repay the initial investment.
