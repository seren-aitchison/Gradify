Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who issues IFRS?

The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), part of the IFRS Foundation. From “The regulatory framework for financial reporting” →

In plain English: Company financial statements are shaped by law, accounting standards and the bodies that set and enforce them. Key idea: UK company reporting combines the Companies Act 2006 with accounting standards: UK-adopted IFRS for listed groups and FRS 102 for most other companies, overseen by the FRC and checked by auditors.

What is share premium?

The amount received for shares above their nominal value. From “Share capital and reserves” →

In plain English: A company's equity is made up of share capital and reserves. Key idea: Equity is share capital (at nominal value), share premium and reserves; only realised profits can be distributed, and dividends are deducted from equity, not charged to profit.

Where are dividends paid shown?

In the statement of changes in equity (and as cash paid in the statement of cash flows). From “Statement of changes in equity” →

In plain English: The statement of changes in equity reconciles opening and closing equity for each component. Key idea: The SOCIE reconciles each equity component from opening to closing, separating total comprehensive income from transactions with owners, and links profit and OCI to the statement of financial position.

Which cost formula is prohibited by IAS 2?

LIFO (last in, first out). From “Inventories under IAS 2” →

In plain English: IAS 2 requires inventories to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Key idea: Inventories are measured at the lower of cost and NRV; cost includes purchase and conversion costs; FIFO or weighted average may be used, but not LIFO.

Is a dividend declared after the year end a liability at the year end?

No: it is disclosed, not recognised. From “Events after the reporting period” →

In plain English: IAS 10 decides how events between the reporting date and the date the accounts are authorised are treated. Key idea: Adjust for events that give evidence of conditions at the reporting date; disclose material events arising after it; dividends declared after the year end aren't liabilities; loss of going concern overrides the normal rules.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Financial Reporting for Companies

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which companies must use UK-adopted IFRS for their consolidated accounts? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Groups with securities traded on a UK regulated market. Listed groups must use adopted international standards; most other companies may choose FRS 102.

  2. Which is the 2018 Conceptual Framework's definition of an asset? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. The 2018 definition stresses control of a present economic resource, a right with potential to produce benefits.

  3. Which is an enhancing qualitative characteristic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Comparability. Comparability is enhancing; relevance and faithful representation are fundamental; materiality is an aspect of relevance.

  4. A private company with no listed securities wants simpler reporting than full IFRS. Which standard is it most likely to use? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    FRS 102. FRS 102 is the main UK GAAP standard for unlisted companies.

  5. A company plans to buy a new factory next year. Should it recognise a liability now? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No: there is no present obligation from a past event. Intentions don't create present obligations; a liability arises when there is an obligation from a past event, such as a signed contract.

  6. A small fraud of £500 is found in a company with profits of £50 million, but it involved the finance director. Why might it be material? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Its nature, involving a director, could influence users even though the amount is small. Materiality depends on nature as well as size; director involvement could affect users' assessment of management.

  7. An investor compares a listed company using IFRS with a private rival using FRS 102. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Check accounting differences, such as goodwill amortisation, before comparing figures. FRS 102 differs from IFRS in several areas, so adjusted comparisons are needed.

  8. A firm has spent heavily training staff who are free to leave at any time. Why isn't the training recognised as an asset? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The firm doesn't control the economic resource, because staff can leave. Control is missing: the benefits depend on employees who aren't under the entity's control.

  9. A finance director wants to make an extra large provision "to be prudent". What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A provision beyond the best estimate isn't neutral and would misrepresent the company's position. The Framework says prudence doesn't allow deliberate overstatement of liabilities; faithful representation needs a neutral estimate.

Key takeaways

  • UK company reporting combines the Companies Act 2006 with accounting standards: UK-adopted IFRS for listed groups and FRS 102 for most other companies, overseen by the FRC and checked by auditors.
  • The Framework's objective is decision-useful information for investors, lenders and creditors; assets and liabilities are defined first, and the other elements follow from changes in them.
  • Useful information must be relevant (including material) and faithfully represented; comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability enhance it, subject to cost.
  • Recognise items that meet an element definition when that gives relevant, faithful information; measure them at historical cost or a current value, with fair value graded by the observability of its inputs.
  • Equity is share capital (at nominal value), share premium and reserves; only realised profits can be distributed, and dividends are deducted from equity, not charged to profit.
  • Rights issues raise cash from existing holders at a discount, giving a TERP between the old and offer prices; bonus issues capitalise reserves without cash, leaving equity unchanged.

Common mistakes in Financial Reporting for Companies

  • Thinking IFRS and UK GAAP are the same. FRS 102 is based on IFRS principles but differs in many areas, such as goodwill amortisation and simpler disclosures.
  • Defining an asset as something the entity owns. The 2018 Framework focuses on control of an economic resource (a right), which includes leased items and excludes things the entity owns but can't benefit from.
  • Treating prudence as permission to understate profits. Deliberate understatement isn't neutral; it misleads users just as overstatement does.
  • Treating fair value as "what the company thinks it is worth". Fair value is a market-based exit price from the perspective of market participants, not the entity's own estimate of its value to the business (which is value in use).
  • Treating share premium as profit. It is capital contributed by shareholders and can't be paid out as dividends.
  • Thinking a bonus issue makes shareholders richer. Each shareholder has more shares but the same proportion of the company; the share price falls accordingly.

Lessons in this school

  1. The regulatory framework for financial reporting
    Company financial statements are shaped by law, accounting standards and the bodies that set and enforce them.
  2. The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting
    The IASB's Conceptual Framework sets out the purpose of financial reporting and defines its building blocks.
  3. Qualitative characteristics of useful information
    The Conceptual Framework identifies the qualities that make financial information useful.
  4. Recognition and measurement
    Recognition decides whether an item appears in the financial statements; measurement decides at what amount.
  5. Share capital and reserves
    A company's equity is made up of share capital and reserves.
  6. Issues of shares, rights issues and bonus issues
    Companies raise capital by issuing shares and can reorganise equity without new cash through bonus issues.
  7. Presenting company financial statements
    IAS 1 sets out what a complete set of financial statements contains and the general principles of presentation.
  8. Statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income
    The statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income reports a company's performance for the period.
  9. Statement of changes in equity
    The statement of changes in equity reconciles opening and closing equity for each component.
  10. Statement of cash flows
    The statement of cash flows shows how a company generated and used cash in the period.
  11. Property, plant and equipment
    IAS 16 sets out how to account for property, plant and equipment (PPE).
  12. Intangible assets
    Intangible assets are identifiable non-monetary assets without physical substance, such as patents, software and licences.
  13. Impairment of assets
    An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds what can be recovered from it.
  14. Inventories under IAS 2
    IAS 2 requires inventories to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
  15. Revenue from contracts with customers
    IFRS 15 sets out a single five-step model for recognising revenue from contracts with customers.
  16. Leases
    IFRS 16 brings almost all leases onto the lessee's statement of financial position.
  17. Provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets
    IAS 37 decides when uncertain obligations are recognised as provisions and when they are only disclosed.
  18. Events after the reporting period
    IAS 10 decides how events between the reporting date and the date the accounts are authorised are treated.
  19. Accounting policies, estimates and errors
    IAS 8 governs how companies select and change accounting policies, revise estimates and correct errors.
  20. Income taxes
    IAS 12 covers current tax and deferred tax.
  21. Financial instruments
    Financial instruments include loans, bonds, shares and derivatives.
  22. Earnings per share
    Earnings per share (EPS) is one of the most widely used performance measures for listed companies.

Financial Reporting for Companies: questions students ask

Who are the primary users of general purpose financial reports?

Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors.

What are the two fundamental qualitative characteristics?

Relevance and faithful representation.

What is a level 1 input in the IFRS 13 hierarchy?

A quoted price in an active market for an identical asset or liability.

What are pre-emption rights?

The right of existing shareholders to be offered new shares issued for cash before outsiders.
