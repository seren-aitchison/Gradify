Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who is responsible for preparing the financial statements?

The directors. From “What is an audit?” →

In plain English: An external audit gives users independent assurance that financial statements are fairly presented. Key idea: An external audit is an independent examination giving reasonable, not absolute, assurance that financial statements give a true and fair view, reducing the agency problem between directors and shareholders.

To whom should internal audit report functionally?

The audit committee. From “Internal audit” →

In plain English: Internal audit is an independent function within an organisation that evaluates risk management, control and governance. Key idea: Internal audit gives independent assurance on risk, control and governance across an organisation and reports to the audit committee, while external audit gives an opinion to shareholders on the financial statements; external auditors can use internal audit's work after evaluating it.

Why is performance materiality lower than overall materiality?

To allow for undetected and uncorrected misstatements adding up. From “Risk assessment and materiality” →

In plain English: A risk-based audit concentrates effort where misstatement is most likely and most important. Key idea: Audit risk combines inherent, control and detection risk; auditors identify where material misstatement is likely, set materiality from a suitable benchmark with a lower performance materiality, and focus procedures on significant risks.

What happens to sample size when assessed risk rises?

It increases. From “Audit sampling and data analytics” →

In plain English: Auditors rarely test every item, so they sample, and increasingly they analyse whole populations with data analytics. Key idea: Sampling lets auditors draw conclusions from part of a population, with statistical methods measuring sampling risk; monetary unit sampling favours large balances, and data analytics allows whole populations to be examined for unusual items.

What opinion is given when evidence is unavailable and the effect is pervasive?

A disclaimer of opinion. From “The auditor's report” →

In plain English: The auditor's report communicates the opinion to shareholders. Key idea: The auditor's report gives an opinion on truth and fairness; modified opinions are qualified, adverse or disclaimers depending on whether the issue is a misstatement or a lack of evidence, and whether it is pervasive, while key audit matters and emphasis of matter add information without modifying the opinion.

  5. Who can demand an audit of an exempt small company? (Exam style)

  6. Who should make up the audit committee of a listed company? (Exam style)

  7. Why does separating ownership from management increase demand for audit? (Stretch)

Key takeaways

  • An external audit is an independent examination giving reasonable, not absolute, assurance that financial statements give a true and fair view, reducing the agency problem between directors and shareholders.
  • UK audit is underpinned by the Companies Act 2006, with standards set and quality monitored by the FRC, auditors registered through recognised supervisory bodies, and audits carried out under ISAs (UK) and the Ethical Standard.
  • Corporate governance directs and controls companies; the UK Code applies on a comply-or-explain basis and emphasises independent boards, separated roles and an independent audit committee overseeing reporting and the auditor.
  • Internal audit gives independent assurance on risk, control and governance across an organisation and reports to the audit committee, while external audit gives an opinion to shareholders on the financial statements; external auditors can use internal audit's work after evaluating it.
  • Auditors must uphold integrity, objectivity, competence, confidentiality and professional behaviour; they identify self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation threats, apply safeguards, and decline work when threats cannot be reduced.
  • Firms accept clients only after checking independence, competence, integrity and professional clearance; an engagement letter agrees the terms, and planning sets an overall strategy and detailed plan focused on the key risks.

Common mistakes in Audit, Assurance and Ethics

  • Thinking auditors prepare the accounts or guarantee them. Directors prepare the financial statements; the auditor gives an opinion with reasonable assurance.
  • Assuming every company must be audited. Many small private companies are exempt under the Companies Act.
  • Thinking the Code is law. It is applied through the listing rules on a comply-or-explain basis, not as statute.
  • Treating internal auditors as part of finance staff who check bookkeeping. Their remit covers risk and control across the whole organisation.
  • Thinking independence is only about being honest. It also requires being seen to be independent by a reasonable and informed third party.
  • Treating planning as a one-off at the start. The strategy and plan are revised whenever new risks emerge during the audit.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is an audit?
    An external audit gives users independent assurance that financial statements are fairly presented.
  2. The regulation of audit
    Audit in the UK is governed by law, standards and professional bodies.
  3. Corporate governance
    Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled.
  4. Internal audit
    Internal audit is an independent function within an organisation that evaluates risk management, control and governance.
  5. Professional ethics
    Auditors must be, and be seen to be, independent and ethical.
  6. Accepting and planning an engagement
    Before an audit starts, the firm must decide whether to accept the client and then plan the work.
  7. Risk assessment and materiality
    A risk-based audit concentrates effort where misstatement is most likely and most important.
  8. Internal control
    Internal controls help a business run reliably and prevent errors and fraud.
  9. Tests of controls
    When auditors plan to rely on a client's controls, they must test that the controls work.
  10. Audit evidence and substantive procedures
    The auditor's opinion rests on sufficient appropriate evidence.
  11. Audit sampling and data analytics
    Auditors rarely test every item, so they sample, and increasingly they analyse whole populations with data analytics.
  12. Auditing inventory, receivables and cash
    Some balances need specific procedures.
  13. Completion and review
    At the end of an audit, the auditor draws the evidence together before signing.
  14. The auditor's report
    The auditor's report communicates the opinion to shareholders.
  15. Fraud and error
    Misstatements can arise from honest error or deliberate fraud.
  16. The audit expectation gap
    The public often expects more from auditors than they deliver or are required to deliver.
  17. Audit failures and reform
    High-profile corporate collapses have repeatedly led to audit reform.

Audit, Assurance and Ethics: questions students ask

Why can an audit not give absolute assurance?

It relies on sampling, judgement and evidence that is persuasive rather than conclusive.

Who appoints the statutory auditor?

The shareholders, usually at the annual general meeting.

What does "comply or explain" mean?

Companies follow the Code's provisions or explain why they have not.

What does a value-for-money audit examine?

Economy, efficiency and effectiveness of spending.
