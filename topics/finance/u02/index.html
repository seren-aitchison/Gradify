Key terms and formulas at a glance

Capital expenditure
Spending on acquiring or improving non-current assets that benefit more than one period
Accrual
An expense incurred but not yet paid at the year end, shown as a current liability
Depreciable amount
The cost of an asset less its residual value
Disposal account
A temporary ledger account that brings together cost, accumulated depreciation and proceeds of an asset disposed of
Irrecoverable debt
A receivable that will not be collected and is written off as an expense
Net realisable value
Estimated selling price less estimated costs of completion and of making the sale
Extended trial balance
A worksheet that adjusts the trial balance and sorts each figure into the statement of profit or loss or of financial position
Gross profit
Revenue less cost of sales: the profit from trading before overheads
Net current assets
Current assets less current liabilities; also called working capital
Statement of affairs
A list of assets and liabilities at a date, used to calculate capital when full records are not kept
Overtrading
Expanding sales faster than the working capital available to finance them, leading to cash shortages
Partnership agreement
A contract, written or oral, setting out the partners' rights and duties, including how profits are shared

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define capital expenditure.

Spending on acquiring or improving non-current assets whose benefits last more than one accounting period. From “Capital and revenue expenditure” →

In plain English: Spending that brings benefits over several years is capital expenditure and goes to the statement of financial position; spending used up in the year is revenue expenditure and goes to profit. Key idea: Capital expenditure creates or improves an asset with benefits beyond the year and is depreciated; revenue expenditure maintains the business and is an expense now.

How is the profit or loss on disposal calculated?

Proceeds minus the carrying amount (cost less accumulated depreciation) at the date of disposal. From “Disposal of non-current assets” →

In plain English: When a non-current asset is sold or scrapped, its cost and accumulated depreciation are removed and any profit or loss on disposal is calculated. Key idea: Disposal removes cost and accumulated depreciation; comparing proceeds with the carrying amount gives a profit or loss that corrects past depreciation.

What are the four pairs of columns in an extended trial balance?

Ledger balances, adjustments, statement of profit or loss, statement of financial position. From “The extended trial balance” →

In plain English: The extended trial balance is a worksheet that takes the trial balance, adds year-end adjustments and sorts every figure into the statement of profit or loss or the statement of financial position. Key idea: The extended trial balance carries each balance, adjusted, to one of the two statements; the profit figure is the balancing item that makes both pairs of columns agree.

How is profit found from statements of affairs?

Closing capital − opening capital + drawings − capital introduced. From “Incomplete records” →

In plain English: Many small businesses don't keep full double-entry records. Key idea: With incomplete records, profit comes from the change in net assets adjusted for drawings and capital introduced; control accounts, the cash book and mark-up or margin fill in missing figures.

Name the items in a partnership appropriation account.

Profit for the year, interest on drawings (added), salaries and interest on capital (deducted), and residual profit shared in the profit-sharing ratio. From “The appropriation account” →

In plain English: A partnership's profit is shared out in an appropriation account: interest on drawings is added, salaries and interest on capital are deducted, and the residue is split in the profit-sharing ratio. Key idea: Profit for the year plus interest on drawings, less salaries and interest on capital, leaves a residue shared in the profit-sharing ratio.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Preparing Financial Statements

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is revenue expenditure for a haulage firm? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Fuel for its lorries. Fuel is consumed in day-to-day operations; the others create or improve non-current assets.

  2. Electricity used in the last month of the year but not yet billed is an example of: (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An accrual. The benefit has been received but not paid for, so it is an accrued expense, a current liability.

  3. Which asset is normally not depreciated? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Freehold land. Land usually has an unlimited useful life, so it isn't depreciated; buildings on it are.

  4. A café replaces its worn floor tiles with identical new tiles for £4,000. How should this be treated? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Revenue expenditure: it restores the original condition. Like-for-like replacement maintains the asset's condition; it doesn't improve it, so it is a repair.

  5. A landlord receives £9,000 rent in the year, including £1,500 paid in advance for next year, and £750 for this year is still owed by a tenant. What is rent income for the year? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    £8,250. 9,000 − 1,500 deferred + 750 accrued = £8,250.

  6. Which method best suits a mining machine whose wear depends on tonnes extracted? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Units of production. Units of production links the charge to use, matching the consumption of the machine's benefits.

  7. A company capitalises £50,000 of routine repairs as an improvement to its building. What is the effect this year? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Profit and non-current assets are both overstated, by up to £50,000 less any depreciation charged. Moving an expense into assets raises profit and assets this year; the cost then leaks into later years through depreciation.

  8. A business fails to record a closing accrual of £2,000. What is the effect on this year's statements? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Profit is overstated and liabilities understated by £2,000. The expense is missing from this year, so profit is too high, and the obligation is missing from liabilities.

  9. A company extends the estimated useful life of its fleet from 4 to 6 years. What is the main effect, and why might users be cautious? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Annual depreciation falls and profit rises, without any change in cash or operations. A longer life spreads the cost over more years, raising current profit; users should ask whether the new estimate reflects reality or flatters results.

Key takeaways

  • Capital expenditure creates or improves an asset with benefits beyond the year and is depreciated; revenue expenditure maintains the business and is an expense now.
  • Accruals increase expenses (liability); prepayments decrease expenses (asset); accrued income increases income (asset); deferred income decreases income (liability).
  • Depreciation allocates cost less residual value over useful life using the method that best reflects how the asset's benefits are consumed; it is an expense, not a cash flow or a valuation.
  • Disposal removes cost and accumulated depreciation; comparing proceeds with the carrying amount gives a profit or loss that corrects past depreciation.
  • Irrecoverable debts are written off against the customer; the allowance estimates further losses, and only its change each year affects profit.
  • Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and NRV, item by item; FIFO and weighted average are allowed, LIFO is not.

Common mistakes in Preparing Financial Statements

  • Capitalising repairs because they are large. Size doesn't decide the treatment: a costly repair that restores the original condition is still revenue expenditure.
  • Mixing up the direction. Ask "has the business had the benefit yet?" An accrual is a benefit received but not paid; a prepayment is a payment made for a benefit still to come.
  • Thinking depreciation provides money to replace the asset. It is a book entry: no cash moves when it is charged.
  • Recording the new asset at the cash paid in a part-exchange. The new asset is recorded at its full cost; the allowance is the proceeds of the old asset.
  • Charging the whole allowance to profit every year. Only the increase or decrease since last year is recognised; the balance carries forward.
  • Including selling costs in inventory cost. Delivery to customers and sales commission are deducted in arriving at NRV, but are never part of cost.

Preparing Financial Statements mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Capital and revenue expenditure
    Spending that brings benefits over several years is capital expenditure and goes to the statement of financial position; spending used up in the year is revenue expenditure and goes to profit.
  2. Accruals and prepayments
    The accruals concept matches income and expenses to the period they belong to, not to when cash moves.
  3. Depreciation
    Depreciation spreads the cost of a non-current asset over the years it is used.
  4. Disposal of non-current assets
    When a non-current asset is sold or scrapped, its cost and accumulated depreciation are removed and any profit or loss on disposal is calculated.
  5. Irrecoverable debts and allowances for receivables
    Some customers never pay.
  6. Inventory valuation
    Closing inventory directly changes cost of sales and profit.
  7. The extended trial balance
    The extended trial balance is a worksheet that takes the trial balance, adds year-end adjustments and sorts every figure into the statement of profit or loss or the statement of financial position.
  8. The statement of profit or loss
    The statement of profit or loss shows a business's income and expenses for a period and the profit that results.
  9. The statement of financial position
    The statement of financial position shows what a business owns and owes, and the owner's capital, at one date.
  10. Incomplete records
    Many small businesses don't keep full double-entry records.
  11. Profit and cash
    A profitable business can run out of cash, and a loss-making one can have plenty.
  12. Partnership agreements and the Partnership Act 1890
    A partnership's accounts follow its partnership agreement, and where there is none, the default rules of the Partnership Act 1890.
  13. The appropriation account
    A partnership's profit is shared out in an appropriation account: interest on drawings is added, salaries and interest on capital are deducted, and the residue is split in the profit-sharing ratio.
  14. Partners' capital and current accounts
    Partners usually keep fixed capital accounts for the money they have invested and current accounts for their shares of profit and their drawings.
  15. Goodwill and changes in partners
    When a partner joins or leaves, or the profit-sharing ratio changes, the existing partners must be credited with the value they have built up.
