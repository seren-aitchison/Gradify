Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is maturity transformation?

Borrowing short term, such as through deposits, and lending long term, such as through mortgages. From “The financial system” →

In plain English: The financial system moves money from those who save to those who want to borrow and invest. Key idea: The financial system channels funds, transforms maturity, pools risk, provides payments and discovers prices, through direct and indirect finance in primary and secondary markets, overseen by the Bank of England and the FCA.

When does a bond trade above par?

When its coupon rate is higher than the market's required yield. From “Bonds and bond valuation” →

In plain English: Bonds are a major way for governments and firms to borrow. Key idea: A bond's price is the present value of its coupons and face value at the required yield; prices fall when yields rise, longer bonds are more sensitive, and riskier issuers pay credit spreads.

What does standard deviation measure in investment?

How widely returns vary around the expected return. From “Risk and return” →

In plain English: Investors expect higher returns for taking more risk. Key idea: Return is measured by holding period and expected returns, risk by standard deviation; risk-averse investors demand a risk premium over the risk-free rate, and historically shares have outperformed bonds with more volatility.

What does weak-form efficiency imply about technical analysis?

Analysing past prices cannot consistently earn excess returns. From “Market efficiency” →

In plain English: If markets are efficient, prices already reflect available information and it is hard to beat the market. Key idea: The EMH says prices reflect information in weak, semi-strong or strong forms; evidence supports the first two in major markets, but insider profits and anomalies show markets are not perfectly efficient.

What is the minimum Common Equity Tier 1 ratio under Basel III?

4.5% of risk-weighted assets, before buffers. From “Financial regulation” →

In plain English: Financial regulation aims to keep banks safe, markets fair and consumers protected. Key idea: Finance is regulated because of systemic risk, information asymmetry and moral hazard; the UK's PRA, FCA and FPC oversee safety, conduct and system risks, and Basel III sets capital, leverage and liquidity standards.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Finance and Financial Markets

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A company sells new shares to investors for the first time. This takes place in (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The primary market. Primary markets issue new securities and raise funds.

  2. How does a Treasury bill give the investor a return? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It is bought at a discount and repaid at face value. The gain is the gap between price paid and face value.

  3. Which bond is investment grade? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    One rated BBB. Ratings of BBB− and above are investment grade.

  4. Why do financial intermediaries exist? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They reduce transaction costs, pool risk and overcome information asymmetry. Intermediaries make it cheaper and safer to match savers and borrowers.

  5. According to the Bank of England, what happens when a commercial bank makes a new loan? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A matching new deposit is created. Lending creates new money in the form of deposits.

  6. Market interest rates fall. What happens to the price of an existing fixed-coupon bond? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It rises. Lower yields increase the present value of fixed cash flows.

  7. Why does an active secondary market help companies raise funds in the primary market? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Investors pay more for securities they know they can sell easily. Liquidity makes securities more attractive, lowering the cost of capital.

  8. Why are banks vulnerable to runs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They hold long-term illiquid loans funded by deposits repayable on demand. Maturity transformation leaves banks unable to repay all depositors at once.

  9. Which bond's price will change most if yields rise by 1%? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A 30-year bond with a low coupon. Long maturity and low coupon mean high duration and sensitivity.

Key takeaways

  • The financial system channels funds, transforms maturity, pools risk, provides payments and discovers prices, through direct and indirect finance in primary and secondary markets, overseen by the Bank of England and the FCA.
  • Banks profit from the interest margin while managing credit, liquidity and interest rate risk; their lending creates deposits, and money markets provide short-term funding through Treasury bills, commercial paper and repos, priced against SONIA.
  • A bond's price is the present value of its coupons and face value at the required yield; prices fall when yields rise, longer bonds are more sensitive, and riskier issuers pay credit spreads.
  • Ordinary shares give votes and dividends with residual risk; they can be valued with the dividend growth model or P/E ratios, and investor ratios such as dividend yield and cover measure income and its safety.
  • Forwards, futures, options and swaps derive value from underlying assets; they let firms hedge risks but also allow leveraged speculation, with counterparty and complexity risks.
  • Return is measured by holding period and expected returns, risk by standard deviation; risk-averse investors demand a risk premium over the risk-free rate, and historically shares have outperformed bonds with more volatility.

Common mistakes in Finance and Financial Markets

  • Thinking secondary markets raise money for companies. Only primary issues raise new funds; secondary trading gives investors liquidity.
  • Believing banks simply lend out existing deposits. In the modern system, new loans create new deposits.
  • Thinking a rise in interest rates increases the price of existing bonds. Prices and yields move inversely.
  • Treating a high P/E ratio as proof a share is overpriced. It may reflect justified expectations of growth.
  • Thinking an option buyer must exercise the option. The buyer has the right, not the obligation, and can let it lapse.
  • Thinking higher risk guarantees a higher return. It means a higher expected return with a wider range of outcomes.

Lessons in this school

  1. The financial system
    The financial system moves money from those who save to those who want to borrow and invest.
  2. Banks and money markets
    Banks are at the centre of the financial system and depend on short-term money markets.
  3. Bonds and bond valuation
    Bonds are a major way for governments and firms to borrow.
  4. Equities and stock markets
    Shares give investors ownership of companies and a claim on their profits.
  5. Derivatives
    Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset.
  6. Risk and return
    Investors expect higher returns for taking more risk.
  7. Portfolio theory and diversification
    Combining investments can reduce risk without reducing expected return.
  8. The capital asset pricing model
    The capital asset pricing model links an investment's required return to its market risk.
  9. Market efficiency
    If markets are efficient, prices already reflect available information and it is hard to beat the market.
  10. Behavioural finance
    Behavioural finance studies how psychology affects investors and markets.
  11. Option pricing
    Options have value because they give rights without obligations.
  12. Financial regulation
    Financial regulation aims to keep banks safe, markets fair and consumers protected.
  13. Financial crises
    Financial crises recur throughout history and can cause deep recessions.
  14. Fintech and digital finance
    Technology is reshaping financial markets and services.

Finance and Financial Markets: questions students ask

What is liquidity risk for a bank?

The risk that depositors withdraw funds faster than the bank can turn its assets into cash.

What is a gilt?

A bond issued by the UK government.

What is market capitalisation?

Share price multiplied by the number of shares in issue.

What is the difference between a forward and a future?

A forward is a private, tailored agreement; a future is standardised and traded on an exchange with a clearing house.
