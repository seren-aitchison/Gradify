Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define accounting.

The identification, measurement, recording and communication of financial information about an entity so that users can make decisions. From “What is accounting?” →

In plain English: Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording and communicating financial information about an organisation so that people can make decisions. Key idea: Accounting turns transactions into information for decisions; bookkeeping is the recording stage within it.

What is unlimited liability?

The owner is personally liable for all the business's debts, so creditors can claim personal assets. From “Types of business organisation” →

In plain English: How a business is organised decides who owns it, who is liable for its debts and what accounts it must keep. Key idea: Incorporation creates a separate legal person whose owners have limited liability, in exchange for more regulation and public disclosure.

Give three examples of current assets.

Inventory, trade receivables, bank and cash (also prepayments). From “Assets, liabilities and capital” →

In plain English: The statement of financial position groups items into current and non-current assets, current and non-current liabilities, and capital. Key idea: Assets and liabilities are classified by purpose and timing; the 12-month line separates current from non-current, and equity is what is left over.

Where is the balance c/d entered?

On the side with the smaller total, so that both sides agree. From “Balancing off accounts” →

In plain English: At the end of a period each account is balanced off to find what it now holds: a debit or credit balance carried down to start the next period. Key idea: A balance is the difference between the two sides; it is carried down on the smaller side and brought down on the larger side to open the next period.

What is the difference between zero-rated and exempt supplies?

Zero-rated supplies are taxable at 0%, so related input VAT is reclaimable; exempt supplies are outside VAT and input VAT on them can't be reclaimed. From “Sales tax in the accounts” →

In plain English: VAT-registered businesses charge VAT on sales and reclaim VAT on purchases, paying the difference to HMRC. Key idea: Sales and purchases are recorded net of VAT; the VAT goes to a VAT account, whose balance is the amount owed to or by HMRC.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Accounting and the Double-Entry System

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which activity is bookkeeping rather than the wider work of accounting? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Entering each day's sales invoices in the sales day book. Bookkeeping is the recording stage: entering transactions accurately. Interpreting, reporting and advising are the accountant's wider work.

  2. Which user is an internal user of accounting information? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The managing director. Managers and directors work inside the organisation and can obtain any internal report; the others are external users.

  3. Which report is a product of management accounting? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A monthly budget-versus-actual report for each department. Departmental budget reports are internal, frequent and detailed, the hallmarks of management accounting.

  4. A shop owner signs an agreement on 30 June to buy a new till, which will be delivered and invoiced on 5 July. When is the purchase first recorded? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    5 July, when the till is delivered and invoiced. An agreement to buy in the future isn't yet a transaction: nothing has been exchanged. The purchase is recorded when the till is received and the obligation to pay arises.

  5. An employee representative is negotiating pay. Which figure in the accounts is most relevant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Profit and cash generated, showing whether a rise is affordable. Employees want to know whether the business can afford higher pay and stay secure, so profitability and cash are the key figures.

  6. A hotel manager wants to decide whether to open the restaurant on Mondays. Which information is most relevant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Expected extra revenue and extra costs of Monday opening. The decision needs forward-looking, specific information about the change, which management accounting provides.

  7. Why do accounting failures such as Carillion's collapse matter beyond the company's own shareholders? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lenders, suppliers, employees and pension schemes all rely on the accounts when dealing with the company. Many stakeholders make decisions using published accounts; when the accounts mislead, losses spread to creditors, staff, pensioners and the public.

  8. A company proposes paying a large dividend that would leave little cash. Whose interests are most likely to conflict with shareholders' here? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lenders, who want cash retained to protect repayment. Paying out cash raises the risk that lenders won't be repaid in full; loan agreements often restrict dividends (covenants) for this reason.

  9. Which is the strongest argument that financial accounting still matters to managers? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its regulated, audited records are the reliable base from which management information is built. Management analysis depends on accurate underlying records; financial accounting's rules and audit provide that reliability, even though it is too summarised for many decisions.

Key takeaways

  • Accounting turns transactions into information for decisions; bookkeeping is the recording stage within it.
  • Each user asks a different question of the same accounts; general purpose reports are aimed mainly at investors, lenders and other creditors.
  • Financial accounting reports the whole entity's past performance to outsiders under rules; management accounting serves managers' decisions with flexible, detailed and forward-looking information.
  • Incorporation creates a separate legal person whose owners have limited liability, in exchange for more regulation and public disclosure.
  • Concepts such as going concern, accruals, entity, consistency, prudence and materiality decide how transactions are measured and reported; they sometimes pull in different directions and require judgement.
  • Assets = Capital + Liabilities holds after every transaction, because each one has two equal effects; profit increases capital and drawings reduce it.

Common mistakes in Accounting and the Double-Entry System

  • Thinking accounts capture everything of value. Only events that can be measured reliably in money are recorded, so a firm's reputation, staff skill and customer loyalty usually appear nowhere in its accounts.
  • Assuming managers need published accounts. Managers are internal users who get detailed, frequent management information; the annual published accounts are mainly for outsiders.
  • Treating the two as separate record-keeping systems. Both usually draw on the same underlying transaction records; they differ in how the data is analysed and presented.
  • Thinking limited liability means the company's debts are limited. The company is liable for all its debts; it is the shareholders' liability that is limited to what they agreed to pay for their shares.
  • Treating prudence as permission to understate profit deliberately. The 2018 Conceptual Framework describes prudence as caution that supports neutrality; building hidden reserves by overstating liabilities is as misleading as overstating assets.
  • Treating profit as cash. A profitable sale raises capital and assets, but if it is on credit the asset that rises is a receivable, not the bank balance.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is accounting?
    Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording and communicating financial information about an organisation so that people can make decisions.
  2. Users of accounting information
    Different people use accounts for different decisions.
  3. Financial and management accounting
    Accounting splits into two branches.
  4. Types of business organisation
    How a business is organised decides who owns it, who is liable for its debts and what accounts it must keep.
  5. Accounting concepts and conventions
    Accounts rest on a small set of basic assumptions and principles.
  6. The accounting equation
    Everything in double entry follows from one identity: assets equal capital plus liabilities.
  7. Assets, liabilities and capital
    The statement of financial position groups items into current and non-current assets, current and non-current liabilities, and capital.
  8. Income, expenses and drawings
    Income increases capital and expenses reduce it; drawings reduce capital too but are not an expense.
  9. The principles of double entry
    Double entry records every transaction twice, as a debit in one account and an equal credit in another.
  10. Recording cash and credit transactions
    Most business transactions are purchases, sales, returns, payments and receipts, made either for cash or on credit.
  11. Balancing off accounts
    At the end of a period each account is balanced off to find what it now holds: a debit or credit balance carried down to start the next period.
  12. The trial balance
    A trial balance lists every ledger balance to check that total debits equal total credits.
  13. Books of prime entry
    Transactions are first listed in books of prime entry, from source documents, before being posted to the ledger in totals.
  14. Sales tax in the accounts
    VAT-registered businesses charge VAT on sales and reclaim VAT on purchases, paying the difference to HMRC.
  15. Control accounts
    A control account summarises all the individual customer or supplier accounts in one total account.
  16. Bank reconciliations
    The cash book and the bank statement rarely agree on a given day.
  17. Errors and the suspense account
    When a trial balance doesn't agree, the difference goes temporarily to a suspense account until the errors are found.

Accounting and the Double-Entry System: questions students ask

How does bookkeeping differ from accounting?

Bookkeeping is recording transactions; accounting also classifies, summarises, reports and interprets them.

Who are the primary users of general purpose financial reports under the Conceptual Framework?

Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors.

Is management accounting regulated by law?

No. Its format and content are whatever helps managers; only financial statements must follow law and standards.

What is the business entity concept?

The business is treated as separate from its owner, so only business transactions are recorded in its accounts.
