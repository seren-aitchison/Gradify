Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a sunk cost?

A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered, and so is irrelevant to decisions. From “Relevant costing” →

In plain English: Good decisions depend on the costs and revenues that change as a result of them. Key idea: Relevant costs are future incremental cash flows caused by a decision; sunk, committed and notional costs are irrelevant, while opportunity costs and replacement costs are relevant.

When should a product line be closed?

When the fixed costs saved exceed the contribution lost. From “Make-or-buy and other short-term decisions” →

In plain English: Many decisions compare the relevant costs of alternatives. Key idea: Make-or-buy compares relevant making costs, including opportunity costs, with the purchase price; segments should close only if lost contribution is below fixed costs saved; further processing is worthwhile if extra revenue exceeds extra cost.

How is the production budget calculated?

Sales plus closing finished goods inventory minus opening finished goods inventory. From “Functional budgets” →

In plain English: Functional budgets translate the sales forecast into plans for production, materials and labour. Key idea: The sales budget drives the production budget, adjusted for finished goods inventory; production drives materials usage and, with materials inventory, purchases; production also drives the labour budget.

What level of budget difficulty tends to motivate best?

Challenging but achievable. From “Behavioural aspects of budgeting” →

In plain English: Budgets affect how people behave as well as recording plans. Key idea: Challenging but accepted targets motivate best; budgets can cause slack, year-end spending, short-termism and gaming; participation, fair evaluation, broader measures and culture reduce these effects.

What does an operating statement reconcile?

Budgeted profit with actual profit. From “The operating statement” →

In plain English: The operating statement reconciles budgeted profit with actual profit using variances. Key idea: The operating statement reconciles budgeted to actual profit through sales and cost variances; it should agree with actual results and helps managers focus on the most significant causes.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Management Accounting: Planning, Control and Decisions

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which cost is relevant to a decision? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A future cost that changes because of the decision. Only future incremental cash flows are relevant.

  2. At the break-even point, what is profit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Zero. Applying the standard result: Total revenue equals total costs.

  3. On a profit–volume chart, where does the profit line start at zero sales? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    At minus the fixed costs. With no sales, the loss equals fixed costs.

  4. Material in stock cost £5 a kg; it is regularly used and now costs £7 to replace. What is its relevant cost? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    £7 per kg. Using it means buying more at the replacement cost.

  5. Fixed costs are £45,000, price £25 and variable cost £10. What is the break-even point? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    3,000 units. Applying the standard result: 45,000 ÷ 15 = 3,000 units.

  6. Budgeted sales are 10,000 units and break-even is 8,000 units. What is the margin of safety as a percentage? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    20%. Applying the standard result: 2,000 ÷ 10,000 = 20%.

  7. A special order's price exceeds relevant cost, but accepting it would cause regular customers to demand the same low price. What should managers do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Weigh the long-term impact on regular prices alongside the short-term contribution. Qualitative and longer-term effects can outweigh one-off gains.

  8. Fixed costs rise by £10,000 while contribution per unit stays at £20. What happens to break-even? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It rises by 500 units. 10,000 ÷ 20 = 500 extra units are needed.

  9. An airline has high fixed costs. What happens to its profits when passenger numbers fall below break-even? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Losses grow rapidly, because most costs do not fall. High operating gearing amplifies the effect of sales changes.

Key takeaways

  • Relevant costs are future incremental cash flows caused by a decision; sunk, committed and notional costs are irrelevant, while opportunity costs and replacement costs are relevant.
  • CVP analysis uses contribution to find break-even points, break-even revenue and sales needed for target profits, under assumptions of linear costs and revenues within a relevant range.
  • Break-even, contribution and P/V charts show CVP visually; the margin of safety measures how far sales can fall before losses; high operating gearing makes profits more sensitive to sales.
  • When a resource is scarce, products are ranked by contribution per unit of the limiting factor and the resource is allocated in that order to maximise total contribution.
  • Make-or-buy compares relevant making costs, including opportunity costs, with the purchase price; segments should close only if lost contribution is below fixed costs saved; further processing is worthwhile if extra revenue exceeds extra cost.
  • Cost-plus pricing adds a mark-up to full or marginal cost; economic theory sets price where marginal revenue equals marginal cost; practical pricing combines costs, demand, competition and strategy.

Common mistakes in Management Accounting: Planning, Control and Decisions

  • Including absorbed fixed overheads in a one-off decision. If total fixed costs do not change, they are irrelevant.
  • Using CVP far outside the relevant range. Prices, variable costs and fixed costs may change at very different volumes.
  • Treating a large margin of safety as guaranteeing profit. Budgeted sales may be optimistic, and costs may change.
  • Ranking by contribution per unit. A product with high unit contribution may use so much of the scarce resource that it earns less per hour.
  • Closing a product line because it shows a loss after allocated fixed costs. If those costs continue, closing may reduce total profit.
  • Setting prices by cost alone. Customers judge value, not the supplier's costs.

Lessons in this school

  1. Relevant costing
    Good decisions depend on the costs and revenues that change as a result of them.
  2. Cost–volume–profit analysis
    Cost–volume–profit (CVP) analysis shows how profit changes with sales volume.
  3. Break-even charts and the margin of safety
    Break-even charts show CVP relationships visually, and the margin of safety measures how far sales can fall before losses occur.
  4. Limiting factor analysis
    When a resource is scarce, a business must decide how to use it to maximise profit.
  5. Make-or-buy and other short-term decisions
    Many decisions compare the relevant costs of alternatives.
  6. Pricing decisions
    Setting prices combines cost information with understanding of demand.
  7. Decisions under risk and uncertainty
    Most business decisions involve uncertain outcomes.
  8. Budgeting: purposes and process
    A budget is a quantified plan for a future period.
  9. Functional budgets
    Functional budgets translate the sales forecast into plans for production, materials and labour.
  10. Cash budgets
    A cash budget forecasts cash receipts and payments to ensure an organisation can pay its bills.
  11. Fixed and flexible budgets
    A fixed budget is set for one level of activity; a flexible budget adjusts for actual activity.
  12. Approaches to budgeting
    Organisations prepare budgets in different ways, each with strengths and weaknesses.
  13. Behavioural aspects of budgeting
    Budgets affect how people behave as well as recording plans.
  14. Standard costing
    Standard costing sets predetermined costs for each unit, providing a benchmark for control.
  15. Material and labour variances
    Variance analysis splits the difference between standard and actual cost into causes.
  16. Overhead and sales variances
    Overhead and sales variances complete the picture of performance against standard.
  17. The operating statement
    The operating statement reconciles budgeted profit with actual profit using variances.
  18. Financial performance indicators
    Financial performance indicators measure how well an organisation or division is doing.
  19. The balanced scorecard
    The balanced scorecard measures performance from financial and non-financial perspectives linked to strategy.
  20. Divisional performance and transfer pricing
    Large organisations are divided into divisions whose performance must be measured fairly.

Fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.

What is the margin of safety?

The amount by which budgeted or actual sales exceed break-even sales.

What is a limiting factor?

A scarce resource that restricts the volume of production or sales.

At what output is profit maximised?

Where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.
