Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the leading preventable cause of death in England?

Smoking. From “Why health behaviours matter” →

In plain English: Everyday behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and activity have a huge effect on health. Key idea: Smoking, poor diet, inactivity and alcohol are the main behavioural risks; combined healthy behaviours were linked with a risk of death equivalent to being 14 years younger; behaviours cluster with deprivation, so individual and population approaches are both needed.

What are the five main stages of the transtheoretical model?

Precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance. From “The transtheoretical model” →

In plain English: The transtheoretical model, or stages of change model, describes behaviour change as a process through stages. Key idea: The transtheoretical model describes precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance, with frequent relapse; it suggests matching support to readiness, but critics question whether the stages are real and stage-matching works.

Which technique involves recording one's own behaviour?

Self-monitoring. From “Behaviour change techniques” →

In plain English: Behaviour change techniques (BCTs) are the active ingredients of interventions. Key idea: BCTs are the active ingredients of interventions, catalogued in a 93-item taxonomy; goal setting, action planning, self-monitoring, feedback, social support and problem solving are common and effective, especially in combination.

What are the three steps of very brief advice on smoking?

Ask, Advise, Act. From “Making Every Contact Count” →

In plain English: Making Every Contact Count (MECC) encourages health and care staff to use everyday interactions to support healthier behaviour. Key idea: MECC uses everyday contacts for brief, supportive conversations about health behaviours; very brief advice on smoking follows Ask, Advise, Act; staff should seek permission, listen and signpost to services.

What did the 2024 Cochrane review find about vaping and quitting?

High-certainty evidence that nicotine vapes help more people stop smoking than nicotine replacement therapy. From “Vaping” →

In plain English: E-cigarettes, or vapes, are an effective aid to stopping smoking but raise concerns about use by young people. Key idea: Vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking and helps smokers quit more effectively than NRT, but it is not risk-free; youth vaping is a concern, leading to the ban on disposable vapes and age restrictions.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a major behavioural risk factor? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Smoking. Applying the standard result: Smoking is a leading modifiable risk.

  2. Which component of the health belief model concerns how likely someone thinks they are to get ill? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Perceived susceptibility. Susceptibility is perceived personal risk.

  3. Who developed the theory of planned behaviour? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Icek Ajzen. Applying the standard result: Ajzen set out the theory in 1991.

  4. What does clustering of health behaviours mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Unhealthy behaviours tend to occur together in the same people. Smoking, drinking and inactivity often co-occur.

  5. A woman delays a cervical screening test because she finds it embarrassing. Which component is involved? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Perceived barriers. Applying the standard result: Embarrassment is a barrier to action.

  6. A teenager drinks less because his friends disapprove of heavy drinking. Which component is at work? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Subjective norms. Perceived approval of others shapes intention.

  7. Why are population-wide policies such as tobacco taxes important alongside individual support? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They change the environment for everyone and can reduce inequalities. Price and availability shape behaviour across groups.

  8. Why might a campaign that only stresses how deadly a disease is fail to increase screening? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It raises threat without reducing barriers or building confidence to act. Action also needs benefits, low barriers and self-efficacy.

  9. Many people intend to eat more vegetables but do not. What would help most, according to research? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Forming specific if–then plans, such as adding vegetables to every evening meal. Implementation intentions translate intentions into action.

Key takeaways

  • Smoking, poor diet, inactivity and alcohol are the main behavioural risks; combined healthy behaviours were linked with a risk of death equivalent to being 14 years younger; behaviours cluster with deprivation, so individual and population approaches are both needed.
  • The health belief model links behaviour to perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy; it helps design health messages but underplays social and emotional influences.
  • The theory of planned behaviour predicts intentions from attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control; intentions predict behaviour only partly, and implementation intentions help close the intention–behaviour gap.
  • The transtheoretical model describes precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance, with frequent relapse; it suggests matching support to readiness, but critics question whether the stages are real and stage-matching works.
  • COM-B explains behaviour through capability, opportunity and motivation; the behaviour change wheel maps these to nine intervention functions and seven policy categories, guiding systematic intervention design.
  • Habits are automatic behaviours cued by context and formed through repetition, typically taking around two months but varying widely; building habits links simple actions to stable cues, while breaking them involves avoiding or changing cues.

Common mistakes in Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change

  • Blaming individuals alone for unhealthy behaviour. Environments, income and stress strongly shape what people do.
  • Assuming that increasing fear always changes behaviour. Without lowering barriers and building self-efficacy, threat messages can backfire.
  • Assuming strong intentions guarantee behaviour. Many people intend to act but do not, so planning and support are needed.
  • Refusing to help people who are not "ready". Many people make sudden, unplanned quit attempts that succeed.
  • Jumping to an education campaign without diagnosing the problem. If the barrier is opportunity or habit, information alone will not change behaviour.
  • Expecting a new habit to form in 21 days. Evidence suggests a median of about 66 days, with wide variation.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why health behaviours matter
    Everyday behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and activity have a huge effect on health.
  2. The health belief model
    The health belief model explains health behaviour in terms of people's perceptions of threat and of the benefits and barriers to action.
  3. The theory of planned behaviour
    The theory of planned behaviour explains behaviour through intentions shaped by attitudes, social norms and perceived control.
  4. The transtheoretical model
    The transtheoretical model, or stages of change model, describes behaviour change as a process through stages.
  5. The COM-B model and the behaviour change wheel
    The COM-B model explains behaviour as the product of capability, opportunity and motivation, and the behaviour change wheel links these to intervention types and policies.
  6. Habits
    Many health behaviours are habits, triggered automatically by cues.
  7. Behaviour change techniques
    Behaviour change techniques (BCTs) are the active ingredients of interventions.
  8. Motivational interviewing
    Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change.
  9. Nudges and choice architecture
    Nudges change the way choices are presented to make healthier options easier, without restricting freedom.
  10. Health literacy
    Health literacy is the ability to find, understand and use health information.
  11. Making Every Contact Count
    Making Every Contact Count (MECC) encourages health and care staff to use everyday interactions to support healthier behaviour.
  12. Alcohol
    Alcohol is widely used in the UK but causes substantial harm.
  13. Smoking
    Smoking is the leading preventable cause of illness and death in the UK.
  14. Vaping
    E-cigarettes, or vapes, are an effective aid to stopping smoking but raise concerns about use by young people.
  15. Drugs and harm reduction
    Drug use causes deaths, illness and social harm.
  16. Sexual health
    Sexual health includes preventing sexually transmitted infections, unplanned pregnancy and HIV, and positive, respectful relationships.
  17. Gambling harms
    Gambling harms affect individuals, families and communities, and are increasingly treated as a public health issue.

Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change: questions students ask

What did the EPIC-Norfolk study find about combined health behaviours?

People with four healthy behaviours had a mortality risk equivalent to being 14 years younger than those with none.

What is a cue to action?

A trigger that prompts a health behaviour, such as a reminder letter.

What are the three determinants of intention in the theory of planned behaviour?

Attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.

What does COM-B stand for?

Capability, Opportunity, Motivation and Behaviour.
