Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is folic acid recommended before and in early pregnancy?

To reduce the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida. From “Preconception and pregnancy” →

In plain English: The health of parents before and during pregnancy affects the health of the baby for life. Key idea: Folic acid, stopping smoking and avoiding alcohol before and during pregnancy improve outcomes; antenatal care offers scans, screening and monitoring for risks such as pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes.

What position should babies sleep in to reduce SIDS risk?

On their back. From “Infant health and development” →

In plain English: The first year of life brings rapid growth and development. Key idea: Infants develop motor, language and social skills at typical but variable ages; growth is tracked on centile charts; immunisations start at 8 weeks; weaning starts around 6 months; babies should sleep on their backs.

Which contraceptive method also protects against STIs?

Condoms. From “Sexual and reproductive health” →

In plain English: Sexual and reproductive health covers contraception, sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy choices and healthy relationships. Key idea: LARC methods are the most effective contraception, while condoms also protect against STIs; chlamydia is common and often symptomless, HPV vaccination and HIV treatment and PrEP prevent infections; free confidential services and statutory RSE support sexual health.

Who is offered AAA screening?

Men in the year they turn 65. From “Adult health checks” →

In plain English: Health checks and screening programmes aim to find risks and diseases early in adults. Key idea: The NHS Health Check assesses cardiovascular risk in adults aged 40 to 74 every five years; national screening covers cervical, breast, bowel and AAA; benefits must be weighed against inequalities in uptake, false positives and overdiagnosis.

About what proportion of people over 65 fall each year?

About one in three. From “Frailty and falls” →

In plain English: Frailty and falls are major causes of loss of independence in older people. Key idea: Frailty reduces reserve and increases vulnerability; falls affect about a third of over-65s each year and cause fractures, long lies and loss of confidence; strength and balance training, medication review and home changes prevent falls.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Health Across the Life Course

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When is the anomaly scan usually offered? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    At about 18 to 21 weeks of pregnancy. The mid-pregnancy scan checks the baby's development.

  2. What happens in the third stage of labour? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The placenta is delivered. The third stage ends with delivery of the placenta.

  3. At about what age do most babies first smile socially? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 6 weeks. A social smile is an early social milestone.

  4. Which risk is increased by smoking in pregnancy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Low birth weight. Smoking restricts the baby's oxygen and growth.

  5. What does the newborn physical examination check? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Eyes, heart, hips and testes. It identifies conditions needing early treatment.

  6. What is a sign of concern on a growth chart? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Weight crossing two centile lines downwards. Falling across centiles may signal a problem.

  7. Why are outcomes of pregnancy unequal in the UK? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Deprivation and ethnicity are linked to worse access, conditions and outcomes. Maternal outcomes reflect wider health inequalities.

  8. Why is the heel-prick test described as screening? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It identifies babies at higher risk who then need diagnostic tests. Screening finds those who need further testing.

  9. Why does high immunisation uptake protect babies too young to be vaccinated? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Herd immunity reduces the spread of infection in the community. Fewer infected people means less exposure.

Key takeaways

  • Folic acid, stopping smoking and avoiding alcohol before and during pregnancy improve outcomes; antenatal care offers scans, screening and monitoring for risks such as pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes.
  • Labour has three stages; newborns are assessed with the Apgar score, a physical examination within 72 hours, blood spot and hearing screening; skin-to-skin and breastfeeding support health and bonding.
  • Infants develop motor, language and social skills at typical but variable ages; growth is tracked on centile charts; immunisations start at 8 weeks; weaning starts around 6 months; babies should sleep on their backs.
  • The Healthy Child Programme offers universal reviews by health visitors and school nurses with extra support where needed; priorities include obesity, oral health, mental health and the effects of early adversity.
  • Adolescence brings puberty, changes in sleep and a still-developing prefrontal cortex; it is a key period for the onset of mental health problems and risk behaviours, and young people have rights to confidential care.
  • LARC methods are the most effective contraception, while condoms also protect against STIs; chlamydia is common and often symptomless, HPV vaccination and HIV treatment and PrEP prevent infections; free confidential services and statutory RSE support sexual health.

Common mistakes in Health Across the Life Course

  • Starting folic acid only once pregnancy is confirmed. The neural tube forms very early, so it should start before conception where possible.
  • Assuming the blood spot test diagnoses conditions. It is screening; positive results need confirmatory tests.
  • Treating a single late milestone as a problem. Development varies widely; patterns of delay matter more.
  • Thinking the Healthy Child Programme is only for families with problems. It is universal, with extra support proportionate to need.
  • Assuming teenagers take risks only because they lack information. Brain development and social influences are important.
  • Assuming no symptoms means no infection. Many STIs, especially chlamydia, are symptomless, so testing matters.

Lessons in this school

  1. Preconception and pregnancy
    The health of parents before and during pregnancy affects the health of the baby for life.
  2. Birth and the newborn
    Birth and the first days of life are a critical time for the health of mother and baby.
  3. Infant health and development
    The first year of life brings rapid growth and development.
  4. Child health and the Healthy Child Programme
    The Healthy Child Programme is England's universal public health programme for children and young people from pregnancy to 19.
  5. Adolescent health
    Adolescence brings rapid physical, emotional and social change and is when many health behaviours and mental health problems begin.
  6. Sexual and reproductive health
    Sexual and reproductive health covers contraception, sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy choices and healthy relationships.
  7. Young adults and health
    Young adulthood, roughly 18 to 25, involves major transitions in education, work and relationships that shape lifelong health.
  8. Adult health checks
    Health checks and screening programmes aim to find risks and diseases early in adults.
  9. Menopause
    Menopause is a natural stage of life when periods stop, with effects that can be short- and long-term.
  10. Healthy ageing
    People are living longer, but not always in good health.
  11. Frailty and falls
    Frailty and falls are major causes of loss of independence in older people.
  12. Carers and caring
    Millions of people provide unpaid care to family members or friends.
  13. End of life care
    Good end of life care helps people live as well as possible until they die and to die with dignity.

Health Across the Life Course: questions students ask

What does the Apgar score assess?

Appearance, pulse, grimace, activity and respiration of the newborn.

At about what age do babies develop a pincer grip?

About 9 to 12 months.

In which school years are children measured in the National Child Measurement Programme?

Reception and Year 6.

Which part of the brain continues developing into the mid-twenties?

The prefrontal cortex.
