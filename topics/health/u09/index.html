Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Give the Acheson definition of public health.

The science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society. From “What public health is” →

In plain English: Public health is about the health of whole populations rather than individual patients. Key idea: Public health is the organised effort of society to prevent disease, prolong life and promote health in whole populations.

What is a false positive result?

A positive screening result in a person who does not have the condition. From “Levels of prevention” →

In plain English: Prevention can act before disease starts, catch it early, or reduce its harm once it has developed. Key idea: Primary prevention stops disease starting, secondary prevention finds it early, and tertiary prevention reduces its impact; screening is a form of secondary prevention with benefits and harms.

What does an SMR of 100 mean?

The number of deaths observed is the same as would be expected from the standard rates. From “Rates and standardisation” →

In plain English: Comparing death or disease rates between places is misleading if their populations differ in age. Key idea: Crude rates mislead when populations differ in age; direct and indirect standardisation remove the effect of age, and an SMR above 100 means more deaths than expected.

Name three ways to control confounding.

Randomisation, restriction, matching, stratification or multivariable analysis (any three). From “Bias, confounding and chance” →

In plain English: Before deciding that an association is real, epidemiologists must rule out bias, confounding and chance. Key idea: An association may be explained by bias, confounding or chance; good design, randomisation and statistical analysis reduce each.

What does a DALY combine?

Years of life lost through early death and years lived with disability. From “The global burden of disease” →

In plain English: Measuring the burden of disease shows which conditions cause the most death and disability worldwide. Key idea: DALYs combine years of life lost and years lived with disability; as countries develop, non-communicable diseases replace infections as the main burden, though many face a double burden.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Public Health and Epidemiology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the main focus of public health? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The health of populations. Public health works at the level of whole populations, mainly through prevention.

  2. Who wrote the 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Edwin Chadwick. Chadwick's report linked disease with poor living conditions and led to sanitary reform.

  3. A smoking cessation campaign belongs mainly to which domain? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Health improvement. Helping people stop smoking is health improvement.

  4. Since 2013, who has led most public health work in England at a local level? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Local authorities. The Health and Social Care Act 2012 moved public health responsibilities to local authorities.

  5. Why is John Snow's work seen as a founding moment of epidemiology? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He used patterns of cases to find the source of disease. Snow used data about where cases occurred to identify contaminated water as the cause.

  6. Deciding whether the NHS should fund a new screening programme belongs mainly to which domain? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Healthcare public health. Planning and evaluating services is healthcare public health.

  7. Which question is most typical of a public health approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Why does this town have high rates of lung disease?. Public health asks why disease is common in a population, not just why one person is ill.

  8. What did the Black Report (1980) and Marmot Review (2010) both highlight? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Persistent health inequalities between social groups. Both reports showed that poorer groups have worse health and shorter lives.

  9. Which response to a tuberculosis cluster draws on all three domains? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Contact tracing, better housing and accessible TB clinics. TB needs protection (tracing), improvement (housing and poverty) and services (accessible clinics).

Key takeaways

  • Public health is the organised effort of society to prevent disease, prolong life and promote health in whole populations.
  • Public health began with nineteenth-century sanitary reform and Snow's epidemiology, and has since moved through vaccination, the NHS, action on smoking and a focus on inequalities.
  • Public health has three overlapping domains: health protection, health improvement and improving health services.
  • Primary prevention stops disease starting, secondary prevention finds it early, and tertiary prevention reduces its impact; screening is a form of secondary prevention with benefits and harms.
  • The Ottawa Charter sees health promotion as more than education: it combines healthy public policy, supportive environments, community action, personal skills and reoriented services.
  • Most cases come from the many at moderate risk, so shifting the whole population's risk can prevent more disease than targeting only high-risk individuals.

Common mistakes in Public Health and Epidemiology

  • Thinking public health is only about individual lifestyle choices. Most public health action changes environments, services and policies that shape everyone's choices.
  • Assuming that medical treatment caused most of the fall in deaths in the nineteenth century. Clean water, sanitation, better nutrition and housing played a much larger part.
  • Thinking each domain works separately. Most problems, from measles to obesity, need action across all three.
  • Calling screening a guarantee. Screening reduces risk but misses some cases and can cause harm through false positives and overdiagnosis.
  • Equating health promotion with leaflets and campaigns. Information alone rarely changes behaviour; policies and environments are usually more powerful.
  • Assuming the high-risk approach is always better because it targets those most likely to benefit. It helps them but misses most of the future cases.

Lessons in this school

  1. What public health is
    Public health is about the health of whole populations rather than individual patients.
  2. A short history of public health
    Modern public health grew out of the overcrowded, disease-ridden cities of the industrial revolution.
  3. The three domains of public health
    Public health work is often divided into three domains: health protection, health improvement and improving health services.
  4. Levels of prevention
    Prevention can act before disease starts, catch it early, or reduce its harm once it has developed.
  5. Health promotion and the Ottawa Charter
    Health promotion enables people to increase control over their health and its determinants.
  6. The prevention paradox
    Geoffrey Rose observed that most cases of disease come from the many people at moderate risk, not the few at high risk.
  7. Incidence and prevalence
    Incidence and prevalence are the two basic measures of how common a disease is.
  8. Rates and standardisation
    Comparing death or disease rates between places is misleading if their populations differ in age.
  9. Relative risk and odds ratios
    Epidemiologists measure how strongly an exposure is linked with a disease by comparing groups.
  10. Epidemiological study designs
    Different questions need different study designs.
  11. Bias, confounding and chance
    Before deciding that an association is real, epidemiologists must rule out bias, confounding and chance.
  12. Causation and the Bradford Hill viewpoints
    An association between an exposure and a disease does not prove that one causes the other.
  13. Outbreak investigation
    An outbreak is more cases of a disease than expected in a particular place and time.
  14. Health surveillance
    Public health surveillance is the continuous collection, analysis and use of health data to guide action.
  15. The global burden of disease
    Measuring the burden of disease shows which conditions cause the most death and disability worldwide.
  16. Global health goals and organisations
    Global health is shaped by international goals and by organisations that fund, coordinate and deliver health programmes.
  17. Climate change and health
    The WHO describes climate change as the biggest health threat facing humanity.
  18. Pandemic preparedness
    Pandemics are infrequent but cause enormous harm to health, society and the economy.

Public Health and Epidemiology: questions students ask

Which organisation leads on infectious disease threats in England?

The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA).

What did John Snow show in 1854?

That cholera deaths in Soho clustered around the Broad Street water pump, pointing to contaminated water as the source.

What are the three domains of public health?

Health protection, health improvement and healthcare public health (improving services).

What is secondary prevention?

Detecting disease early, before symptoms, so that treatment is more effective, mainly through screening.
