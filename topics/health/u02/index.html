Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the four basic tissue types.

Epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue. From “Cells, tissues and organs” →

In plain English: The body is built in levels: cells form tissues, tissues form organs and organs work together in systems. Key idea: The body is a hierarchy from cells to systems; most disease begins with a change in cells and spreads its effects upwards to organs, systems and the whole person.

Which cells release insulin, and which release glucagon?

Beta cells of the islets of Langerhans release insulin; alpha cells release glucagon. From “Blood glucose regulation” →

In plain English: Blood glucose must stay within narrow limits because the brain depends on it and high levels damage blood vessels. Key idea: Insulin lowers blood glucose and glucagon raises it; they work in opposition through negative feedback, with the liver as the main store.

What is the heart's natural pacemaker?

The sinoatrial node (SAN) in the right atrium. From “The heart” →

In plain English: The heart is a muscular pump that drives blood through two circulations. Key idea: The heart is two pumps in one, driven by its own pacemaker; cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume) rises to meet the body's demand.

What happens to the diaphragm during inspiration?

It contracts and flattens, increasing the volume of the chest. From “Gas exchange and breathing” →

In plain English: Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; gas exchange moves oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out. Key idea: Air moves in and out because muscles change the chest's volume and therefore its pressure; gases cross the alveoli by diffusion across a huge, thin, moist, well-ventilated surface.

What does ADH do?

It increases the permeability of the collecting ducts to water, so more water is reabsorbed and urine is more concentrated. From “The kidneys and fluid balance” →

In plain English: The kidneys filter the blood, remove wastes and control the body's water and salt balance. Key idea: The nephron filters blood, reabsorbs what the body needs and excretes the rest; ADH adjusts how much water is reabsorbed, keeping blood concentration stable.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Human Body

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which cell structure controls what enters and leaves the cell? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The cell membrane. The phospholipid membrane is selectively permeable; the nucleus holds DNA and ribosomes make proteins.

  2. What is the normal range of blood pH? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 7.35 to 7.45. Blood is kept slightly alkaline; values outside this range (acidosis or alkalosis) are dangerous.

  3. Below what core temperature is a person considered hypothermic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    35 °C. Hypothermia is defined as a core temperature below 35 °C.

  4. Blood is classed as which type of tissue? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Connective. Blood is a connective tissue: cells in a fluid matrix (plasma), like bone and cartilage, which have cells in a solid matrix.

  5. During labour, oxytocin strengthens contractions, which push the baby against the cervix and release more oxytocin. What type of control is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Positive feedback. The response amplifies the original change until the end point, the birth, is reached.

  6. Why is a hot, humid day more dangerous than a hot, dry day of the same temperature? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High humidity reduces evaporation of sweat, the main way to lose heat in hot conditions. When air temperature approaches skin temperature, heat is lost mainly by evaporation, which humidity slows.

  7. Why does damage to the small blood vessels in diabetes cause problems in so many different organs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Every organ depends on small vessels for its supply, so damage at the tissue level affects eyes, kidneys and nerves alike. The endothelium lines vessels throughout the body; when it is damaged, organs with dense small-vessel networks show the effects first.

  8. Why are older adults at greater risk during heatwaves? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their homeostatic responses, such as sweating and thirst, are weaker and they often have conditions or medicines that impair them. Reduced sweating, thirst and cardiovascular reserve, plus medicines such as diuretics, impair heat loss and fluid balance.

  9. Which group is generally at greatest risk of harm during a heatwave? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Isolated older adults with long-term conditions. Older adults have weaker thermoregulation and thirst, often take medicines that affect fluid balance, and may not be checked on.

Key takeaways

  • The body is a hierarchy from cells to systems; most disease begins with a change in cells and spreads its effects upwards to organs, systems and the whole person.
  • Negative feedback reverses a change and keeps a variable close to its set point; positive feedback amplifies a change and is used only for processes that must be completed quickly.
  • The hypothalamus balances heat production and loss through sweating, shivering, and dilation or constriction of skin blood vessels, plus behaviour.
  • Insulin lowers blood glucose and glucagon raises it; they work in opposition through negative feedback, with the liver as the main store.
  • The nervous system uses electrical impulses along neurones and chemical signals across synapses; reflexes are fast because they bypass conscious processing in the brain.
  • Hormones travel in the blood to targets with matching receptors; most endocrine control uses negative feedback, often through the hypothalamus and pituitary.

Common mistakes in The Human Body

  • Thinking each organ belongs to only one system. The pancreas is part of both the digestive and endocrine systems; the kidneys belong to the renal system but also control blood pressure and make a hormone.
  • Thinking that homeostasis keeps variables at one exact value. Variables fluctuate within a normal range around a set point, and some set points change, for example temperature during a fever.
  • Thinking that a fever is a failure of temperature control. In fever the hypothalamus deliberately raises its set point; the temperature is still regulated, just around a higher value.
  • Thinking that insulin breaks glucose down. Insulin is a signal: it causes cells to take up and store glucose; it is not an enzyme.
  • Thinking the brain sends every response. In a spinal reflex, the spinal cord produces the response; the brain is informed afterwards.
  • Thinking hormones affect every cell they reach. A hormone affects only target cells that have receptors for it.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cells, tissues and organs
    The body is built in levels: cells form tissues, tissues form organs and organs work together in systems.
  2. Homeostasis
    Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite changes outside and inside the body.
  3. Body temperature regulation
    The body keeps its core temperature close to 37 °C by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  4. Blood glucose regulation
    Blood glucose must stay within narrow limits because the brain depends on it and high levels damage blood vessels.
  5. The nervous system
    The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates fast responses.
  6. The endocrine system
    The endocrine system controls the body with hormones carried in the blood.
  7. The heart
    The heart is a muscular pump that drives blood through two circulations.
  8. Blood vessels and blood pressure
    Arteries, capillaries and veins carry blood around the body, and blood pressure keeps it moving.
  9. Blood
    Blood transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and wastes, defends against infection and seals wounds.
  10. The respiratory system
    The respiratory system brings air into the lungs so oxygen can enter the blood and carbon dioxide can leave.
  11. Gas exchange and breathing
    Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; gas exchange moves oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out.
  12. The digestive system
    The digestive system breaks food down into molecules small enough to be absorbed into the blood.
  13. The liver
    The liver is the body's chemical processing centre, with hundreds of functions.
  14. The kidneys and fluid balance
    The kidneys filter the blood, remove wastes and control the body's water and salt balance.
  15. The immune system
    The immune system defends the body against pathogens.
  16. The musculoskeletal system
    Bones, joints and muscles support the body, protect organs and make movement possible.
  17. The skin
    The skin is the body's largest organ, forming a barrier between the body and the outside world.

The Human Body: questions students ask

What is the function of mitochondria?

Aerobic respiration, releasing energy in the form of ATP.

What are the three components of a negative feedback system?

A receptor (sensor), a control centre and an effector.

What happens to skin arterioles when the body is cold?

They constrict (vasoconstriction), reducing blood flow near the surface and heat loss.

What do the letters in FAST stand for?

Face, Arms, Speech, Time to call 999.
