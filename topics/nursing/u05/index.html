Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between incidence and prevalence?

Incidence is the number of new cases in a period; prevalence is the number of existing cases at a point or period in time. From “Introduction to disease processes” →

In plain English: Pathophysiology is the study of how normal body functions are disrupted by disease. Key idea: Linking pathophysiology to signs and symptoms helps you recognise deterioration early: you know what to look for and why.

How should a person use GTN for angina?

Sit down, use the spray, repeat after 5 minutes if needed, and call 999 if pain persists 5 minutes after the second dose. From “Ischaemic heart disease” →

In plain English: Ischaemic heart disease (coronary heart disease) occurs when the coronary arteries are narrowed by atherosclerosis, reducing blood supply to the heart muscle. Key idea: Angina that changes pattern (more frequent, at rest, longer) is unstable and needs urgent assessment.

What is the commonest bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia?

Streptococcus pneumoniae. From “Pneumonia” →

In plain English: Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue, causing inflammation and consolidation of the alveoli. Key idea: Good oral care and early mobilisation help prevent hospital-acquired and aspiration pneumonia.

What are the two main forms of inflammatory bowel disease?

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. From “Gastrointestinal disorders” →

In plain English: Gastrointestinal (GI) disorders range from common, mild problems such as reflux and constipation to life-threatening bleeding, obstruction and cancer. Key idea: Use the Bristol Stool Chart for every bowel record: "BNO" or "bowels fine" doesn't tell the next nurse enough.

What are the components of neurovascular observations?

Colour, warmth, movement, sensation, pulses, capillary refill and pain. From “Musculoskeletal disease” →

In plain English: Musculoskeletal (MSK) conditions affect bones, joints, muscles and connective tissue. Key idea: After a fracture or cast, regular neurovascular observations detect compartment syndrome early.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pathophysiology and Clinical Conditions

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which term describes the cause of a disease? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Aetiology. Aetiology means cause; pathogenesis is how the disease develops.

  2. What causes the swelling in acute inflammation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Leaky capillaries letting fluid into tissues. Inflammatory mediators such as histamine make capillaries leaky, so fluid and proteins move into the tissues and cause swelling (oedema).

  3. Which is the most effective single measure to prevent healthcare-associated infection? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Hand hygiene. Hand hygiene breaks the chain at transmission.

  4. Why should nurses understand pathophysiology? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To link signs to causes and spot deterioration. Knowing the mechanism helps nurses assess and act safely.

  5. A patient's surgical wound is red, warm and swollen, with a rising temperature and heart rate. What should the nurse do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Assess, consider infection and sepsis, escalate. Inflammatory signs with systemic changes may signal infection.

  6. An older patient becomes newly confused with a raised respiratory rate, but no fever. What should the nurse consider? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Infection may present without fever: assess, escalate. Atypical presentations are common in older adults.

  7. A patient who had chemotherapy 8 days ago phones the ward feeling shivery with a temperature of 38.2°C. What should the nurse advise? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Attend urgently: possible neutropenic sepsis. Neutropenic sepsis can deteriorate rapidly.

  8. A patient with known angina has chest pain at rest that hasn't settled after two doses of GTN. What should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Possible ACS: get help and record an ECG. Chest pain at rest that persists after GTN suggests unstable angina or myocardial infarction, so it needs an urgent ECG and escalation, not more waiting.

  9. A patient's monitor shows a broad complex tachycardia; they are unresponsive with no pulse. What should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Call the arrest team and start CPR. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia is a cardiac arrest: start CPR, call the arrest team and defibrillate per the advanced life support algorithm.

Key takeaways

  • Linking pathophysiology to signs and symptoms helps you recognise deterioration early: you know what to look for and why.
  • Raised CRP and white cells show inflammation, not necessarily infection. Interpret them with the clinical picture.
  • Hand hygiene is the single most important measure to prevent healthcare-associated infection.
  • A person on chemotherapy with a temperature or feeling unwell must be treated as possible neutropenic sepsis until proven otherwise.
  • Most cardiovascular risk is modifiable: every contact is an opportunity to support healthier choices ("Making Every Contact Count").
  • Hypertension is a "silent" disease: education about why to take medicines when you feel well is a key nursing task.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to disease processes
    Pathophysiology is the study of how normal body functions are disrupted by disease.
  2. Inflammation
    Inflammation is the body's protective response to injury or infection.
  3. Infection
    Infection is the invasion and multiplication of micro-organisms in the body, causing harm.
  4. Immune dysfunction
    Immune dysfunction occurs when the immune system is underactive (immunodeficiency), overactive (hypersensitivity), or misdirected against the body (autoimmunity).
  5. Cardiovascular disease
    Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a group of conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels, mostly caused by atherosclerosis.
  6. Hypertension
    Hypertension is persistently raised arterial blood pressure.
  7. Heart failure
    Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can only do so at high filling pressures.
  8. Ischaemic heart disease
    Ischaemic heart disease (coronary heart disease) occurs when the coronary arteries are narrowed by atherosclerosis, reducing blood supply to the heart muscle.
  9. Myocardial infarction
    A myocardial infarction (MI, heart attack) is death of heart muscle caused by prolonged ischaemia, usually from a blood clot on a ruptured coronary plaque.
  10. Arrhythmias
    An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm.
  11. Stroke
    A stroke is a sudden loss of brain function caused by an interruption of blood supply: either a blocked artery (ischaemic stroke) or bleeding (haemorrhagic stroke).
  12. Respiratory disease
    Respiratory diseases affect the airways, lung tissue, blood vessels or breathing muscles.
  13. Asthma
    Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways causing variable, reversible airflow obstruction, with symptoms of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
  14. COPD
    Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable lung condition with persistent breathlessness and airflow obstruction that is not fully reversible, usually caused by smoking.
  15. Pneumonia
    Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue, causing inflammation and consolidation of the alveoli.
  16. Pulmonary embolism
    A pulmonary embolism (PE) is a blockage of the pulmonary arteries, usually by a blood clot from a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the leg.
  17. Diabetes
    Diabetes mellitus is a group of conditions in which blood glucose is persistently raised because the body produces too little insulin, responds poorly to it, or both.
  18. Thyroid disease
    Thyroid disease is common, especially in women.
  19. Renal disease
    The kidneys filter blood, remove waste, balance fluid and electrolytes, regulate acid-base and blood pressure, make erythropoietin and activate vitamin D.
  20. Acute kidney injury
    Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function over hours to days.
  21. Chronic kidney disease
    Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is long-term reduced kidney function or kidney damage lasting more than 3 months.
  22. Liver disease
    Liver disease is a growing cause of death in the UK, largely preventable and often diagnosed late.
  23. Gastrointestinal disorders
    Gastrointestinal (GI) disorders range from common, mild problems such as reflux and constipation to life-threatening bleeding, obstruction and cancer.
  24. Neurological disorders
    Neurological disorders affect the brain, spinal cord, nerves or muscles.
  25. Epilepsy
    Epilepsy is a condition of recurrent, unprovoked seizures caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain.
  26. Dementia
    Dementia is a syndrome of progressive decline in memory, thinking, behaviour and the ability to perform everyday activities, caused by brain disease.
  27. Parkinson's disease
    Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurological condition caused by loss of dopamine-producing neurones in the brain.
  28. Cancer
    Cancer is uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells that can invade nearby tissue and spread (metastasise).
  29. Haematological disorders
    Haematological disorders affect the blood and blood-forming organs: red cells, white cells, platelets, clotting and the bone marrow.
  30. Musculoskeletal disease
    Musculoskeletal (MSK) conditions affect bones, joints, muscles and connective tissue.
  31. Rheumatological conditions
    Rheumatological conditions are inflammatory and autoimmune diseases of joints, muscles and connective tissue.
  32. Skin conditions
    The skin is the largest organ, and skin conditions are common causes of discomfort, distress and infection.
  33. Sepsis
    Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection in which the body's response damages its own tissues and organs.
  34. Shock
    Shock is a life-threatening state of circulatory failure in which tissues don't receive enough oxygen.
  35. Multimorbidity
    Multimorbidity is the presence of two or more long-term health conditions in one person.
  36. Frailty
    Frailty is a long-term condition in which reduced reserve across many body systems makes a person vulnerable to sudden, dramatic changes in health after minor events such as an infection or a new medicine.
  37. Palliative and end-of-life conditions
    Palliative care improves quality of life for people with life-limiting illness and their families, through relief of pain and other symptoms and attention to psychological, social and spiritual needs.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Conditions: questions students ask

What is inflammation?

The body's protective response to injury or infection, bringing immune cells and fluid to the area.

Why should urinary catheters be reviewed daily?

Because the risk of catheter-associated urinary tract infection rises with every day the catheter remains.

Which type of hypersensitivity causes anaphylaxis?

Type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity.

What happens when an atherosclerotic plaque ruptures?

A blood clot forms on it and can block the artery, causing myocardial infarction or stroke.
