Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

List the five stages of the nursing process.

Assess, identify needs (diagnose), plan, implement, evaluate. From “Introduction to clinical assessment” →

In plain English: Clinical assessment is the systematic gathering and interpretation of information about a person's health to identify needs, problems and risks, and to plan care. Key idea: Assessment is a professional responsibility of the registered nurse (NMC platform 3). Others may collect data, but interpreting it and acting on it needs a registrant's judgement.

List the six physiological parameters in NEWS2.

Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, pulse rate, level of consciousness or new confusion, and temperature (plus a score for supplemental oxygen). From “Vital signs” →

In plain English: Vital signs (observations) are measurements of the body's most basic functions: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse, consciousness level and temperature. Key idea: An observation that isn't acted on is no better than one that isn't taken. Scores trigger responses: know your local escalation policy.

Name two pain tools for people who cannot self-report.

Abbey Pain Scale, PAINAD, FLACC (any two). From “Pain assessment” →

In plain English: Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience. Key idea: Assess, treat, reassess, document. Unrelieved pain is harmful, not just uncomfortable.

What is the approximate minimum hourly urine output in adults?

About 0.5 mL per kg per hour (follow local guidance). From “Hydration assessment” →

In plain English: Hydration assessment identifies dehydration and fluid overload by combining history, observation, examination, fluid balance and test results. Key idea: Fluid balance charts are only useful if they are complete and acted on. An unrecorded drink or catheter emptying makes the chart wrong.

Name four stages of the clinical reasoning cycle.

Consider the situation, collect cues, process information, identify problems, establish goals, take action, evaluate, reflect (any four). From “Clinical reasoning” →

In plain English: Clinical reasoning is the thinking process nurses use to collect and interpret information, identify problems, decide on actions, and evaluate their effect. Key idea: Clinical reasoning is visible in your documentation and handover: what you found, what you think it means, what you did and what happened next.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which comes first in the nursing process? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Assessment. Care must be based on an assessment of needs.

  2. Which question best supports person-centred assessment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "What matters to you most while you're here?". Asking what matters to the person centres care on their goals.

  3. Which is an open question? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "Tell me about the pain.". Open questions invite the person to describe in their own words.

  4. A patient's NEWS2 score is 0, but the nurse feels something is wrong because the patient is unusually quiet. What should the nurse do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Escalate per local policy and reassess sooner. Clinical concern is a valid reason to escalate regardless of the score.

  5. A man with a learning disability becomes agitated; staff say "that's just his behaviour". What should the nurse do first? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Assess for a physical cause such as pain or infection. Behaviour change may signal pain or illness; avoid diagnostic overshadowing.

  6. A confused older woman can't say what medicines she takes. What is the best next step? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Get a collateral history and check GP records. A collateral history and records help build an accurate medicines history.

  7. A patient's temperature is 35.4°C, pulse 118 and respiratory rate 24. They seem confused. What should the nurse consider? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Possible sepsis: escalate urgently. A low temperature with tachycardia, tachypnoea and new confusion is a red flag.

  8. A patient with a head injury had GCS 15; now their right pupil is larger and sluggish and GCS is 12. What should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Call for emergency help and keep monitoring. Pupil changes with falling GCS suggest raised intracranial pressure.

  9. A patient complains of central crushing chest pain radiating to the left arm. What should the nurse do first? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Get urgent help, assess ABCDE and record an ECG. Possible acute coronary syndrome needs immediate escalation.

Key takeaways

  • Assessment is a professional responsibility of the registered nurse (NMC platform 3). Others may collect data, but interpreting it and acting on it needs a registrant's judgement.
  • Person-centred care is a core NMC value (Code 1 and 2): treat people as individuals and listen and respond to their preferences and concerns.
  • Record allergies with the reaction: "penicillin: rash" is safer than "penicillin allergy".
  • Pain is what the person says it is. Subjective data aren't less important than measurements.
  • Advanced examination skills such as percussion and detailed auscultation need further training. Use the skills you are competent in, and refer or escalate for the rest.
  • Trust your first impression of "unwell". Mottled skin, confusion and laboured breathing are warning signs.

Common mistakes in Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning

  • A normal score on a tool does not overrule your clinical concern. If you are worried, escalate.
  • Diagnostic overshadowing is a known cause of avoidable deaths in people with learning disabilities. Assess new symptoms as new.
  • Treating a fever with antipyretics doesn't treat its cause. Look for the source and escalate as needed.
  • Never estimate or write "18" by habit. Inaccurate respiratory rates hide deterioration.
  • Don't give high-concentration oxygen to someone with known type 2 respiratory failure without monitoring their target, but never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic person in an emergency.
  • Severely malnourished people are at risk of refeeding syndrome when feeding restarts. Follow medical and dietetic advice.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to clinical assessment
    Clinical assessment is the systematic gathering and interpretation of information about a person's health to identify needs, problems and risks, and to plan care.
  2. Person-centred assessment
    Person-centred assessment puts the person's own experiences, values, preferences and goals at the centre.
  3. History taking
    History taking is a structured conversation to understand a person's presenting problem and its context.
  4. Subjective and objective data
    Assessment uses two kinds of information: subjective data (what the person tells you) and objective data (what you observe, measure or test).
  5. Physical assessment
    Physical assessment is the systematic examination of the body to gather objective data.
  6. General inspection
    General inspection is the first impression you form when you approach a person: how they look, behave and breathe.
  7. Vital signs
    Vital signs (observations) are measurements of the body's most basic functions: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, pulse, consciousness level and temperature.
  8. Temperature
    Body temperature reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss.
  9. Pulse
    The pulse is the wave of pressure felt as the heart ejects blood into the arteries.
  10. Respiratory rate
    Respiratory rate is the number of breaths per minute.
  11. Blood pressure
    Blood pressure is the pressure of blood against arterial walls: systolic (when the heart contracts) over diastolic (when it relaxes).
  12. Oxygen saturation
    Oxygen saturation (SpO2) measured by pulse oximetry estimates the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen.
  13. Pain assessment
    Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience.
  14. Neurological observations
    Neurological observations assess consciousness, pupils, limb power and vital signs to detect changes in brain function.
  15. Cardiovascular assessment
    Cardiovascular assessment examines the heart and circulation to identify problems with cardiac output, perfusion and fluid status.
  16. Respiratory assessment
    Respiratory assessment looks at how well a person is breathing and oxygenating.
  17. Abdominal assessment
    Abdominal assessment explores symptoms and signs from the digestive, urinary and reproductive systems.
  18. Skin assessment
    Skin assessment examines the skin, hair and nails for colour, integrity, temperature, moisture and lesions.
  19. Hydration assessment
    Hydration assessment identifies dehydration and fluid overload by combining history, observation, examination, fluid balance and test results.
  20. Nutritional assessment
    Nutritional assessment identifies people who are malnourished or at risk, so that they get the right support.
  21. Mental state examination
    The mental state examination (MSE) is a structured description of a person's mental state at a point in time, based on observation and conversation.
  22. Mobility assessment
    Mobility assessment considers how a person moves, transfers and walks, and what support they need.
  23. Falls assessment
    Falls are a leading cause of injury in older people and are common in hospitals and care homes.
  24. Pressure ulcer risk assessment
    Pressure ulcers are localised damage to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, caused by pressure or pressure combined with shear.
  25. Clinical reasoning
    Clinical reasoning is the thinking process nurses use to collect and interpret information, identify problems, decide on actions, and evaluate their effect.
  26. Differential considerations
    Differential considerations are the possible explanations for a person's symptoms and signs.
  27. Recognising deterioration
    Recognising deterioration means noticing early signs that a person is becoming more unwell and acting promptly.
  28. Escalation of concerns
    Escalation is communicating concerns about a person's condition to someone who can provide the right level of help, in time.
  29. Structured handover
    Handover transfers responsibility and information about a person's care from one professional or team to another.
  30. Documentation and reassessment
    Documentation of assessment and reassessment shows what was found, what was done and whether it worked.

Health Assessment and Clinical Reasoning: questions students ask

What is diagnostic overshadowing?

Wrongly attributing new symptoms to an existing diagnosis (such as a learning disability or mental illness) instead of assessing them.

What does SOCRATES stand for?

Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Time course, Exacerbating and relieving factors, Severity.

Why is the abdomen auscultated before palpation?

Because palpation can stimulate the bowel and alter the bowel sounds.

What does speaking in only short phrases suggest?

Significant breathlessness.
