Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is low blood pressure described as a late sign in children?

Children compensate well and keep blood pressure normal until they are severely unwell. From “Introduction to paediatric nursing” →

In plain English: Children's nursing cares for babies, children and young people from birth to adulthood, together with their families. Key idea: Children differ from adults in anatomy, physiology, development and rights; children's nurses care for the child and family together, recognising that children compensate and then deteriorate quickly.

What does a hospital play specialist do?

Uses play to prepare children for procedures, help them cope and express feelings. From “Communication with children” →

In plain English: Communicating well with children and young people builds trust, reduces fear and helps them take part in their care. Key idea: Match communication to the child's development and needs, use play and distraction, prepare honestly and listen to what children say.

Name three conditions screened for by the newborn blood spot test.

Sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, congenital hypothyroidism and inherited metabolic diseases. From “Neonatal fundamentals” →

In plain English: The first weeks of life bring major physiological changes and specific risks. Key idea: Support the transition, give routine care and screening, keep babies warm and fed, and act urgently on jaundice in the first 24 hours and subtle signs of infection.

What are the three categories of NICE's traffic light system?

Green (low risk), amber (intermediate) and red (high risk). From “Paediatric infections” →

In plain English: Infections are the most common illnesses of childhood. Key idea: Use the traffic light system, treat a non-blanching rash in an unwell child as an emergency, consider UTI in unexplained fever, and support immunisation.

What should be explained before a confidential conversation with a young person?

Confidentiality and its limits. From “Adolescent health” →

In plain English: Adolescence is a period of rapid physical, emotional and social change. Key idea: Respect young people's independence and confidentiality, use HEEADSSS to explore psychosocial health, and recognise risk and safeguarding duties up to 18.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Children's and Young People's Nursing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Why do children lose heat and fluid faster than adults? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They have a larger surface area relative to their body size. A large surface area for size increases heat and fluid loss.

  2. At around what age do most children walk independently? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Around 12 months, with a normal range to about 18 months. Walking usually starts around 12 months; later walking up to about 18 months can be normal.

  3. Which growth charts are used in the UK from birth to 4 years? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The UK-WHO growth charts. UK-WHO charts are the standard for young children.

  4. Which sign usually appears late when a child is becoming seriously unwell? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Low blood pressure. Children compensate, so hypotension is a late and worrying sign.

  5. Which principle guides decisions about a child's care? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The child's best interests, with their views given weight by age and maturity. The child's welfare is paramount, and their views are heard.

  6. A 5-year-old in hospital says she is ill because she was naughty. Which stage of thinking does this reflect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Preoperational, with magical thinking. Preschool children often link events magically; reassure her it isn't her fault.

  7. A baby born at 32 weeks is now 12 weeks old. At what age should her growth be plotted? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    4 weeks corrected age. Corrected age is actual age minus the weeks born early (8 weeks here).

  8. A child's sibling has started bedwetting and refusing school since her brother's long admission. What should the nurse consider? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The illness is affecting the sibling, so offer support and involve her where appropriate. Siblings are affected by a child's illness and benefit from support.

  9. A competent 15-year-old in England refuses a life-saving treatment that her parents and doctors support. What is the appropriate course? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Seek senior and legal advice, as the court may need to decide. Refusals by young people raise complex legal issues; legal advice and possibly a court decision are needed.

Key takeaways

  • Children differ from adults in anatomy, physiology, development and rights; children's nurses care for the child and family together, recognising that children compensate and then deteriorate quickly.
  • Development spans motor, language, cognitive and social-emotional domains; theories such as Piaget, Erikson and Bowlby help nurses tailor explanations and support.
  • Measure carefully, plot on the right UK-WHO chart (correcting for prematurity), and act on centile crossing, very low centiles or abnormal head growth.
  • Use universal reviews and tools such as ASQ-3, act on red flags such as loss of skills, and take parental concern seriously.
  • Families are partners: share information, negotiate roles, respect diversity and support the whole family, with the child's welfare paramount.
  • Match communication to the child's development and needs, use play and distraction, prepare honestly and listen to what children say.

Common mistakes in Children's and Young People's Nursing

  • Being reassured by a normal blood pressure in an unwell child. Children maintain blood pressure until late; tachycardia, tachypnoea and capillary refill change earlier.
  • Explaining a procedure to a 4-year-old using abstract ideas. Preschool children think concretely and magically; use simple words, play and reassurance.
  • Plotting a premature baby's measurements at their actual age. Correct for prematurity, or growth will look falsely poor.
  • Reassuring parents that a child "will catch up" without assessment. Red flags and parental concern need proper assessment and referral.
  • Treating parents as visitors who must leave at set times. Parents should be able to stay and take part in their child's care.
  • Telling a child "this won't hurt" before a painful procedure. Honest preparation builds trust; false reassurance destroys it.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to paediatric nursing
    Children's nursing cares for babies, children and young people from birth to adulthood, together with their families.
  2. Child development
    Child development describes how children grow in their physical, cognitive, language, social and emotional abilities.
  3. Growth and development
    Monitoring growth is one of the most important ways of checking a child's health.
  4. Developmental assessment
    Developmental assessment checks whether a child's skills are progressing as expected, so that problems are identified early and support starts sooner.
  5. Family-centred care
    Family-centred care recognises that children are part of families and that families are central to children's health and wellbeing.
  6. Communication with children
    Communicating well with children and young people builds trust, reduces fear and helps them take part in their care.
  7. Consent and parental responsibility
    Children and young people's consent depends on their age, competence and the law of each UK nation, and on who holds parental responsibility.
  8. Safeguarding children
    Children's nurses have a central role in safeguarding: recognising when a child may be at risk, offering early help, and working with other agencies.
  9. Paediatric assessment
    Assessing an unwell child needs a structured, rapid approach that recognises how children compensate and how quickly they can deteriorate.
  10. Paediatric vital signs
    Taking vital signs accurately in children requires the right equipment, technique and age-specific charts.
  11. Neonatal fundamentals
    The first weeks of life bring major physiological changes and specific risks.
  12. Prematurity
    Babies born before 37 weeks of pregnancy are premature.
  13. Congenital conditions
    Congenital conditions are present from birth.
  14. Paediatric respiratory disease
    Respiratory illness is the most common reason children are admitted to hospital.
  15. Paediatric cardiovascular conditions
    Children's cardiovascular conditions range from congenital heart disease to acquired conditions such as Kawasaki disease and arrhythmias.
  16. Paediatric neurological conditions
    Neurological conditions in children include febrile seizures, epilepsy, meningitis, head injury, cerebral palsy and hydrocephalus.
  17. Paediatric infections
    Infections are the most common illnesses of childhood.
  18. Paediatric emergencies
    Paediatric emergencies are rare but frightening, and children can deteriorate rapidly.
  19. Paediatric medication safety
    Children are at higher risk of medication errors because doses are calculated individually, preparations vary and small mistakes can cause large harm.
  20. Childhood diabetes
    Type 1 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes in children and young people.
  21. Childhood cancer
    Cancer in children is rare, but it is the most common cause of death from disease in children over 1 year in the UK.
  22. Adolescent health
    Adolescence is a period of rapid physical, emotional and social change.
  23. Mental health in children
    Mental health problems often start in childhood and adolescence.
  24. Child protection
    Child protection is the part of safeguarding that responds to children who are suffering, or likely to suffer, significant harm.
  25. Transition to adult services
    Young people with long-term conditions move from children's to adult services as they grow up.
  26. Preparing for a children's nursing placement
    Placements in children's nursing take students into hospitals, community teams, schools, neonatal units and other settings.
  27. Reflective learning and case studies in children's nursing
    Reflection and case studies help children's nursing students and registrants learn from experience, link theory to practice, and process the emotional demands of caring for children and families.

Children's and Young People's Nursing: questions students ask

Why are parents encouraged to stay with young children in hospital?

Separation from attachment figures causes distress, and parents provide security.

Why should premature babies' measurements be plotted at corrected age?

Plotting at actual age makes growth look falsely poor.

When is the NIPE examination carried out?

Within 72 hours of birth and again at 6–8 weeks.

What should a nurse check when a child arrives with a new carer?

Who has parental responsibility and who the child wants involved.
