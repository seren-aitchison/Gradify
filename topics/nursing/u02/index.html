Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the anatomical position?

Standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides with palms facing forward, feet together. From “Anatomical terminology” →

In plain English: Anatomical terminology is the shared, precise language health professionals use to describe where structures are and how they relate to each other. Key idea: Describe locations from the anatomical position and the patient's own left and right, using standard directional terms, planes and regions.

What does myelin do?

Insulates axons and speeds conduction of nerve impulses. From “Nervous system” →

In plain English: The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates the body's responses through electrical and chemical signals. Key idea: The CNS integrates information; impulses travel as action potentials and cross synapses as neurotransmitters, which many medicines target.

How is most oxygen carried in the blood?

Bound to haemoglobin in red blood cells. From “Gas exchange” →

In plain English: Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli. Key idea: Oxygen diffuses from alveoli to blood and rides on haemoglobin; SpO2 is useful but has real limitations, so always look at the whole patient.

Which hormone surge triggers ovulation?

Luteinising hormone (LH). From “Reproductive system” →

In plain English: The reproductive system produces sex cells and hormones and, in females, supports pregnancy. Key idea: Reproductive hormones from the hypothalamus, pituitary and gonads control the menstrual cycle and sperm production; care should be accurate and inclusive.

Which part of the brain regulates body temperature?

The hypothalamus. From “Temperature regulation” →

In plain English: The body keeps its core temperature close to 37°C by balancing heat production and heat loss. Key idea: The hypothalamus balances heat production against loss by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation; both high and low temperatures need attention.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Human Anatomy and Physiology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. The wrist is ___ to the elbow. (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Distal. Distal means further from the limb's attachment to the trunk; the wrist is further from the shoulder than the elbow.

  2. What is the main function of ribosomes? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Making proteins. Ribosomes assemble proteins from amino acids, following instructions copied from DNA.

  3. Which is an example of positive feedback? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Oxytocin increasing uterine contractions during labour. Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point (delivery); the other options reverse a change.

  4. A patient has pain just below the right ribs, lateral to the midline. Which quadrant is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Right upper quadrant. Below the right costal margin and lateral to the midline is the right upper quadrant, which contains the liver and gallbladder.

  5. A patient lying face down on a theatre table has a lesion on the side nearest you as you stand on their left. How should it be documented? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    On the patient's left side. Left and right always refer to the patient's own sides, whatever their position or yours.

  6. Which process keeps potassium concentrated inside cells? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Active transport by the sodium-potassium pump. Keeping potassium high inside cells means moving it against its gradient, which needs energy: the sodium-potassium pump uses ATP.

  7. Red blood cells are placed in a solution with a much lower solute concentration than their cytoplasm. What happens? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Water enters by osmosis and the cells swell and may burst. Water moves towards the higher solute concentration, which is inside the cells, so they swell and can lyse.

  8. A post-operative patient has a heart rate of 118, respiratory rate of 24 and blood pressure of 124/78. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The body may be compensating for a problem such as bleeding or sepsis, so this needs escalation. Tachycardia and tachypnoea can maintain blood pressure early in shock; the pattern needs escalating per the early warning score and local policy.

  9. Why does prolonged bed rest weaken bones? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Without weight-bearing, osteoclast breakdown exceeds osteoblast formation. Mechanical loading stimulates bone formation; without it, resorption dominates and bone mass falls.

Key takeaways

  • Describe locations from the anatomical position and the patient's own left and right, using standard directional terms, planes and regions.
  • Cells keep their internal environment stable by controlling membrane transport; tissues share structure and function, and differ in how well they repair.
  • Negative feedback keeps variables stable; rising heart and respiratory rates are often the body compensating, an early sign to act on.
  • The skin is a barrier, a thermostat and a sense organ; assess it by look and touch, adjusting for skin tone and age.
  • Bone is living tissue that supports, protects, stores minerals and makes blood; remodelling explains healing and osteoporosis.
  • Skeletal muscle contracts when calcium lets actin and myosin slide together, using ATP; disuse rapidly wastes muscle, so keep people moving.

Common mistakes in Human Anatomy and Physiology

  • Describing "left" as the left side of your view when facing the patient. Anatomical left is always the patient's left.
  • Thinking osmosis moves solutes. Osmosis is the movement of water, towards the side with more dissolved particles.
  • Being reassured by a normal blood pressure in a patient who is tachycardic and breathing fast. They may be compensating.
  • Relying on redness alone to detect early pressure damage. On darker skin, check temperature, firmness and the person's own report.
  • Assuming bone is inert. It is constantly remodelled, and immobility, steroids and low oestrogen all weaken it.
  • Assuming a few days in bed is harmless for an older person. Muscle strength falls quickly and increases falls risk.

Lessons in this school

  1. Anatomical terminology
    Anatomical terminology is the shared, precise language health professionals use to describe where structures are and how they relate to each other.
  2. Cells and tissues
    Cells are the basic units of life, and tissues are groups of similar cells working together.
  3. Homeostasis
    Homeostasis is the body's ability to keep its internal environment within narrow limits despite changes outside and inside.
  4. The integumentary system
    The integumentary system is the skin and its appendages: hair, nails and glands.
  5. Skeletal system
    The skeletal system is the bones, joints, cartilage and ligaments that support the body, protect organs and allow movement.
  6. Muscular system
    Muscle tissue moves the body, pumps blood, moves food along the gut and generates heat.
  7. Nervous system
    The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates the body's responses through electrical and chemical signals.
  8. Brain and spinal cord
    The brain and spinal cord make up the central nervous system.
  9. Autonomic nervous system
    The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, digestion and bladder emptying.
  10. Cardiovascular system
    The cardiovascular system is the heart and blood vessels, which deliver oxygen and nutrients to every cell and remove waste.
  11. Blood and circulation
    Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones, heat and immune cells around the body through a network of arteries, capillaries and veins.
  12. Respiratory system
    The respiratory system brings air into the lungs so oxygen can enter the blood and carbon dioxide can leave.
  13. Gas exchange
    Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli.
  14. Digestive system
    The digestive system breaks food down into molecules the body can absorb, and eliminates what it can't use.
  15. Liver and pancreas
    The liver and pancreas are accessory organs of digestion with major metabolic roles.
  16. Renal system
    The kidneys filter the blood, remove waste and control the body's water, electrolytes, acid-base balance and blood pressure.
  17. Urinary system
    The urinary system stores and eliminates urine through the ureters, bladder and urethra.
  18. Endocrine system
    The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood to regulate growth, metabolism, fluid balance, stress responses and reproduction.
  19. Reproductive system
    The reproductive system produces sex cells and hormones and, in females, supports pregnancy.
  20. Immune system
    The immune system defends the body against micro-organisms, toxins and abnormal cells.
  21. Lymphatic system
    The lymphatic system returns excess tissue fluid to the blood, absorbs fats from the gut and carries immune cells.
  22. Fluid balance
    Water makes up about 60% of an adult's body weight, divided between the cells, the tissues and the blood.
  23. Electrolytes
    Electrolytes are charged particles dissolved in body fluids, such as sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium.
  24. Acid-base balance
    Acid-base balance keeps the blood pH within a narrow range, about 7.35–7.45.
  25. Temperature regulation
    The body keeps its core temperature close to 37°C by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  26. Pain physiology
    Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage.
  27. Age-related physiological changes
    The body changes across the lifespan, from infancy to older age.
  28. Cellular pathology
    Cellular pathology is the study of how cells respond to injury and disease.
  29. Microbiology
    Microbiology is the study of micro-organisms: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa and parasites, and agents such as prions.
  30. Genetics and genomics
    Genetics is the study of individual genes and how traits are inherited; genomics studies the whole genome and how genes interact with each other and the environment.

Human Anatomy and Physiology: questions students ask

What are the three parts of a negative feedback loop?

A receptor, a control centre and an effector.

How does the skin help cool the body?

By sweating, and by dilating dermal blood vessels to lose heat.

Where are blood cells made?

In red bone marrow, mainly in spongy bone of the axial skeleton and the ends of long bones in adults.

What is sarcopenia?

The age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and function.
