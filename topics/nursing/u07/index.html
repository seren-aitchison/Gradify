Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the five stages of the nursing process.

Assessment, diagnosis or problem identification, planning, implementation and evaluation. From “Nursing process” →

In plain English: The nursing process is a systematic, problem-solving approach to planning and delivering individualised care. Key idea: Assess, identify problems, plan with the person, implement and evaluate, then start again: care is a continuous, documented cycle.

Give an example of an independent nursing intervention.

Repositioning, mouth care, health education or emotional support. From “Nursing interventions” →

In plain English: Interventions are the actions nurses take, or coordinate, to achieve the goals in the care plan. Key idea: Interventions may be independent, interdependent or dependent, direct or indirect; they must be within scope, consented, evidence-based, monitored and documented.

What is "This is me"?

An Alzheimer's Society document recording the preferences and history of a person living with dementia. From “Individualised care” →

In plain English: Individualised care means tailoring care to the specific person: their needs, preferences, abilities, background and circumstances, rather than applying the same care to everyone with the same condition. Key idea: Individualised care adapts evidence-based care to the person, using reasonable adjustments, passports and their own preferences.

When should discharge planning begin?

On or before admission. From “Discharge planning” →

In plain English: Discharge planning prepares a person to leave a care setting safely, with the right support in place. Key idea: Plan discharge from admission: medicines, information, follow-up, practical arrangements and carers, with the person at the centre.

Should risk tools alone be used to predict suicide or decide who receives care?

No. NICE advises against using risk tools or scales alone for this. From “Risk assessment” →

In plain English: Risk assessment identifies hazards to a person's safety or wellbeing, judges how likely and serious harm would be, and plans how to reduce it. Key idea: Identify, assess, plan, communicate and review risk using tools and judgement, choosing the least restrictive measures and respecting informed choices.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Clinical Practice and Care Planning

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which stage of the nursing process judges whether goals have been achieved? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Evaluation. Evaluation compares outcomes with the goals set in the plan and leads to revising it.

  2. A patient says, "I feel sick and dizzy." What type of data is this? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Subjective data. It is what the person reports, which is subjective data.

  3. Which is a nursing problem rather than a medical diagnosis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Breathlessness on walking that limits washing and dressing. Nursing problems describe the person's response to illness, which nurses can help with.

  4. A patient's mobility improves faster than expected after surgery. What should happen to the care plan? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is evaluated and updated to reflect new goals. The nursing process is cyclical: new information leads to reassessment and a revised plan.

  5. Which NMC platform most closely matches the planning stage of the nursing process? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Platform 3: Assessing needs and planning care. Platform 3 covers person-centred assessment and evidence-based care planning.

  6. A patient becomes suddenly breathless. Which type of assessment is needed first? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A rapid emergency assessment using ABCDE. Sudden deterioration needs a prioritised emergency assessment.

  7. A patient's MUST score is 0, but the nurse notices they have eaten almost nothing for three days. What should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Act on the observation: record intake, reassess and refer according to local policy. Tools support judgement; recent poor intake is a concern even with a low score.

  8. A nurse finds a pressure ulcer prevention care plan that hasn't been updated since admission two weeks ago, although the patient is now walking independently. What is the best action? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reassess, evaluate the plan with the patient and update or close it, documenting the change. Plans must reflect current needs; evaluation leads to updating or discontinuing interventions.

  9. A goal was for a patient to eat half of each meal by day five. On day five they are eating a quarter, and say the hospital food is unfamiliar. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Evaluate why, involve the patient and a dietitian, and revise the plan, for example with culturally suitable meals. Evaluation explores why a goal wasn't met and changes the plan to address the cause.

Key takeaways

  • Assess, identify problems, plan with the person, implement and evaluate, then start again: care is a continuous, documented cycle.
  • Assessment combines what the person says with what you observe, using structured frameworks and validated tools, adjusted so everyone can take part.
  • Nursing problems describe the person's response to illness that nurses can address; write them specifically, with cause and evidence, and prioritise by risk and the person's priorities.
  • A good care plan states the problem, a person-centred goal, specific interventions with rationale, and an evaluation date, written with the person and kept up to date.
  • SMART goals are outcomes for the person that are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, so evaluation is clear.
  • Interventions may be independent, interdependent or dependent, direct or indirect; they must be within scope, consented, evidence-based, monitored and documented.

Common mistakes in Clinical Practice and Care Planning

  • Treating the care plan as paperwork completed on admission and never revisited. A plan that isn't evaluated and updated stops reflecting the person.
  • Letting a risk tool score override your clinical judgement. Tools support decisions; escalate a concern even if the score is low.
  • Writing a medical diagnosis, such as "COPD", as a nursing problem. Describe the person's response, such as breathlessness limiting self-care.
  • Copying a standard care plan without tailoring it. Standard plans must be individualised to the person's needs and goals.
  • Writing a nursing task as a goal. Goals describe what the person will achieve; tasks are interventions.
  • Thinking dependent interventions transfer accountability to the prescriber. The nurse is accountable for checking and administering safely.

Lessons in this school

  1. Nursing process
    The nursing process is a systematic, problem-solving approach to planning and delivering individualised care.
  2. Assessment
    Assessment is the systematic gathering and interpretation of information about a person's physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs.
  3. Nursing diagnoses and problem identification
    After assessment, nurses decide what the person's nursing problems or needs are.
  4. Care planning
    A care plan sets out the agreed goals for a person's care and the interventions to achieve them.
  5. SMART goals
    A goal is the outcome the person and the team are aiming for.
  6. Nursing interventions
    Interventions are the actions nurses take, or coordinate, to achieve the goals in the care plan.
  7. Evidence-based care
    Evidence-based care combines the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values and preferences.
  8. Evaluation of outcomes
    Evaluation judges whether the care given has achieved the agreed goals, and what should change.
  9. Person-centred care
    Person-centred care puts the person, not the condition or the system, at the centre of care.
  10. Holistic care
    Holistic care considers the whole person: physical, psychological, social, spiritual and cultural needs, and how these affect each other.
  11. Individualised care
    Individualised care means tailoring care to the specific person: their needs, preferences, abilities, background and circumstances, rather than applying the same care to everyone with the same condition.
  12. Shared decision-making
    Shared decision-making is a collaborative process in which a person and a professional decide together about care, based on the best evidence and what matters to the person.
  13. Patient education
    Patient education helps people understand their health, manage conditions and make informed choices.
  14. Health promotion
    Health promotion enables people to increase control over and improve their health.
  15. Preventative care
    Preventative care aims to stop illness from happening, detect it early, or limit its effects.
  16. Rehabilitation
    Rehabilitation helps people regain, maintain or adapt function after illness, injury or surgery, so they can live as independently and fully as possible.
  17. Discharge planning
    Discharge planning prepares a person to leave a care setting safely, with the right support in place.
  18. Continuity of care
    Continuity of care means care that is coherent and connected over time and between people, teams and settings.
  19. Complex care
    Complex care involves people whose needs span several conditions, services or domains of life, such as multimorbidity, frailty, disability, or the overlap of health and social problems.
  20. Multidisciplinary care
    Multidisciplinary care brings together professionals from different disciplines to plan and deliver care.
  21. Care coordination
    Care coordination is the deliberate organisation of a person's care across professionals, services and settings, so that it is safe, efficient and makes sense to the person.
  22. Risk assessment
    Risk assessment identifies hazards to a person's safety or wellbeing, judges how likely and serious harm would be, and plans how to reduce it.
  23. Clinical prioritisation
    Clinical prioritisation means deciding what needs doing first when several people or tasks compete for time.
  24. Delegation
    Delegation is transferring responsibility for a task to another person while keeping overall accountability for the decision to delegate.
  25. Safe escalation
    Escalation is raising a concern to the right person, at the right time, in a way that gets the right response.
  26. Patient advocacy
    Advocacy means acting to protect and promote a person's rights, interests and wishes, and helping them to have their voice heard.
  27. End-of-life care
    End-of-life care supports people in the last months, weeks and days of life, and those close to them, with dignity, comfort and respect for their wishes.

Clinical Practice and Care Planning: questions students ask

Why is the nursing process described as cyclical?

Evaluation leads back to reassessment, so the plan is continually revised as the person's needs change.

What is a focused assessment?

An assessment concentrating on one specific problem, such as pain or breathlessness.

What is a risk problem?

A problem that isn't present now but is likely to develop without intervention.

What is the rationale in a care plan?

The reason, ideally evidence-based, for choosing each intervention.
