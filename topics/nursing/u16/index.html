Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does PICO stand for?

Population, intervention, comparison and outcome. From “Research literacy” →

In plain English: Research literacy is the ability to find, understand, judge and use research. Key idea: Research literacy means asking focused PICO questions, searching systematically, and judging evidence by design and quality rather than hierarchy alone.

How is relative risk calculated in a cohort study?

Incidence in the exposed group divided by incidence in the unexposed group. From “Cohort studies” →

In plain English: Cohort studies follow groups of people over time to see who develops an outcome, comparing those with and without an exposure. Key idea: Cohort studies follow exposed and unexposed people over time to measure incidence and relative risk; they show temporality but face loss to follow-up and confounding.

What is a consultee in research?

A person consulted about whether someone who lacks capacity should take part. From “Informed consent in research” →

In plain English: Informed consent is the foundation of ethical research. Key idea: Valid research consent needs information, understanding, voluntariness and capacity; it is ongoing and can be withdrawn; strict legal rules govern research with children and adults who lack capacity.

What does SEIPS stand for?

Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety. From “Human factors” →

In plain English: Human factors (ergonomics) is the science of designing systems, equipment and tasks around how people actually work. Key idea: Human factors designs work around people; use SEIPS to analyse systems, prefer strong design-based controls, and use checklists and standardisation.

What are the three steps of Lewin's change model?

Unfreeze, change and refreeze. From “Service improvement” →

In plain English: Service improvement means redesigning how care is delivered so it works better for patients and staff. Key idea: Plan change with Lewin and Kotter, use diffusion of innovations to spread it, engage people and listen to resistance, and design for sustainability.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which database focuses on nursing and allied health literature? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    CINAHL. CINAHL indexes nursing and allied health research.

  2. In a trial of a new dressing, healing rate is which variable? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The dependent variable. Healing rate is the outcome being measured, so it is the dependent variable.

  3. Which qualitative approach develops theory from data? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Grounded theory. Grounded theory builds theory inductively from data.

  4. In PICO, what is "C"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The comparison. C is the comparison intervention or control.

  5. Which design is usually best for understanding patients' experiences of living with a condition? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Qualitative research. Qualitative designs explore meaning and experience.

  6. A study reports a relative risk of 0.8 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.6 to 1.1. What does this suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The result isn't statistically significant, as the interval includes 1. An interval crossing 1 (no effect) means the result isn't statistically significant.

  7. A very large trial finds a statistically significant reduction in blood pressure of 1 mmHg. How should a nurse interpret this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Statistically significant but probably not clinically important. Large samples can detect tiny differences; clinical significance matters.

  8. A ward's falls fell after a new initiative, but winter staffing also improved at the same time. What is the main limitation of concluding the initiative worked? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Other changes over time may explain the improvement. Before-and-after designs can't separate the intervention from other changes.

  9. A meta-analysis shows an I² of 85%. What should the reader consider? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Substantial heterogeneity, so the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously. High I² suggests important differences between studies.

Key takeaways

  • Research literacy means asking focused PICO questions, searching systematically, and judging evidence by design and quality rather than hierarchy alone.
  • Quantitative research measures variables in samples; judge validity and reliability, and interpret p-values with confidence intervals, effect sizes and clinical significance.
  • Qualitative research explores meaning through approaches such as phenomenology and grounded theory, uses purposive sampling and thematic analysis, and is judged by trustworthiness.
  • Mixed methods combine numbers and meaning in convergent, explanatory sequential or exploratory sequential designs; their value depends on integration.
  • Choose the design that fits the question: trials for effectiveness, cohort and case-control for risk, cross-sectional for prevalence, qualitative for experience; watch for bias in each.
  • Good RCTs use randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding and intention-to-treat analysis; medicine trials progress through phases I–IV under MHRA, ethics and GCP oversight.

Common mistakes in Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership

  • Assuming any published study is trustworthy. Publication doesn't guarantee quality; appraise it.
  • Treating "statistically significant" as meaning "clinically important". A tiny difference can be statistically significant in a large study.
  • Criticising a qualitative study for having a small sample that isn't statistically representative. Qualitative research aims for depth, not statistical generalisation.
  • Assuming mixed methods are automatically better. They add value only when integration answers the question better than one method alone.
  • Concluding that a change caused an improvement from a simple before-and-after comparison. Other changes over time may explain it.
  • Analysing only participants who completed the treatment. Intention-to-treat analysis preserves the benefits of randomisation.

Research, Evidence-Based Practice and Nursing Leadership mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Research literacy
    Research literacy is the ability to find, understand, judge and use research.
  2. Quantitative research
    Quantitative research uses numerical data to measure, compare and test relationships.
  3. Qualitative research
    Qualitative research explores people's experiences, meanings and perspectives in depth, using words rather than numbers.
  4. Mixed methods
    Mixed methods research combines quantitative and qualitative approaches in one study, so that each answers questions the other can't.
  5. Study design
    The design of a study determines what questions it can answer and how much its results can be trusted.
  6. Clinical trials
    Clinical trials test whether interventions work and are safe.
  7. Cohort studies
    Cohort studies follow groups of people over time to see who develops an outcome, comparing those with and without an exposure.
  8. Case-control studies
    Case-control studies compare people who have a condition (cases) with people who don't (controls), looking back at their past exposures.
  9. Systematic reviews
    A systematic review uses explicit, reproducible methods to find, appraise and synthesise all relevant studies on a question.
  10. Meta-analysis
    Meta-analysis statistically combines the results of several studies to give a more precise overall estimate of effect.
  11. Critical appraisal
    Critical appraisal is the systematic assessment of a study's trustworthiness, results and relevance.
  12. Research ethics
    Research ethics protects the rights, safety, dignity and wellbeing of research participants.
  13. Informed consent in research
    Informed consent is the foundation of ethical research.
  14. Evidence-based practice
    Evidence-based practice integrates research evidence, clinical expertise and the person's preferences.
  15. Clinical guidelines
    Clinical guidelines are systematically developed recommendations that help professionals and patients make decisions about care.
  16. Quality improvement
    Quality improvement (QI) uses systematic methods to make care safer, more effective, more person-centred and more efficient.
  17. Clinical audit
    Clinical audit is a quality improvement process that measures care against explicit standards and makes changes to close the gap.
  18. Patient safety
    Patient safety is the avoidance of unintended or unexpected harm to people during healthcare.
  19. Human factors
    Human factors (ergonomics) is the science of designing systems, equipment and tasks around how people actually work.
  20. Leadership
    Leadership is about influencing and supporting others towards shared goals.
  21. Management
    Management is about planning, organising and controlling resources to deliver safe, effective care.
  22. Teamwork
    Healthcare is delivered by teams, and how well they work together affects safety and outcomes.
  23. Delegation
    Delegation transfers responsibility for a task to another person while the delegator stays accountable for the decision to delegate.
  24. Supervision
    Clinical supervision and preceptorship support practitioners to reflect, learn and stay well.
  25. Service improvement
    Service improvement means redesigning how care is delivered so it works better for patients and staff.
  26. Clinical governance
    Clinical governance is the framework through which healthcare organisations are accountable for continuously improving quality and safeguarding high standards of care.
  27. Risk management
    Risk management is the systematic identification, assessment and control of risks to patients, staff and organisations.
  28. Healthcare policy
    Health policy shapes what services exist, how they are funded and organised, and what nurses are expected to do.
  29. Digital health
    Digital health uses technology such as electronic records, apps, remote monitoring and artificial intelligence to improve care.
  30. Health informatics
    Health informatics is the use of information, data and technology to improve health and care.
