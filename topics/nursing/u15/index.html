Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is homeostenosis?

The progressive narrowing of physiological reserve with ageing. From “Ageing physiology” →

In plain English: Ageing brings gradual changes in every body system that reduce physiological reserve. Key idea: Older people have narrower physiological reserve (homeostenosis), altered medicine handling and sensory changes; anticipate atypical presentations and adjust medicines.

What does the 4AT assess?

Alertness, orientation (AMT4), attention and acute change or fluctuating course. From “Delirium” →

In plain English: Delirium is a sudden, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually caused by physical illness. Key idea: Screen with the 4AT, prevent delirium with multicomponent measures, find and treat the cause, use non-drug care first, and support families.

What is a category 2 pressure injury?

Partial-thickness skin loss with exposed dermis, such as a shallow open wound or intact blister. From “Pressure injuries” →

In plain English: Pressure injuries (pressure ulcers) are localised damage to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, caused by pressure or pressure with shear. Key idea: Classify pressure injuries by category, assess risk and skin carefully across skin tones, prevent with repositioning, equipment, nutrition and device checks, and report to learn.

What can advance care planning lead to?

Advance statements, advance decisions, lasting powers of attorney, ReSPECT or DNACPR decisions, and organ donation wishes. From “Advanced care planning” →

In plain English: Advance care planning is a voluntary process of discussion between a person, those close to them and professionals about future wishes and priorities for care. Key idea: Advance care planning is voluntary, person-led and ongoing; start early, explore what matters, record outcomes clearly and share plans so they are followed.

What is collusion in end-of-life care?

A request, often from family, to withhold information from the person. From “End-of-life communication” →

In plain English: Conversations about serious illness, dying and death are among the most important nurses have. Key idea: Use SPIKES and ask-tell-ask, respond to emotions, be honest while supporting realistic hope, address collusion sensitively, adapt for culture and children, and look after yourself.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does "start low, go slow" mean in prescribing for older people? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Begin with low doses and increase gradually. Altered pharmacokinetics make older people more sensitive to medicines.

  2. How many frailty phenotype criteria indicate frailty? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Three or more. Three or more of the five criteria define frailty in Fried's model.

  3. Which medicines increase falls risk? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sedatives and antihypertensives. Many medicines cause dizziness, sedation or hypotension.

  4. Why do some fat-soluble sedatives last longer in older people? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The proportion of body fat increases with age. More fat increases the distribution and duration of fat-soluble drugs.

  5. Which intervention has strong evidence for improving frailty? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Progressive resistance and balance exercise. Exercise is among the most effective interventions.

  6. Which model underpins the electronic Frailty Index? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The cumulative deficit model. The electronic Frailty Index counts accumulated deficits in primary care records, following Rockwood's cumulative deficit model.

  7. A previously independent 88-year-old becomes confused and falls after starting a new sleeping tablet. Which concept best explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Homeostenosis. Reduced reserve means a small change causes major decline.

  8. A woman with dementia keeps asking for her mother, who died years ago, and becomes distressed when told. What is the best approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Acknowledge her feelings, don't keep correcting. Validation reduces distress; repeated corrections cause renewed grief.

  9. An 84-year-old becomes acutely agitated on day two after admission. Before considering medicines, what should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Look for causes such as pain or retention. Treating the underlying cause is the priority.

Key takeaways

  • Older people have narrower physiological reserve (homeostenosis), altered medicine handling and sensory changes; anticipate atypical presentations and adjust medicines.
  • Frailty can be understood as a phenotype (Fried) or as accumulated deficits (Rockwood); screen, assess comprehensively, and intervene with exercise, nutrition and medication review.
  • Falls have multiple causes; assess medicines, orthostatic hypotension, strength, balance, vision, continence and environment; address the long lie, fear of falling and bone health.
  • Know the dementia types (including Lewy body sensitivity to antipsychotics), support diagnosis and planning, give person-centred care that seeks causes of distress, and recognise advanced dementia as life-limiting.
  • Screen with the 4AT, prevent delirium with multicomponent measures, find and treat the cause, use non-drug care first, and support families.
  • Distinguish appropriate from problematic polypharmacy, use STOPP/START and anticholinergic burden tools, deprescribe carefully with the person, and watch for side effects.

Common mistakes in Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing

  • Assuming normal blood results mean normal kidney function in an older person. Low muscle mass can make creatinine look normal despite reduced function.
  • Assuming frailty is fixed and irreversible. Exercise, nutrition and treating reversible causes can improve it.
  • Measuring blood pressure only lying down. Lying and standing measurements are needed to detect orthostatic hypotension.
  • Giving an antipsychotic to a person with dementia with Lewy bodies for hallucinations without specialist advice. Severe, sometimes fatal reactions can occur.
  • Treating agitation in delirium with sedatives before looking for the cause. Treat the underlying cause and use non-drug approaches first.
  • Stopping several medicines at once without a plan. Deprescribe one at a time, with monitoring and gradual reduction where needed.

Lessons in this school

  1. Ageing physiology
    Ageing brings gradual changes in every body system that reduce physiological reserve.
  2. Frailty
    Frailty is a distinct clinical state of vulnerability.
  3. Falls
    Falls are common in older people and can cause injury, fear, loss of independence and death.
  4. Dementia
    Dementia is a progressive syndrome affecting memory, thinking, behaviour and daily function.
  5. Delirium
    Delirium is a sudden, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, usually caused by physical illness.
  6. Polypharmacy
    Many older people take several medicines, often for multiple conditions.
  7. Continence
    Bladder and bowel problems are common in older people but aren't an inevitable part of ageing.
  8. Nutrition and hydration
    Malnutrition and dehydration are common in older people, in hospital, in care homes and at home.
  9. Pressure injuries
    Pressure injuries (pressure ulcers) are localised damage to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, caused by pressure or pressure with shear.
  10. Mobility
    Mobility underpins independence, dignity and health in older age.
  11. Rehabilitation
    Rehabilitation and intermediate care help older people recover function after illness, injury or surgery, often outside hospital.
  12. Care homes
    Several hundred thousand older people in the UK live in care homes.
  13. Safeguarding older people
    Older people can experience abuse and neglect at home, in care homes and in hospitals, often from people they trust.
  14. Advanced care planning
    Advance care planning is a voluntary process of discussion between a person, those close to them and professionals about future wishes and priorities for care.
  15. Palliative care
    Palliative care for older people often involves non-cancer conditions such as frailty, dementia, heart failure and COPD, with uncertain trajectories.
  16. Symptom management
    Good symptom management relieves suffering and improves quality of life at the end of life.
  17. Pain management
    Pain is one of the most feared symptoms at the end of life.
  18. End-of-life communication
    Conversations about serious illness, dying and death are among the most important nurses have.
  19. Last days of life
    The last days of life are a time when the focus of care shifts to comfort, dignity and the wishes of the dying person and those close to them.
  20. Bereavement support
    Bereavement is the experience of losing someone close; grief is the response.
  21. Family support
    Families and those close to older and dying people are often carers, decision-makers and people in their own distress.
  22. Ethical decision-making
    Caring for older and dying people raises difficult ethical questions about treatment, autonomy, burden and benefit.

Older Adult, Palliative and End-of-Life Nursing: questions students ask

What is the electronic Frailty Index?

A primary care tool that identifies frailty using accumulated health deficits in electronic records.

What blood pressure change on standing suggests orthostatic hypotension?

A fall in systolic of 20 mmHg or more, or diastolic of 10 mmHg or more, within 3 minutes.

Which type of dementia carries severe sensitivity to antipsychotics?

Dementia with Lewy bodies.

What do STOPP/START criteria identify?

Potentially inappropriate medicines to stop and beneficial medicines that may have been omitted.
