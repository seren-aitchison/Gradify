Key terms and formulas at a glance

Five moments
The WHO's five points in care when hand hygiene is needed: before patient contact, before an aseptic task, after body fluid exposure risk, after patient contact, after contact with patient surroundings
Standard infection control precautions (SICPs)
The basic infection prevention practices used with everyone, in all care settings, whether or not infection is known
FFP3 respirator
A tight-fitting respiratory protective device that filters airborne particles; must be fit tested and fit checked
Key part
A part of equipment that, if contaminated, is likely to infect the patient
TIME
A framework for assessing the wound bed: Tissue, Infection or inflammation, Moisture, Edge
Moist wound healing
Maintaining a moist, not wet, wound environment to support healing
Shear
Deformation of tissues when skin stays still while deeper tissues move, as in sliding down a bed
CAUTI
Catheter-associated urinary tract infection
Residual volume
The volume of urine drained immediately after catheterisation, or left in the bladder after voiding
Running balance
The cumulative difference between recorded intake and output during a period
Fluid balance chart
A record of all measured intake and output, with running and 24-hour totals
Oral nutritional supplements (ONS)
Prescribed or recommended nutritional products taken by mouth to increase intake

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the WHO five moments for hand hygiene.

Before touching a patient; before a clean or aseptic procedure; after body fluid exposure risk; after touching a patient; after touching a patient's surroundings. From “Hand hygiene” →

In plain English: Hand hygiene is the single most effective way to prevent the spread of infection in health and social care. Key idea: Clean your hands at each of the five moments, choose soap and water when rub won't work, and use good technique every time.

What is the most effective way to prevent CAUTI?

Avoid unnecessary catheters and remove them as soon as they are no longer needed. From “Catheter care” →

In plain English: Catheter care keeps an indwelling urinary catheter working and reduces the risk of infection and other harm. Key idea: The best catheter care is a catheter that's no longer there: review the need every day.

Why use a mouthpiece rather than a mask where possible?

Better delivery to the lungs and less medicine deposited on the face and eyes. From “Nebuliser administration principles” →

In plain English: A nebuliser turns a liquid medicine into a fine mist inhaled through a mask or mouthpiece. Key idea: Use the right driving gas for the person, monitor the response, and keep equipment clean.

Name three time-critical medicines.

Parkinson's medicines, insulin, anticonvulsants, some antimicrobials (any three). From “Medication administration” →

In plain English: This lesson brings together the practical skill of giving medicines in a clinical setting: preparation, checks, administration by different routes, documentation and monitoring, within the law and your competence. Key idea: Same process every time: check, identify, explain, give, document, monitor.

Name three things you can assess during personal care.

Skin and pressure areas, mobility, pain, breathing, mood and cognition, signs of neglect or abuse (any three). From “Personal care” →

In plain English: Personal care, including washing, dressing, toileting and grooming, is central to dignity, comfort and wellbeing. Key idea: Personal care done well protects dignity, promotes independence and gives you a chance to assess.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. You have just helped a patient with C. difficile to use the commode. How should you clean your hands? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Soap and water. Alcohol does not reliably kill C. difficile spores; mechanical washing with soap and water removes them.

  2. What is the relationship between standard and transmission-based precautions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Transmission-based precautions are added to standard ones. Transmission-based precautions are always used in addition to SICPs, never instead of them.

  3. What decides which PPE to wear? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A risk assessment of the task and likely exposure. PPE choice depends on what you may be exposed to during the task.

  4. Which moment applies immediately before you access a patient's peripheral cannula to give a flush? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Before a clean or aseptic procedure. Accessing a vascular device is an aseptic procedure, so hands are cleaned immediately before it, even if they were cleaned on entering the bay.

  5. A patient with no known infection has a blood spill on the floor. What applies? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Standard precautions, including safe spillage management. Spillage management is part of SICPs and applies whether or not infection is known.

  6. A nurse wears a fluid-resistant surgical mask for an aerosol-generating procedure on a patient with suspected tuberculosis. What is the problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A surgical mask doesn't give airborne protection; a fit-tested respirator is needed. Airborne infections need respiratory protection (FFP3) that has been fit tested.

  7. A port is wiped with an antiseptic and accessed immediately while still wet. Why is this a problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The antiseptic needs to dry to work, and wet ports can carry organisms in. Antiseptics need contact and drying time to decontaminate effectively.

  8. An antimicrobial dressing has been used on a non-infected wound for six weeks without review. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Raise a review: antimicrobial dressings are for local infection and limited periods. Antimicrobial dressings should be used for a defined, reviewed period when infection is present.

  9. A patient has had a catheter for five days "for monitoring". Observations are stable and he is drinking and mobile. What is the best action? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Prompt a review of whether the catheter is still needed, with a plan to remove it. The commonest reason for CAUTI is a catheter that is no longer needed; daily review and prompt removal are the key measures.

Key takeaways

  • Clean your hands at each of the five moments, choose soap and water when rub won't work, and use good technique every time.
  • SICPs apply to every person, every time. Additional precautions build on them; they never replace them.
  • PPE is chosen by risk assessment, and it only protects if it's removed without contaminating yourself.
  • Identify the key parts and key sites, protect them, and never touch them.
  • Assess the person, describe the wound with a framework, measure it the same way each time, and escalate spreading or systemic infection.
  • Choose dressings from the assessment and the goal, manage moisture, and follow the care plan and formulary.

Common mistakes in Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills

  • Using alcohol rub after caring for someone with diarrhoea. C. difficile spores survive alcohol: wash with soap and water.
  • Thinking SICPs only matter for "infectious" patients. Most transmission comes from people not yet known to carry an infection.
  • Wearing the same gloves between tasks or patients. Gloves become contaminated; change them and clean your hands.
  • Assuming sterile gloves make any touch acceptable. Non-touch technique still matters, because gloves can be contaminated during the procedure.
  • Describing a wound as "healing well" without measurements. Without consistent measurement and description, deterioration can be missed.
  • Changing the dressing type at every change. Inconsistent products make healing hard to judge; follow the plan and review it formally.

Lessons in this school

  1. Hand hygiene
    Hand hygiene is the single most effective way to prevent the spread of infection in health and social care.
  2. Standard infection precautions
    Standard infection control precautions (SICPs) are the basic practices used by all staff, in all settings, with every person, whether or not an infection is known.
  3. Personal protective equipment
    Personal protective equipment (PPE) protects staff and patients from exposure to infection and hazardous substances.
  4. Aseptic technique
    Aseptic technique prevents micro-organisms being introduced into susceptible sites such as wounds, the bloodstream and the urinary tract during clinical procedures.
  5. Wound assessment
    Wound assessment is the systematic evaluation of a wound and the person who has it, so that the right care can be planned, progress monitored and complications spotted early.
  6. Wound dressing principles
    Dressings protect wounds and create conditions for healing.
  7. Pressure area care
    Pressure area care prevents pressure ulcers by reducing pressure and shear, managing moisture, and supporting nutrition and mobility.
  8. Catheter care
    Catheter care keeps an indwelling urinary catheter working and reduces the risk of infection and other harm.
  9. Urinary catheterisation principles
    Urinary catheterisation is an invasive procedure with real risks.
  10. Fluid balance monitoring
    Fluid balance monitoring tracks the fluid a person takes in and loses, alongside clinical signs and weight, so that dehydration and overload are recognised early.
  11. Intake and output recording
    Accurate intake and output recording is the practical skill underneath fluid balance monitoring.
  12. Nutrition support
    Nutrition support helps people who are malnourished, or at risk, to meet their needs: from help with eating and food fortification to oral nutritional supplements, tube feeding and parenteral nutrition.
  13. Enteral feeding principles
    Enteral feeding gives nutrition through a tube into the stomach or small intestine when someone can't eat enough safely by mouth.
  14. Oxygen therapy
    Oxygen is a medicine.
  15. Nebuliser administration principles
    A nebuliser turns a liquid medicine into a fine mist inhaled through a mask or mouthpiece.
  16. Blood glucose monitoring
    Capillary blood glucose monitoring guides the care of people with diabetes and others at risk of high or low glucose.
  17. Insulin administration principles
    Insulin is a high-alert medicine: small errors in type, dose, timing or device can cause serious harm.
  18. Blood sampling
    Blood samples are only useful if they are taken from the right person, into the right tubes, labelled correctly, at the right time and sent promptly.
  19. Venepuncture principles
    Venepuncture is the puncture of a vein to obtain blood.
  20. Cannulation principles
    Peripheral intravenous cannulation provides venous access for fluids and medicines.
  21. Intravenous therapy principles
    Intravenous (IV) therapy delivers fluids, medicines and blood products directly into the circulation.
  22. Medication administration
    This lesson brings together the practical skill of giving medicines in a clinical setting: preparation, checks, administration by different routes, documentation and monitoring, within the law and your competence.
  23. Specimen collection
    Specimens such as urine, stool, sputum and swabs guide diagnosis and treatment.
  24. ECG recording
    A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart's electrical activity from different angles.
  25. Resuscitation principles
    Resuscitation aims to restore breathing and circulation after cardiac or respiratory arrest.
  26. Basic life support
    Basic life support (BLS) keeps oxygenated blood flowing to the brain and heart until defibrillation and advanced care are available.
  27. Patient positioning
    How a person is positioned affects their breathing, circulation, comfort, skin, swallowing and recovery.
  28. Safe moving and handling
    Moving and handling people safely protects both the person and staff from injury.
  29. Personal care
    Personal care, including washing, dressing, toileting and grooming, is central to dignity, comfort and wellbeing.
  30. Oral care
    Good oral care prevents pain, infection and malnutrition, supports dignity and communication, and reduces the risk of pneumonia in people who are unwell.
  31. Continence care
    Continence care helps people maintain continence where possible and manages incontinence with dignity when it occurs.
  32. Stoma care
    A stoma is a surgically created opening that brings part of the bowel or urinary tract to the abdominal surface.
  33. Drain management
    Surgical and other drains remove fluid, blood or air from a body cavity or wound.
  34. Observations and escalation
    Observations are only valuable if they are accurate, timely and acted on.
  35. Documentation of procedures
    Documenting a procedure records what was done, why, how it went and what happens next.

Core Nursing Procedures and Clinical Skills: questions students ask

When should you use soap and water rather than alcohol hand rub?

When hands are visibly soiled, after caring for someone with vomiting or diarrhoea or suspected C. difficile or norovirus, and after using the toilet.

Why do standard precautions apply to everyone?

Because infection can be carried without symptoms or before diagnosis, so all blood, body fluids, non-intact skin and mucous membranes are treated as potentially infectious.

When is an FFP3 respirator needed?

For airborne precautions, and for aerosol-generating procedures on people with infections spread by the airborne route, as local guidance specifies; it must be fit tested.

What is a key part?

A part of equipment that, if contaminated, is likely to cause infection, such as a syringe tip or needle.
