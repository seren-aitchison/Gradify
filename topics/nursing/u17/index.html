Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many hours of CPD are needed for revalidation, and how many must be participatory?

35 hours, of which 20 must be participatory. From “Professional regulation” →

In plain English: Professional regulation protects the public by setting standards for education and practice, keeping a register of who may practise, and acting when a professional's fitness to practise is in question. Key idea: The NMC protects the public through standards, the register, the Code, revalidation and fitness to practise; systems regulators inspect services, and employers manage locally.

How long can someone be detained under section 2?

Up to 28 days. From “Mental Health Act” →

In plain English: The Mental Health Act 1983 (as amended) allows people in England and Wales with a mental disorder to be assessed and treated in hospital without their consent in defined circumstances, with safeguards. Key idea: The MHA allows detention for assessment or treatment with strict criteria and safeguards; nurses must know section 5(4), patients' rights and the nation's law.

How long does an organisation usually have to respond to a subject access request?

One month. From “Data protection” →

In plain English: Data protection law governs how personal information is collected, used, stored and shared. Key idea: Process health data lawfully, minimally, accurately and securely; respect people's rights; report breaches at once.

What is vicarious liability?

An employer's legal liability for the wrongful acts of employees in the course of their employment. From “Negligence” →

In plain English: Clinical negligence is a civil claim that a healthcare professional or organisation breached their duty of care and caused harm. Key idea: Negligence needs duty, breach, causation and foreseeable damage; employers are usually vicariously liable, and only grossly negligent conduct is criminal.

Name the four principles of biomedical ethics.

Autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. From “Ethical dilemmas” →

In plain English: Ethical dilemmas arise when values, duties or principles conflict and there is no clearly right answer. Key idea: Use the four principles and other theories in a structured process, follow the law and Code, and seek support; conscientious objection is narrow and must not delay care.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which legislation established the Nursing and Midwifery Council? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Nursing and Midwifery Order 2001. The NMC was established by the Nursing and Midwifery Order 2001.

  2. Which theme of the Code includes keeping clear and accurate records? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Practise effectively. Record keeping falls under practising effectively.

  3. Which case is regarded as founding the modern law of negligence? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Donoghue v Stevenson. Donoghue v Stevenson established the "neighbour" principle of duty of care.

  4. How many written reflective accounts are required for NMC revalidation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Five. Revalidation requires five written reflective accounts, discussed with another registrant.

  5. Which organisation inspects hospitals and care services in England? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Care Quality Commission. The CQC is the systems regulator for health and social care services in England.

  6. A nurse posts a photo on social media showing a patient's face, with a joke about them. Which part of the Code is most clearly breached? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Respecting privacy and confidentiality, and upholding the profession's reputation. Posting identifiable patient images breaches confidentiality and undermines trust in the profession.

  7. A newly qualified nurse is unsure how to manage a deteriorating patient but doesn't ask for help, and the patient comes to harm. What standard will their care be judged against? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    That of a reasonably competent registered nurse in that post. The standard relates to the post; asking for help would have been the reasonable action.

  8. An adult with capacity refuses a blood transfusion for religious reasons, knowing he may die. What is the legal position? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    His refusal must be respected, even if death may result. Adults with capacity may refuse treatment for any reason, as confirmed in cases such as Re T and Re MB.

  9. A woman with mild dementia understands the risks of going home alone, weighs them and decides to go, though staff think it unwise. What is the correct conclusion? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She has capacity for this decision, so her choice must be respected and risks managed. Capacity is decision-specific; a diagnosis and an unwise choice don't establish incapacity.

Key takeaways

  • The NMC protects the public through standards, the register, the Code, revalidation and fitness to practise; systems regulators inspect services, and employers manage locally.
  • The Code's four themes set the standards registrants must uphold at and outside work; it is the benchmark in fitness to practise.
  • A duty of care arises when you accept responsibility for someone; the standard is that of a reasonably competent practitioner in your role, judged by Bolam and Bolitho.
  • Valid consent needs capacity, information about material risks and alternatives, and voluntariness; young people may consent depending on age, competence and the nation's law.
  • Assume capacity, support decision-making, respect unwise decisions, act in best interests and choose the least restrictive option; assess capacity for each decision.
  • The MHA allows detention for assessment or treatment with strict criteria and safeguards; nurses must know section 5(4), patients' rights and the nation's law.

Common mistakes in Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding

  • Thinking the NMC's main purpose is to represent nurses. It regulates to protect the public; trade unions and professional bodies such as the RCN represent nurses.
  • Treating the Code as applying only at work. Conduct outside work, including online, can affect fitness to practise.
  • Thinking inexperience lowers the standard expected. The standard is that of the post; seek help when unsure.
  • Thinking a signed form proves consent. Consent depends on capacity, information and voluntariness; the form is only evidence.
  • Concluding someone lacks capacity because their decision seems unwise. Unwise decisions alone don't show incapacity.
  • Using the Mental Capacity Act to detain someone for mental disorder treatment when the Mental Health Act is the appropriate framework. Seek expert advice on which applies.

Lessons in this school

  1. Professional regulation
    Professional regulation protects the public by setting standards for education and practice, keeping a register of who may practise, and acting when a professional's fitness to practise is in question.
  2. The NMC Code
    The NMC Code sets out the professional standards that nurses, midwives and nursing associates must uphold.
  3. Duty of care
    A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid causing foreseeable harm to others.
  4. Consent
    Valid consent is required before examination, treatment or care.
  5. Mental Capacity Act 2005
    The Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA) provides the legal framework in England and Wales for making decisions for, and supporting, people who may lack capacity.
  6. Mental Health Act
    The Mental Health Act 1983 (as amended) allows people in England and Wales with a mental disorder to be assessed and treated in hospital without their consent in defined circumstances, with safeguards.
  7. Safeguarding adults
    Safeguarding adults means protecting an adult's right to live in safety, free from abuse and neglect, while respecting their wishes.
  8. Safeguarding children
    Safeguarding children means protecting them from maltreatment, preventing impairment of their health and development, and ensuring they grow up with safe and effective care.
  9. Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards and relevant reforms
    The Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS) protect people who lack capacity to consent to arrangements for their care that amount to a deprivation of liberty.
  10. Human rights
    Human rights are basic rights and freedoms belonging to everyone.
  11. Equality law
    Equality law protects people from discrimination, harassment and victimisation, and requires public bodies to advance equality.
  12. Data protection
    Data protection law governs how personal information is collected, used, stored and shared.
  13. Confidentiality
    Confidentiality is a legal, ethical and professional duty to protect information people share in confidence.
  14. Information governance
    Information governance is the framework of law, policy and practice that ensures information is handled legally, securely, efficiently and effectively.
  15. Record keeping
    Health records are legal documents that support safe care, communication and accountability.
  16. Duty of candour
    The duty of candour requires openness and honesty with people when things go wrong in their care.
  17. Negligence
    Clinical negligence is a civil claim that a healthcare professional or organisation breached their duty of care and caused harm.
  18. Professional accountability
    Accountability means being answerable for your decisions and actions.
  19. Clinical incidents
    A clinical or patient safety incident is any unintended or unexpected event that could have harmed, or did harm, a person receiving care.
  20. Raising concerns
    Raising concerns about care that puts people at risk is a professional duty under the NMC Code.
  21. Whistleblowing
    Whistleblowing is making a disclosure in the public interest about wrongdoing, such as risks to patient safety, in a way that is protected by law.
  22. Professional boundaries
    Professional boundaries are the limits that keep the relationship between a nurse and the people in their care safe, therapeutic and focused on the person's needs.
  23. Ethical dilemmas
    Ethical dilemmas arise when values, duties or principles conflict and there is no clearly right answer.
  24. End-of-life law
    The law at the end of life covers decisions to withhold or withdraw treatment, the limits on hastening death, and what must happen after death.
  25. Advance decisions
    Advance decisions let people refuse specified medical treatment in advance, for a time when they lack capacity.
  26. Best interests
    When a person lacks capacity to make a decision, any decision made for them must be in their best interests.
  27. Professional misconduct
    Misconduct is a serious departure from the standards expected of a registered professional.
  28. Regulatory investigations
    When a concern about a nurse is raised with the NMC, it follows a defined fitness to practise process.

Nursing Law, Ethics and Safeguarding: questions students ask

Does the NMC Code apply outside work?

Yes. Conduct outside work, including online, can affect fitness to practise.

What did Bolitho add to the Bolam test?

The professional opinion relied on must be capable of withstanding logical analysis.

What are the three requirements for valid consent?

Capacity, adequate information and voluntariness.

What does section 44 of the Mental Capacity Act create?

A criminal offence of ill-treatment or wilful neglect of a person who lacks capacity.
