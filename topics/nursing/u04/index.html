What is the difference between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics?

Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug (ADME); pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body. From “Principles of pharmacology” →

Name the two main types of drug interaction.

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic. From “Drug interactions” →

What should you check before giving digoxin?

The person's pulse (rate and rhythm), and know the most recent levels and electrolytes where relevant. From “The medicines administration process” →

What does "Start Smart then Focus" involve?

Starting appropriate antimicrobials promptly, then reviewing at 48–72 hours to stop, switch, change, continue or move to outpatient therapy. From “Antimicrobials” →

Name four DOACs.

Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban and dabigatran. From “Anticoagulants” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Principles of pharmacology
    Pharmacology is the study of how medicines interact with the body.
  2. Pharmacokinetics
    Pharmacokinetics describes how a drug moves through the body over time: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME).
  3. Pharmacodynamics
    Pharmacodynamics is how drugs produce their effects: the targets they act on, how strongly they act and how the response relates to the dose.
  4. Absorption
    Absorption is the movement of a drug from where it is given into the bloodstream.
  5. Distribution
    Distribution is the movement of a drug from the bloodstream into tissues.
  6. Metabolism
    Metabolism (biotransformation) chemically changes drugs, mainly in the liver, usually making them easier to excrete.
  7. Excretion
    Excretion removes drugs and their metabolites from the body, mainly through the kidneys, but also in bile, faeces, breath, sweat and breast milk.
  8. Drug interactions
    A drug interaction occurs when one substance changes the effect of another.
  9. Adverse drug reactions
    An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful and unintended response to a medicine.
  10. Contraindications
    A contraindication is a reason not to use a medicine because the risk of harm outweighs the benefit.
  11. Allergies
    A drug allergy is an immune-mediated reaction to a medicine.
  12. Medication reconciliation
    Medicines reconciliation is the process of obtaining an accurate, complete list of a person's current medicines and comparing it with what is prescribed at transitions of care: on admission, transfer and discharge.
  13. Medicines legislation
    Medicines law in the UK controls how medicines are classified, prescribed, supplied, stored and administered.
  14. Prescribing and administration responsibilities
    Prescribers decide which medicine a person should have; those administering medicines are responsible for giving them correctly and safely.
  15. The medicines administration process
    Safe administration follows a consistent process every time: preparation, checking, administration, documentation and monitoring.
  16. The rights of medicines administration
    The "rights" of medicines administration are a checklist to prevent errors.
  17. Oral medication
    Oral medicines are the most common route.
  18. Topical medication
    Topical medicines are applied to the skin or mucous membranes for local or, sometimes, systemic effect.
  19. Inhaled medication
    Inhaled medicines deliver drugs straight to the airways for asthma and COPD.
  20. Injectable medication
    Injections deliver medicines into the skin (intradermal), fat (subcutaneous) or muscle (intramuscular).
  21. Intravenous medication principles
    Intravenous (IV) medicines act immediately and can't be retrieved once given, so IV administration carries high risk.
  22. Controlled drugs
    Controlled drugs (CDs) are medicines with potential for misuse and harm, controlled under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 and its regulations.
  23. Antimicrobials
    Antimicrobials (antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitic drugs) treat infections.
  24. Analgesics
    Analgesics relieve pain.
  25. Cardiovascular medicines
    Cardiovascular medicines treat hypertension, heart failure, angina, arrhythmias and high cholesterol, and prevent heart attacks and strokes.
  26. Respiratory medicines
    Respiratory medicines treat asthma, COPD and other lung conditions, mostly by opening the airways, reducing inflammation, or treating infection.
  27. Endocrine medicines
    Endocrine medicines replace or modify hormones.
  28. Gastrointestinal medicines
    Gastrointestinal medicines treat acid-related disease, nausea and vomiting, constipation, diarrhoea and inflammatory bowel disease.
  29. Neurological and psychiatric medicines
    Medicines for epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, dementia, depression, psychosis, bipolar disorder and anxiety are often long term and some have serious risks.
  30. Anticoagulants
    Anticoagulants reduce blood clotting to prevent and treat venous thromboembolism and to prevent stroke in atrial fibrillation.
  31. Insulin and diabetes medicines
    Diabetes medicines lower blood glucose.
  32. Polypharmacy
    Polypharmacy is the use of multiple medicines.
  33. Medicines safety
    Medicines safety is about designing systems and habits that prevent medication harm.
  34. Medication errors
    A medication error is any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medicine use or harm.
  35. Patient education
    Educating people about their medicines helps them take them safely and effectively, recognise side effects and make informed choices.
  36. Medicines optimisation
    Medicines optimisation is a person-centred approach to safe and effective medicine use, so that people get the best possible outcomes from their medicines.
  37. Antimicrobial stewardship
    Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated approach to using antimicrobials responsibly, so they stay effective.

Key terms

Pharmacology
The study of how medicines interact with the body
Pharmacokinetics
How a drug moves through the body: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion
Agonist
A drug that binds a receptor and activates it
Bioavailability
The proportion of a dose that reaches the systemic circulation
Volume of distribution
A measure of how widely a drug spreads into body tissues
Enzyme inducer
A substance that increases drug-metabolising enzyme activity
Renal clearance
The removal of a drug from the blood by the kidneys
Pharmacokinetic interaction
One drug changes the absorption, distribution, metabolism or excretion of another
Adverse drug reaction
A harmful, unintended response to a medicine
Contraindication
A reason not to use a medicine because risk outweighs benefit
Anaphylaxis
A severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction
Medicines reconciliation
Comparing an accurate list of current medicines with what is prescribed at transitions
Patient group direction
Written instructions for supplying or giving medicines to groups of patients without individual prescriptions
Independent prescriber
A practitioner responsible for assessment and prescribing decisions
