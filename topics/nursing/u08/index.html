Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between colonisation and infection?

Colonisation is presence without harm; infection is invasion and multiplication with an immune response. From “Microorganisms” →

In plain English: Microorganisms are living things too small to see without a microscope. Key idea: Most microorganisms are harmless; infection depends on the organism, the dose, the route and the person's susceptibility.

Give an example of an organism spread by the airborne route.

Tuberculosis, measles or chickenpox. From “Transmission routes” →

In plain English: Microorganisms spread from one place to another by specific routes. Key idea: Identify the route, then choose the precautions that break it; many organisms have more than one route.

What is a key part in ANTT?

A part of equipment that must remain free of organisms, such as a syringe tip. From “Aseptic technique” →

In plain English: Aseptic technique prevents microorganisms being introduced into susceptible sites during clinical procedures. Key idea: Aseptic technique protects key parts and key sites; ANTT standardises practice through risk assessment, and organisations must train and assess competence.

What does MRSA decolonisation usually involve?

An antiseptic body wash and a nasal treatment for a set course. From “MRSA” →

In plain English: Meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a strain of a common bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics. Key idea: MRSA is S. aureus resistant to beta-lactams; screen and decolonise as policy directs, use contact precautions by risk, and prevent bacteraemia through device care.

What decisions are made at the 48–72 hour antimicrobial review?

Stop, switch route, change, continue with a review date, or refer to outpatient therapy. From “Antimicrobial stewardship” →

In plain English: Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated approach to using antimicrobials responsibly, so that people get effective treatment while resistance is slowed. Key idea: Start smart then focus: prescribe only when needed, document the plan, and review at 48–72 hours; nurses support stewardship at every step.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Infection Prevention and Control

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which microorganisms can reproduce only inside host cells? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Viruses. Viruses lack the machinery to reproduce independently and must use host cells.

  2. Streptococci are which shape? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cocci in chains. Streptococci are spherical bacteria arranged in chains; staphylococci form clusters.

  3. What is the protein coat of a virus called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The capsid. The capsid surrounds and protects the viral genetic material.

  4. A patient's nasal swab grows MRSA, but they have no signs of infection. How is this best described? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Colonisation. Organisms present without harm is colonisation, though precautions and decolonisation may follow local policy.

  5. Why are hospital patients more susceptible to infection than the general population? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Illness, invasive devices, surgery and medicines reduce their defences. Many hospital patients have breaches in their defences and weakened immunity.

  6. Why are infections of long-term urinary catheters hard to treat with antibiotics alone? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Bacteria form biofilms on the catheter that protect them. Biofilms shield bacteria, so catheter-associated infections often need the catheter changed or removed.

  7. A ward has several patients with sudden vomiting and diarrhoea. Which hand hygiene method should staff use? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Soap and water, because norovirus resists alcohol. Suspected norovirus needs soap and water; alcohol rub isn't reliably effective against non-enveloped viruses.

  8. A man returned from West Africa two months ago and now has fever, headache and aches. What should the nurse ensure? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The travel history is reported so urgent malaria testing is arranged. Malaria can present months after travel; falciparum malaria can be rapidly fatal.

  9. Removing a urinary catheter that is no longer needed breaks which links? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The portal of entry and reduces host susceptibility. Without the catheter there is no device route into the bladder, and the person's risk falls.

Key takeaways

  • Most microorganisms are harmless; infection depends on the organism, the dose, the route and the person's susceptibility.
  • Bacteria are classified by shape and Gram stain; spores, toxins and biofilms explain why some are hard to remove and why devices are a major infection risk.
  • Viruses replicate inside cells; enveloped viruses are easier to inactivate, while non-enveloped viruses such as norovirus need soap and water and stronger disinfectants.
  • Yeasts like Candida and moulds like Aspergillus are usually harmless but cause serious invasive infection in vulnerable people; prevention relies on stewardship, device care and, for C. auris, strict control measures.
  • Parasites include protozoa, worms and ectoparasites; manage scabies by treating contacts together and treat fever after travel to a malaria area as an emergency.
  • Identify the route, then choose the precautions that break it; many organisms have more than one route.

Common mistakes in Infection Prevention and Control

  • Treating colonisation as infection. A positive swab without signs of infection doesn't always need treatment, though it may need precautions.
  • Using alcohol products alone where spore-forming bacteria are involved. Spores survive alcohol.
  • Relying on alcohol hand rub during a norovirus outbreak. Norovirus is non-enveloped and resists alcohol; wash with soap and water.
  • Ignoring oral thrush as trivial. It causes discomfort, affects eating and can signal immunosuppression or antibiotic effects.
  • Missing a travel history in a patient with fever. Malaria can be fatal and needs urgent testing.
  • Assuming every respiratory infection needs the same precautions. Precautions depend on the organism, the route and whether aerosol-generating procedures are performed.

Lessons in this school

  1. Microorganisms
    Microorganisms are living things too small to see without a microscope.
  2. Bacteria
    Bacteria are single-celled organisms responsible for many healthcare-associated infections.
  3. Viruses
    Viruses cause many common and serious infections, from colds and norovirus to influenza, hepatitis and HIV.
  4. Fungi
    Fungi include yeasts and moulds.
  5. Parasites
    Parasites live on or in a host and benefit at the host's expense.
  6. Transmission routes
    Microorganisms spread from one place to another by specific routes.
  7. Chain of infection
    The chain of infection is a model showing the six links needed for infection to spread.
  8. Standard precautions
    Standard infection control precautions (SICPs) are the basic measures used by all staff, for all people, at all times, whether or not infection is known.
  9. Hand hygiene
    Hand hygiene is the most important measure for preventing healthcare-associated infection.
  10. PPE
    Personal protective equipment protects staff and patients from exposure to infectious organisms and hazardous substances.
  11. Isolation precautions
    Isolation and cohorting separate people with infections from others to stop spread.
  12. Aseptic technique
    Aseptic technique prevents microorganisms being introduced into susceptible sites during clinical procedures.
  13. Sterilisation
    Sterilisation destroys all microorganisms, including bacterial spores.
  14. Disinfection
    Disinfection reduces the number of viable microorganisms to a level that isn't harmful, but doesn't necessarily kill spores.
  15. Cleaning
    Cleaning removes dirt, organic matter and many microorganisms from surfaces and equipment.
  16. Healthcare-associated infections
    Healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) are infections acquired as a result of healthcare, in hospital or in the community.
  17. MRSA
    Meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a strain of a common bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics.
  18. Clostridioides difficile
    Clostridioides difficile (C.
  19. Bloodborne viruses
    Bloodborne viruses (BBVs), mainly hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV, are carried in blood and some body fluids.
  20. Respiratory infections
    Respiratory infections, including influenza, COVID-19, RSV and tuberculosis, spread readily in healthcare settings and cause serious illness in vulnerable people.
  21. Sepsis recognition
    Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection that damages the body's own tissues and organs.
  22. Antimicrobial resistance
    Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) happens when microorganisms change so that medicines no longer work against them.
  23. Antimicrobial stewardship
    Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated approach to using antimicrobials responsibly, so that people get effective treatment while resistance is slowed.
  24. Outbreak management
    An outbreak is two or more linked cases of the same infection, or a rise in cases above what is expected.
  25. Waste management
    Healthcare waste must be segregated, stored and disposed of safely to protect people and the environment and to comply with the law.
  26. Sharps safety
    Sharps injuries expose healthcare workers to bloodborne viruses and cause anxiety and harm.
  27. Occupational exposure
    Occupational exposure is contact with blood or body fluids at work that could transmit infection, such as a needlestick injury, a splash to the eyes or mouth, or a bite that breaks the skin.
  28. Infection surveillance
    Surveillance is the systematic collection, analysis and sharing of infection data to guide action.

Infection Prevention and Control: questions students ask

What is a biofilm?

A community of bacteria attached to a surface, protected by a slime layer.

Why do broad-spectrum antibiotics increase the risk of thrush?

They disrupt normal bacteria, allowing Candida to overgrow.

How does scabies usually spread?

By prolonged skin-to-skin contact.

What is a fomite?

An object or surface that can carry infectious organisms, such as a stethoscope or bed rail.
