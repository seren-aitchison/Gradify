Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three domains of public health?

Health improvement, health protection and healthcare public health. From “Public health foundations” →

In plain English: Public health is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society. Key idea: Public health prevents disease and promotes health at population level through health improvement, health protection and healthcare public health; every nurse has a public health role.

Name the five action areas of the Ottawa Charter.

Build healthy public policy, create supportive environments, strengthen community action, develop personal skills, and reorient health services. From “Health promotion” →

In plain English: Health promotion at community and population level goes beyond individual advice to building healthy public policy, supportive environments and community action. Key idea: Community health promotion uses the Ottawa Charter, settings and asset-based approaches, and needs clear planning and process, impact and outcome evaluation.

What does LARC stand for?

Long-acting reversible contraception. From “Reproductive health” →

In plain English: Reproductive health covers the ability to have a satisfying and safe sex life and the freedom to decide whether, when and how often to have children. Key idea: Support informed contraceptive choice (LARC is most effective), preconception health including folic acid, reproductive conditions and menopause, and know the abortion law in each nation.

How much moderate activity a week is recommended for adults?

At least 150 minutes. From “Physical activity” →

In plain English: Physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases, supports mental health and helps people stay independent as they age. Key idea: Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus strength work, children 60 minutes a day; some activity is better than none; support people to start small and reduce sitting.

Name the four pillars of population health.

Wider determinants, behaviours and lifestyles, places and communities, and an integrated health and care system. From “Population health” →

In plain English: Population health is an approach that aims to improve the physical and mental health outcomes and wellbeing of whole populations while reducing inequalities. Key idea: Population health improves whole-population outcomes using four pillars and population health management (segmentation, risk stratification and targeted action), planned through integrated care systems.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Community, Public Health and Health Promotion

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which domain of public health covers outbreaks of infectious disease? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Health protection. Health protection deals with infections and environmental hazards.

  2. Which is a fixed factor in the Dahlgren and Whitehead model? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Age. Age, sex and constitutional factors form the core of the model.

  3. What does the social gradient in health describe? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Health worsening at each step down the social ladder. The gradient runs across the whole population.

  4. Which body leads public health in Scotland? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Public Health Scotland. Each nation has its own public health body.

  5. Which NMC platform covers promoting health and preventing ill health? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Platform 2. Platform 2 sets public health proficiencies for all nurses.

  6. How can cold, damp housing affect health? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It harms respiratory and mental health. Poor housing is a recognised determinant of ill health.

  7. A patient with COPD is frequently readmitted and says he can't afford to heat his flat. What should the nurse do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Refer to welfare, energy and housing support. Addressing social determinants can reduce readmissions.

  8. A GP practice in a deprived area has fewer staff per patient than one in an affluent area. Which concept does this illustrate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The inverse care law. Care is less available where need is greater.

  9. A medicine doubles the risk of a side effect that affects 1 in 100,000 people. How should this be explained? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Relative risk doubles, absolute risk barely changes. Absolute risk gives a clearer picture than relative risk alone.

Key takeaways

  • Public health prevents disease and promotes health at population level through health improvement, health protection and healthcare public health; every nurse has a public health role.
  • Health is shaped mainly by the conditions in which people live, described in the Dahlgren and Whitehead rainbow; nurses should assess and respond to social needs.
  • Health inequalities are unfair, avoidable and follow a social gradient; use proportionate universalism, address the inverse care law and target groups such as through Core20PLUS5.
  • Epidemiology uses incidence, prevalence and standardised rates; interpret relative risk alongside absolute risk, watch for confounding and bias, and match study design to the question.
  • Population strategies shift whole-population risk (Rose's prevention paradox); combine them with high-risk approaches, legislation and environmental change.
  • Community health promotion uses the Ottawa Charter, settings and asset-based approaches, and needs clear planning and process, impact and outcome evaluation.

Common mistakes in Community, Public Health and Health Promotion

  • Thinking public health is only for specialists. Every nurse protects and promotes health in daily practice.
  • Blaming individuals for unhealthy behaviours without considering their living conditions. Behaviour is shaped by social and economic circumstances.
  • Treating everyone identically and expecting equal outcomes. Proportionate universalism gives more support where need is greater.
  • Being persuaded by a large relative risk without checking the absolute risk. A doubling of a very rare risk may still be small.
  • Relying only on advice to individuals to prevent disease. Upstream policy and environmental measures often achieve more.
  • Evaluating a health promotion campaign only by how many leaflets were handed out. Evaluate reach, impact and outcomes.

Lessons in this school

  1. Public health foundations
    Public health is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society.
  2. Social determinants of health
    The social determinants of health are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.
  3. Health inequalities
    Health inequalities are unfair and avoidable differences in health between groups of people.
  4. Epidemiology
    Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health and disease in populations.
  5. Disease prevention
    Disease prevention at population level aims to reduce the burden of illness through action on causes and early detection.
  6. Health promotion
    Health promotion at community and population level goes beyond individual advice to building healthy public policy, supportive environments and community action.
  7. Screening
    Screening identifies people who appear healthy but may be at increased risk of a disease, so they can be offered further tests or treatment.
  8. Vaccination
    Vaccination is giving a vaccine to stimulate immunity against a disease.
  9. Immunisation
    Immunisation is the process of becoming immune to a disease.
  10. Sexual health
    Sexual health is a state of physical, emotional, mental and social wellbeing in relation to sexuality.
  11. Reproductive health
    Reproductive health covers the ability to have a satisfying and safe sex life and the freedom to decide whether, when and how often to have children.
  12. Maternal health
    Maternal health covers the health of women and birthing people during pregnancy, birth and the postnatal period.
  13. Smoking cessation
    Smoking remains the leading preventable cause of death and illness in the UK and a major driver of health inequalities.
  14. Alcohol and substance use
    Alcohol and drug use cause substantial harm to individuals, families and communities.
  15. Nutrition
    Diet is one of the biggest influences on health across the life course.
  16. Physical activity
    Physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases, supports mental health and helps people stay independent as they age.
  17. Community nursing
    Community nursing covers the wide range of nurses who care for people outside hospital: in homes, clinics, schools, workplaces, prisons and communities.
  18. District nursing
    District nurses lead teams providing complex care to housebound people in their homes and care homes.
  19. School nursing
    School nurses are specialist community public health nurses who promote the health and wellbeing of school-aged children and young people.
  20. Health visiting
    Health visitors are specialist community public health nurses who work with families from pregnancy until children start school.
  21. Population health
    Population health is an approach that aims to improve the physical and mental health outcomes and wellbeing of whole populations while reducing inequalities.
  22. Infection control in communities
    Infection prevention and control in community settings, such as homes, care homes, schools and prisons, protects people who are often vulnerable and helps prevent outbreaks spreading.
  23. Health education
    Health education gives people knowledge and skills to make informed decisions about their health.
  24. Behaviour change
    Changing health behaviours is complex.
  25. Community assessment
    Community assessment identifies the health needs, assets and priorities of a defined population, so services and interventions can be targeted where they will make the most difference.

Community, Public Health and Health Promotion: questions students ask

What are the social determinants of health?

The conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.

What is the inverse care law?

Good medical care tends to be least available where it is most needed.

What is the difference between incidence and prevalence?

Incidence counts new cases over a period; prevalence counts all existing cases.

What is the prevention paradox?

Many people at small risk may produce more cases of disease than few people at high risk.
