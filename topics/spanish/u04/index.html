What is la carnicería?

The butcher's. From “Places in town” →

In plain English: Describing your town and saying what there is to do there is a core GCSE topic. Key idea: Hay lists what a town has, estar says where it is, -ería names many shops, and lo bueno/lo malo es que gives balanced opinions.

How do you say "Sit down!" to a friend?

¡Siéntate! From “Tú commands” →

In plain English: Commands (the imperative) give instructions, advice and invitations. Key idea: Affirmative tú commands = the él form of the present (habla, come), with eight irregulars: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven; pronouns attach to the end, often with an accent.

How do you ask "What's the weather like?"

¿Qué tiempo hace? From “Weather and seasons” →

In plain English: Describing the weather uses three different structures, which learners often mix up. Key idea: Use hace + noun (calor, frío, sol, viento), hay + noun (niebla, tormenta), está + adjective (nublado) and the verbs llueve and nieva; very = mucho with hace.

How do you ask "Can I try it on?" (a jacket)

¿Puedo probármela? From “Shopping for clothes” →

In plain English: Buying clothes needs vocabulary for garments, colours, sizes and materials, and a few key phrases for trying things on and returning them. Key idea: Ask ¿Qué talla...? (clothes) and ¿Qué número...? (shoes), ¿Puedo probármelo?; quedar says how clothes fit (me queda grande); colours agree with the garment.

How do you say "Give it to me" (the book, tú)?

Dámelo. From “Double object pronouns” →

In plain English: When a sentence has both an indirect and a direct object pronoun, Spanish has a fixed order and one important change: le and les become se. Key idea: Indirect before direct (me lo, te la, nos los); le/les + lo/la/los/las become se lo, se la, se los, se las; attached pairs need a written accent.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Town, Travel and Directions

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is el polideportivo? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sports centre. El polideportivo is a sports centre; town hall is el ayuntamiento.

  2. What does gire a la izquierda mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Turn left. Izquierda is left and derecha is right; gire is the usted command of girar.

  3. What is the affirmative tú command of escribir? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    escribe. The tú command of a regular verb is the él form of the present: escribe.

  4. How do you say "There isn't a station in my village"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No hay estación en mi pueblo. Saying something doesn't exist uses no hay, usually with no article.

  5. You hear Tome la tercera calle a la derecha. What do you do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Take the third street on the right. Tercera is third and a la derecha on the right.

  6. How do you tell a friend "Do it now!"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    ¡Hazlo ahora!. Hacer has the irregular command haz, and the pronoun attaches: hazlo.

  7. Which answer gives the most balanced view of a city? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lo bueno es que hay muchos museos; lo malo es que es muy ruidosa. It states a positive and a negative, each with a reason, using lo bueno and lo malo.

  8. You are in Buenos Aires. Which instruction is best to give a stranger? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Tome la primera calle a la derecha. Tomar is neutral everywhere; coger is vulgar in Argentina, and usted suits a stranger.

  9. Your little brother is about to touch a hot pan. What do you shout? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    ¡No la toques!. Negative tú commands use the subjunctive, with the pronoun before the verb.

Key takeaways

  • Hay lists what a town has, estar says where it is, -ería names many shops, and lo bueno/lo malo es que gives balanced opinions.
  • Ask with ¿Dónde está...? or ¿Por dónde se va a...?; directions use commands (siga, gire, tome, cruce), ordinals and estar a for distance.
  • Affirmative tú commands = the él form of the present (habla, come), with eight irregulars: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven; pronouns attach to the end, often with an accent.
  • Formal commands = yo form minus -o plus -e (-ar) or -a (-er, -ir), plus -n for ustedes; vaya, sea, sepa, dé and esté are irregular; pronouns attach in the affirmative and precede in the negative.
  • Travel en + transport but a pie; ask ¿A qué hora sale/llega...? and ¿De qué andén sale?; a return ticket is de ida y vuelta.
  • Use hace + noun (calor, frío, sol, viento), hay + noun (niebla, tormenta), está + adjective (nublado) and the verbs llueve and nieva; very = mucho with hace.

Common mistakes in Town, Travel and Directions

  • Translating "library" as librería: a librería sells books; a biblioteca lends them.
  • Mixing up derecho and derecha: a la derecha is "on the right", while todo derecho (like todo recto) is "straight on".
  • Using the affirmative form in the negative: No habla means "He doesn't speak"; "Don't speak" is No hables.
  • Adding the normal present ending: Usted habla states a fact ("You speak"); the command is Hable.
  • Saying por autobús or en pie: "by bus" is en autobús, "on foot" is a pie.
  • Saying Es caliente or Estoy calor for the weather or for feeling hot: it is hace calor (weather) and tengo calor (a person).

Lessons in this school

  1. Places in town
    Describing your town and saying what there is to do there is a core GCSE topic.
  2. Asking for and giving directions
    Asking the way and understanding the answer is a classic role play and a listening favourite.
  3. Tú commands
    Commands (the imperative) give instructions, advice and invitations.
  4. Usted and ustedes commands
    Formal commands are used to strangers, customers and in written instructions, and they are formed from the yo form of the present.
  5. Transport and travel
    Getting around involves choosing transport, buying tickets and understanding announcements.
  6. Weather and seasons
    Describing the weather uses three different structures, which learners often mix up.
  7. Holiday plans
    Talking about holidays lets you combine destinations, accommodation, activities and future time.
  8. At the hotel
    Booking a room and sorting out problems at reception is a standard role play.
  9. Shopping for clothes
    Buying clothes needs vocabulary for garments, colours, sizes and materials, and a few key phrases for trying things on and returning them.
  10. Direct object pronouns
    Direct object pronouns replace the thing or person that receives the action, so you don't repeat nouns.
  11. Indirect object pronouns
    Indirect object pronouns show to whom or for whom something is done.
  12. Double object pronouns
    When a sentence has both an indirect and a direct object pronoun, Spanish has a fixed order and one important change: le and les become se.
  13. Invitations and arrangements
    Inviting someone out, accepting or turning it down politely, and agreeing when and where to meet is a staple of everyday Spanish and of exam role plays.
  14. The present continuous
    The present continuous, estar + gerund, describes what is happening at this moment.
