What should you do in the reading time before a listening recording?

Study the questions, underline key words and predict vocabulary. From “Listening strategies” →

In plain English: Listening exams test whether you can pick out key information from spoken Spanish at natural speed. Key idea: Read the questions first and predict vocabulary, listen for gist then detail, pay close attention to negatives, time markers, tense endings and contrast words, and do not fall for distractors.

What does the suffix "-dad" usually correspond to in English?

-ty, as in ciudad (city). From “Reading strategies” →

In plain English: You do not need to know every word to understand a Spanish text. Key idea: Skim for gist and scan for detail, read the questions first, and work out unknown words using context, word families, prefixes and suffixes, cognates and grammar clues.

What does SSC stand for?

Sound-symbol correspondence. From “Pronunciation and intonation in practice” →

In plain English: Clear pronunciation matters in the speaking exam, especially in the reading aloud task. Key idea: Use regular Spanish sound-symbol rules (j, g, silent h, ñ, ll, r and rr, b and v), stress rules and written accents, link words smoothly, and use rising intonation for yes/no questions and falling for statements.

What does the O-R-T-E formula stand for?

Opinion, Reason, Tense, Example. From “Conversation and spontaneity” →

In plain English: The conversation rewards developed, spontaneous answers. Key idea: Develop conversation answers with an opinion, reason, different tense and example, use three time frames and complex structures, and sound spontaneous with fillers, clarification requests and flexible rather than memorised language.

How do you translate "acabo de llegar"?

I have just arrived. From “Translation into English” →

In plain English: The reading paper includes a translation from Spanish into English. Key idea: Translate the full meaning into natural English, watching tenses, negatives and small words such as ya, todavía and soler, and handling structures such as gustar, hace, desde hace and se that do not work word for word.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Skills: Listening, Reading, Speaking, Writing and Translation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which word signals that the speaker never does something? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nunca. Nunca means never, so it turns the sentence into a negative.

  2. Which number is "cincuenta"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    50. Cincuenta is 50; quince is 15 and quinientos is 500.

  3. Which phrase is negative? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Es una pérdida de tiempo. Es una pérdida de tiempo means it is a waste of time, a negative opinion.

  4. A speaker says "Antes jugaba al tenis, pero ahora prefiero el baloncesto". What sport does he prefer now? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Basketball. Pero ahora signals the change: tennis was in the past and basketball is his current preference.

  5. A speaker says "Me gusta mi trabajo, aunque gano poco". What is the opinion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Positive and negative. She likes the job but earns little, so the opinion is mixed.

  6. Using word families, what is a "zapatería"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A shoe shop. Zapato is shoe and -ería often marks a shop, so a zapatería is a shoe shop.

  7. A question asks "What does Luis think of his school?" Which phrase from the recording would most help you? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lo que más me gusta es que los profes son simpáticos. Only this phrase gives an opinion, which is what the question asks for.

  8. A shop assistant says "Son dos euros con quince". How much is it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    €2.15. Dos euros is €2 and con quince adds 15 céntimos, making €2.15.

  9. Which sentence implies the speaker is unhappy with their town? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Si pudiera, me mudaría a otra ciudad. Wanting to move if possible implies dissatisfaction without stating it directly.

Key takeaways

  • Read the questions first and predict vocabulary, listen for gist then detail, pay close attention to negatives, time markers, tense endings and contrast words, and do not fall for distractors.
  • Catch details by knowing confusable numbers such as sesenta and setenta or quince and cincuenta, recognising prices, 24-hour times, dates and quantities, and writing figures down immediately.
  • Decide whether opinions are positive, negative or mixed by listening for opinion phrases, intensifiers, contrast words, emotion verbs and tone, and infer attitudes from structures such as ojalá, ya no and si pudiera.
  • Skim for gist and scan for detail, read the questions first, and work out unknown words using context, word families, prefixes and suffixes, cognates and grammar clues.
  • Literary texts use the preterite for events, the imperfect for description and the pluperfect for earlier actions, and you should infer feelings and motives from reactions, actions and setting, always supported by evidence.
  • Cognates such as universidad, información and famoso follow regular spelling patterns (-ción, -dad, -oso, e- before s), but false friends such as recordar, contestar, largo and ropa mean something different.

Common mistakes in Skills: Listening, Reading, Speaking, Writing and Translation

  • Writing down the first matching word you hear. Wait for the whole sentence, as pero or no can change the answer.
  • Confusing sesenta (60) and setenta (70). Listen for the s or t sound at the start of the second syllable.
  • Choosing P because you heard "me gusta" in "no me gusta nada". Listen for no and nada, which make it strongly negative.
  • Translating every word of a text before answering. Read the questions first and scan for the relevant section.
  • Answering inference questions only with words copied from the text. Explain what the evidence suggests, for example that shaking hands show she is worried.
  • Translating "largo" as large. Largo means long; large is grande.

Lessons in this school

  1. Listening strategies
    Listening exams test whether you can pick out key information from spoken Spanish at natural speed.
  2. Understanding numbers, dates and details
    Many listening and reading questions ask for precise details such as prices, times, dates and quantities.
  3. Listening for opinions and attitudes
    Higher tier questions often ask what a speaker thinks or feels rather than what they say.
  4. Reading strategies
    You do not need to know every word to understand a Spanish text.
  5. Reading literary texts
    GCSE reading papers include short, adapted literary texts.
  6. Cognates and false friends
    Thousands of Spanish words look like English words, which makes reading easier, but some are misleading.
  7. Pronunciation and intonation in practice
    Clear pronunciation matters in the speaking exam, especially in the reading aloud task.
  8. Role plays
    In the role play you complete a short transactional conversation using prompts.
  9. Describing a photo
    The speaking exam includes a task based on one or more photos, followed by a conversation.
  10. Conversation and spontaneity
    The conversation rewards developed, spontaneous answers.
  11. Writing structured paragraphs
    Good GCSE writing is organised in clear paragraphs that cover every bullet point.
  12. Writing an extended essay
    Higher tier writing includes an extended response that rewards range, complexity and accuracy.
  13. Translation into English
    The reading paper includes a translation from Spanish into English.
  14. Translation into Spanish
    The writing paper includes a translation from English into Spanish, which tests grammatical accuracy.
  15. Dictation
    The listening paper includes a dictation in which you write down short Spanish sentences that you hear.
