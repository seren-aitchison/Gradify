Key terms and formulas at a glance

Digraph
Two letters that together spell one sound, such as ch, ll, rr, qu and gu
Diphthong
Two vowels pronounced in one syllable, one of them a weak i or u, as in bueno and tiempo
Tilde
The written accent mark (´) on a vowel showing stress, or distinguishing two words
Seseo

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many letters are in the Spanish alphabet?

27: the 26 English letters plus ñ. From “The Spanish alphabet and its sounds” →

In plain English: Spanish is spelt almost exactly as it sounds, so learning how each letter is pronounced lets you read aloud any word you meet. Key idea: Spanish has 27 letters, including ñ; ch, ll, rr, qu and gu are digraphs, each spelling a single sound, and spelling is a reliable guide to pronunciation.

How is z pronounced in most of Spain and in Latin America?

Like "th" in "think" in most of Spain; like /s/ in Latin America (seseo). From “Consonants that differ from English” →

In plain English: Most Spanish consonants sound close to English, but a handful are pronounced differently and give away an English accent. Key idea: C before e or i and z sound like "th" in most of Spain and /s/ in Latin America; g before e or i and j are a throaty "loch" sound; h is silent; r is tapped and rr trilled.

Which verb form does usted take?

The third-person singular, like él: usted habla. From “Tú and usted” →

In plain English: Spanish has more than one word for "you", and choosing between them signals friendliness or respect. Key idea: Usted and ustedes take third-person verb forms; vosotros is the informal plural in Spain, while Latin America uses ustedes for every plural "you".

How do you ask "At what time...?"

¿A qué hora...? From “Telling the time” →

In plain English: Times are tested in every listening paper and needed for every arrangement. Key idea: Es la una, but son las dos onwards; add minutes with y and subtract them from the next hour with menos; a las means "at".

Why is it el agua but las aguas?

Feminine nouns starting with stressed a- take el in the singular only; they remain feminine. From “Definite and indefinite articles” →

In plain English: Spanish articles agree with their nouns in gender and number, and they are used in places where English leaves them out. Key idea: Articles agree with the noun; a + el = al and de + el = del; Spanish uses el/la/los/las for generalisations and drops un/una before a bare profession.

Quick quiz: test yourself on First Steps in Spanish

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the Spanish name of the letter j? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    jota. The letter j is called jota; hache is h and ge is g. Knowing the names lets you spell your name aloud.

  2. Which English word contains the vowel closest to Spanish i? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    machine. Spanish i is always the "ee" sound of machine, never the "eye" of ice or the short i of bit.

  3. Where is the stress in hablar (to speak)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    On the last syllable, -blar. Hablar ends in r, a consonant other than n or s, so rule 2 puts the stress on the last syllable.

  4. In the word guitarra, which letters are silent or combine into a single sound? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The u is silent and rr is one trilled sound. In gui the u is silent so g keeps its hard sound; rr is a digraph spelling one trilled r: gi-TA-rra.

  5. How many syllables does tiempo (time, weather) have? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Two: tiem-po. i + e is a weak + strong pair, so it forms a diphthong in one syllable: tiem-po.

  6. Why does canción (song) need a written accent but canciones doesn't? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The plural ends in s and is stressed on the second-to-last syllable. Canción ends in n but is stressed on the last syllable, breaking rule 1; can-cio-nes is stressed on -cio-, which rule 1 predicts.

  7. Why can an English learner usually read an unseen Spanish word aloud correctly but not an unseen English word? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Spanish letters map consistently onto sounds. Spanish spelling is close to phonemic, so the letters predict the sounds; English spelling preserves historical forms, so the same letters spell many sounds.

  8. Which word contains hiatus rather than a diphthong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    poeta. o + e are both strong vowels, so po-e-ta has hiatus; puerta, cuidado and aire all contain a weak vowel forming a diphthong.

  9. Which of these words is spelt correctly? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    árbol. Árbol ends in l and is stressed on the first syllable, so it needs an accent there; lápiz needs one too, and examen follows rule 1 with no accent.

Key takeaways

  • Spanish has 27 letters, including ñ; ch, ll, rr, qu and gu are digraphs, each spelling a single sound, and spelling is a reliable guide to pronunciation.
  • Every Spanish vowel keeps its pure sound, stressed or unstressed; weak i and u join a neighbouring vowel in a diphthong unless a written accent breaks it.
  • Words ending in a vowel, n or s stress the second-to-last syllable; others stress the last; any word that breaks the rule, and every esdrújula, carries a written accent.
  • C before e or i and z sound like "th" in most of Spain and /s/ in Latin America; g before e or i and j are a throaty "loch" sound; h is silent; r is tapped and rr trilled.
  • Choose buenos días, buenas tardes or buenas noches by time of day, ask ¿Qué tal? or ¿Cómo estás?, and return the question with ¿Y tú? or ¿Y usted?.
  • Spanish states age with tener (Tengo quince años), name with llamarse or ser, origin with ser de, and residence with vivir en.

Common mistakes in First Steps in Spanish

  • Pronouncing the silent letters of English habits: in Spanish h is always silent (hola is "OH-la"), and the u in que, qui, gue and gui isn't pronounced.
  • Saying English vowels: no pronounced "noh-oo" or de as "day" sounds foreign at once. Keep each vowel short, steady and unglided.
  • Dropping accents as optional. Papa is "potato" or "Pope", papá is "dad"; esta is "this", está is "is". The accent changes the word.
  • Pronouncing pero (but) with a trill makes it perro (dog). The tap and trill distinguish words, so practise both.
  • Saying buenos noches or buenas días. Día is masculine (buenos días) despite ending in -a; tardes and noches are feminine (buenas).
  • Saying Soy quince años. Age uses tener: Tengo quince años. Soy + number is wrong.

Lessons in this school

  1. The Spanish alphabet and its sounds
    Spanish is spelt almost exactly as it sounds, so learning how each letter is pronounced lets you read aloud any word you meet.
  2. Vowels and diphthongs
    Spanish has five pure vowel sounds, each always pronounced the same way, which is the single biggest step towards sounding Spanish.
  3. Stress and written accents
    Every Spanish word of more than one syllable has one stressed syllable, and two short rules predict where it falls.
  4. Consonants that differ from English
    Most Spanish consonants sound close to English, but a handful are pronounced differently and give away an English accent.
  5. Greetings and goodbyes
    Greetings are the first Spanish you will use and the first impression you will make.
  6. Introducing yourself
    Telling someone your name, age, where you are from and where you live is the core of the first conversation in any language.
  7. Tú and usted
    Spanish has more than one word for "you", and choosing between them signals friendliness or respect.
  8. Numbers 0 to 100
    Numbers come up in every exam paper and every conversation about age, prices, phone numbers and times.
  9. Days, months and dates
    Days, months and dates let you talk about birthdays, timetables and plans.
  10. Telling the time
    Times are tested in every listening paper and needed for every arrangement.
  11. Asking questions
    Asking questions keeps a conversation going and is required in role plays at every level.
  12. Nouns: gender and number
    Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine, and that gender controls the articles and adjectives around it.
  13. Definite and indefinite articles
    Spanish articles agree with their nouns in gender and number, and they are used in places where English leaves them out.
  14. Subject pronouns
    Subject pronouns say who does the action, but in Spanish the verb ending already does that job, so the pronouns are often left out.
  15. Ser: identity, origin and nationality
    Ser is one of the two Spanish verbs for "to be", and the one that describes what someone or something is.
  16. Adjective agreement
    Spanish adjectives change their endings to agree with the noun they describe, and they usually come after it.

First Steps in Spanish: questions students ask

What is hiatus?

Two adjacent vowels pronounced in separate syllables, as in leo (le-o) or río (rí-o).

Where is the stress in a word ending in a vowel, n or s, with no written accent?

On the second-to-last syllable: ca-sa, ha-blan.

What does hasta luego mean?

See you later (a common goodbye).

What does Encantada tell you about the speaker?

The speaker is female; encantado is used by a male speaker.
