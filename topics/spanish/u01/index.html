Lessons in this school

  1. The Spanish alphabet and its sounds
    Spanish is spelt almost exactly as it sounds, so learning how each letter is pronounced lets you read aloud any word you meet.
  2. Vowels and diphthongs
    Spanish has five pure vowel sounds, each always pronounced the same way, which is the single biggest step towards sounding Spanish.
  3. Stress and written accents
    Every Spanish word of more than one syllable has one stressed syllable, and two short rules predict where it falls.
  4. Consonants that differ from English
    Most Spanish consonants sound close to English, but a handful are pronounced differently and give away an English accent.
  5. Greetings and goodbyes
    Greetings are the first Spanish you will use and the first impression you will make.
  6. Introducing yourself
    Telling someone your name, age, where you are from and where you live is the core of the first conversation in any language.
  7. Tú and usted
    Spanish has more than one word for "you", and choosing between them signals friendliness or respect.
  8. Numbers 0 to 100
    Numbers come up in every exam paper and every conversation about age, prices, phone numbers and times.
  9. Days, months and dates
    Days, months and dates let you talk about birthdays, timetables and plans.
  10. Telling the time
    Times are tested in every listening paper and needed for every arrangement.
  11. Asking questions
    Asking questions keeps a conversation going and is required in role plays at every level.
  12. Nouns: gender and number
    Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine, and that gender controls the articles and adjectives around it.
  13. Definite and indefinite articles
    Spanish articles agree with their nouns in gender and number, and they are used in places where English leaves them out.
  14. Subject pronouns
    Subject pronouns say who does the action, but in Spanish the verb ending already does that job, so the pronouns are often left out.
  15. Ser: identity, origin and nationality
    Ser is one of the two Spanish verbs for "to be", and the one that describes what someone or something is.
  16. Adjective agreement
    Spanish adjectives change their endings to agree with the noun they describe, and they usually come after it.

Key terms

Digraph
Two letters that together spell one sound, such as ch, ll, rr, qu and gu
Diphthong
Two vowels pronounced in one syllable, one of them a weak i or u, as in bueno and tiempo
Tilde
The written accent mark (´) on a vowel showing stress, or distinguishing two words
Seseo
Pronouncing c (before e and i) and z as /s/, as in Latin America, the Canaries and parts of Andalusia
Saludo
A greeting, such as hola or buenos días
Apellido
Surname; Spanish speakers usually have two
Tutear
To address someone as tú
Cien
One hundred, used for exactly 100 and before nouns: cien euros
Cumpleaños
Birthday (literally "completes years"), masculine and the same in the plural
Hora
Hour, or the time of day: ¿Qué hora es?
Pronombre interrogativo
A question word such as qué, quién or cuál
Género gramatical
Grammatical gender: the masculine or feminine class of a noun, which other words agree with
Artículo definido
The definite article: el, la, los, las
Pronombre sujeto
Subject pronoun, such as yo, tú or ella
