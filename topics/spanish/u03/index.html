Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the nosotros form of acostarse?

nos acostamos. From “Reflexive verbs” →

In plain English: Reflexive verbs describe actions people do to themselves, and Spanish uses many more of them than English. Key idea: Reflexive pronouns (me, te, se, nos, os, se) match the subject and go before a conjugated verb or attach to an infinitive; body parts take the article.

What does suelo + infinitive mean?

I usually do: Suelo comer a las dos. From “Frequency and time expressions” →

In plain English: Saying how often you do something adds detail to every answer and is rewarded in GCSE marking. Key idea: Frequency is shown by adverbs (siempre, a menudo, a veces, nunca) or soler + infinitive; nunca after the verb needs no before it.

How do you say "worse than"?

peor que. From “Comparatives” →

In plain English: Comparing people, places and things is essential for giving opinions. Key idea: Más/menos ... que compares, tan ... como equates adjectives, tanto/a/os/as ... como equates nouns; mejor, peor, mayor and menor are irregular; más de before numbers.

How do you ask for the bill?

La cuenta, por favor. From “At the restaurant” →

In plain English: Ordering food and drink is a classic GCSE role play. Key idea: Order politely with quisiera, para mí or ¿me trae...?, name courses with de primero, de segundo and de postre, and ask for la cuenta.

What does la planta baja mean?

The ground floor. From “My home and my room” →

In plain English: Describing where you live combines vocabulary for types of home, rooms and furniture with hay, tener and estar. Key idea: Use tener and hay for what a home contains and estar for where it is; piso is both "flat" and "floor".

Quick quiz: test yourself on Daily Life, School and Food

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Complete: Mi hermano ___ a las ocho. (levantarse) (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    se levanta. Third-person singular subject takes se and the verb ending -a.

  2. Which verb means "to have dinner"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    cenar. Cenar is to have dinner; comer is lunch or to eat, desayunar breakfast and merendar an afternoon snack.

  3. What does de vez en cuando mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    From time to time. De vez en cuando is "from time to time", a lower frequency than a menudo.

  4. Which sentence correctly says "We're going to get dressed"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Vamos a vestirnos. With an infinitive, the pronoun matching nosotros attaches to it: vestirnos.

  5. Complete: Antes de ___, me lavo los dientes. (going to bed) (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    acostarme. Antes de takes an infinitive, with the pronoun for yo attached: acostarme.

  6. Which sentence correctly says "I never eat fish"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No como nunca pescado. After the verb, nunca needs no before the verb; nunca first would stand alone.

  7. How do you say "I brush my teeth"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Me lavo los dientes. With reflexive verbs and body parts Spanish uses the article: me lavo los dientes.

  8. Which is the best translation of "First I get dressed, then I have breakfast"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Primero me visto, luego desayuno. Vestirse is reflexive and e to i (me visto); desayunar isn't reflexive.

  9. Which answer to ¿Con qué frecuencia haces deporte? would earn most credit for detail and accuracy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Suelo jugar al baloncesto dos veces a la semana, pero nunca nado. It combines soler, a precise frequency phrase and a correct negative; the others are thin or inaccurate.

Key takeaways

  • Reflexive pronouns (me, te, se, nos, os, se) match the subject and go before a conjugated verb or attach to an infinitive; body parts take the article.
  • Link a routine with primero, luego, después and finalmente, and use antes de or después de + infinitive (with its pronoun) for "before/after doing".
  • Frequency is shown by adverbs (siempre, a menudo, a veces, nunca) or soler + infinitive; nunca after the verb needs no before it.
  • Give opinions with me gusta(n), me encanta(n), odio, prefiero or creo que, justify them with porque, and make adjectives agree with the subject.
  • Tener que is personal obligation, hay que general obligation, deber duty or advice; no tener que means "don't have to", no deber means "mustn't".
  • Más/menos ... que compares, tan ... como equates adjectives, tanto/a/os/as ... como equates nouns; mejor, peor, mayor and menor are irregular; más de before numbers.

Common mistakes in Daily Life, School and Food

  • Leaving out the pronoun: Levanto a las siete means "I lift at seven". The reflexive form is Me levanto a las siete.
  • Using a conjugated verb or gerund after después de: después de comiendo and después de como are wrong; Spanish needs the infinitive: después de comer.
  • Writing Nunca no voy: with nunca first, there is no no. Use Nunca voy or No voy nunca.
  • Saying Me gusta las ciencias: plural subjects need gustan. And El inglés es útil but Las ciencias son útiles.
  • Forgetting que: Tengo estudiar is wrong. Obligation with tener always needs que: Tengo que estudiar.
  • Saying más bueno or más malo for better or worse: use mejor and peor. And "more than ten" is más de diez, not más que diez.

Lessons in this school

  1. Reflexive verbs
    Reflexive verbs describe actions people do to themselves, and Spanish uses many more of them than English.
  2. Daily routine
    Describing a typical day is a staple of GCSE speaking and writing.
  3. Frequency and time expressions
    Saying how often you do something adds detail to every answer and is rewarded in GCSE marking.
  4. School subjects and opinions
    School is a GCSE theme in its own right, and giving and justifying opinions about subjects is the easiest way to show a range of language.
  5. Obligation: tener que, hay que and deber
    Rules, duties and advice are expressed with three structures, each followed by an infinitive.
  6. Comparatives
    Comparing people, places and things is essential for giving opinions.
  7. Superlatives
    Superlatives single out the most or least of a group, and the -ísimo ending intensifies an adjective.
  8. Food and meals
    Food is a favourite exam topic because it combines vocabulary, opinions and culture.
  9. At the restaurant
    Ordering food and drink is a classic GCSE role play.
  10. Shopping for food: quantities and large numbers
    Buying food needs quantities, containers and prices, and prices need numbers above 100.
  11. Demonstratives
    Demonstratives point things out: this, that, these, those.
  12. My home and my room
    Describing where you live combines vocabulary for types of home, rooms and furniture with hay, tener and estar.
  13. Prepositions of place
    Prepositions of place say where things are in relation to each other, and most are two- or three-word phrases ending in de.
  14. Household chores and helping at home
    Talking about chores combines obligation, frequency and opinion, which is why it is a favourite exam topic.
