How do you form the simple future in Spanish?

Add -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án to the whole infinitive. From “The future tense” →

In plain English: The simple future tense says what will happen. Key idea: The simple future adds -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án to the whole infinitive for all verb groups, with accents on every form except nosotros; it is used for predictions, promises and future events, alongside the common ir a + infinitive.

How else can you say "he must be at home"?

Debe de estar en casa. From “The future of probability” →

In plain English: Spanish can use the future tense to guess or wonder about the present. Key idea: Spanish uses the future tense to express probability or guesses about the present, as in estará en casa or ¿qué hora será?, and probability can also be shown with probablemente, quizás, tal vez and deber de + infinitive.

Which tense follows si in possible conditions?

The present tense. From “Si clauses: possible conditions” →

In plain English: Si clauses say what will happen if something occurs. Key idea: Possible conditions use si + present tense with a future, present, ir a or command in the other clause, never the future directly after si; unlikely conditions use si + imperfect subjunctive + conditional, and si (if) differs from sí (yes).

What is "un año sabático"?

A gap year. From “Future plans and careers” →

In plain English: GCSE students often talk about their plans after school. Key idea: Talking about future plans uses a range of structures such as ir a, the simple future, quiero, me gustaría, espero, pienso and tengo la intención de + infinitive, with reasons introduced by porque, ya que and dado que and time phrases such as dentro de diez años and si clauses.

Can que be left out in Spanish as "that" can in English?

No, que must always be used. From “Relative pronouns: que, quien and donde” →

In plain English: Relative pronouns join ideas and make sentences longer and more complex. Key idea: Que means who, which or that for people and things and cannot be omitted; quien and quienes refer to people, mainly after prepositions; donde means where for places, and dónde with an accent is used in questions.

Key takeaways

  • The simple future adds -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án to the whole infinitive for all verb groups, with accents on every form except nosotros; it is used for predictions, promises and future events, alongside the common ir a + infinitive.
  • A small group of verbs, including tener, poner, salir, venir, poder, saber, querer, haber, hacer and decir, have irregular future stems but take the regular future endings; hay becomes habrá, and the same stems are used in the conditional.
  • Spanish uses the future tense to express probability or guesses about the present, as in estará en casa or ¿qué hora será?, and probability can also be shown with probablemente, quizás, tal vez and deber de + infinitive.
  • The conditional adds -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían to the whole infinitive, uses the same irregular stems as the future, and expresses hypothetical situations, wishes such as me gustaría, polite requests, advice and the future in the past.
  • The conditional makes requests more polite, using phrases such as ¿podría…?, ¿le importaría…?, me gustaría and quisiera with usted in formal situations, and advice can be given with deberías, tienes que, yo que tú + conditional, te recomiendo and sería mejor.
  • Possible conditions use si + present tense with a future, present, ir a or command in the other clause, never the future directly after si; unlikely conditions use si + imperfect subjunctive + conditional, and si (if) differs from sí (yes).

Common mistakes in The Future, the Conditional and Work

  • Removing the -ar, -er or -ir before adding the endings. The future uses the whole infinitive: hablaré, not hablé.
  • Writing "teneré" or "haceré". Irregular verbs must use their irregular stems: tendré and haré.
  • Always translating the future as "will". In context it can mean "probably" or "I wonder".
  • Confusing the conditional with the imperfect. Comería (I would eat) uses the whole infinitive; comía (I used to eat) does not.
  • Using tú with a stranger or an official. Use usted in formal situations.
  • Putting the future after si, such as "si tendré tiempo". Use the present: si tengo tiempo.
