Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three sources of solicitors' professional obligations.

The SRA Principles and Codes, statute (such as the Solicitors Act 1974 and POCA 2002), and the general law (contract, tort, fiduciary duties). From “Professional ethics” →

In plain English: Professional ethics are the standards of behaviour expected of lawyers because of their special role: they serve clients, but also the courts, the rule of law and the public. Key idea: When duties conflict, the duties to the court and the public interest generally take precedence over the duty to the client.

Are there any exceptions to the bar on own-interest conflicts?

No. A solicitor must not act where there is an own-interest conflict or significant risk of one. From “Conflicts of interest” →

In plain English: A solicitor must not act where there is a conflict of interests, or a significant risk of one, unless a narrow exception applies. Key idea: Check for conflicts before accepting instructions and keep checking as the matter develops.

Case card: Bolton v Law Society [1994] 1 WLR 512. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A solicitor who had not been dishonest was suspended for misusing client money; he argued the sanction was too harsh. Held: The suspension stood. Principle: The most fundamental purpose of sanctions is to maintain the reputation of the profession: 'a profession whose members may be trusted to the ends of the earth'. From “Honesty and integrity” →

In plain English: Honesty and integrity are among the most important requirements of being a solicitor. Key idea: An honest mistake, promptly corrected, is very different from concealment. Correct errors straight away.

What is the corporate offence under s 7 of the Bribery Act 2010?

Failure by a commercial organisation to prevent bribery by persons associated with it, with a defence of adequate procedures. From “Financial crime” →

In plain English: Beyond money laundering, solicitors must understand other financial crimes that can affect their practice or their clients: fraud, bribery, sanctions breaches, tax evasion facilitation and terrorist financing. Key idea: Firms need systems to prevent, detect and report financial crime, not just individual vigilance.

Case card: Bristol and West Building Society v Mothew [1998] Ch 1. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A solicitor acting for a buyer and a lender failed to tell the lender about a second charge, through carelessness, not deliberately. Held: No breach of fiduciary duty; the claim was in negligence. Principle: Not every breach by a fiduciary is a breach of fiduciary duty; the distinctive obligation is loyalty. From “Duties to clients” →

In plain English: Solicitors owe clients duties under the SRA Principles and Code, in contract, in tort, and as fiduciaries. Key idea: The client decides what outcome to pursue; the solicitor advises and acts within the law and the Code.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Legal Ethics and Professional Conduct

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Why do solicitors owe duties beyond those to their clients? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They are officers of the court. Solicitors' role in the justice system brings wider duties.

  2. Which Principle is engaged by a solicitor who backdates a document without intending to deceive anyone? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Integrity, and possibly honesty. Backdating can show a lack of integrity even without proof of dishonest intent.

  3. What are the COLP and COFA? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A firm's two compliance officers. Firms must appoint compliance officers responsible for systems and reporting.

  4. A client instructs a solicitor to present evidence the solicitor knows is false. Which duty prevails? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The duty not to mislead the court. Duties to the court take precedence over client instructions.

  5. A client's best interests appear to require the solicitor to conceal a document from the court. What should the solicitor do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Don't conceal it: justice takes precedence. Public interest Principles take precedence.

  6. Which body would deal with a solicitor accused of serious dishonesty with client money? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An SRA investigation and likely SDT referral. Serious matters are prosecuted before the SDT.

  7. A solicitor learns confidential financial information while acting for Company A. Company B then asks the firm to act against A in a related dispute. What is the position? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Not act unless the risk is removed or A consents. Para 6.5: the firm holds A's confidential information material to the dispute, so it can act only with effective information barriers (the Bolkiah standard) or A's informed consent.

  8. In a sentencing hearing, the prosecution says the defendant has no previous convictions. The defence solicitor knows this is wrong. What should the solicitor do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Don't rely on it; withdraw if necessary. You mustn't mislead the court, but confidentiality also applies.

  9. A client buying a house asks to pay the deposit in cash and won't explain where it came from. What should the solicitor do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ask for source of funds; report to the MLRO. These are red flags requiring due diligence and possibly a report.

Key takeaways

  • When duties conflict, the duties to the court and the public interest generally take precedence over the duty to the client.
  • Learn all seven Principles by heart; SQE questions often turn on which one is engaged.
  • When answering conduct questions, identify the relevant Principle and the Code paragraph.
  • Confidentiality and disclosure pull against each other when you act for two clients: that's why conflicts must be checked first.
  • Ask two questions: who were the parties to the communication, and what was its dominant purpose?
  • Check for conflicts before accepting instructions and keep checking as the matter develops.

Lessons in this school

  1. Professional ethics
    Professional ethics are the standards of behaviour expected of lawyers because of their special role: they serve clients, but also the courts, the rule of law and the public.
  2. SRA Principles
    The seven SRA Principles are the fundamental ethical requirements for everyone the SRA regulates.
  3. SRA Codes of Conduct
    The SRA has two Codes of Conduct: one for individuals (solicitors, registered European and foreign lawyers) and one for firms.
  4. Client confidentiality
    Solicitors must keep the affairs of current and former clients confidential unless disclosure is required or permitted by law or the client consents (Code, para 6.3).
  5. Legal professional privilege
    Legal professional privilege (LPP) protects certain communications between lawyers and clients from disclosure, even to a court.
  6. Conflicts of interest
    A solicitor must not act where there is a conflict of interests, or a significant risk of one, unless a narrow exception applies.
  7. Client money
    Client money is money held or received by a firm for or on behalf of a client or third party (and certain other categories).
  8. Solicitors' accounts
    The SRA Accounts Rules require firms to keep accurate accounting records, reconcile client accounts regularly and, in most cases, obtain an accountant's report.
  9. Client care
    Client care is about giving clients the information and service they need: who will do the work, what it will cost, what will happen and how to complain.
  10. Competence
    Solicitors must provide a proper standard of service, be competent to carry out their role and keep their professional knowledge and skills up to date (Code paras 3.2–3.6).
  11. Honesty and integrity
    Honesty and integrity are among the most important requirements of being a solicitor.
  12. Independence
    Solicitors must act with independence (Principle 3): their professional judgement must not be compromised by the interests of third parties, introducers, funders, employers or their own interests.
  13. Professional duties to the court
    Solicitors and barristers owe duties to the court that override their duties to clients.
  14. Undertakings
    An undertaking is a statement, given orally or in writing, by which a solicitor (or someone in a firm) agrees to do or not do something.
  15. Anti-money laundering
    Solicitors are a target for money launderers because they handle large sums and give credibility to transactions.
  16. Financial crime
    Beyond money laundering, solicitors must understand other financial crimes that can affect their practice or their clients: fraud, bribery, sanctions breaches, tax evasion facilitation and terrorist financing.
  17. Equality and diversity
    Solicitors must act in a way that encourages equality, diversity and inclusion (Principle 6), and must not unfairly discriminate (Code para 1.1).
  18. Complaints
    Clients who are unhappy with a firm's service have a right to complain, first to the firm and then to the Legal Ombudsman.
  19. Professional negligence
    A solicitor who fails to exercise the reasonable care and skill of a competent solicitor and causes loss may be liable to the client in contract and tort, and sometimes to third parties.
  20. Regulatory compliance
    Firms and individual solicitors must have systems to comply with regulatory requirements, cooperate with the SRA and report serious breaches.
  21. Duties to clients
    Solicitors owe clients duties under the SRA Principles and Code, in contract, in tort, and as fiduciaries.
  22. Duties to third parties
    Solicitors owe duties not only to clients but also to third parties, including the other side, witnesses and others affected by their work.
  23. Duties to the public
    Solicitors serve the public interest: upholding the rule of law and the administration of justice, maintaining public trust in the profession, and protecting the public from harm.
  24. Professional boundaries
    Professional boundaries keep the relationship between solicitor and client, and between colleagues, appropriate.
  25. Ethical decision-making
    Ethical decision-making is a structured way of working out the right course of action when duties, interests and pressures conflict.

Legal Ethics and Professional Conduct: questions students ask

Which regulator's handbook binds barristers?

The Bar Standards Board's BSB Handbook.

Case card: Wingate v Solicitors Regulation Authority [2018] EWCA Civ 366. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Two solicitors were found by the tribunal to have lacked integrity, without dishonesty being proved. Held: Integrity is a broader concept than honesty. Principle: A lack of integrity can be found without dishonesty: integrity connotes adherence to the ethical standards of the profession.

Which body can strike a solicitor off the roll?

The Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal.

Case card: Prince Jefri Bolkiah v KPMG [1999] 2 AC 222. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: KPMG had acted for Prince Jefri in litigation, then acted for the Brunei government investigating him, using information barriers. Held: KPMG was restrained: the barriers weren't effective enough to remove all risk. Principle: A firm holding a former client's confidential information must show there is no real risk of disclosure if it acts against them.
