Five sample flashcards

What is the overriding objective of the Criminal Procedure Rules?

That criminal cases be dealt with justly. From “The criminal justice system” →

In plain English: The criminal justice system investigates, prosecutes and tries offences, and punishes and rehabilitates offenders. Key idea: Know who does what, how offences are classified, and that the CrimPR overriding objective is to deal with cases justly.

Case card: R v Beckles [2004] EWCA Crim 2766. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Beckles made no comment in interview on his solicitor's advice; the jury was invited to draw an adverse inference. Held: Conviction quashed for misdirection: the jury must consider whether the silence was genuinely on advice or because he had no answer. Principle: Silence on legal advice: the question is whether D genuinely and reasonably relied on the advice, or was merely using it as cover. From “Police interviews” →

In plain English: A police interview is the questioning of a suspect about their involvement in an offence, under caution and recorded. Key idea: Weigh the strength of the disclosed evidence against the risk of s 34 inferences when advising on silence, a prepared statement or answering questions.

Can a private individual bring a prosecution?

Yes (s 6 POA 1985), subject to the DPP's power to take over and discontinue. From “Prosecution” →

In plain English: Most prosecutions are brought by the Crown Prosecution Service, which must act fairly, independently and in the public interest. Key idea: The prosecution acts as a minister of justice: independently, fairly and with full disclosure, and keeps cases under review.

Case card: R v Riat [2012] EWCA Crim 1509. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Several appeals concerned the admission of hearsay statements of absent witnesses. Held: The court set out a structured approach to the statutory hearsay provisions. Principle: Hearsay admission needs a statutory gateway, then consideration of reliability, safeguards and the court's power to exclude or stop the case. From “Hearsay” →

In plain English: Hearsay is a statement made out of court and offered to prove the truth of what it says. Key idea: Identify hearsay, then find a gateway in s 114, and consider the safeguards in ss 78, 125 and 126.

Which case is authority for this? Expert evidence is admissible only on matters outside the ordinary experience of the tribunal of fact.

R v Turner [1975] QB 834 From “Expert evidence” →

In plain English: Expert evidence gives the court opinions on matters beyond ordinary knowledge, such as forensic science or medicine. Key idea: Admit expert evidence only on matters beyond the jury's knowledge, from a qualified, independent expert, with reliable methods.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Criminal Law and Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where is an indictable-only offence tried? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Crown Court. Indictable-only offences are tried in the Crown Court.

  2. Which PACE Code governs identification procedures? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Code D. PACE Code D governs identification procedures such as video identification.

  3. Which statute contains the power to search for controlled drugs on reasonable suspicion? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Misuse of Drugs Act 1971. Section 23 Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 allows searches for controlled drugs on reasonable suspicion.

  4. Which body decides charges in most serious cases? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Crown Prosecution Service. The CPS makes most charging decisions under the Director's Guidance.

  5. Police arrest a suspect for burglary and want to search his flat for stolen goods. What power may they use? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Section 18, with an inspector's authority. Section 18 allows searching premises the arrested person occupies or controls.

  6. An officer searches a teenager in a hoodie solely because young people in hoodies often carry knives. Is the search lawful under s 1? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No: generalisations aren't reasonable suspicion. Code A forbids suspicion based on personal factors or stereotypes.

  7. Police disclose very little before interview. The client has a strong alibi. What strategy might the solicitor consider? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A prepared statement setting out the alibi. A prepared statement can put the alibi on record while limiting risk.

  8. The prosecution says the defendant may contact a witness who lives nearby. What condition could address this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Not to contact the witness or enter her street. Targeted conditions address specific risks.

  9. A suspect with a learning disability confesses after an interview without an appropriate adult or solicitor. What is the likely argument? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Exclusion under s 76(2)(b) for unreliability. McGovern: such circumstances are likely to render a confession unreliable.

Key takeaways

  • Know who does what, how offences are classified, and that the CrimPR overriding objective is to deal with cases justly.
  • PACE and its Codes govern police powers; breaches can lead to exclusion of evidence under s 78.
  • A s 1 PACE search needs objective reasonable suspicion, the GOWISELY information and a record.
  • Police arrest needs reasonable suspicion plus necessity; tell the person the grounds and caution them.
  • The custody officer authorises detention only if necessary; strict time limits apply, and suspects have rights to legal advice and to have someone informed.
  • Weigh the strength of the disclosed evidence against the risk of s 34 inferences when advising on silence, a prepared statement or answering questions.

Common mistakes in Criminal Law and Practice

  • Assuming the police decide all charges: the CPS decides most.
  • Assuming every Code breach makes the evidence inadmissible.
  • Treating a person's age or clothing alone as reasonable grounds to search.
  • Arresting someone who is fully cooperating and could be interviewed voluntarily, without considering necessity.
  • Assuming police can always delay access to a solicitor: delay is exceptional and needs authority.
  • Advising silence without recording the reasons, leaving the client unable to show genuine and reasonable reliance.

Lessons in this school

  1. The criminal justice system
    The criminal justice system investigates, prosecutes and tries offences, and punishes and rehabilitates offenders.
  2. Police powers
    The Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE) and its Codes of Practice regulate police powers to stop, search, enter premises, seize evidence, arrest, detain and question.
  3. Stop and search
    Stop and search allows police to search people and vehicles in public for prohibited items.
  4. Arrest
    A police officer may arrest without warrant under s 24 of PACE if they have reasonable grounds to suspect an offence and believe arrest is necessary for a listed reason.
  5. Detention and questioning
    After arrest, the custody officer decides whether to detain the suspect without charge.
  6. Police interviews
    A police interview is the questioning of a suspect about their involvement in an offence, under caution and recorded.
  7. PACE
    The Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 is the central statute on police powers and the treatment of suspects.
  8. Disclosure
    Disclosure in criminal cases ensures the defence receives material that might undermine the prosecution or assist the defence.
  9. Bail
    Bail is release from custody while proceedings continue.
  10. Charging decisions
    Charging decisions are made by the police for minor offences and by the CPS for most others, applying the Code for Crown Prosecutors.
  11. Prosecution
    Most prosecutions are brought by the Crown Prosecution Service, which must act fairly, independently and in the public interest.
  12. Magistrates' Court
    Magistrates' courts deal with almost all criminal cases at first, and try most summary and many either-way offences.
  13. Crown Court
    The Crown Court tries indictable offences before a judge and jury, and hears either-way offences sent or elected for trial.
  14. Allocation of offences
    Either-way offences can be tried in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court.
  15. Plea and trial preparation
    Effective case preparation means early identification of the issues, timely disclosure and pleas.
  16. Evidence
    Criminal evidence law decides what facts must be proved, by whom, and what evidence can be used to prove them.
  17. Hearsay
    Hearsay is a statement made out of court and offered to prove the truth of what it says.
  18. Confessions
    A confession is any statement adverse to the person who made it.
  19. Identification evidence
    Eyewitness identification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions.
  20. Bad character evidence
    Evidence of a defendant's or witness's bad character, such as previous convictions, is admissible only through the gateways in the Criminal Justice Act 2003.
  21. Witnesses
    Witnesses give oral evidence that is tested by cross-examination.
  22. Expert evidence
    Expert evidence gives the court opinions on matters beyond ordinary knowledge, such as forensic science or medicine.
  23. Trial procedure
    A criminal trial follows a set structure: the prosecution opens and calls evidence, the defence may submit there is no case to answer, then presents its evidence, and both sides make closing speeches.
  24. Sentencing
    Sentencing is governed by the Sentencing Act 2020 (the Sentencing Code) and Sentencing Council guidelines.
  25. Appeals
    Criminal appeals allow convictions and sentences to be challenged.
  26. Criminal legal aid
    Criminal legal aid funds advice and representation for people suspected or accused of crimes.
  27. Professional conduct in criminal practice
    Criminal defence work raises distinctive ethical issues.

Criminal Law and Practice: questions students ask

What does s 18 PACE allow?

Search of premises occupied or controlled by a person arrested for an indictable offence, for evidence of that or a related offence.

What does GOWISELY stand for?

Grounds, Object, Warrant card, Identity, Station, Entitlement to a record, Legal power, You are detained.

What two conditions does s 24 PACE require for arrest?

Reasonable grounds to suspect an offence and reasonable grounds to believe arrest is necessary.

What is the basic time limit for detention without charge?

24 hours from the relevant time.
