Case card: Earl of Oxford's Case (1615) 1 Ch Rep 1. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A common law judgment was obtained by fraud, and the Chancellor issued a common injunction restraining its enforcement. Held: Lord Ellesmere held that equity could act on the conscience of the party, and James I backed the Chancery. Principle: Where common law and equity conflict, equity prevails (now Senior Courts Act 1981, s 49). From “Historical development of equity” →

In plain English: Equity grew up to correct the rigidity of the common law. Key idea: Equity supplements the common law; after the Judicature Acts both are administered together, and equity prevails where they conflict.

Case card: Vandervell v IRC [1967] 2 AC 291. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Vandervell transferred shares to the Royal College of Surgeons, with an option to repurchase held by his trustee company on undefined trusts. Held: The option was held on resulting trust for him, so he was taxed on the dividends. Principle: Where a beneficial interest isn't effectively disposed of, it results to the transferor. From “Resulting trusts” →

In plain English: Resulting trusts carry the beneficial interest back to the person who provided the property. Key idea: Presumed resulting trusts depend on rebuttable presumptions; automatic ones arise when a trust fails, including Quistclose loans.

Which case is authority for this? A gift must be completed by the method appropriate to the property; a failed transfer isn't treated as a declaration of trust.

Milroy v Lord (1862) 4 De GF and J 264 From “Constitution of trusts” →

In plain English: A trust is completely constituted only when the trust property is vested in the trustees. Key idea: Unless the property is vested in the trustees, equity won't help a volunteer, save for the recognised exceptions.

Case card: Re Hallett's Estate (1880) 13 Ch D 696. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A solicitor mixed client trust money with his own and withdrew sums for himself. Held: He was presumed to have spent his own money first. Principle: When tracing into a mixed account, a trustee is presumed to spend their own money first. From “Tracing” →

In plain English: Tracing is the process of identifying new property as a substitute for the original, so that a claimant can assert proprietary rights in it. Key idea: Tracing identifies substitute assets; the rules in Hallett, Oatway and Roscoe v Winder protect beneficiaries against wrongdoing trustees.

Which case is authority for this? A fully secret trust needs intention, communication to the legatee in the testator's lifetime and acceptance.

Ottaway v Norman [1972] Ch 698 From “Secret trusts” →

In plain English: Secret trusts allow a testator to leave property by will to someone who has agreed to hold it on trust for another, without the trust appearing in the will. Key idea: Fully secret: communication and acceptance before death. Half-secret: communication before or at the time of the will.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Equity and Trusts

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statute now provides that equity prevails over the common law where they conflict? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Senior Courts Act 1981, s 49. Section 49 continues the Judicature Acts rule.

  2. Which maxim underlies Walsh v Lonsdale? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Equity regards as done that which ought to be done. A contract for a lease is treated as if the lease had been granted.

  3. Who creates an express trust? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The settlor. The settlor creates the trust by transfer or declaration.

  4. What is the "two streams" view of fusion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Law and equity remain distinct, administered together. The two streams view says only administration was fused.

  5. A creditor is pressured into accepting less than owed because the debtor knows the creditor is desperate. The debtor relies on promissory estoppel. What is the likely result? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Estoppel fails: no clean hands. D and C Builders v Rees: intimidation meant the debtor lacked clean hands.

  6. Property is held on trust for A, aged 30, absolutely. A wants it transferred now, though the trust says to wait until A is 35. Can A demand it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Yes, under Saunders v Vautier. A is an adult beneficiary absolutely entitled, so can end the trust.

  7. A company borrows money "solely to pay employees' wages" and goes into liquidation before paying them. Who is entitled to the money? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The lender, under a Quistclose trust. The specific purpose failed, so the money is held on trust for the lender.

  8. A trustee buys a house from the trust at its full market value without the beneficiaries' consent. A beneficiary objects. What is the position? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The sale is voidable under the self-dealing rule. The self-dealing rule allows any beneficiary to set aside the sale, however fair.

  9. A trustee mixes £10,000 of trust money with £10,000 of his own, buys shares with £10,000, then spends the rest on holidays. Can the beneficiaries claim the shares? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Yes, under Re Oatway. Re Oatway: where the remaining money is dissipated, the beneficiaries can claim the asset bought.

Key takeaways

  • Equity supplements the common law; after the Judicature Acts both are administered together, and equity prevails where they conflict.
  • Use the maxims to explain and test equitable intervention: conscience, clean hands, delay and the refusal to help volunteers.
  • A trust separates legal and equitable ownership and imposes binding obligations on the trustee.
  • An express trust is created by transfer or declaration, satisfies the three certainties, and meets any formality for land, wills or dispositions.
  • Resulting trusts return benefit to the transferor; constructive trusts respond to unconscionability. Neither needs writing.
  • Presumed resulting trusts depend on rebuttable presumptions; automatic ones arise when a trust fails, including Quistclose loans.

Common mistakes in Equity and Trusts

  • Thinking equity was abolished in 1875: only the separate courts were.
  • Treating the maxims as rigid rules rather than guiding principles.
  • Confusing a power (discretion) with a trust (obligation).
  • Overlooking s 53(1)(c) when a beneficiary tries to transfer their equitable interest orally.
  • Applying s 53(1)(b) to defeat a constructive trust of land.
  • Assuming the presumption of advancement has been abolished: s 199 of the Equality Act 2010 isn't in force.

Lessons in this school

  1. Historical development of equity
    Equity grew up to correct the rigidity of the common law.
  2. Equitable principles
    Equity's maxims summarise the principles guiding equitable intervention.
  3. Trusts
    A trust arises where a trustee holds property for the benefit of beneficiaries or for a permitted purpose.
  4. Express trusts
    An express trust is deliberately created by a settlor, either by declaring themselves trustee or by transferring property to trustees.
  5. Implied trusts
    Implied trusts arise without an express declaration.
  6. Resulting trusts
    Resulting trusts carry the beneficial interest back to the person who provided the property.
  7. Constructive trusts
    A constructive trust is imposed by law where it would be unconscionable for the legal owner to deny another's beneficial interest.
  8. Certainty of intention
    The first of the three certainties is that the settlor must intend to create a trust: to impose a binding obligation on the trustee, not merely express a hope or wish.
  9. Certainty of subject matter
    A trust must identify the property held on trust and, where relevant, each beneficiary's share.
  10. Certainty of objects
    A private trust must have beneficiaries who can be identified with sufficient certainty.
  11. Constitution of trusts
    A trust is completely constituted only when the trust property is vested in the trustees.
  12. Trustees' duties
    Trustees must follow the trust terms, act in the beneficiaries' best interests, and meet the standards of care set by statute and equity.
  13. Trustees' powers
    Trustees have powers to invest, delegate, maintain and advance, and appoint new trustees.
  14. Fiduciary duties
    A fiduciary is someone who has undertaken to act for another in circumstances giving rise to a relationship of trust and confidence.
  15. Breach of trust
    A trustee who breaches the trust is personally liable to restore the trust fund or compensate the beneficiaries.
  16. Equitable remedies
    Equity provides remedies the common law can't: specific performance, injunctions, rescission, rectification and account of profits.
  17. Tracing
    Tracing is the process of identifying new property as a substitute for the original, so that a claimant can assert proprietary rights in it.
  18. Knowing receipt
    A person who receives trust property transferred in breach of trust, for their own benefit, is personally liable to account for it if their knowledge makes it unconscionable to retain the benefit.
  19. Dishonest assistance
    A person who dishonestly assists a breach of trust or fiduciary duty is personally liable for the loss, even if they never received any trust property.
  20. Charitable trusts
    Charitable trusts are purpose trusts for the public benefit.
  21. Purpose trusts
    A private trust must generally have human beneficiaries who can enforce it: the beneficiary principle.
  22. Secret trusts
    Secret trusts allow a testator to leave property by will to someone who has agreed to hold it on trust for another, without the trust appearing in the will.
  23. Trusts of land
    Trusts of land are governed by the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996.
  24. Co-ownership
    When couples or families share a home, equity decides who owns the beneficial interest and in what shares.
  25. Wills and testamentary trusts
    Many trusts are created by will.
  26. Equitable interests
    Equitable interests arise under trusts and other equitable doctrines.
  27. Remedies and defences
    Claimants for breach of trust and fiduciary duty can bring personal claims against wrongdoers and recipients, and proprietary claims against identifiable assets.

Equity and Trusts: questions students ask

What does "equity will not assist a volunteer" mean?

Equity won't perfect an incomplete gift for someone who gave no consideration.

Case card: Saunders v Vautier (1841) 4 Beav 115. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Stock was left on trust to accumulate dividends until the beneficiary was 25; at 21 he claimed the fund. Held: He was entitled to have the fund transferred to him once he was an adult. Principle: Adult beneficiaries of full capacity, together absolutely entitled, can end the trust and call for the property.

Case card: Grey v IRC [1960] AC 1. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A settlor orally directed trustees to hold shares on other trusts, to avoid stamp duty on a written transfer. Held: The oral direction was a disposition of an equitable interest, void for lack of writing. Principle: A direction to trustees to hold for another is a disposition needing writing under LPA 1925, s 53(1)(c).

Case card: Westdeutsche Landesbank Girozentrale v Islington LBC [1996] AC 669. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A bank paid money to a council under an interest rate swap later found void, and claimed compound interest on the basis of a resulting trust. Held: No resulting trust arose; the bank had a personal claim in restitution only. Principle: A trust requires the trustee's conscience to be affected; Lord Browne-Wilkinson set out when resulting trusts arise.
