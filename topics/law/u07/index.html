Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three key questions in a land law problem?

What right is claimed, was it validly created, and does it bind the current holder of the land? From “Introduction to land law” →

In plain English: Land law governs the rights people can hold in land: who owns it, who can use it, and whose rights win when they conflict. Key idea: Ask three questions: what right is claimed, was it validly created, and does it bind the person now holding the land?

What are the three registers in a registered title?

The property, proprietorship and charges registers. From “Registered land” →

In plain English: Most land in England and Wales is now registered under the Land Registration Act 2002. Key idea: Registration makes the register conclusive as to the legal title and protects buyers, subject to overriding interests.

Case card: Jones v Kernott [2011] UKSC 53. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: An unmarried couple bought a house in joint names; after they separated he stopped contributing for about 14 years. Held: Their intentions changed after separation; shares of 90% and 10% were the fair result. Principle: Where intention about shares can't be inferred, the court may impute the intention it considers fair in light of the whole course of dealing. From “Tenancy in common” →

In plain English: Tenants in common each have a distinct share in the land, which they can sell, mortgage or leave by will. Key idea: In domestic joint-name cases, equity follows the law (joint tenancy) unless a different common intention is shown; commercial cases favour a tenancy in common.

Case card: Cuckmere Brick Co Ltd v Mutual Finance Ltd [1971] Ch 949. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A mortgagee sold land advertising planning permission for houses but not for flats, which would have raised the price. Held: The mortgagee was liable for the shortfall. Principle: A selling mortgagee owes a duty to take reasonable care to obtain the true market value at the time of sale. From “Mortgages” →

In plain English: A mortgage is security for a debt: the borrower (mortgagor) grants the lender (mortgagee) rights over land that can be enforced if the loan isn't repaid. Key idea: A modern legal mortgage is a charge by deed; equity prevents clogs on redemption, and statute lets courts give residential borrowers time.

Case card: Link Lending Ltd v Bustard [2010] EWCA Civ 424. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: The registered owner, detained in a psychiatric unit, had been tricked into transferring her home, which was then mortgaged. Held: She was still in actual occupation, so her right to set aside the transfer bound the lender. Principle: Actual occupation can continue during involuntary absence where there is continued intention to return and a physical link to the property. From “Priorities and third-party rights” →

In plain English: Priority rules decide whether a buyer or lender takes land subject to an existing third-party right. Key idea: For a buyer for value: check notices and restrictions, and inspect for overriding interests, especially people in actual occupation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Land Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statute reduced the number of legal estates to two? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Law of Property Act 1925. Section 1 of the LPA 1925 limits legal estates to the fee simple and the term of years.

  2. Which test is now the more important in deciding whether an object is a fixture? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Purpose of annexation. Courts treat purpose as decisive, with degree raising a presumption.

  3. Which is NOT capable of being a legal estate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A life interest. Since 1925, a life interest can exist only in equity behind a trust.

  4. A neighbour lets you cross her field as a favour. She sells the field. Is the buyer bound? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Probably not: a bare licence is personal. A bare licence is a personal permission that doesn't bind a successor.

  5. A seller removes a fitted kitchen and a freestanding fridge before completion. The contract is silent. What is the likely position? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The kitchen is a fixture; the fridge is a chattel. Botham v TSB: fitted units were fixtures; white goods were chattels.

  6. What does the doctrine of tenure say about land ownership? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    All land is held from the Crown. In theory, only the Crown owns land outright; others hold estates.

  7. H, the sole registered owner, mortgages the house to a bank. W contributed to the price and lives there. Is W's interest overreached? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: only one trustee received the money. Boland: with a sole trustee, there was no overreaching, so W's interest bound the bank.

  8. When A sells part of his land to B, A's tenant on that part already used A's driveway with permission. The conveyance says nothing about it. What might give B a legal easement? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Section 62 LPA 1925. Wright v Macadam: s 62 can convert a permission enjoyed with the land into a legal easement on conveyance.

  9. A lender sells a repossessed house quickly at auction without advertising its planning permission, getting a low price. What can the borrower claim? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Breach of the duty to get the true market value. Cuckmere Brick: failing to mention planning permission breached the duty of care in selling.

Key takeaways

  • Ask three questions: what right is claimed, was it validly created, and does it bind the person now holding the land?
  • Fixtures pass with the land; chattels don't. Purpose of annexation matters more than degree.
  • Only the freehold and the leasehold can be legal estates; everything else takes effect in equity.
  • A legal freehold must be absolute and in possession; determinable fees take effect only in equity.
  • Over three years, a legal lease needs a deed; over seven years, registration too. Otherwise, a written contract may create an equitable lease.
  • Check whether the interest is in s 1(2) and was created by deed; if not, it can only be equitable.

Common mistakes in Land Law

  • Treating every agreement about land as creating a proprietary right: many create only personal rights.
  • Assuming everything attached to a house is a fixture: the purpose of the attachment decides.
  • Assuming a life interest can exist as a legal estate.
  • Confusing a determinable fee (ends automatically) with a conditional fee (gives a right of re-entry).
  • Forgetting that a short lease must be at the best rent and in possession to fall within s 54(2).
  • Assuming a restrictive covenant can be a legal interest: it is always equitable.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to land law
    Land law governs the rights people can hold in land: who owns it, who can use it, and whose rights win when they conflict.
  2. Nature of land
    "Land" in law means more than the soil.
  3. Estates in land
    English law doesn't recognise outright ownership of land by anyone but the Crown.
  4. Freehold estates
    The fee simple absolute in possession is the freehold estate: the largest estate in land, lasting indefinitely and freely transferable.
  5. Leasehold estates
    The term of years absolute is the leasehold estate.
  6. Legal and equitable interests
    Interests in land can be legal or equitable.
  7. Registered land
    Most land in England and Wales is now registered under the Land Registration Act 2002.
  8. Unregistered land
    A shrinking minority of land is still unregistered.
  9. Land registration
    Land registration involves first registration of unregistered land and the registration of later dealings.
  10. Title investigation
    Title investigation is the buyer's solicitor checking that the seller owns the land and can sell it free of unexpected burdens.
  11. Co-ownership
    Co-ownership arises whenever two or more people own land at the same time.
  12. Joint tenancy
    A joint tenancy treats co-owners as a single owner: each owns the whole, and no one has a separate share.
  13. Tenancy in common
    Tenants in common each have a distinct share in the land, which they can sell, mortgage or leave by will.
  14. Severance
    Severance converts a beneficial joint tenancy into a tenancy in common, so that the severing co-owner's share no longer passes by survivorship.
  15. Trusts of land
    The Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996 governs trusts of land, including all co-owned land.
  16. Overreaching
    Overreaching allows a buyer of trust land to take free of the beneficiaries' interests, which shift from the land into the purchase money.
  17. Easements
    An easement is a right enjoyed by one piece of land (the dominant tenement) over another (the servient tenement), such as a right of way or a right to light.
  18. Covenants
    A covenant is a promise in a deed.
  19. Mortgages
    A mortgage is security for a debt: the borrower (mortgagor) grants the lender (mortgagee) rights over land that can be enforced if the loan isn't repaid.
  20. Proprietary estoppel
    Proprietary estoppel prevents a landowner from going back on an assurance that someone would have rights in land, where that person relied on it to their detriment and it would be unconscionable to deny the right.
  21. Adverse possession
    Adverse possession allows a person in long, factual possession of another's land, with the intention to possess, to acquire title.
  22. Leases
    A lease gives exclusive possession of land for a term certain.
  23. Licences
    A licence is a permission to be on land that would otherwise be a trespass.
  24. Landlord and tenant relationships
    The landlord and tenant relationship is shaped by the lease, implied covenants and a large body of statute.
  25. Priorities and third-party rights
    Priority rules decide whether a buyer or lender takes land subject to an existing third-party right.
  26. Restrictive covenants
    A restrictive covenant is a promise not to do something on land, such as not to build or not to run a business.
  27. Freehold covenants
    Positive freehold covenants, such as obligations to repair or contribute to costs, don't bind later owners of the burdened land.
  28. Land disputes
    Land disputes include boundary disputes, rights of way, trespass, nuisance and disagreements between co-owners.
  29. Property transactions
    A property transaction moves through three main stages: pre-contract, exchange of contracts and completion, followed by post-completion steps.
  30. Conveyancing fundamentals
    Conveyancing is the legal work of transferring land.

Land Law: questions students ask

Case card: Holland v Hodgson (1872) LR 7 CP 328. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Looms in a mill were attached to the floor by nails; the question was whether they passed with the land to a mortgagee. Held: They were fixtures and passed with the land. Principle: Whether an object is a fixture depends on the degree and purpose of annexation.

What are the two legal estates?

The fee simple absolute in possession and the term of years absolute.

What does "in possession" mean for a fee simple?

It takes effect now, including receipt of rents and profits.

When can a legal lease be created without a deed?

A lease in possession for not more than three years at the best rent without a fine (s 54(2) LPA 1925).
