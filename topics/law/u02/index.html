What does "uncodified" mean for the UK constitution?

It isn't contained in a single document. From “The UK constitution” →

In plain English: The UK constitution is uncodified: there is no single document setting out the rules of government. Key idea: The UK constitution is uncodified and flexible, drawn from statute, common law, conventions, prerogative and authoritative works.

Case card: Carltona Ltd v Commissioners of Works [1943] 2 All ER 560. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A factory was requisitioned in wartime by an official acting in the minister's name. Held: Valid: the official's decision was the minister's. Principle: The Carltona principle: officials may exercise a minister's powers on the minister's behalf. From “Executive power” →

In plain English: The executive is the government: the Crown, the Prime Minister, Cabinet, ministers and the civil service. Key idea: Executive power comes from statute, prerogative and the third source; Carltona lets officials act for ministers.

What did Osborn v Parole Board emphasise?

That common law rights should be considered alongside, and often before, Convention rights. From “Human rights and constitutional protection” →

In plain English: Rights in the UK are protected by the common law, statute (especially the Human Rights Act 1998), and parliamentary scrutiny. Key idea: Rights are protected by common law, the HRA and Parliament, but aren't entrenched.

What is the Wednesbury test?

A decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have come to it. From “Irrationality” →

In plain English: Irrationality, or Wednesbury unreasonableness, allows the courts to quash decisions so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made them. Key idea: Irrationality requires a decision outside the range of reasonable responses; the intensity of review rises with the importance of the rights involved.

What is a suspended quashing order?

A quashing order that takes effect after a set time, allowing the defect to be remedied. From “Remedies” →

In plain English: Judicial review remedies are discretionary. Key idea: Choose quashing, prohibiting or mandatory orders, declarations or injunctions; relief is discretionary and may be suspended or prospective.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Constitutional and Administrative Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an authoritative work on parliamentary procedure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Erskine May. Erskine May's Parliamentary Practice is the leading work on procedure.

  2. Which case recognised the convention of collective Cabinet responsibility but declined to enforce it as such? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Attorney-General v Jonathan Cape. The court restrained publication on confidentiality grounds, not the convention itself.

  3. Which judge set out the constitutional statute doctrine in Thoburn? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Laws LJ. Laws LJ gave the leading judgment, describing a hierarchy of statutes.

  4. Why is the UK constitution described as flexible? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It can be changed by ordinary legislation. No special amendment procedure is required.

  5. A minister breaches the convention of collective responsibility by publicly attacking Cabinet policy. What is the likely consequence? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Political: pressure to resign. Conventions carry political, not legal, consequences.

  6. A new Act conflicts with an earlier constitutional statute but doesn't mention it. What would the Thoburn approach suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The earlier constitutional statute survives. Under Thoburn, implied repeal doesn't apply to constitutional statutes.

  7. An Act states that decisions of a tribunal "shall not be called in question in any court". A claimant says the tribunal made an error of law. What approach might the courts take? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Read the ouster narrowly. Anisminic and Privacy International show courts read ouster clauses narrowly.

  8. A government advises a five-week prorogation to avoid parliamentary scrutiny at a critical time, giving no reason. Following Miller II, what is likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The prorogation is unlawful and void. Miller II applied the principle of parliamentary accountability.

  9. The government wants to withdraw from a treaty that statute has incorporated, removing rights given by that statute. Can it do so by prerogative? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: changing statutory rights needs Parliament. Miller (2017) required an Act of Parliament.

Key takeaways

  • The UK constitution is uncodified and flexible, drawn from statute, common law, conventions, prerogative and authoritative works.
  • Conventions bind politically, not legally; courts recognise but don't enforce them.
  • Constitutional statutes resist implied repeal; Parliament must amend them expressly.
  • Sovereignty survived EU membership through statute, and courts read ouster clauses narrowly; obiter limits remain untested.
  • Courts enforce the rule of law through the principle of legality and by protecting access to courts.
  • Courts police the boundaries: ministers are subject to the law, the prerogative can't frustrate statute, and prorogation can't stop Parliament without justification.

Common mistakes in Constitutional and Administrative Law

  • Saying the UK has "no constitution": it has one, just not codified in a single document.
  • Arguing that the Sewel convention is legally enforceable because it appears in the Scotland Act.
  • Assuming constitutional statutes can never be changed: they can, but only by clear words.
  • Treating the Jackson obiter as settled law that courts can strike down Acts.
  • Assuming general statutory words can override fundamental rights without clear language.
  • Assuming ministers are immune from court orders because they act for the Crown.

Lessons in this school

  1. The UK constitution
    The UK constitution is uncodified: there is no single document setting out the rules of government.
  2. Constitutional conventions
    Constitutional conventions are non-legal rules that regulate how constitutional actors behave.
  3. Constitutional statutes
    Some statutes are recognised by the courts as "constitutional statutes" because they deal with the relationship between citizen and state or fundamental rights.
  4. Parliamentary sovereignty
    Parliamentary sovereignty is the central principle of the UK constitution.
  5. The rule of law
    The rule of law requires that government acts under law and that law is accessible, applied equally and enforced by independent courts.
  6. Separation of powers
    The separation of powers in the UK is partial, but courts use it to police the boundaries between executive, legislature and judiciary.
  7. Executive power
    The executive is the government: the Crown, the Prime Minister, Cabinet, ministers and the civil service.
  8. Royal prerogative
    The royal prerogative is the residue of discretionary power left in the Crown.
  9. Parliament and legislative scrutiny
    Parliament holds the government to account and scrutinises legislation.
  10. Devolution
    Devolution transferred power from Westminster to legislatures and executives in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  11. The UK Supreme Court
    The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal for civil cases across the UK and for criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  12. Judicial independence
    Judicial independence is protected in the UK by statute, convention and institutional design.
  13. Human rights and constitutional protection
    Rights in the UK are protected by the common law, statute (especially the Human Rights Act 1998), and parliamentary scrutiny.
  14. The Human Rights Act 1998
    The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates most ECHR rights into UK law.
  15. The European Convention on Human Rights
    The European Convention on Human Rights is a treaty of the Council of Europe, signed in 1950.
  16. Judicial review
    Judicial review is the procedure by which the High Court supervises the lawfulness of decisions by public bodies.
  17. Grounds of judicial review
    The grounds of judicial review were classified by Lord Diplock in the GCHQ case as illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, with proportionality as a possible future ground.
  18. Illegality
    Illegality covers decisions where the public body misunderstood or exceeded its legal powers.
  19. Irrationality
    Irrationality, or Wednesbury unreasonableness, allows the courts to quash decisions so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made them.
  20. Procedural impropriety
    Procedural impropriety covers failures to follow statutory procedures and the common law rules of natural justice: the rule against bias and the right to a fair hearing.
  21. Legitimate expectations
    A legitimate expectation arises when a public body's promise or established practice leads someone to expect a particular procedure or outcome.
  22. Proportionality
    Proportionality asks whether a measure that interferes with rights is justified: whether its aim is important enough, whether it is rationally connected to that aim, whether a less intrusive measure w
  23. Standing
    To bring judicial review, a claimant must have a "sufficient interest" in the matter (Senior Courts Act 1981, s 31(3)).
  24. Justiciability
    Some decisions are unsuitable for judicial resolution because they involve high policy, national security, foreign affairs or matters better decided by Parliament.
  25. Remedies
    Judicial review remedies are discretionary.
  26. Administrative tribunals
    Tribunals decide disputes between individuals and the state across areas such as social security, immigration, tax and employment.
  27. Public authorities
    Who counts as a public authority matters for judicial review and for duties under the Human Rights Act.
  28. Government accountability
    Government is accountable to Parliament, the courts, independent bodies and the public.
  29. Constitutional reform
    The UK constitution has changed significantly since 1997: devolution, the Human Rights Act, House of Lords reform, the Supreme Court, Brexit and changes to the timing of elections.
  30. Contemporary constitutional challenges
    The UK constitution faces pressures from Brexit's legacy, tensions within the Union, clashes between government and the courts, debates over the ECHR, and questions about executive power.

Constitutional and Administrative Law: questions students ask

Case card: R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union [2017] UKSC 5. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: The government planned to give notice under Article 50 to leave the EU using prerogative powers. Held: An Act of Parliament was needed, because withdrawal would change domestic law and remove rights; the Sewel Convention wasn't legally enforceable. Principle: The prerogative can't be used to change domestic law or rights, and conventions are not enforced by courts.

Case card: R (HS2 Action Alliance Ltd) v Secretary of State for Transport [2014] UKSC 3. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Objectors argued that EU directives required greater scrutiny of the HS2 hybrid Bill than Parliament's procedures gave. Held: The challenge failed; the court mustn't question Parliament's internal procedures. Principle: There may be fundamental constitutional principles (such as article 9 of the Bill of Rights) that EU law can't be taken to abrogate.

Case card: R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal [2019] UKSC 22. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A statute said the Investigatory Powers Tribunal's decisions 'shall not be subject to appeal or be liable to be questioned in any court'. Held: The ouster clause didn't protect decisions vitiated by errors of law. Principle: Ouster clauses are read narrowly, and courts presume Parliament doesn't intend to exclude review for errors of law.

Case card: R (Evans) v Attorney General [2015] UKSC 21. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: The Attorney General vetoed a tribunal's decision that Prince Charles's letters to ministers should be disclosed under FOI. Held: The veto was unlawful: a minister can't override a judicial decision merely because he disagrees with it. Principle: The rule of law limits the use of an executive power to override judicial decisions.
