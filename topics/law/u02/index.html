Case card: Carltona Ltd v Commissioners of Works [1943] 2 All ER 560. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A factory was requisitioned in wartime by an official acting in the minister's name. Held: Valid: the official's decision was the minister's. Principle: The Carltona principle: officials may exercise a minister's powers on the minister's behalf. From “Executive power” →

What did Osborn v Parole Board emphasise?

That common law rights should be considered alongside, and often before, Convention rights. From “Human rights and constitutional protection” →

What is the Wednesbury test?

A decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have come to it. From “Irrationality” →

What is a suspended quashing order?

Lessons in this school

  1. The UK constitution
    The UK constitution is uncodified: there is no single document setting out the rules of government.
  2. Constitutional conventions
    Constitutional conventions are non-legal rules that regulate how constitutional actors behave.
  3. Constitutional statutes
    Some statutes are recognised by the courts as "constitutional statutes" because they deal with the relationship between citizen and state or fundamental rights.
  4. Parliamentary sovereignty
    Parliamentary sovereignty is the central principle of the UK constitution.
  5. The rule of law
    The rule of law requires that government acts under law and that law is accessible, applied equally and enforced by independent courts.
  6. Separation of powers
    The separation of powers in the UK is partial, but courts use it to police the boundaries between executive, legislature and judiciary.
  7. Executive power
    The executive is the government: the Crown, the Prime Minister, Cabinet, ministers and the civil service.
  8. Royal prerogative
    The royal prerogative is the residue of discretionary power left in the Crown.
  9. Parliament and legislative scrutiny
    Parliament holds the government to account and scrutinises legislation.
  10. Devolution
    Devolution transferred power from Westminster to legislatures and executives in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  11. The UK Supreme Court
    The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal for civil cases across the UK and for criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  12. Judicial independence
    Judicial independence is protected in the UK by statute, convention and institutional design.
  13. Human rights and constitutional protection
    Rights in the UK are protected by the common law, statute (especially the Human Rights Act 1998), and parliamentary scrutiny.
  14. The Human Rights Act 1998
    The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates most ECHR rights into UK law.
  15. The European Convention on Human Rights
    The European Convention on Human Rights is a treaty of the Council of Europe, signed in 1950.
  16. Judicial review
    Judicial review is the procedure by which the High Court supervises the lawfulness of decisions by public bodies.
  17. Grounds of judicial review
    The grounds of judicial review were classified by Lord Diplock in the GCHQ case as illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, with proportionality as a possible future ground.
  18. Illegality
    Illegality covers decisions where the public body misunderstood or exceeded its legal powers.
  19. Irrationality
    Irrationality, or Wednesbury unreasonableness, allows the courts to quash decisions so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made them.
  20. Procedural impropriety
    Procedural impropriety covers failures to follow statutory procedures and the common law rules of natural justice: the rule against bias and the right to a fair hearing.
  21. Legitimate expectations
    A legitimate expectation arises when a public body's promise or established practice leads someone to expect a particular procedure or outcome.
  22. Proportionality

  23. Standing
    To bring judicial review, a claimant must have a "sufficient interest" in the matter (Senior Courts Act 1981, s 31(3)).
  24. Justiciability
    Some decisions are unsuitable for judicial resolution because they involve high policy, national security, foreign affairs or matters better decided by Parliament.
  25. Remedies
    Judicial review remedies are discretionary.
  26. Administrative tribunals
    Tribunals decide disputes between individuals and the state across areas such as social security, immigration, tax and employment.
  27. Public authorities
    Who counts as a public authority matters for judicial review and for duties under the Human Rights Act.
  28. Government accountability
    Government is accountable to Parliament, the courts, independent bodies and the public.
  29. Constitutional reform
    The UK constitution has changed significantly since 1997: devolution, the Human Rights Act, House of Lords reform, the Supreme Court, Brexit and changes to the timing of elections.
  30. Contemporary constitutional challenges
    The UK constitution faces pressures from Brexit's legacy, tensions within the Union, clashes between government and the courts, debates over the ECHR, and questions about executive power.

Key terms

Uncodified constitution
A constitution not contained in a single document
Convention
A non-legal rule regarded as binding by those it applies to
Constitutional statute
A statute governing the citizen-state relationship or fundamental rights
Manner and form
The theory that Parliament can bind itself as to how future laws are made
Principle of legality
Fundamental rights can't be overridden by general words in a statute
Parliamentary accountability
The government's answerability to Parliament
Carltona principle
Officials can exercise powers conferred on ministers, because the minister is accountable
Royal prerogative
The residue of discretionary authority left in the hands of the Crown
Select committee
A cross-party committee scrutinising a government department or issue
Reserved powers model
A model where everything is devolved except listed reserved matters
Supreme Court
The UK's highest court for most appeals, established by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005
Security of tenure
Senior judges hold office during good behaviour and can be removed only by address of both Houses
Common law rights
Fundamental rights recognised and protected by the courts at common law
Section 3
The duty to read legislation compatibly with Convention rights so far as possible
