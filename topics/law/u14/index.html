Which treaty made the Charter of Fundamental Rights legally binding?

The Treaty of Lisbon (2009). From “Foundations of EU law” →

Case card: Van Gend en Loos (Case 26/62) [1963] ECR 1. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A Dutch importer challenged a customs duty increase as breaching a Treaty provision. Held: The Treaty provision created rights individuals could enforce in national courts. Principle: Direct effect: clear, precise and unconditional Treaty provisions confer rights enforceable by individuals. From “Direct effect” →

Case card: Dano v Jobcenter Leipzig (Case C-333/13). Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A Romanian national living in Germany, who hadn't worked or sought work, claimed basic subsistence benefits. Held: Germany could refuse the benefits. Principle: Economically inactive EU citizens without sufficient resources may be refused social assistance under the Citizenship Directive. From “EU citizenship” →

What are the main sources listed in Article 38(1)?

Treaties, custom, general principles, and judicial decisions and writings as subsidiary means. From “International law sources” →

Case card: Legal Consequences of the Separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965 (Advisory Opinion) [2019] ICJ Rep 95. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: The UN General Assembly asked whether the UK's separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius before independence was lawful. Held: Decolonisation wasn't lawfully completed; the UK should end its administration as quickly as possible. Principle: An advisory opinion of the ICJ, not binding as such, on the right to self-determination. From “International courts” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Foundations of EU law
    The European Union grew from post-war economic cooperation into a union with its own legal order.
  2. EU institutions
    The EU's institutions make, implement and interpret EU law.
  3. Sources of EU law
    EU law has several sources.
  4. EU legislation
    The main EU legislative acts are regulations, directives and decisions.
  5. Direct effect
    Direct effect allows individuals to rely on EU provisions in national courts.
  6. Supremacy
    Supremacy means EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law in member states.
  7. State liability
    State liability allows individuals to claim damages from a member state for loss caused by its breach of EU law.
  8. Free movement
    The internal market rests on the four freedoms: free movement of goods, persons, services and capital.
  9. EU citizenship
    EU citizenship is held by every national of a member state.
  10. Competition law
    EU competition law prohibits anti-competitive agreements (Article 101 TFEU), abuse of a dominant position (Article 102 TFEU) and controls mergers.
  11. UK-EU relationship
    Since leaving the EU, the UK's relationship with it is governed mainly by the Withdrawal Agreement (with the Northern Ireland arrangements now known as the Windsor Framework) and the Trade and Cooperation Agreement.
  12. Retained and assimilated law
    To avoid gaps on Brexit, the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 converted EU law into domestic "retained EU law".
  13. International law sources
    Public international law governs relations between states and other international actors.
  14. Treaties
    Treaties are written agreements between states (and international organisations) governed by international law.
  15. Customary international law
    Customary international law arises from general state practice accepted as law (opinio juris).
  16. State responsibility
    A state is responsible under international law for its internationally wrongful acts.
  17. International courts
    International courts and tribunals settle disputes and develop international law.
  18. Jurisdiction in international law
    Jurisdiction in international law concerns a state's power to make, apply and enforce laws.
  19. International human rights
    International human rights law imposes obligations on states through treaties and custom, enforced mainly through monitoring rather than courts.
  20. International dispute resolution
    International disputes can be resolved by diplomatic means or by legal methods.

Key terms

Conferral
The EU acts only within the competences the member states confer on it (Article 5 TEU)
Commission
The EU's executive, which proposes legislation and enforces the Treaties
Primary law
The Treaties and the Charter of Fundamental Rights
Regulation
A binding act directly applicable in all member states
Vertical direct effect
Reliance on EU law against the state or an emanation of the state
Supremacy
The priority of EU law over conflicting national law
State liability
A member state's liability in damages for breach of EU law
Measure having equivalent effect
A national rule capable of hindering trade between member states (Article 34 TFEU)
EU citizenship
Status held by nationals of member states, additional to national citizenship (Article 20 TFEU)
Cartel
An agreement between competitors to fix prices, share markets or limit output
Withdrawal Agreement
The 2020 treaty governing the UK's exit, including citizens' rights and Northern Ireland
Retained EU law
EU-derived law preserved in UK law after exit by the 2018 Act
Article 38(1)
The provision listing the sources the ICJ applies
Pacta sunt servanda
Every treaty in force binds the parties and must be performed in good faith (art 26)
