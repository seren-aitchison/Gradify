Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which treaty made the Charter of Fundamental Rights legally binding?

The Treaty of Lisbon (2009). From “Foundations of EU law” →

In plain English: The European Union grew from post-war economic cooperation into a union with its own legal order. Key idea: The EU acts only within conferred competences, respecting subsidiarity and proportionality, under the TEU and TFEU.

Case card: Van Gend en Loos (Case 26/62) [1963] ECR 1. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A Dutch importer challenged a customs duty increase as breaching a Treaty provision. Held: The Treaty provision created rights individuals could enforce in national courts. Principle: Direct effect: clear, precise and unconditional Treaty provisions confer rights enforceable by individuals. From “Direct effect” →

In plain English: Direct effect allows individuals to rely on EU provisions in national courts. Key idea: Clear, precise and unconditional provisions have direct effect; directives bind only the state directly, so use indirect effect or state liability against private parties.

Case card: Dano v Jobcenter Leipzig (Case C-333/13). Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A Romanian national living in Germany, who hadn't worked or sought work, claimed basic subsistence benefits. Held: Germany could refuse the benefits. Principle: Economically inactive EU citizens without sufficient resources may be refused social assistance under the Citizenship Directive. From “EU citizenship” →

In plain English: EU citizenship is held by every national of a member state. Key idea: EU citizens can move and reside under Directive 2004/38, with limits on access to benefits; Brexit ended UK nationals' citizenship, with protections for existing residents.

What are the main sources listed in Article 38(1)?

Treaties, custom, general principles, and judicial decisions and writings as subsidiary means. From “International law sources” →

In plain English: Public international law governs relations between states and other international actors. Key idea: Treaties, custom and general principles are the main sources, with judicial decisions and writings as subsidiary means; jus cogens and Article 103 add hierarchy.

Case card: Legal Consequences of the Separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965 (Advisory Opinion) [2019] ICJ Rep 95. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: The UN General Assembly asked whether the UK's separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius before independence was lawful. Held: Decolonisation wasn't lawfully completed; the UK should end its administration as quickly as possible. Principle: An advisory opinion of the ICJ, not binding as such, on the right to self-determination. From “International courts” →

In plain English: International courts and tribunals settle disputes and develop international law. Key idea: The ICJ decides state disputes by consent and gives advisory opinions; other tribunals cover crimes, the sea, human rights and trade.

Quick quiz: test yourself on European Union and International Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When did the UK leave the EU? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    31 January 2020. The UK left on 31 January 2020; the transition period ended on 31 December 2020.

  2. Which EU institution brings infringement proceedings against member states? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Commission. Article 258 TFEU gives the Commission this role.

  3. Which source of EU law has the same legal value as the Treaties? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Charter of Fundamental Rights. Article 6 TEU gives the Charter Treaty status.

  4. The EU wants to legislate on an issue in an area of shared competence that member states can handle well alone. Which principle is engaged? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Subsidiarity. Subsidiarity limits EU action where member states can achieve the objective.

  5. A member state's highest court faces an unclear question of EU law on which the Court of Justice hasn't ruled. What must it generally do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Make a preliminary reference. CILFIT: courts of last instance must refer unless the answer is clear or settled.

  6. A Commission recommendation advises member states on data retention. Is it binding? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No: recommendations are non-binding. Article 288 says recommendations have no binding force.

  7. The deadline for implementing a directive passes and a member state hasn't transposed it. What can individuals do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Rely on its clear provisions against the state. Vertical direct effect allows reliance against the state after the deadline.

  8. An employee of a private company wants to rely on an unimplemented directive against her employer. What is her best route? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Indirect effect, or state liability against the state. Directives lack horizontal direct effect, so she should use indirect effect or Francovich.

  9. A state completely fails to transpose a directive giving package holidaymakers protection, and they suffer loss. Is the breach sufficiently serious? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Yes: non-transposition is serious per se. Dillenkofer: total failure to transpose is sufficiently serious.

Key takeaways

  • The EU acts only within conferred competences, respecting subsidiarity and proportionality, under the TEU and TFEU.
  • The Commission proposes, the Parliament and Council legislate, and the Court of Justice interprets EU law, largely through preliminary references.
  • EU law flows from the Treaties and Charter, secondary acts, general principles, case law and international agreements.
  • Regulations apply directly; directives require transposition; decisions bind addressees; failure to transpose has legal consequences.
  • Clear, precise and unconditional provisions have direct effect; directives bind only the state directly, so use indirect effect or state liability against private parties.
  • Supremacy requires national courts to set aside conflicting national law; in the UK it ended with the 2023 Act.

Common mistakes in European Union and International Law

  • Assuming the EU can legislate on any subject: it needs a legal basis in the Treaties.
  • Confusing the Council of the EU (ministers) with the Council of Europe (the ECHR body).
  • Treating recommendations and opinions as binding EU law.
  • Assuming a directive applies automatically like a regulation.
  • Relying on an unimplemented directive directly against a private employer.
  • Saying EU supremacy still applies to UK law after 2023.

Lessons in this school

  1. Foundations of EU law
    The European Union grew from post-war economic cooperation into a union with its own legal order.
  2. EU institutions
    The EU's institutions make, implement and interpret EU law.
  3. Sources of EU law
    EU law has several sources.
  4. EU legislation
    The main EU legislative acts are regulations, directives and decisions.
  5. Direct effect
    Direct effect allows individuals to rely on EU provisions in national courts.
  6. Supremacy
    Supremacy means EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law in member states.
  7. State liability
    State liability allows individuals to claim damages from a member state for loss caused by its breach of EU law.
  8. Free movement
    The internal market rests on the four freedoms: free movement of goods, persons, services and capital.
  9. EU citizenship
    EU citizenship is held by every national of a member state.
  10. Competition law
    EU competition law prohibits anti-competitive agreements (Article 101 TFEU), abuse of a dominant position (Article 102 TFEU) and controls mergers.
  11. UK-EU relationship
    Since leaving the EU, the UK's relationship with it is governed mainly by the Withdrawal Agreement (with the Northern Ireland arrangements now known as the Windsor Framework) and the Trade and Cooperation Agreement.
  12. Retained and assimilated law
    To avoid gaps on Brexit, the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 converted EU law into domestic "retained EU law".
  13. International law sources
    Public international law governs relations between states and other international actors.
  14. Treaties
    Treaties are written agreements between states (and international organisations) governed by international law.
  15. Customary international law
    Customary international law arises from general state practice accepted as law (opinio juris).
  16. State responsibility
    A state is responsible under international law for its internationally wrongful acts.
  17. International courts
    International courts and tribunals settle disputes and develop international law.
  18. Jurisdiction in international law
    Jurisdiction in international law concerns a state's power to make, apply and enforce laws.
  19. International human rights
    International human rights law imposes obligations on states through treaties and custom, enforced mainly through monitoring rather than courts.
  20. International dispute resolution
    International disputes can be resolved by diplomatic means or by legal methods.

European Union and International Law: questions students ask

Case card: CILFIT (Case 283/81) [1982] ECR 3415. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: An Italian court of last instance asked whether it had to refer a question of EU law to the Court of Justice. Held: Courts of last instance must refer unless the question is irrelevant, already decided, or the answer is so obvious as to leave no reasonable doubt (acte clair). Principle: Sets the limits of the duty to make a preliminary reference under what is now Article 267 TFEU.

What is primary EU law?

The Treaties (TEU and TFEU) and the Charter of Fundamental Rights.

What is the difference between a regulation and a directive?

A regulation applies directly; a directive must be transposed into national law.

Case card: Costa v ENEL (Case 6/64) [1964] ECR 585. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: An Italian consumer challenged the nationalisation of electricity as contrary to the Treaty; Italian law said a later Italian statute prevailed. Held: EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law, including later legislation. Principle: The supremacy of EU law over the law of member states.
