Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Where can you find official free judgments of the senior courts since 2022?

The National Archives' Find Case Law service. From “Finding primary legal sources” →

In plain English: Primary sources are the law itself: legislation and case law. Key idea: Use official sources (legislation.gov.uk, Find Case Law, the Law Reports) and cite the most authoritative version.

Case card: R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin). Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Fictitious case citations appeared in pleadings and witness statements in two cases. Held: The court warned of serious sanctions and called on regulators and leaders to act. Principle: Every authority found through any tool, including AI, must be checked against an authoritative database before use. From “Legal databases” →

In plain English: Legal research relies on free and subscription databases. Key idea: Know each database's coverage, search precisely, and verify every result, especially anything generated by AI.

What are the main parts of a legal memorandum?

Question presented, short answer, facts, analysis, and conclusion with recommendations. From “Legal memoranda” →

In plain English: A legal memorandum is an internal document analysing a legal question for a supervisor or colleague. Key idea: A memo gives a clear short answer, objective analysis issue by issue, and practical recommendations, supported by authority.

What is a BATNA?

The best alternative to a negotiated agreement. From “Negotiation” →

In plain English: Negotiation is the most common way legal disputes and transactions are resolved. Key idea: Prepare thoroughly, focus on interests and the BATNA, act honestly within authority, and record the deal.

What is issue spotting?

Identifying the legal questions raised by the facts. From “Legal problem solving” →

In plain English: Legal problem solving turns facts into legal answers and practical advice. Key idea: Spot the issues, state and apply the law, address missing facts and alternatives, and give practical advice tied to the client's goals.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Legal Research, Writing and Practical Skills

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who publishes the official Law Reports? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Incorporated Council of Law Reporting. The ICLR publishes the Law Reports (AC, KB, Ch, Fam).

  2. Which work is the leading encyclopaedia of the law of England and Wales? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Halsbury's Laws of England. Halsbury's Laws summarises the whole of the law.

  3. Which statute provides general rules and definitions for interpreting legislation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Interpretation Act 1978. The Interpretation Act 1978 applies across most legislation.

  4. A case is reported in two series: the official reports and a commercial series. Which should you cite to a court? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The official Law Reports version. The 2012 Practice Direction gives the official Law Reports priority.

  5. A trainee is asked about an unfamiliar area of planning law with a tight deadline. What is a sensible first step? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Read a practitioner work or practice note for an overview. Secondary sources orient you quickly before checking primary law.

  6. A client's dispute arose in 2021. The statute was amended in 2024. Which version should you apply? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The version in force in 2021. Apply the law as it stood when the events occurred, subject to transitional rules.

  7. A trainee finds a Court of Appeal case directly in point from 1995. What should they check before relying on it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Whether it was appealed, overruled or affected by statute. A case may have been overruled or superseded since.

  8. An AI tool suggests three cases supporting a client's argument. What should the solicitor do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Check each case exists and says what is claimed. Verification is essential before relying on any authority.

  9. A precedent imposed a duty on police for a positive act injuring a bystander. Your client's case concerns police failing to warn of a third party's attack. What is the key analytical step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Distinguish acts from omissions. The act/omission distinction is a legally significant difference.

Key takeaways

  • Use official sources (legislation.gov.uk, Find Case Law, the Law Reports) and cite the most authoritative version.
  • Use secondary sources to orient and find primary law, but rely on the primary sources themselves.
  • Confirm the provision is in force, current and applicable to the right jurisdiction and date, then interpret it with permitted aids.
  • Find cases through citations and subjects, then verify their status and authority before relying on them.
  • Know each database's coverage, search precisely, and verify every result, especially anything generated by AI.
  • Use case analysis and status signals to check treatment, but read the later cases themselves.

Common mistakes in Legal Research, Writing and Practical Skills

  • Citing a newspaper report or summary instead of the judgment itself.
  • Quoting a textbook's summary of a case without reading the judgment.
  • Relying on an amended provision when the events happened before the amendment came into force.
  • Relying on a case that has been overruled because you didn't check its subsequent history.
  • Citing a case suggested by an AI tool without checking it exists and says what is claimed.
  • Treating a red flag as meaning the whole case is no longer good law.

Lessons in this school

  1. Finding primary legal sources
    Primary sources are the law itself: legislation and case law.
  2. Finding secondary legal sources
    Secondary sources explain, analyse and comment on the law: textbooks, practitioner works, encyclopaedias, journals and law reform reports.
  3. Legislation research
    Researching legislation means finding the right provision, checking that it is in force and up to date, understanding how it has been amended, and interpreting it correctly using permitted aids.
  4. Case law research
    Case law research means finding relevant judgments, identifying the binding authorities, and checking that they are still good law.
  5. Legal databases
    Legal research relies on free and subscription databases.
  6. Westlaw and LexisNexis concepts
    Westlaw and LexisNexis are the main subscription legal research platforms in the UK.
  7. Research methodology
    Good legal research follows a method: define the question, identify the issues and facts, plan sources, search systematically, update, record the trail, and know when to stop.
  8. Case analysis
    Case analysis goes beyond reading a single judgment.
  9. Legal memoranda
    A legal memorandum is an internal document analysing a legal question for a supervisor or colleague.
  10. Opinion writing
    An opinion is a formal written advice, traditionally from counsel, on a client's legal position and prospects.
  11. Client letters
    Client letters explain the law and options in a way the client understands and can act on.
  12. Legal drafting
    Legal drafting produces documents that create rights and obligations: contracts, wills, deeds and statements of case.
  13. Negotiation
    Negotiation is the most common way legal disputes and transactions are resolved.
  14. Advocacy
    Advocacy is presenting a client's case persuasively to a court or tribunal.
  15. Interviewing clients
    Client interviews gather facts, understand the client's goals and give initial advice.
  16. Taking instructions
    Taking instructions means confirming who the client is, what they want, the scope of the work and the authority to act.
  17. Legal problem solving
    Legal problem solving turns facts into legal answers and practical advice.
  18. Applying IRAC
    IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) is a simple structure for answering legal problems.
  19. Legal argumentation
    Legal argument persuades a decision-maker using different kinds of reasoning: text, purpose, precedent, principle and policy.
  20. Professional communication
    Lawyers communicate constantly by email, letter, phone and in meetings.
