What is a tort?

A civil wrong, other than breach of contract, for which the law gives a remedy, usually damages. From “Introduction to tort” →

In plain English: A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which the law gives a remedy, usually damages. Key idea: In any tort problem, identify the tort, then each element, then defences, then remedies.

Case card: Nettleship v Weston [1971] 2 QB 691. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A learner driver hit a lamp post, injuring her instructor. Held: She was judged by the standard of a reasonably competent qualified driver. Principle: The standard of care is objective; inexperience doesn't lower it. From “Standard of care” →

In plain English: The standard of care varies with the defendant's role and the circumstances. Key idea: Identify the defendant's role first: it sets the yardstick for breach.

Case card: Spartan Steel and Alloys Ltd v Martin and Co (Contractors) Ltd [1973] QB 27. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Contractors negligently cut a power cable, stopping a steel factory. Held: The factory recovered for damaged metal and the profit on it, but not for lost profit on future melts. Principle: Pure economic loss, not consequential on physical damage, is generally not recoverable in negligence. From “Pure economic loss” →

In plain English: Pure economic loss is financial loss not consequent on physical injury to the claimant or damage to their property. Key idea: Distinguish consequential economic loss (recoverable with the property damage) from pure economic loss (usually not, unless there is an assumption of responsibility).

Case card: Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd [2019] UKSC 27. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Newspapers published articles alleging that Mr Lachaux had abused his ex-wife. Held: Serious harm under s 1 must be shown as a fact, by actual or likely impact, not merely the words' tendency, but it was proved here. Principle: A statement is defamatory only if its publication has caused or is likely to cause serious harm to reputation. From “Defamation” →

In plain English: Defamation protects reputation. Key idea: Since 2013, the claimant must show serious harm. Check it before analysing the defences.

Case card: Bird v Jones (1845) 7 QB 742. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Part of a bridge was fenced off for spectators; the claimant was stopped from going through but was free to go back. Held: Not false imprisonment: he could leave another way. Principle: False imprisonment needs complete restraint; a reasonable means of escape defeats it. From “False imprisonment” →

In plain English: False imprisonment is the unlawful, total restraint of another person's freedom of movement, for however short a time. Key idea: Ask whether the restraint was total and whether there was lawful authority for every minute of it.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Tort Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which tort is actionable without proof of damage? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Trespass to the person. Trespass torts are actionable per se, vindicating rights even without loss.

  2. Which element asks whether the type of damage was reasonably foreseeable? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Remoteness. Remoteness limits recovery to foreseeable kinds of damage.

  3. Which is an established duty situation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A driver to other road users. Road users owe each other a duty of care.

  4. A client wants to sue a surveyor they hired for a negligent report. What is the best analysis? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They may sue in contract and in tort. Concurrent liability lets a claimant choose the more advantageous route.

  5. Sacks of flour fall from a warehouse window onto a passer-by. No one knows how. What may help the claimant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Res ipsa loquitur: the accident suggests negligence by the occupier in control. Byrne v Boadle (1863) is the classic example.

  6. Police officers arresting a suspect in a busy street knock over an elderly pedestrian. Is a duty owed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Yes: a positive act created the danger. Robinson concerned exactly this situation.

  7. A hospital negligently delays treatment; even with prompt treatment the patient had a 25% chance of a full recovery. What is the likely position in English law? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No recovery: causation isn't proved on balance. Loss of a chance under 50% is not recoverable in clinical negligence.

  8. Police negligently fail to take precautions with a prisoner known to be a suicide risk, and he takes his life. Does his act break the chain? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: it was the very thing the duty covered. Reeves v Commissioner of Police (with a reduction for contributory negligence).

  9. A mother watches a live TV broadcast of a stadium disaster in which her son is injured, and develops PTSD. Can she recover as a secondary victim? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: she didn't perceive it with her own senses. Watching on TV fails the Alcock perception requirement.

Key takeaways

  • In any tort problem, identify the tort, then each element, then defences, then remedies.
  • Work through the elements in order and don't skip one: a claim fails if any element is missing.
  • First ask: does an established category or precedent decide this? If so, follow it: precedent may recognise a duty (where harm to this claimant was reasonably foreseeable) or deny one. Only in genuinely novel situations does the incremental Caparo-style analysis come in.
  • The narrow ratio is about manufacturers and consumers; the neighbour principle is the general idea behind all duties of care.
  • Always say which stage you are discussing. Foreseeability means slightly different things at duty, breach and remoteness.
  • Breach is a balancing exercise: risk against the burden of guarding against it.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to tort
    A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which the law gives a remedy, usually damages.
  2. Negligence
    Negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take care, causing damage that is not too remote.
  3. Duty of care
    A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care not to cause certain kinds of harm to others.
  4. Neighbour principle
    Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 is the foundation of the modern law of negligence: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably fo
  5. Foreseeability
    Reasonable foreseeability is central to negligence.
  6. Breach of duty
    Breach of duty occurs when the defendant falls below the standard of care the law requires: that of the reasonable person in the defendant's position.
  7. Standard of care
    The standard of care varies with the defendant's role and the circumstances.
  8. Causation in fact
    Factual causation asks whether the defendant's breach actually caused the claimant's damage.
  9. Legal causation
    Even if the breach factually caused the damage, the law may decide it was not the legal cause.
  10. Remoteness of damage
    Even where breach caused damage, the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable.
  11. Novus actus interveniens
    A novus actus interveniens is a new intervening act that breaks the chain of causation between the defendant's breach and the claimant's damage, so the defendant isn't liable for the later loss.
  12. Psychiatric injury
    The law allows claims for a recognised psychiatric illness caused by negligence, but it restricts them, especially for "secondary victims" who witness harm to others, to limit the number of claims.
  13. Pure economic loss
    Pure economic loss is financial loss not consequent on physical injury to the claimant or damage to their property.
  14. Professional negligence
    Professional negligence is the failure of a professional (such as a solicitor, accountant, surveyor, architect or doctor) to exercise the skill and care of a reasonably competent member of their profession, causing loss.
  15. Occupiers' liability
    Occupiers owe duties to people on their premises under two statutes: the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 for lawful visitors, and the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 for others, mainly trespassers.
  16. Employers' liability
    Employers owe their employees a personal, non-delegable duty to take reasonable care for their safety.
  17. Vicarious liability
    Vicarious liability makes one person (usually an employer) liable for a tort committed by another (usually an employee), without any fault of their own.
  18. Product liability
    People injured by defective products can sue under the Consumer Protection Act 1987, Part 1, which imposes strict liability on producers, or in negligence under Donoghue v Stevenson.
  19. Defamation
    Defamation protects reputation.
  20. Private nuisance
    Private nuisance is an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over or in connection with it.
  21. Public nuisance
    Public nuisance is an act or omission that endangers the life, health, property or comfort of the public, or obstructs the public in exercising rights common to all.
  22. Trespass to the person
    Trespass to the person protects bodily integrity and liberty.
  23. Assault
    Assault, as a tort, is an act that causes the claimant reasonably to apprehend the immediate infliction of unlawful force on them.
  24. Battery
    Battery is the intentional and direct application of unlawful force to another person.
  25. False imprisonment
    False imprisonment is the unlawful, total restraint of another person's freedom of movement, for however short a time.
  26. Trespass to land
    Trespass to land is the unjustified, direct and intentional interference with land in another's possession.
  27. Defences
    Even where the elements of a tort are proved, the defendant may escape or reduce liability through a defence.
  28. Contributory negligence
    Contributory negligence is a partial defence: where the claimant's own failure to take reasonable care for their safety contributed to their damage, damages are reduced to the extent the court thinks just and equitable.
  29. Voluntary assumption of risk
    Volenti non fit injuria ("to a willing person, no injury is done") is a complete defence where the claimant, with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk, freely and voluntarily agreed to accept it.
  30. Remedies and damages
    The main remedy in tort is compensatory damages, aimed at putting the claimant, as far as money can, in the position they would have been in if the tort hadn't been committed.

Tort Law: questions students ask

Case card: Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer, bought for her by a friend, from an opaque bottle said to contain a decomposed snail, and became ill. Held: The manufacturer owed her a duty of care despite there being no contract. Principle: Lord Atkin's neighbour principle: take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour.

Case card: Caparo Industries plc v Dickman [1990] 2 AC 605. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Caparo bought shares in reliance on audited accounts that overstated profits, and sued the auditors. Held: The auditors owed no duty to investors deciding to buy shares: the accounts were prepared for shareholders to exercise their governance rights. Principle: In novel situations a duty needs foreseeability, proximity and that imposing it is fair, just and reasonable.

Who is my "neighbour" in law according to Lord Atkin?

Persons so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation when acting.

Case card: Bourhill v Young [1943] AC 92. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A pregnant fishwife heard a motorcycle crash out of sight and later saw the blood; she suffered shock and a stillbirth. Held: The motorcyclist owed her no duty: she was outside the area of foreseeable danger. Principle: A duty is owed only to those it is reasonably foreseeable could be harmed.
