Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What two features distinguish a legal rule from a moral rule?

Its source (made or recognised by a legitimate legal authority) and its enforceability by the state. From “What is law?” →

In plain English: Law is a system of rules, recognised and enforced by the state, that governs how people, organisations and the state itself behave. Key idea: What makes a rule legal is its source and its enforceability, not its content. An unjust law can still be valid law, and a moral duty can exist without any legal duty.

Name two features of the English legal system that distinguish it from civil law systems.

Judge-made case law as a major source, and adversarial (rather than inquisitorial) procedure. From “The English legal system” →

In plain English: The English legal system is the law, institutions and people that make, interpret and apply law in England and Wales. Key idea: Always state the jurisdiction. "English law" usually means the law of England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems (School 17).

Case card: Thoburn v Sunderland City Council [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin). Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Market traders ('metric martyrs') were convicted for using only imperial measures, against regulations made under the European Communities Act 1972. Held: The 1972 Act was a constitutional statute not impliedly repealed by a later Act. Principle: Constitutional statutes can't be impliedly repealed; only express words or necessary implication will do. From “Parliamentary sovereignty” →

In plain English: Parliamentary sovereignty means the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court or other body can set aside an Act of Parliament. Key idea: Sovereignty is a legal principle. Political, conventional and practical limits exist, but no court will hold an Act invalid.

Who is the head of the judiciary of England and Wales?

The Lord Chief Justice. From “The judiciary” →

In plain English: The judiciary is the body of judges and magistrates who decide cases. Key idea: Judges declare and develop the law, but the democratic legitimacy to make major policy choices lies with Parliament. That tension runs through debates on judicial law-making.

Which Act governs legal aid in England and Wales?

The Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 (LASPO). From “Legal aid” →

In plain English: Legal aid is public funding for legal advice and representation for people who cannot afford it. Key idea: Police station advice is free and non-means-tested. Make sure detained clients know that.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Legal Foundations and the English Legal System

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes legal positivism? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Law is identified by its source, whatever its moral content. Positivists separate the question "is it law?" from "is it good law?"; natural lawyers connect them.

  2. Which area of law is mainly facilitative? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Contract law. Contract law lets people create binding obligations they choose; the others mainly restrain or regulate conduct.

  3. Which is a primary source of law? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A Court of Appeal judgment. Judgments are primary sources; the rest are secondary and at most persuasive.

  4. A client says, "Breaking my promise to help a friend move house must be illegal because it's wrong." What is the best response? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A social promise is usually unenforceable because there is no intention to create legal relations. Contract law requires, among other things, an intention to create legal relations, which is presumed absent in social and domestic arrangements (Balfour v Balfour [1919]).

  5. A minister says a new rule should be "fixed and mechanical, with no exceptions". Which value does this mainly promote, at the cost of which? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Certainty, at the cost of fairness in individual cases. Rigid rules are predictable but cannot adjust to unusual facts; discretion does the reverse.

  6. A client relies on an unincorporated treaty the UK has ratified to challenge a decision in an English court. What is the best advice? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The treaty does not of itself create rights enforceable in domestic courts. Under the dualist approach, an unincorporated treaty creates no domestic rights, though courts may use it to help interpret ambiguous legislation.

  7. Delegated legislation conflicts with the Act under which it was made. What follows? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The delegated legislation can be declared ultra vires and invalid. Delegated legislation is valid only within the powers granted by its parent Act; courts can quash it on judicial review.

  8. What did the Judicature Acts 1873–75 achieve? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    All divisions of the new High Court could administer both law and equity, with equity prevailing in a conflict. Administration was fused; the two bodies of rules remain distinct, with equity prevailing.

  9. A buyer seeks specific performance of a contract to buy a flat. The seller shows that the buyer misled him about her finances during negotiations, though not in a way that made the contract voidable. What is the most likely result? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The court may refuse specific performance and leave the buyer to damages. Equitable remedies are discretionary; "clean hands" can justify refusal, while the common law right to damages remains.

Key takeaways

  • What makes a rule legal is its source and its enforceability, not its content. An unjust law can still be valid law, and a moral duty can exist without any legal duty.
  • When you analyse a rule, ask what it is for. A purposive reading often explains how courts decide hard cases.
  • Hierarchy: Act of Parliament > delegated legislation (if within powers) > binding case law, with case law interpreting legislation. Where an Act and a common law rule conflict, the Act prevails.
  • The Judicature Acts fused the administration of law and equity, not their rules. Where the rules conflict, equity prevails (now Senior Courts Act 1981, s 49); the Earl of Oxford's Case (1615) first settled that priority.
  • An acquittal does not prevent a civil claim, because the civil standard of proof is lower.
  • The test is what kind of relationship and what kind of right is involved, not just who the parties are. A council can be sued in contract like anyone else.

Common mistakes in Legal Foundations and the English Legal System

  • Don't assume "illegal" means "immoral" or that everything immoral is illegal. Exam answers that blur the two lose marks for imprecision.
  • "The purpose of law is justice" is too vague to score marks. Name the specific purpose and show how the rule serves it.
  • Don't cite a textbook as authority for a legal rule. Cite the statute or the case; use the textbook only for commentary.
  • Damages are available as of right; equitable remedies are not. Say "the court may grant" an injunction, not "the claimant is entitled to" one.
  • Don't say a defendant is "guilty" in a civil case, or that a criminal court awards "damages" as its main outcome. Use "liable" for civil and "guilty" for criminal. (Criminal courts can make compensation orders, but that is ancillary.)
  • Don't assume every claim against a public body is public law. A council's breach of a building contract is a private law claim.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is law?
    Law is a system of rules, recognised and enforced by the state, that governs how people, organisations and the state itself behave.
  2. The nature and purposes of law
    Law serves several purposes at once: keeping order, settling disputes, protecting rights, regulating activity and expressing a society's values.
  3. Sources of law
    The sources of law in England and Wales are legislation, case law (common law and equity), retained or assimilated law from the UK's EU membership, and, less directly, custom, conventions and international law.
  4. Common law and equity
    The common law is the law developed by judges in the royal courts from the twelfth century; equity grew up in the Court of Chancery to soften its rigidity.
  5. Civil and criminal law
    Criminal law deals with wrongs against society, prosecuted by the state and punished; civil law deals with disputes between private parties, usually ended with compensation or another remedy.
  6. Public and private law
    Public law governs the state's relationship with individuals and the organisation of the state itself; private law governs relationships between individuals and organisations.
  7. The English legal system
    The English legal system is the law, institutions and people that make, interpret and apply law in England and Wales.
  8. Court structures and jurisdiction
    Courts in England and Wales are divided into civil and criminal, and into trial (first instance) and appellate courts.
  9. The hierarchy of courts
    Courts are arranged in a hierarchy that decides which decisions bind which courts.
  10. The doctrine of precedent
    Under the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis, "to stand by things decided"), courts follow the legal principles in earlier decisions of higher courts so that like cases are treated alike.
  11. Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta
    The ratio decidendi ("reason for deciding") is the legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts, and it is the binding part of a judgment.
  12. Statutory interpretation
    Statutory interpretation is how courts work out what an Act means when it is applied to facts.
  13. Parliamentary sovereignty
    Parliamentary sovereignty means the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court or other body can set aside an Act of Parliament.
  14. The rule of law
    The rule of law is the principle that everyone, including government, is subject to the law, and that law should be clear, public, stable and applied fairly by independent courts.
  15. Separation of powers
    The separation of powers divides state power between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary so that no one branch holds unchecked power.
  16. Judicial independence
    Judicial independence means judges decide cases free from pressure by government, Parliament, parties, the media or anyone else.
  17. Parliament and the legislative process
    Parliament makes primary legislation through a structured process of readings, committee scrutiny and Royal Assent.
  18. Delegated legislation
    Delegated (secondary) legislation is law made by a person or body other than Parliament, under powers given by an Act (the parent or enabling Act).
  19. The judiciary
    The judiciary is the body of judges and magistrates who decide cases.
  20. Tribunals
    Tribunals decide disputes in specialist areas, mostly between individuals and the state (benefits, immigration, tax) and some between private parties (employment).
  21. Alternative dispute resolution
    Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) covers ways of settling disputes outside a court trial: negotiation, mediation, conciliation, early neutral evaluation, adjudication and arbitration.
  22. Legal institutions and regulators
    A web of institutions supports the legal system: government departments, prosecutors, law reform bodies and professional regulators.
  23. The legal profession
    The legal profession in England and Wales is split mainly between solicitors and barristers, with chartered legal executives and other authorised professionals.
  24. Access to justice
    Access to justice means people can understand their rights, get advice and bring or defend claims fairly, whatever their means.
  25. Legal aid
    Legal aid is public funding for legal advice and representation for people who cannot afford it.
  26. Legal research and authority
    Legal research is finding the law that applies to a question and judging how much weight each source carries.
  27. Case law analysis
    Case law analysis is reading a judgment to extract the material facts, the issues, the decision, the ratio, and how the case fits with others.
  28. Legal citation and referencing
    Legal citation identifies sources precisely so a reader can find them.
  29. Legal writing
    Legal writing must be accurate, clear and organised for its reader, whether that is an examiner, a judge or a client.
  30. Introduction to legal argument
    Legal argument persuades a decision-maker that the law, applied to the facts, produces a particular result.

Legal Foundations and the English Legal System: questions students ask

Which purpose does company law mainly serve?

Facilitation: it lets people create businesses with separate legal personality and limited liability.

What is "assimilated law"?

EU-derived law kept in UK law after Brexit, renamed from "retained EU law" from 1 January 2024 by the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023.

What is the main common law remedy?

Damages, available as of right once liability is established.

What is the standard of proof in criminal cases?

Beyond reasonable doubt: the jury or magistrates must be sure of guilt.
