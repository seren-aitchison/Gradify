What two features distinguish a legal rule from a moral rule?

Its source (made or recognised by a legitimate legal authority) and its enforceability by the state. From “What is law?” →

Name two features of the English legal system that distinguish it from civil law systems.

Judge-made case law as a major source, and adversarial (rather than inquisitorial) procedure. From “The English legal system” →

Case card: Thoburn v Sunderland City Council [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin). Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Market traders ('metric martyrs') were convicted for using only imperial measures, against regulations made under the European Communities Act 1972. Held: The 1972 Act was a constitutional statute not impliedly repealed by a later Act. Principle: Constitutional statutes can't be impliedly repealed; only express words or necessary implication will do. From “Parliamentary sovereignty” →

Who is the head of the judiciary of England and Wales?

The Lord Chief Justice. From “The judiciary” →

Lessons in this school

  1. What is law?
    Law is a system of rules, recognised and enforced by the state, that governs how people, organisations and the state itself behave.
  2. The nature and purposes of law
    Law serves several purposes at once: keeping order, settling disputes, protecting rights, regulating activity and expressing a society's values.
  3. Sources of law
    The sources of law in England and Wales are legislation, case law (common law and equity), retained or assimilated law from the UK's EU membership, and, less directly, custom, conventions and international law.
  4. Common law and equity
    The common law is the law developed by judges in the royal courts from the twelfth century; equity grew up in the Court of Chancery to soften its rigidity.
  5. Civil and criminal law
    Criminal law deals with wrongs against society, prosecuted by the state and punished; civil law deals with disputes between private parties, usually ended with compensation or another remedy.
  6. Public and private law
    Public law governs the state's relationship with individuals and the organisation of the state itself; private law governs relationships between individuals and organisations.
  7. The English legal system
    The English legal system is the law, institutions and people that make, interpret and apply law in England and Wales.
  8. Court structures and jurisdiction
    Courts in England and Wales are divided into civil and criminal, and into trial (first instance) and appellate courts.
  9. The hierarchy of courts
    Courts are arranged in a hierarchy that decides which decisions bind which courts.
  10. The doctrine of precedent
    Under the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis, "to stand by things decided"), courts follow the legal principles in earlier decisions of higher courts so that like cases are treated alike.
  11. Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta
    The ratio decidendi ("reason for deciding") is the legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts, and it is the binding part of a judgment.
  12. Statutory interpretation
    Statutory interpretation is how courts work out what an Act means when it is applied to facts.
  13. Parliamentary sovereignty
    Parliamentary sovereignty means the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court or other body can set aside an Act of Parliament.
  14. The rule of law
    The rule of law is the principle that everyone, including government, is subject to the law, and that law should be clear, public, stable and applied fairly by independent courts.
  15. Separation of powers
    The separation of powers divides state power between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary so that no one branch holds unchecked power.
  16. Judicial independence
    Judicial independence means judges decide cases free from pressure by government, Parliament, parties, the media or anyone else.
  17. Parliament and the legislative process
    Parliament makes primary legislation through a structured process of readings, committee scrutiny and Royal Assent.
  18. Delegated legislation
    Delegated (secondary) legislation is law made by a person or body other than Parliament, under powers given by an Act (the parent or enabling Act).
  19. The judiciary
    The judiciary is the body of judges and magistrates who decide cases.
  20. Tribunals
    Tribunals decide disputes in specialist areas, mostly between individuals and the state (benefits, immigration, tax) and some between private parties (employment).
  21. Alternative dispute resolution
    Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) covers ways of settling disputes outside a court trial: negotiation, mediation, conciliation, early neutral evaluation, adjudication and arbitration.
  22. Legal institutions and regulators
    A web of institutions supports the legal system: government departments, prosecutors, law reform bodies and professional regulators.
  23. The legal profession
    The legal profession in England and Wales is split mainly between solicitors and barristers, with chartered legal executives and other authorised professionals.
  24. Access to justice
    Access to justice means people can understand their rights, get advice and bring or defend claims fairly, whatever their means.
  25. Legal aid
    Legal aid is public funding for legal advice and representation for people who cannot afford it.
  26. Legal research and authority
    Legal research is finding the law that applies to a question and judging how much weight each source carries.
  27. Case law analysis
    Case law analysis is reading a judgment to extract the material facts, the issues, the decision, the ratio, and how the case fits with others.
  28. Legal citation and referencing
    Legal citation identifies sources precisely so a reader can find them.
  29. Legal writing
    Legal writing must be accurate, clear and organised for its reader, whether that is an examiner, a judge or a client.
  30. Introduction to legal argument
    Legal argument persuades a decision-maker that the law, applied to the facts, produces a particular result.

Key terms

Legal positivism
The view that law is a matter of social fact, separate from morality
Social control
Using law to maintain order and regulate behaviour
Primary legislation
Acts of Parliament
Equity
A body of rules developed in the Court of Chancery to soften the common law
Burden of proof
The obligation to prove a case
Public law
Law governing the state and its relationship with individuals
Common law system
A legal system in which judicial decisions are a major source of law
First instance court
A court where a case is first tried
Binding precedent
A decision a lower court must follow
Stare decisis
Standing by things decided: following earlier decisions
Ratio decidendi
The legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts
Purposive approach
Interpreting legislation to give effect to its purpose
Parliamentary sovereignty
The principle that Parliament can make or unmake any law
Rule of law
The principle that everyone, including government, is subject to the law
