Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the minimum allotted share capital of a public company?

£50,000 (CA 2006, s 763). From “Business organisations” →

In plain English: Businesses can be run through several legal structures: sole traders, partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability partnerships and companies. Key idea: Match the structure to the client's priorities: liability protection, tax, cost, privacy and finance.

Case card: Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: In divorce proceedings a wife sought properties held by companies the husband controlled. Held: The veil wasn't pierced, but the companies held the properties on resulting trust for him. Principle: The veil can be pierced only where a person deliberately evades or frustrates an existing legal obligation through a company they control. From “Corporate personality” →

In plain English: A registered company is a legal person separate from its shareholders and directors. Key idea: The company is a separate person; the veil is pierced only to stop deliberate evasion of an existing obligation.

Within how many days must a special resolution be filed at Companies House?

15 days (s 30 CA 2006). From “Company decision-making” →

In plain English: Company decisions are made either by the board of directors or by the shareholders. Key idea: Identify the decision-maker and resolution type; follow procedure; file within the deadlines.

Case card: Aluminium Industrie Vaassen BV v Romalpa Aluminium Ltd [1976] 1 WLR 676. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A Dutch supplier sold aluminium foil on terms that ownership stayed with it until payment; the buyer became insolvent. Held: The supplier could recover unsold foil and the proceeds of sales. Principle: Retention of title (Romalpa) clauses can protect sellers on the buyer's insolvency. From “Commercial transactions” →

In plain English: Businesses use a range of commercial arrangements to sell goods and services: agency, distribution, franchising, licensing and joint ventures. Key idea: Choose the arrangement that fits the client's need for control, risk and revenue, and check competition law and agent protection.

Case card: BNY Corporate Trustee Services Ltd v Eurosail-UK 2007-3BL plc [2013] UKSC 28. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Noteholders argued that the issuer was balance-sheet insolvent, triggering default. Held: Not shown: the test is whether the company can reasonably be expected to meet future and contingent liabilities. Principle: The balance sheet test under s 123(2) of the Insolvency Act 1986 is a question of fact, not a strict accounting exercise. From “Insolvency” →

In plain English: A company is insolvent when it can't pay its debts as they fall due or its liabilities exceed its assets. Key idea: Apply the cash flow and balance sheet tests, then advise on procedures and directors' exposure.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Business Law and Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which structure gives separate legal personality but is taxed like a partnership? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A limited liability partnership. An LLP is a body corporate but tax-transparent.

  2. What tax does a sole trader pay on business profits? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Income tax and National Insurance. Sole traders are taxed personally on their trading profits.

  3. Which section of the Partnership Act 1890 makes each partner an agent of the firm? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Section 5. Section 5 sets out partners' authority to bind the firm.

  4. A client wants to start a small business with a friend, protect personal assets and keep things simple. Which structure is usually most suitable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A private limited company. A private company gives limited liability with modest formality.

  5. A sole trader's business owes suppliers £60,000 it can't pay. What is the risk to the owner's home? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It may be used to pay the debts. Unlimited liability exposes personal assets, including the home.

  6. A retires from a firm but no notice is given to existing customers. A customer later supplies goods on credit, thinking A is still a partner. Is A liable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Yes, without notice under s 36. Without notice, A remains liable to those who dealt with the firm before.

  7. A former employee bound by a non-compete covenant sets up a company owned by his wife to compete. What may the court do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Pierce the veil and restrain both. Gilford Motor v Horne: the company was a device to evade his existing obligation.

  8. A director sells his land to the company for £250,000 without shareholder approval. The company's net assets are £1m. What is the position? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is a substantial property transaction needing approval. Section 190 requires member approval for transactions over £100,000.

  9. In a family company run as a quasi-partnership, two shareholders exclude the third from management without reason. What remedy is likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A buy-out order under s 994. O'Neill v Phillips and Ebrahimi: exclusion from management contrary to an understanding can be unfairly prejudicial.

Key takeaways

  • Match the structure to the client's priorities: liability protection, tax, cost, privacy and finance.
  • A sole trader is simple and private but bears unlimited personal liability.
  • A partnership can arise informally; the 1890 Act's default rules apply unless the partners agree otherwise.
  • An LLP combines a company's limited liability and separate personality with a partnership's flexibility and tax treatment.
  • Companies are created by registration and governed by their articles and the Act; the constitution is a statutory contract among the company and members.
  • Register with the right documents, then complete the post-incorporation steps; beware personal liability for pre-incorporation contracts.

Common mistakes in Business Law and Practice

  • Assuming a limited company always gives complete protection: banks often require personal guarantees.
  • Treating the sole trader's business as a separate person that can be sued independently.
  • Assuming no partnership exists because nothing was signed.
  • Treating an LLP as a type of partnership under the 1890 Act: it is a body corporate.
  • Assuming an outsider named in the articles can enforce them: s 33 binds only members as members.
  • Signing contracts for a company that hasn't yet been registered, assuming the company will be liable.

Lessons in this school

  1. Business organisations
    Businesses can be run through several legal structures: sole traders, partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability partnerships and companies.
  2. Sole traders
    A sole trader runs a business personally, without a separate legal entity.
  3. Partnerships
    A partnership is the relation between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit (Partnership Act 1890, s 1).
  4. Limited liability partnerships
    A limited liability partnership is a body corporate with separate legal personality, created under the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000.
  5. Companies
    A registered company is a legal person separate from its members, created by registration under the Companies Act 2006.
  6. Company formation
    A company is formed by sending an application for registration to Companies House.
  7. Corporate personality
    A registered company is a legal person separate from its shareholders and directors.
  8. Directors
    Directors manage the company's business.
  9. Directors' duties
    Directors owe general duties to the company, codified in ss 171–177 of the Companies Act 2006.
  10. Shareholders
    Shareholders own the company but don't manage it.
  11. Share capital
    Share capital is the money members invest in exchange for shares.
  12. Corporate governance
    Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled.
  13. Company decision-making
    Company decisions are made either by the board of directors or by the shareholders.
  14. Company meetings
    Shareholders make decisions at general meetings unless they use written resolutions.
  15. Dividends
    Dividends are distributions of profit to shareholders.
  16. Company accounts
    Companies must keep accounting records, prepare annual accounts and reports, and file them at Companies House.
  17. Corporate finance
    Companies raise money through equity (shares) or debt (loans, overdrafts and bonds).
  18. Business contracts
    Business contracts are governed by general contract law, with commercial terms that allocate risk: payment, delivery, liability limits, boilerplate clauses and dispute resolution.
  19. Commercial transactions
    Businesses use a range of commercial arrangements to sell goods and services: agency, distribution, franchising, licensing and joint ventures.
  20. Agency
    Agency arises when one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) and to affect the principal's legal relations with third parties.
  21. Sale of goods
    Business-to-business sales of goods are governed by the Sale of Goods Act 1979.
  22. Supply of goods and services
    Contracts for services and for work and materials between businesses are governed by the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982.
  23. Consumer protection
    Businesses dealing with consumers face stricter rules than in B2B contracts.
  24. Business taxation
    Business structure affects tax.
  25. Insolvency
    A company is insolvent when it can't pay its debts as they fall due or its liabilities exceed its assets.
  26. Liquidation
    Liquidation (winding up) ends a company's life: a liquidator collects and sells its assets, distributes the proceeds to creditors in a statutory order, and the company is dissolved.
  27. Administration
    Administration is a rescue procedure under Schedule B1 to the Insolvency Act 1986.
  28. Restructuring
    Restructuring tools let financially distressed companies compromise their debts and continue trading.
  29. Corporate transactions
    Corporate transactions include buying and selling companies or businesses, investments, mergers and joint ventures.

Business Law and Practice: questions students ask

Can a sole trader grant a floating charge?

No; only companies and LLPs can.

What is the default rule on sharing profits in a partnership?

Equal shares (s 24 PA 1890).

Does the Partnership Act 1890 apply to LLPs?

No; LLPs are bodies corporate under the LLPA 2000.

Case card: Salomon v A Salomon and Co Ltd [1897] AC 22. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Salomon sold his boot business to a company in which he held almost all the shares and a secured debenture; it became insolvent. Held: The company was a separate legal person; he wasn't liable for its debts and his security was valid. Principle: Separate legal personality: a properly incorporated company is distinct from its members.
