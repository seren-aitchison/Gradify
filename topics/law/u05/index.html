Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Case card: Woolmington v DPP [1935] AC 462. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Woolmington shot his estranged wife and said it was an accident; the judge told the jury he had to prove it was. Held: Conviction quashed: the prosecution must prove guilt. Principle: The 'golden thread': the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt, subject to insanity and statutory exceptions. From “Introduction to criminal liability” →

In plain English: Criminal liability arises when a person commits the conduct an offence prohibits (actus reus) with the state of mind it requires (mens rea), without a valid defence. Key idea: Every element must be proved by the prosecution beyond reasonable doubt; a defence once raised must be disproved.

Case card: R v Jogee [2016] UKSC 8. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Jogee encouraged a friend who stabbed and killed a man; he was convicted on the basis of foresight that the friend might use the knife. Held: Conviction set aside: foresight is only evidence of intention. Principle: An accessory must intend to assist or encourage the principal's crime; foresight alone isn't enough (correcting Chan Wing-Siu). From “Secondary liability” →

In plain English: A person who aids, abets, counsels or procures an offence is liable to be tried and punished as a principal. Key idea: Secondary liability needs assistance or encouragement plus intention to assist or encourage, with knowledge of the essential facts (Jogee).

Which case is authority for this? Infidelity may be taken into account where it is integral to the context of a permissible trigger.

R v Clinton [2012] EWCA Crim 2 From “Loss of control” →

In plain English: Loss of control is a partial defence to murder under ss 54–56 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, replacing the old defence of provocation. Key idea: Loss of control needs an actual loss of self-control, a qualifying trigger and a reaction a normal person might have had; revenge and sexual infidelity alone don't qualify.

Case card: R v Bree [2007] EWCA Crim 804. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: After both had drunk heavily, Bree had sex with a woman who was very drunk. Held: Conviction quashed for misdirection: a drunk complainant may still have capacity to consent; capacity can be lost before unconsciousness. Principle: Voluntarily drunk consent is still consent if the complainant had capacity to choose. From “Sexual offences” →

In plain English: The Sexual Offences Act 2003 sets out the main sexual offences, including rape, assault by penetration and sexual assault. Key idea: Consent is agreement by choice with freedom and capacity; deception about the act itself, or breach of a condition about the physical act, can negate it.

Which case is authority for this? Self-defence is judged on D's honest belief about the circumstances; the force must be reasonable in those circumstances.

R v Gladstone Williams (1984) 78 Cr App R 276 From “Self-defence” →

In plain English: Self-defence, defence of others and the prevention of crime allow the use of reasonable force. Key idea: Ask whether D honestly believed force was necessary, and whether the force was reasonable on the facts as D believed them.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Criminal Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who bears the legal burden of proving the defendant's guilt? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The prosecution. Woolmington v DPP: the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, subject to limited exceptions.

  2. In R v Pittwood, the duty to act arose from what? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    His contract of employment. Pittwood was a railway gatekeeper whose contract imposed a duty that protected the public.

  3. Which case established the subjective test of recklessness that replaced Caldwell? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    R v G. R v G [2003] UKHL 50 requires the defendant to be aware of the risk.

  4. A defendant raises self-defence and adduces some evidence of it. What must happen next? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The prosecution must disprove it. Once the evidential burden is met, the prosecution must disprove the defence beyond reasonable doubt.

  5. A man drops a lit cigarette on a sofa, notices it smouldering, and walks away. The house burns. What is his position on actus reus? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He may be liable: he created the danger. Under R v Miller, a person who creates a dangerous situation is under a duty to take reasonable steps to counter it.

  6. D sets fire to a house to claim insurance, knowing a child is asleep upstairs and will almost certainly die. The child dies. Can the jury find intention to kill? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Yes, under the Woollin direction. Death was a virtual certainty and D appreciated it, so the jury may find oblique intention.

  7. D throws a stone at V, misses and breaks a window behind V. Can D's intention to injure V be used for criminal damage to the window? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: malice transfers only within the same offence. R v Pembliton (1874): intent to injure a person doesn't transfer to property damage.

  8. D supplies heroin to V, a fully informed adult, who injects himself and dies. Did D cause V's death? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: V's free, informed act broke the chain. R v Kennedy (No 2): the victim's free, deliberate and informed self-injection broke the chain.

  9. D buys powder believing it is cocaine and agrees to sell it. It is harmless sugar. Is D guilty of attempting to supply? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Yes: impossibility is no defence. R v Shivpuri: D can be guilty of attempting an offence that was impossible on the facts.

Key takeaways

  • Every element must be proved by the prosecution beyond reasonable doubt; a defence once raised must be disproved.
  • An omission is criminal only if the defendant was under a duty to act and the offence can be committed by omission.
  • Intention and recklessness are subjective: the question is what this defendant intended or foresaw, not what a reasonable person would have.
  • Look for a continuing act or a single transaction where mens rea and actus reus seem to be separated in time.
  • The chain of causation is broken only by an intervening act that is free, deliberate and informed, or so unforeseeable or potent that D's contribution becomes insignificant.
  • Start with the presumption of mens rea (Sweet v Parsley) and apply the Gammon factors to decide whether it is displaced.

Common mistakes in Criminal Law

  • Jumping straight to defences before establishing that the elements of the offence are made out.
  • Assuming a bystander who fails to help commits an offence: without a duty, there is generally no liability.
  • Treating foresight of virtual certainty as automatically equal to intention: it is evidence from which the jury may find it.
  • Concluding there is no liability simply because the fatal act came after the defendant believed the victim was already dead.
  • Assuming negligent medical treatment always breaks the chain of causation.
  • Assuming an offence is strict liability because it is regulatory, without applying the presumption of mens rea.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to criminal liability
    Criminal liability arises when a person commits the conduct an offence prohibits (actus reus) with the state of mind it requires (mens rea), without a valid defence.
  2. Actus reus
    The actus reus is the external element of an offence: the conduct, circumstances and, for result crimes, the consequences.
  3. Mens rea
    Mens rea is the mental element of an offence.
  4. Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea
    For most offences, the actus reus and mens rea must coincide in time.
  5. Causation
    For result crimes, the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct caused the prohibited result, both in fact and in law.
  6. Strict liability
    Strict liability offences require no mens rea as to at least one element of the actus reus.
  7. Attempts
    An attempt is an inchoate offence: the defendant tries to commit an offence but doesn't complete it.
  8. Secondary liability
    A person who aids, abets, counsels or procures an offence is liable to be tried and punished as a principal.
  9. Joint enterprise
    "Joint enterprise" describes cases where two or more people set out to commit one crime and, in the course of it, one commits a further crime.
  10. Corporate criminal liability
    Companies can commit crimes.
  11. Homicide
    Homicide is the killing of a human being by another.
  12. Murder
    Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace with malice aforethought.
  13. Voluntary manslaughter
    Voluntary manslaughter is a killing with the mens rea of murder that is reduced to manslaughter because a partial defence applies.
  14. Diminished responsibility
    Diminished responsibility is a partial defence to murder under s 2 of the Homicide Act 1957, as amended.
  15. Loss of control
    Loss of control is a partial defence to murder under ss 54–56 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, replacing the old defence of provocation.
  16. Involuntary manslaughter
    Involuntary manslaughter is an unlawful killing where D lacked the mens rea for murder.
  17. Gross negligence manslaughter
    Gross negligence manslaughter applies where D owes V a duty of care, breaches it, and the breach causes death in circumstances so bad that the jury considers it criminal.
  18. Unlawful act manslaughter
    Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter applies where D intentionally does an act that is unlawful and dangerous, and it causes death.
  19. Non-fatal offences
    The non-fatal offences against the person form a ladder of seriousness, from common assault and battery to wounding or causing GBH with intent.
  20. Assault and battery
    Assault and battery are the least serious offences against the person.
  21. Actual bodily harm
    Assault occasioning actual bodily harm under s 47 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 requires an assault or battery that causes ABH.
  22. Grievous bodily harm
    Sections 20 and 18 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 cover the most serious non-fatal injuries.
  23. Sexual offences
    The Sexual Offences Act 2003 sets out the main sexual offences, including rape, assault by penetration and sexual assault.
  24. Theft
    Theft is defined by s 1 of the Theft Act 1968: dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.
  25. Robbery
    Robbery is theft with force.
  26. Burglary
    Burglary under s 9 of the Theft Act 1968 involves entering a building or part of a building as a trespasser, either with intent to commit certain offences (s 9(1)(a)) or then committing or attempting
  27. Fraud
    The Fraud Act 2006 created a general offence of fraud, committed in one of three ways: false representation, failing to disclose information, or abuse of position.
  28. Criminal damage
    The Criminal Damage Act 1971 makes it an offence to destroy or damage property belonging to another, intending to or being reckless as to that result, without lawful excuse.
  29. Defences
    Criminal defences either deny an element of the offence or justify or excuse conduct that would otherwise be criminal.
  30. Self-defence
    Self-defence, defence of others and the prevention of crime allow the use of reasonable force.
  31. Insanity
    Insanity is a defence based on the M'Naghten Rules (1843).
  32. Automatism
    Automatism is a defence where D's acts were involuntary because of an external factor that caused a complete loss of voluntary control.
  33. Intoxication
    Intoxication is not a defence as such, but evidence of intoxication may show that D lacked the mens rea for an offence.
  34. Duress
    Duress excuses D who committed an offence because of a threat of death or serious injury.
  35. Necessity
    Necessity covers situations where D commits an offence to avoid a greater evil.
  36. Consent
    Consent is a defence to assault and battery, and in limited, recognised situations to more serious harm.
  37. Criminal attempts and inchoate offences
    Inchoate offences criminalise conduct before a substantive offence is completed.

Criminal Law: questions students ask

Case card: R v Stone and Dobinson [1977] QB 354. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A couple took in Stone's frail sister, who became bedridden and died of neglect. Held: Guilty of manslaughter: by voluntarily assuming her care they came under a duty and grossly breached it. Principle: Liability for omission arises where there is a duty to act, including one voluntarily assumed towards a dependant.

Case card: R v G [2003] UKHL 50. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Two boys aged 11 and 12 set fire to newspapers behind a shop; the fire spread and caused about £1 million of damage. They hadn't foreseen the risk. Held: Convictions quashed: recklessness is subjective. Principle: D is reckless if aware of a risk and it is unreasonable to take it in the circumstances known to them (overruling Caldwell).

Case card: Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner [1969] 1 QB 439. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Fagan accidentally drove onto a police officer's foot, then refused to move when told. Held: Guilty of assault: the act was continuing, and mens rea arose during it. Principle: A continuing act lets mens rea formed during it coincide with the actus reus.

Case card: R v White [1910] 2 KB 124. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: White poisoned his mother's drink; she died of a heart attack before the poison could act. Held: Not guilty of murder (but guilty of attempt): the poison didn't cause death. Principle: Factual causation: D's act must be a 'but for' cause of the result.
