Case card: Woolmington v DPP [1935] AC 462. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Woolmington shot his estranged wife and said it was an accident; the judge told the jury he had to prove it was. Held: Conviction quashed: the prosecution must prove guilt. Principle: The 'golden thread': the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt, subject to insanity and statutory exceptions. From “Introduction to criminal liability” →

Case card: R v Jogee [2016] UKSC 8. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Jogee encouraged a friend who stabbed and killed a man; he was convicted on the basis of foresight that the friend might use the knife. Held: Conviction set aside: foresight is only evidence of intention. Principle: An accessory must intend to assist or encourage the principal's crime; foresight alone isn't enough (correcting Chan Wing-Siu). From “Secondary liability” →

Which case is authority for this? Infidelity may be taken into account where it is integral to the context of a permissible trigger.

R v Clinton [2012] EWCA Crim 2 From “Loss of control” →

Case card: R v Bree [2007] EWCA Crim 804. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: After both had drunk heavily, Bree had sex with a woman who was very drunk. Held: Conviction quashed for misdirection: a drunk complainant may still have capacity to consent; capacity can be lost before unconsciousness. Principle: Voluntarily drunk consent is still consent if the complainant had capacity to choose. From “Sexual offences” →

Which case is authority for this? Self-defence is judged on D's honest belief about the circumstances; the force must be reasonable in those circumstances.

R v Gladstone Williams (1984) 78 Cr App R 276 From “Self-defence” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to criminal liability
    Criminal liability arises when a person commits the conduct an offence prohibits (actus reus) with the state of mind it requires (mens rea), without a valid defence.
  2. Actus reus
    The actus reus is the external element of an offence: the conduct, circumstances and, for result crimes, the consequences.
  3. Mens rea
    Mens rea is the mental element of an offence.
  4. Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea
    For most offences, the actus reus and mens rea must coincide in time.
  5. Causation
    For result crimes, the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct caused the prohibited result, both in fact and in law.
  6. Strict liability
    Strict liability offences require no mens rea as to at least one element of the actus reus.
  7. Attempts
    An attempt is an inchoate offence: the defendant tries to commit an offence but doesn't complete it.
  8. Secondary liability
    A person who aids, abets, counsels or procures an offence is liable to be tried and punished as a principal.
  9. Joint enterprise
    "Joint enterprise" describes cases where two or more people set out to commit one crime and, in the course of it, one commits a further crime.
  10. Corporate criminal liability
    Companies can commit crimes.
  11. Homicide
    Homicide is the killing of a human being by another.
  12. Murder
    Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace with malice aforethought.
  13. Voluntary manslaughter
    Voluntary manslaughter is a killing with the mens rea of murder that is reduced to manslaughter because a partial defence applies.
  14. Diminished responsibility
    Diminished responsibility is a partial defence to murder under s 2 of the Homicide Act 1957, as amended.
  15. Loss of control
    Loss of control is a partial defence to murder under ss 54–56 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, replacing the old defence of provocation.
  16. Involuntary manslaughter
    Involuntary manslaughter is an unlawful killing where D lacked the mens rea for murder.
  17. Gross negligence manslaughter
    Gross negligence manslaughter applies where D owes V a duty of care, breaches it, and the breach causes death in circumstances so bad that the jury considers it criminal.
  18. Unlawful act manslaughter
    Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter applies where D intentionally does an act that is unlawful and dangerous, and it causes death.
  19. Non-fatal offences
    The non-fatal offences against the person form a ladder of seriousness, from common assault and battery to wounding or causing GBH with intent.
  20. Assault and battery
    Assault and battery are the least serious offences against the person.
  21. Actual bodily harm
    Assault occasioning actual bodily harm under s 47 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 requires an assault or battery that causes ABH.
  22. Grievous bodily harm
    Sections 20 and 18 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 cover the most serious non-fatal injuries.
  23. Sexual offences
    The Sexual Offences Act 2003 sets out the main sexual offences, including rape, assault by penetration and sexual assault.
  24. Theft
    Theft is defined by s 1 of the Theft Act 1968: dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.
  25. Robbery
    Robbery is theft with force.
  26. Burglary
    Burglary under s 9 of the Theft Act 1968 involves entering a building or part of a building as a trespasser, either with intent to commit certain offences (s 9(1)(a)) or then committing or attempting
  27. Fraud
    The Fraud Act 2006 created a general offence of fraud, committed in one of three ways: false representation, failing to disclose information, or abuse of position.
  28. Criminal damage
    The Criminal Damage Act 1971 makes it an offence to destroy or damage property belonging to another, intending to or being reckless as to that result, without lawful excuse.
  29. Defences
    Criminal defences either deny an element of the offence or justify or excuse conduct that would otherwise be criminal.
  30. Self-defence
    Self-defence, defence of others and the prevention of crime allow the use of reasonable force.
  31. Insanity
    Insanity is a defence based on the M'Naghten Rules (1843).
  32. Automatism
    Automatism is a defence where D's acts were involuntary because of an external factor that caused a complete loss of voluntary control.
  33. Intoxication
    Intoxication is not a defence as such, but evidence of intoxication may show that D lacked the mens rea for an offence.
  34. Duress
    Duress excuses D who committed an offence because of a threat of death or serious injury.
  35. Necessity
    Necessity covers situations where D commits an offence to avoid a greater evil.
  36. Consent
    Consent is a defence to assault and battery, and in limited, recognised situations to more serious harm.
  37. Criminal attempts and inchoate offences
    Inchoate offences criminalise conduct before a substantive offence is completed.

Key terms

Actus reus
The conduct, circumstances and consequences an offence prohibits
Conduct crime
An offence defined by conduct regardless of result, such as perjury
Direct intention
Where the result is the defendant's aim or purpose
Contemporaneity
The requirement that actus reus and mens rea coincide
Factual causation
The "but for" test: would the result have occurred but for D's conduct?
Strict liability
Liability without proof of mens rea as to at least one element
Inchoate offence
An offence committed before, or without, the completion of a substantive offence
Principal
The person who performs the actus reus of the offence
Parasitic accessorial liability
The former rule that foresight of a possible further crime was enough
Identification doctrine
Attributing the acts and mind of a company's directing mind to the company
Murder
Unlawful killing with malice aforethought
Malice aforethought
Intention to kill or to cause grievous bodily harm
Partial defence
A defence that reduces murder to manslaughter rather than securing an acquittal
Abnormality of mental functioning
A state of mind so different from that of ordinary people that a reasonable person would term it abnormal
