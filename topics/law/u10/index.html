What is a non-probate asset?

Property passing outside the will or intestacy, such as joint tenancy property or a life policy in trust. From “Introduction to succession law” →

In plain English: Succession law decides what happens to a person's property on death. Key idea: Identify what passes by will or intestacy and what passes outside the estate, then apply the relevant rules.

Case card: Cheese v Lovejoy (1877) 2 PD 251. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A testator drew lines through parts of his will, wrote 'This is revoked' on the back, and threw it among waste paper; a servant kept it. Held: Not revoked: there was no sufficient act of destruction. Principle: Revocation by destruction needs an actual destruction (burning, tearing or otherwise destroying) with intention to revoke. From “Revocation” →

In plain English: A will can be revoked by a later will or codicil, by written declaration executed like a will, by destruction with intention to revoke, and (in England and Wales) by marriage or civil partnership. Key idea: Revocation needs a later will, executed writing or destruction with intention; marriage revokes, divorce removes the former spouse.

Statute card: what does Administration of Estates Act 1925, s 46 provide?

The intestacy rules: a surviving spouse or civil partner takes the personal chattels, a statutory legacy and half the rest absolutely (if there are issue) or everything (if not); otherwise issue, then parents, siblings and remoter relatives in order. The statutory legacy changes: check the current figure. From “Intestacy” →

In plain English: When someone dies without a valid will, or a will that doesn't dispose of everything, the intestacy rules in s 46 of the Administration of Estates Act 1925 decide who inherits. Key idea: Apply s 46: spouse first (chattels, statutory legacy and half residue if there are issue), then issue and other relatives in order; cohabitants take nothing.

What has replaced the executor's oath?

The legal statement. From “Probate procedure” →

In plain English: Applying for a grant is now mostly done online with HM Courts and Tribunals Service. Key idea: Value the estate, deal with IHT, then apply online with the will and legal statement.

Can a minor give a valid receipt for a legacy?

No; the share is held on trust. From “Distribution of estates” →

In plain English: Distribution is the final stage: PRs transfer gifts and residue to beneficiaries. Key idea: Confirm who is entitled, protect against unknown claims, hold minors' shares on trust, and close with accounts and receipts.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How does a beneficial joint tenant's share pass on death? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    By survivorship to the other joint tenants. Survivorship takes priority over the will.

  2. Which case emphasised testamentary freedom in a claim by an adult daughter? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ilott v The Blue Cross. Ilott [2017] UKSC 17 gave real weight to the testator's wishes against an adult child.

  3. What is the minimum age for making a non-privileged will? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    18. Wills Act 1837, s 7 sets 18 as the minimum age, save for privileged wills.

  4. A deceased left a will, a home owned jointly with her husband as joint tenants, and a life policy written in trust for her children. Which pass under the will? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Neither the home nor the policy. The home passes by survivorship and the policy to the trustees.

  5. A widower wants to leave everything to an animal charity and nothing to his adult son. What should the solicitor advise? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He can, but a 1975 Act claim may follow. Testamentary freedom applies, but family provision claims are possible.

  6. A solicitor drafts a will leaving most of the estate to himself for an elderly client. What must be proved if challenged? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Knowledge and approval, affirmatively. Wintle v Nye: suspicious circumstances require affirmative proof of knowledge and approval.

  7. An intestate leaves a wife, two adult children and an estate of £600,000 (excluding chattels). Roughly how is it divided? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Wife: £322,000 plus half the rest; children share half the rest. With issue, the spouse takes the statutory legacy and half the residue.

  8. A relative believes a will was forged and wants to stop the executor getting a grant. What should they do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Enter a caveat. A caveat prevents a grant without notice.

  9. A cohabitant of three years receives nothing under her partner's will. What standard applies to her claim? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The maintenance standard. Cohabitants are limited to the maintenance standard, not the higher spouse standard.

Key takeaways

  • Identify what passes by will or intestacy and what passes outside the estate, then apply the relevant rules.
  • Testators can leave property as they choose, subject to the 1975 Act, estoppel, mutual wills and contracts.
  • Apply Banks v Goodfellow at the time of the will, check knowledge and approval, and follow the golden rule for vulnerable testators.
  • Check writing, signature, intention, two witnesses present together, and that no witness is a beneficiary.
  • Revocation needs a later will, executed writing or destruction with intention; marriage revokes, divorce removes the former spouse.
  • Post-execution alterations need proper execution; mistakes may be corrected by rectification within six months of the grant.

Common mistakes in Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates

  • Assuming a will controls a jointly owned home held as beneficial joint tenants.
  • Telling a client they can't disinherit a child: they can, though a 1975 Act claim may follow.
  • Applying the Mental Capacity Act test instead of Banks v Goodfellow to testamentary capacity.
  • Letting a beneficiary's spouse act as a witness.
  • Forgetting that marriage revokes an existing will unless made in contemplation of it.
  • Allowing a client to handwrite changes on their original will without execution formalities.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to succession law
    Succession law decides what happens to a person's property on death.
  2. Testamentary freedom
    English law gives testators broad freedom to leave their property as they wish.
  3. Capacity to make a will
    A testator must have testamentary capacity when making a will, and must know and approve its contents.
  4. Formalities of wills
    A will is valid only if it meets the formalities of s 9 of the Wills Act 1837.
  5. Revocation
    A will can be revoked by a later will or codicil, by written declaration executed like a will, by destruction with intention to revoke, and (in England and Wales) by marriage or civil partnership.
  6. Alteration and rectification
    Changes made to a will after execution are only valid if executed like a will.
  7. Interpretation of wills
    When the meaning of a will is disputed, the court seeks to identify the testator's intention from the words used, read in context.
  8. Gifts and legacies
    Gifts in a will are classified as specific, general, demonstrative, pecuniary or residuary.
  9. Intestacy
    When someone dies without a valid will, or a will that doesn't dispose of everything, the intestacy rules in s 46 of the Administration of Estates Act 1925 decide who inherits.
  10. Personal representatives
    Personal representatives (PRs) administer a deceased person's estate.
  11. Executors and administrators
    Executors are appointed by the will; administrators are appointed by the court where there is no will or no executor able and willing to act.
  12. Grants of representation
    A grant of representation is the court document confirming who has authority to deal with a deceased person's estate.
  13. Probate procedure
    Applying for a grant is now mostly done online with HM Courts and Tribunals Service.
  14. Estate administration
    After the grant, personal representatives collect the assets, pay debts, expenses and tax, and then distribute the estate.
  15. Inheritance tax
    Inheritance tax is charged on the value of a person's estate on death and on some lifetime gifts.
  16. Estate liabilities
    Personal representatives must pay the deceased's debts and the estate's expenses before distributing.
  17. Distribution of estates
    Distribution is the final stage: PRs transfer gifts and residue to beneficiaries.
  18. Family provision claims
    The Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 allows certain people to apply to court for reasonable financial provision from an estate where the will or intestacy doesn't make it.
  19. Proprietary estoppel in succession
    Proprietary estoppel often arises in families where someone was promised they would inherit property, relied on that promise, and was then left out of the will.

Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates: questions students ask

Case card: Thorner v Major [2009] UKHL 18. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A farmer, by hints over years such as handing over a life policy 'for my death duties', led his cousin's son to believe he'd inherit the farm, for which David worked unpaid for decades. Held: Proprietary estoppel was established. Principle: An assurance needn't be express; it must be clear enough in context, and reasonably relied on to the claimant's detriment.

Case card: Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A testator with delusions made a will in favour of his niece. Held: The will was valid, as the delusions didn't affect it. Principle: The test for testamentary capacity (understanding the act, the property, and the claims on the testator, without disorder poisoning the dispositions); Clitheroe v Bond confirmed it survives the Mental Capacity Act 2005.

Case card: Marley v Rawlings [2014] UKSC 2. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A husband and wife each signed the other's will by mistake; the husband's will left everything to Mr Marley. Held: The will was rectified under s 20 of the Administration of Justice Act 1982 and was valid. Principle: A clerical error includes a mistake in preparing or executing a will, and a mirror will signed in error can be rectified.

Statute card: what does Administration of Justice Act 1982, s 20 provide?

The court may rectify a will that fails to carry out the testator's intentions because of a clerical error or a failure to understand their instructions; the claim must normally be brought within six months of the grant.
