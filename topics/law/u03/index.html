List the essential elements of a binding contract.

Agreement (offer and acceptance), consideration, intention to create legal relations, certainty, capacity, and any required formalities. From “Introduction to contract law” →

In plain English: A contract is a legally binding agreement: an exchange of promises (or a promise for an act) that the law will enforce. Key idea: For any problem question, work through: formation, terms, vitiating factors, breach or discharge, remedies.

Case card: Arnold v Britton [2015] UKSC 36. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: Lease service charge clauses provided for a fixed sum rising by 10% compound each year, which became very large. Held: The clear words were applied despite the commercially poor result. Principle: Interpretation focuses on the natural meaning of the words in context; commercial common sense can't rewrite clear language. From “Contractual terms” →

In plain English: Contractual terms are the promises and obligations that make up the contract. Key idea: In any problem: identify each term, check it was incorporated, classify it, and interpret it.

Case card: Royscot Trust Ltd v Rogerson [1991] 2 QB 297. Facts, decision and principle?

Facts: A car dealer misstated the price and deposit to a finance company; the buyer later sold the car and defaulted. Held: Damages under s 2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967 were assessed as if for fraud, covering all direct loss. Principle: The 'fiction of fraud': s 2(1) damages are measured as for the tort of deceit. From “Negligent misrepresentation” →

In plain English: Negligent misrepresentation is a false statement made carelessly, without reasonable grounds for believing it true. Key idea: Between contracting parties, s 2(1) is usually the best route: the defendant has to disprove negligence.

Which case is authority for this? Whether an exclusion clause covers a breach is a question of construction, not a rule of law.

Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd [1980] AC 827 From “Exclusion clauses” →

In plain English: An exclusion clause excludes or limits a party's liability for breach of contract, negligence or misrepresentation. Key idea: Incorporated? Covers the loss? Valid under statute? Answer all three in order.

Which case is authority for this? Contract damages protect the expectation interest.

Robinson v Harman (1848) 1 Ex 850 From “Contractual remedies” →

In plain English: Remedies for breach of contract aim to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed. Key idea: The default is damages. Other remedies are exceptions that need justifying.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Contract Law

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which contract must be in writing and signed by both parties? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A contract for the sale of an interest in land. LP(MP)A 1989, s 2 requires land contracts to be in writing, incorporating all terms, signed by both parties.

  2. In a self-service shop, when is the contract made? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    When the cashier accepts the customer's offer at the till. Boots: display is an invitation to treat, the customer offers at the till, and the cashier accepts.

  3. Which case concerns the "battle of the forms"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O. Butler applied the last-shot approach to exchanged standard terms.

  4. A client says, "I didn't really mean to agree, so there's no contract." What principle answers this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The objective approach of the reasonable person. English law judges agreement objectively (Smith v Hughes).

  5. A offers to sell B a car for £5,000. B replies, "I'll pay £4,500." A refuses. B then says, "Fine, I accept £5,000." Is there a contract? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No: B's counter-offer destroyed the original offer, so B is now making a new offer. Hyde v Wrench: a counter-offer rejects the original offer.

  6. Is a price-marked item on a shop shelf normally an offer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    No: it is an invitation to treat. Following Pharmaceutical Society v Boots (1953), displays are invitations to treat.

  7. A promises £500 to anyone who completes a marathon in a set week. B has run 20 miles when A announces the offer is withdrawn. What is the likely position? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A cannot revoke once B has started performance of a unilateral offer. In unilateral contracts, the offeror cannot revoke once the offeree has begun performance (Errington; Daulia v Four Millbank).

  8. A sends standard terms with an order; B replies with its own terms and a delivery note; A takes delivery without objection. Whose terms probably govern? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    B's, as the last shot accepted by A's conduct. Under the traditional analysis, B's form was a counter-offer accepted by A's conduct.

  9. An offer says acceptance must be by "notice in writing to me". B posts an acceptance that is lost. What is the best view? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No contract: the offer required actual notice, which excludes the postal rule. Holwell Securities v Hughes: by requiring actual "notice in writing", the offeror excluded the postal rule, so acceptance had to be received.

Key takeaways

  • For any problem question, work through: formation, terms, vitiating factors, breach or discharge, remedies.
  • Ask: was the statement a definite promise to be bound on acceptance, or an invitation to negotiate?
  • First check whether the "acceptance" introduced anything new. If it did, it is a counter-offer.
  • Postal acceptance: posting. Postal revocation: receipt. That asymmetry decides most exam problems.
  • Ask: what did the promisee give or promise in exchange? Was it at the promisor's request, and was it more than an existing duty?
  • Identify the context, apply the presumption, then look for evidence that rebuts it.

Contract Law mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to contract law
    A contract is a legally binding agreement: an exchange of promises (or a promise for an act) that the law will enforce.
  2. Agreement and offer
    An offer is an expression of willingness to contract on specified terms, made with the intention that it will become binding as soon as it is accepted.
  3. Acceptance
    Acceptance is an unqualified, final agreement to all the terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror.
  4. Communication of acceptance
    The general rule is that acceptance takes effect when it is communicated to the offeror.
  5. Consideration
    Consideration is the price of a promise: something of value given or promised in return.
  6. Intention to create legal relations
    An agreement is only a contract if the parties intended it to be legally binding.
  7. Certainty and completeness
    Courts will only enforce an agreement whose terms are certain enough to know what each party must do.
  8. Contractual terms
    Contractual terms are the promises and obligations that make up the contract.
  9. Express terms
    Express terms are those the parties specifically agree, orally or in writing.
  10. Implied terms
    Implied terms are not expressly agreed but are read into the contract by statute, by the courts as a matter of law for certain types of contract, as a matter of fact to give effect to the parties' pre
  11. Conditions, warranties and innominate terms
    Terms are classified by importance, and the classification decides whether the innocent party can terminate the contract for breach or can only claim damages.
  12. Representations and terms
    Statements made before a contract may become terms (promises the maker guarantees) or remain representations (statements that induced the contract).
  13. Misrepresentation
    A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact or law, made by one party to the other, which induces the other to enter into the contract.
  14. Fraudulent misrepresentation
    A fraudulent misrepresentation is a false statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly, careless whether it is true or false.
  15. Negligent misrepresentation
    Negligent misrepresentation is a false statement made carelessly, without reasonable grounds for believing it true.
  16. Innocent misrepresentation
    An innocent misrepresentation is a false statement made with reasonable grounds for believing it to be true.
  17. Duress
    Duress is illegitimate pressure that coerces a party into a contract.
  18. Undue influence
    Undue influence is an equitable doctrine that sets aside transactions procured by the abuse of a relationship of trust and confidence.
  19. Mistake
    The doctrine of mistake can make a contract void where a fundamental mistake prevents genuine agreement or makes the contract impossible to perform as both parties assumed.
  20. Illegality
    Illegality covers contracts that involve a crime, a civil wrong or conduct contrary to public policy.
  21. Privity of contract
    Privity means only the parties to a contract can enforce it or be bound by it.
  22. Exclusion clauses
    An exclusion clause excludes or limits a party's liability for breach of contract, negligence or misrepresentation.
  23. Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977
    UCTA 1977 controls exclusion and limitation clauses in business-to-business contracts (and non-consumer notices).
  24. Consumer Rights Act 2015
    The Consumer Rights Act 2015 is the main statute for contracts between traders and consumers.
  25. Discharge by performance
    A contract is discharged by performance when both parties have performed their obligations exactly.
  26. Breach of contract
    A breach of contract occurs when a party, without lawful excuse, fails to perform an obligation, performs defectively or repudiates the contract.
  27. Anticipatory breach
    Anticipatory breach occurs when, before performance is due, a party clearly indicates that it will not perform, or puts itself in a position where it cannot.
  28. Frustration
    A contract is frustrated when, after it was made, an event without the fault of either party makes performance impossible, illegal or radically different from what was agreed.
  29. Contractual remedies
    Remedies for breach of contract aim to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed.
  30. Damages
    Contract damages compensate the innocent party for loss caused by the breach, usually by putting them in the position they would have been in if the contract had been performed (the expectation interest).
  31. Specific performance
    Specific performance is an equitable order compelling a party to perform its contractual obligations.
  32. Injunctions
    An injunction is an equitable order requiring a party to do something (mandatory) or refrain from doing something (prohibitory).
  33. Restitution
    Restitution reverses unjust enrichment: where a defendant has been enriched at the claimant's expense in circumstances the law regards as unjust, the claimant may recover the value.
  34. Third-party rights
    The Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 lets a person who isn't a party to a contract enforce a term in their own right in certain circumstances, reforming the strict privity rule.
  35. Consumer contracts
    Consumer contracts are subject to extra protection on top of the Consumer Rights Act 2015, especially for contracts made at a distance or off-premises, and against unfair commercial practices.
  36. Digital and electronic contracts
    Contracts can be made electronically, by email, click-wrap, browse-wrap and even smart contracts.
