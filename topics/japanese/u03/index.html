Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the two irregular verbs?

する (suru, do) and くる (kuru, come). From “Verb groups: ru-verbs, u-verbs and irregular verbs” →

In plain English: Every Japanese verb belongs to one of three groups, and the group decides how the verb changes. Key idea: Ru-verbs end in -iru or -eru and drop る; u-verbs are all the others; する and くる are irregular; some -iru and -eru verbs, such as かえる and はいる, are u-verbs.

How do you say "Let's eat"?

たべましょう (tabemashō). From “Mashou and masen ka: suggestions and invitations” →

In plain English: Japanese uses verb endings to make suggestions and invitations politely. Key idea: ましょう means "let's", ましょうか means "shall we" or "shall I", and ませんか makes a polite invitation; accept with いいですね and decline gently with ちょっと….

How do you say "I go by bus"?

バスで いきます (basu de ikimasu). From “De: place of action and means” →

In plain English: The particle で has several uses. Key idea: で marks the place where an action happens and the means, tool or language used; に marks destinations and where things exist; "on foot" is あるいて.

How is the topic particle は pronounced?

Wa. From “Wa and ga: a first look” →

In plain English: The particles は and が both appear before the subject, but they do different jobs. Key idea: は marks the topic and can show contrast, while が marks the subject, especially after question words, in existence sentences and with すき and じょうず; question words never take は.

How do you say "I take a bath"?

おふろに はいります (ofuro ni hairimasu). From “Daily routine” →

In plain English: Describing a daily routine brings together verbs, particles and time expressions. Key idea: Describe routines with verbs such as おきます, たべます, いきます, かえります and ねます, using time first, the right particles and それから or そして to link steps, in the non-past for habits and the past for a specific day.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Verbs, Particles and Daily Routine

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which verb is a ru-verb? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    たべる (taberu). たべる ends in -eru and is a regular ru-verb, so it drops る to conjugate.

  2. What is the masu form of かく (kaku, write)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    かきます. かく is a u-verb, so く changes to き before ます: かきます (kakimasu).

  3. Which form means "I ate"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    たべました. ました is the polite past ending, so たべました means "I ate".

  4. Which verb ending in -iru or -eru is actually a u-verb? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    はいる (hairu, enter). はいる is a known exception: it ends in -iru but conjugates as a u-verb.

  5. What does あした えいがを みます (ashita eiga o mimasu) mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    I will watch a film tomorrow. The masu form is non-past, and あした (tomorrow) makes it future.

  6. How do you answer "No, I didn't" to べんきょうしましたか? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    いいえ、しませんでした. The past negative of します (shimasu) is しませんでした (shimasen deshita).

  7. Why must learners know a verb's group? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Each group forms the masu, negative and other forms differently. The group tells you which rule to use to change the ending for every other form.

  8. When is the masu form the right choice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Speaking to a teacher or someone you do not know well. The polite masu form suits teachers, strangers and colleagues.

  9. What does きのう テレビを みませんでした (kinō terebi o mimasen deshita) mean? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    I didn't watch TV yesterday. きのう sets the past, and みませんでした is the past negative.

Key takeaways

  • Ru-verbs end in -iru or -eru and drop る; u-verbs are all the others; する and くる are irregular; some -iru and -eru verbs, such as かえる and はいる, are u-verbs.
  • Ru-verbs drop る and add ます, u-verbs change u to i and add ます, and する and くる become します and きます; the masu form is polite and non-past, covering habits and the future.
  • From the masu stem, add ます, ません, ました or ませんでした for the polite non-past, negative, past and past negative; answer questions by repeating the verb in the right form.
  • ましょう means "let's", ましょうか means "shall we" or "shall I", and ませんか makes a polite invitation; accept with いいですね and decline gently with ちょっと….
  • を marks the direct object, is pronounced o and comes before the verb; Japanese sentences usually follow subject, object, verb order, but some verbs, such as あいます, take other particles.
  • に and へ mark destinations with movement verbs; へ is pronounced e; いきます means go, きます means come and かえります means return home; どこも with a negative means "nowhere".

Common mistakes in Verbs, Particles and Daily Routine

  • Treating every verb ending in -eru or -iru as a ru-verb. かえる, はいる, はしる and しる are u-verbs and conjugate differently.
  • Forgetting to change the u sound in u-verbs. のむ becomes のみます, not のむます.
  • Saying ませんでした as ましたでした or ませんました. The past negative is always ません plus でした.
  • Taking ちょっと… as "a little" in a refusal. It signals a polite no.
  • Writing the object particle as お. It is always written を, though pronounced o.
  • Using を for a destination, as in がっこうを いきます. Destinations take に or へ.

Lessons in this school

  1. Verb groups: ru-verbs, u-verbs and irregular verbs
    Every Japanese verb belongs to one of three groups, and the group decides how the verb changes.
  2. The masu form: present and future
    The masu form is the polite verb form used with people you do not know well, in class and at work.
  3. Negative and past masu forms
    Once you know the masu form, its negative and past forms follow one pattern for every verb.
  4. Mashou and masen ka: suggestions and invitations
    Japanese uses verb endings to make suggestions and invitations politely.
  5. The object particle o
    Particles are short words that show the role of each noun in a sentence.
  6. Ni and e: direction and destination
    To say where you are going, Japanese uses the particles に or へ with verbs of movement.
  7. De: place of action and means
    The particle で has several uses.
  8. Ni for time and to for together
    The particle に can mark a specific time, and と can mean "with" a person.
  9. Arimasu and imasu: existence and location
    Japanese has two verbs for "there is" or "to be in a place": one for things and one for living beings.
  10. Wa and ga: a first look
    The particles は and が both appear before the subject, but they do different jobs.
  11. Telling the time
    Telling the time in Japanese uses numbers with counters for hours and minutes, with some irregular readings.
  12. Days of the week and dates
    Days, months and dates in Japanese follow patterns with some irregular readings.
  13. Daily routine
    Describing a daily routine brings together verbs, particles and time expressions.
  14. Frequency adverbs
    Frequency adverbs say how often you do something.
  15. Hoshii and tai: wants
    Japanese has two main ways to say what you want: ほしい for things and ～たい for actions.
