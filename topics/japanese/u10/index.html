How many kanji are on the Jōyō list?

2,136. From “The Joyo kanji list and the JLPT levels” →

In plain English: Japan has an official list of kanji for everyday use, and learners plan their study around it and the JLPT levels. Key idea: The Jōyō list has 2,136 kanji for everyday use and the school list 1,026; the JLPT no longer publishes kanji lists, but estimates run from about 100 at N5 to 2,000 at N1, and kanji are best learned through vocabulary.

How is 手紙 read?

てがみ (tegami), with rendaku. From “Rendaku and sound changes in compounds” →

In plain English: When words combine in Japanese, sounds often change. Key idea: Rendaku voices the first consonant of the second part of many compounds, as in 手紙 and 本棚, but usually not when the second part already has a voiced consonant; gemination doubles consonants in on-reading compounds such as 学校 and 発表.

What does 一石二鳥 (isseki nichō) mean?

Killing two birds with one stone. From “Four-character compounds and set phrases” →

In plain English: Four-character compounds, or yojijukugo, express ideas concisely, often with origins in Chinese classics. Key idea: Yojijukugo such as 一石二鳥, 十人十色, 一期一会, 一生懸命 and 自業自得 express ideas concisely; many come from Chinese classics or Buddhism, and they suit speeches, essays and advice when used sparingly.

What does 立入禁止 (tachiiri kinshi) mean?

No entry. From “Reading signs and notices” →

In plain English: Signs and notices in Japan use short, formal kanji expressions. Key idea: Signs use short kanji expressions such as 入口, 出口, 押, 引, 営業中, 準備中 and ～禁止; notices omit particles and follow patterns such as 日時, 場所 and 料金, so recognising key kanji allows fast reading.

What tense are Japanese stories usually narrated in?

The plain past. From “Reading stories” →

In plain English: Stories in Japanese use narrative techniques such as plain past narration, dialogue and explanatory のだ. Key idea: Stories are usually narrated in the plain past with dialogue in 「」; speakers are identified by context and speech style, explanatory のだ gives reasons, onomatopoeia add atmosphere, and extensive reading of graded readers builds fluency.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Kanji and Reading to N3

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the Jōyō kanji list? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The official list of kanji for everyday use. The Jōyō list sets the kanji used in everyday and official writing.

  2. Which radical is linked to speech and language? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    言. 言 appears in kanji such as 語, 話 and 読, all related to language.

  3. How is the compound 電車 (train) read? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    でんしゃ (densha). Most compounds such as 電車 use on readings for both kanji.

  4. Roughly how many kanji are commonly estimated for JLPT N3? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    About 650. Common estimates put N3 at around 650 kanji, though no official list exists.

  5. What does a semantic-phonetic kanji combine? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A meaning component and a sound component. Most kanji pair a meaning part with a part that suggests the reading.

  6. Which word has a special whole-word reading (jukujikun)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    大人 (おとな). 大人 is read おとな, which cannot be guessed from the individual kanji.

  7. What is the most effective way to learn kanji for reading? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Learning them through common vocabulary in context. Words such as 学校 and 学生 give kanji meaning and useful readings.

  8. An unknown kanji contains 艹 at the top. What is the best guess about its meaning? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It probably relates to plants or grass. 艹 is the grass radical, found in 花, 茶 and 薬.

  9. Which reading does 山 usually take when it stands alone? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its kun reading, やま. Single kanji usually take kun readings, while compounds such as 富士山 use on readings.

Key takeaways

  • The Jōyō list has 2,136 kanji for everyday use and the school list 1,026; the JLPT no longer publishes kanji lists, but estimates run from about 100 at N5 to 2,000 at N1, and kanji are best learned through vocabulary.
  • Radicals classify kanji and often show meaning, such as 氵 for water and 言 for speech; most kanji are semantic-phonetic, combining a meaning component and a sound component such as 青 for セイ, though sounds are not always regular.
  • Most kanji compounds use on readings, some use kun readings, jūbako-yomi mixes on and kun, yutō-yomi mixes kun and on, and jukujikun are special whole-word readings such as 今日 and 大人.
  • Rendaku voices the first consonant of the second part of many compounds, as in 手紙 and 本棚, but usually not when the second part already has a voiced consonant; gemination doubles consonants in on-reading compounds such as 学校 and 発表.
  • Japanese has many homophones, such as こうえん (公園, 講演, 公演) and かみ (紙, 髪, 神); context and kanji distinguish them, and IME choices must be checked carefully when typing.
  • Japanese vocabulary includes wago, native words that sound everyday; kango, Sino-Japanese words that sound formal; and gairaigo, loanwords in katakana that sound modern; choosing among them adjusts register.

Common mistakes in Kanji and Reading to N3

  • Memorising every reading of each kanji in isolation. Learning kanji through common words is more efficient.
  • Assuming the sound component always gives the exact reading. Sound families have exceptions.
  • Reading 大人 as だいにん. It has the special reading おとな.
  • Expecting rendaku in every compound. It is common but irregular, so learn compounds individually.
  • Accepting the IME's first suggestion without checking. Homophones can give a completely different meaning.
  • Using many kango in casual chat. It sounds stiff; choose wago in everyday conversation.

Lessons in this school

  1. The Joyo kanji list and the JLPT levels
    Japan has an official list of kanji for everyday use, and learners plan their study around it and the JLPT levels.
  2. Radicals and semantic-phonetic kanji
    Most kanji are built from smaller parts called radicals and components.
  3. Kanji compounds: on and kun readings together
    Most Japanese words written in kanji are compounds of two or more characters.
  4. Rendaku and sound changes in compounds
    When words combine in Japanese, sounds often change.
  5. Homophones and choosing the right kanji
    Japanese has many words that sound the same but are written with different kanji.
  6. Native, Sino-Japanese and loan vocabulary
    Japanese vocabulary comes from three main sources, and each tends to have a different feel.
  7. Four-character compounds and set phrases
    Four-character compounds, or yojijukugo, express ideas concisely, often with origins in Chinese classics.
  8. Onomatopoeia: giongo and gitaigo
    Japanese has a rich system of sound-symbolic words that describe sounds, states and feelings.
  9. Using a Japanese dictionary
    Finding an unknown kanji or word is an important skill.
  10. Reading signs and notices
    Signs and notices in Japan use short, formal kanji expressions.
  11. Reading emails and messages
    Emails and messages range from formal business mail to casual chats.
  12. Reading news and articles
    News articles use a formal written style with dense kango vocabulary.
  13. Reading stories
    Stories in Japanese use narrative techniques such as plain past narration, dialogue and explanatory のだ.
  14. Vertical writing and Japanese punctuation
    Japanese can be written vertically or horizontally, and it uses its own punctuation marks.
  15. Strategies for unknown kanji
    Readers at every level meet unknown kanji.
