Key terms and formulas at a glance

Mora
A unit of timing in Japanese, each roughly equal in length; one kana letter is one mora
Long vowel
A vowel held for two morae, such as the ā in おばあさん; it contrasts with the short vowel
Pitch accent
A system in which words are distinguished by the pattern of high and low pitch on their morae, not by loudness
Hiragana
The rounded Japanese syllabary of 46 basic letters, used for native words, grammatical endings and particles
Bilabial
Made with both lips, as Japanese ふ (fu) is, rather than with the teeth on the lower lip
Moraic nasal
The kana ん, a nasal consonant that forms a mora on its own and takes its sound from the following consonant
Dakuten
The mark ゛ added to a kana to make its consonant voiced, as in か (ka) to が (ga)
Yōon
A combined sound such as kya or sho, written with an i-column kana and a small ya, yu or yo, counting as one mora
Sokuon
The small っ, which doubles the following consonant and forms a mora of held closure
Topic particle
は, read wa, which marks what a sentence is about
Ojigi
A bow, the usual accompaniment to Japanese greetings, deeper for more respect
Jikoshōkai
A self-introduction (自己紹介), with a conventional structure

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a mora?

A unit of timing in Japanese; each kana letter is one mora, and each takes roughly the same time to say. From “The sounds of Japanese and the mora” →

In plain English: Japanese has a small, regular sound system built from about a hundred short units called morae, and almost every sound will already feel familiar to an English speaker. Key idea: Japanese rhythm counts morae: every kana, including ん, the small っ and the second half of a long vowel, takes one beat of roughly equal length.

In what order are the vowel columns of the kana chart?

a, i, u, e, o. From “Hiragana: the vowel, k and s rows” →

In plain English: Hiragana is the basic Japanese script, a set of 46 letters each standing for one mora, and every learner starts here. Key idea: Hiragana has 46 basic letters, ordered in the gojūon chart by vowel (a i u e o) and consonant row; the vowel, k and s rows are あいうえお, かきくけこ and さしすせそ.

Which row takes the handakuten (maru)?

Only the h row, turning ha hi fu he ho into pa pi pu pe po. From “Voiced sounds: dakuten and handakuten” →

In plain English: Two small marks turn twenty hiragana into twenty-five new sounds: the dakuten makes a consonant voiced, and the handakuten turns h into p. Key idea: The dakuten voices k, s, t and h into g, z, d and b; the handakuten turns h into p; ぢ and づ sound like じ and ず and are used only in compounds and repeated sounds.

How is せんせい (teacher) pronounced?

Sensē, with a long e: the い after え lengthens the vowel. From “Long vowels in hiragana and particle spellings” →

In plain English: Hiragana spells long vowels by adding a vowel kana, but long e and long o have their own rules, and three particles are written with letters that are no longer pronounced as they look. Key idea: Long a, i and u add the same vowel; long e is usually えい and long o usually おう, with a short list of おお words; the particles は, へ and を are read wa, e and o.

What do you say when you get home?

ただいま (tadaima), "I'm home"; the reply is おかえりなさい. From “Set phrases for home, school and the table” →

In plain English: Japanese daily life runs on fixed phrases (aisatsu) that are said at set moments: before eating, leaving home, coming back, entering a room. Key idea: Fixed phrases mark daily moments: いただきます and ごちそうさまでした at meals, いってきます and いってらっしゃい on leaving, ただいま and おかえりなさい on returning, おじゃまします on entering a home.

Quick quiz: test yourself on First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How many vowel sounds does standard Japanese have? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Five: a, i, u, e and o. Japanese has five vowels, a, i, u, e and o, each short and steady, which makes the vowel system far simpler than English.

  2. Which English word has the vowel closest to Japanese い (i)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    machine. Japanese い is always the "ee" of machine, short and steady, never the "eye" sound of ice.

  3. How does a Japanese pitch accent differ from English stress? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It uses high and low pitch, not loudness and length. Japanese keeps morae roughly equal in length and loudness and marks the accent by a fall in pitch.

  4. How many morae are in とうきょう (Tōkyō)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Four. と, the lengthening う, きょ (one mora written with two kana) and the final う make four beats, though English hears two syllables.

  5. ゆき means "snow". What does ゆうき, with a long u, mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    courage. Length makes a different word: ゆうき (yūki) means "courage". It is not a slower way of saying ゆき.

  6. 箸 (chopsticks) and 橋 (bridge) are both はし. How does a Tokyo speaker tell them apart? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    箸 is high-low; 橋 is low-high, falling on the particle. 箸 is atamadaka (high-low); 橋 is odaka (low-high, then a low particle). Vowel length and stress don't differ.

  7. A learner pronounces おばあさん (obāsan, "grandmother") with a short a. What has gone wrong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A mora has been dropped, giving おばさん, "aunt". Long vowels count as two morae. Dropping one turns obāsan (grandmother, five morae) into obasan (aunt, four morae): a different word.

  8. A learner asks for ビル (biru) in a bar and is directed to an office block. What should they have said? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    ビール, holding the i for an extra mora. ビール (bīru, beer) has a long i, three morae; ビル (biru) is "building". Stress and r quality don't distinguish these words, length does.

  9. A learner says 雨が ふります ("it is raining") with あめ low-high and が high. What will a Tokyo listener hear? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The pattern of 飴 (sweets), though context shows rain. Low-high with a high particle is the heiban pattern of 飴 (ame, sweets); 雨 is high-low. Context makes the meaning clear, but the pitch sounds wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Japanese rhythm counts morae: every kana, including ん, the small っ and the second half of a long vowel, takes one beat of roughly equal length.
  • Five short, pure vowels; a long vowel is the same vowel held for a second mora and makes a different word; i and u are often whispered between voiceless consonants.
  • Tokyo Japanese marks words with high and low pitch; the first two morae differ, pitch never rises again after it falls, and the place of the fall can distinguish words.
  • Hiragana has 46 basic letters, ordered in the gojūon chart by vowel (a i u e o) and consonant row; the vowel, k and s rows are あいうえお, かきくけこ and さしすせそ.
  • The t row is たちつてと (ta chi tsu te to), the n row なにぬねの and the h row はひふへほ (ha hi fu he ho); chi, tsu and fu are the irregular sounds.
  • The final rows are まみむめも, やゆよ, らりるれろ, わ and を, and ん; を is only the object particle (read o), and ん is a full mora whose sound adapts to the next consonant.

Common mistakes in First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana

  • Squashing the special morae. Nihon said as two beats, or Tōkyō as "Tokyo", loses beats that Japanese ears expect, and in other words the lost beat changes the meaning.
  • Treating long vowels as optional. Kōkō (high school) said as koko (here) is a different word; listeners rely on length as much as on consonants.
  • Adding English stress. Making one syllable louder and longer, as in "to-KYO", distorts mora length; change the pitch, not the volume.
  • Writing し as si. In Hepburn it is shi, and it is pronounced like English "she": sushi, not "susi". Kunrei-shiki romanisation does write si, but pronunciation is the same.
  • Pronouncing つ as "tsoo" with English rounded lips or as "su". Keep the t closure at the start, then release into s, and spread the lips for u.
  • Using を inside ordinary words. Okāsan (mother) is おかあさん, never をかあさん: を appears only as the particle.

First Steps: Sounds and Hiragana mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. The sounds of Japanese and the mora
    Japanese has a small, regular sound system built from about a hundred short units called morae, and almost every sound will already feel familiar to an English speaker.
  2. Vowels, long vowels and devoicing
    Japanese vowels are short, pure and few, but each can also be held for a second beat, and that length changes meaning.
  3. Pitch accent basics
    Standard Japanese marks words with pitch, high and low, rather than the loud stressed syllables of English.
  4. Hiragana: the vowel, k and s rows
    Hiragana is the basic Japanese script, a set of 46 letters each standing for one mora, and every learner starts here.
  5. Hiragana: the t, n and h rows
    The t, n and h rows add fifteen more hiragana and bring in the first irregular sounds, chi, tsu and fu, where the letter doesn't simply combine a consonant with a vowel.
  6. Hiragana: the m, y, r and w rows and n
    The last rows of the chart complete the 46 basic hiragana: the m, y and r rows, the two letters of the w row, and ん, the only kana that stands for a consonant on its own.
  7. Voiced sounds: dakuten and handakuten
    Two small marks turn twenty hiragana into twenty-five new sounds: the dakuten makes a consonant voiced, and the handakuten turns h into p.
  8. Combined sounds: small ya, yu and yo
    By writing a small や, ゆ or よ after a kana of the i column, Japanese spells sounds like kya, sho and ryu in a single mora.
  9. The small tsu and double consonants
    A small っ before a consonant doubles it, adding a beat of held silence or hiss that changes meaning as much as any letter.
  10. Long vowels in hiragana and particle spellings
    Hiragana spells long vowels by adding a vowel kana, but long e and long o have their own rules, and three particles are written with letters that are no longer pronounced as they look.
  11. Greetings through the day
    Japanese greetings change with the time of day and with how close you are to the person, and they come with a bow rather than a handshake.
  12. Hajimemashite: introducing yourself
    A Japanese self-introduction (jikoshōkai) follows a fixed and polite shape: it opens with hajimemashite and closes with yoroshiku onegaishimasu.
  13. Set phrases for home, school and the table
    Japanese daily life runs on fixed phrases (aisatsu) that are said at set moments: before eating, leaving home, coming back, entering a room.
  14. Numbers 0 to 100
    Japanese numbers are among the most regular in any language: learn one to ten and you can build every number to ninety-nine.
  15. Thank you, sorry and excuse me
    Japanese thanks and apologies come in several strengths, and one word, sumimasen, does the work of "sorry", "excuse me" and even "thank you".
