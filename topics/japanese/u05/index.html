Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the te-form of のむ (nomu, drink)?

のんで (nonde). From “Making the te-form” →

In plain English: The て-form is one of the most useful verb forms in Japanese, used for requests, ongoing actions, permission and linking. Key idea: Ru-verbs drop る and add て; する and くる become して and きて; u-verbs change by ending: う, つ, る to って, む, ぶ, ぬ to んで, く to いて, ぐ to いで, す to して, with いく becoming いって.

How do you ask "May I go to the toilet?"

トイレに いっても いいですか (toire ni ittemo ii desu ka). From “Te mo ii desu and te wa ikemasen” →

In plain English: Japanese uses the て-form to give and ask permission and to forbid actions. Key idea: Use て-form + も いいです to give permission, ～ても いいですか to ask, and て-form + は いけません to forbid; answer permission requests with ええ、どうぞ or a gentle refusal.

What is the dictionary form of かいます (kaimasu, buy)?

かう (kau). From “The dictionary form” →

In plain English: The dictionary form is the plain non-past form of a verb. Key idea: The dictionary form is the plain non-past, made by changing masu forms back to る, the u sound, する or くる; it is used in casual speech, before nouns and in patterns such as ～ことが すきです and しゅみは ～ことです.

What polite form matches たべなかった (tabenakatta)?

たべませんでした (tabemasen deshita). From “Plain past negative: nakatta” →

In plain English: The plain past negative says "didn't" in casual speech. Key idea: The plain past negative is made by changing ない to なかった; the four plain forms are dictionary form, ない-form, た-form and なかった-form, each matching a polite masu form.

What does the tari-tari pattern imply?

That the actions are examples and there are others. From “Tari tari: listing actions” →

In plain English: ～たり～たり します lists examples of activities without giving a complete list or order. Key idea: た-form + り, repeated and followed by します, lists representative actions without order; the tense goes on します, and it contrasts with the て-form, which lists actions in sequence.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Te-form and Plain Forms

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the te-form of たべる (taberu, eat)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    たべて. たべる is a ru-verb, so drop る and add て to make たべて.

  2. Which phrase means "please wait a moment" (chotto matte kudasai)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    ちょっと まって ください. まつ becomes まって in the te-form, then ください makes it a request.

  3. What does いま ほんを よんで います (ima hon o yonde imasu) mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    I am reading a book now. ～て います with いま shows an action in progress right now.

  4. What is the te-form of かく (kaku, write)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    かいて. Verbs ending in く change to いて, so かく becomes かいて.

  5. How do you say "Please don't eat here" (koko de tabenaide kudasai)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    ここで たべないで ください. Negative requests use the ない-form followed by で ください.

  6. How do you say "My sister is married" (ane wa kekkon shite imasu)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    あねは けっこんして います. Resulting states such as being married use ～て います.

  7. What is the te-form of いく (iku), and why is it unusual? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    いって, because く verbs normally give いて. いく is the one く-verb that takes って instead of いて.

  8. Which request is most polite for asking a professor to check your essay (chekku shite kudasaimasen ka)? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    チェックして くださいませんか. くださいませんか softens the request, which suits a superior.

  9. Why is しって いません (shitte imasen) usually wrong for "I don't know"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The standard negative of しって います is しりません. Japanese uses しりません for "I don't know", not しって いません.

Key takeaways

  • Ru-verbs drop る and add て; する and くる become して and きて; u-verbs change by ending: う, つ, る to って, む, ぶ, ぬ to んで, く to いて, ぐ to いで, す to して, with いく becoming いって.
  • Use て-form + ください for polite requests and ない-form + で ください for "please don't"; for superiors, softer forms such as ～て くださいませんか are more polite.
  • ～て います shows an action in progress, a resulting state with change-of-state verbs such as けっこんする, ongoing habits and jobs; すんで います means lives, and しって います means knows, with the negative しりません.
  • Use て-form + も いいです to give permission, ～ても いいですか to ask, and て-form + は いけません to forbid; answer permission requests with ええ、どうぞ or a gentle refusal.
  • The て-form links actions in sequence with the final verb carrying tense; い-adjectives link with くて and な-adjectives and nouns with で; the て-form can also show manner, as in あるいて いきます.
  • て-form + から means "after doing", stressing that one action is completed before the next; the final verb carries the tense, and it is useful in routines and instructions.

Common mistakes in The Te-form and Plain Forms

  • Writing いいて for いく. The て-form of いく is the exception いって.
  • Using ～て ください to ask a senior person a favour. ～て くださいませんか sounds more polite.
  • Saying けっこんします to mean "I am married". That means "I will marry"; "I am married" is けっこんして います.
  • Answering ～ても いいですか with いけません to a guest. This sounds harsh; a softer refusal with a reason is more polite.
  • Putting every verb in the past, as in かえりました、たべました、しました in one sentence. Use て-forms and put only the last verb in the past.
  • Confusing ～てから with から meaning "because". ～てから always follows the te-form and means "after".

Lessons in this school

  1. Making the te-form
    The て-form is one of the most useful verb forms in Japanese, used for requests, ongoing actions, permission and linking.
  2. Te kudasai: requests
    The て-form plus ください makes a polite request.
  3. Te imasu: ongoing actions and resulting states
    The て-form plus います has two main meanings: an action in progress and a state that results from a past change.
  4. Te mo ii desu and te wa ikemasen
    Japanese uses the て-form to give and ask permission and to forbid actions.
  5. Linking actions and states with the te-form
    The て-form links verbs, adjectives and nouns into longer sentences.
  6. Te kara: after doing
    ～てから means "after doing" and makes the order of actions explicit.
  7. The dictionary form
    The dictionary form is the plain non-past form of a verb.
  8. The nai form
    The ない-form is the plain negative of a verb.
  9. The plain past: the ta form
    The た-form is the plain past of a verb.
  10. Plain past negative: nakatta
    The plain past negative says "didn't" in casual speech.
  11. Casual speech between friends
    Friends and family speak casually using plain forms and other features.
  12. Ta koto ga aru: experiences
    ～たことが あります means "have done" in the sense of experience.
  13. Tari tari: listing actions
    ～たり～たり します lists examples of activities without giving a complete list or order.
  14. To omoimasu and to iimasu
    と おもいます and と いいます let you give opinions and report what people say.
  15. Noun-modifying clauses
    Japanese describes nouns by putting a whole clause before them, like English relative clauses but without "who" or "which".
  16. Kara and node: giving reasons
    から and ので both mean "because" or "so".
