Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which katakana is tsu, and how is it different from shi?

ツ is tsu: its short strokes sit across the top and the long stroke falls from the top right; シ (shi) has the short strokes on the left and a long stroke rising from the bottom. From “Katakana: the basic chart” →

In plain English: Katakana is the second Japanese syllabary, with the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana but sharper, more angular shapes. Key idea: Katakana spells the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana, in sharper shapes, and is used mainly for loanwords, foreign names, sound effects and emphasis.

What is the difference between an on reading and a kun reading?

An on reading is based on a Chinese pronunciation and is used mainly in compounds; a kun reading is the native Japanese word for the meaning, used mainly on its own. From “What kanji are: on and kun readings” →

In plain English: Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese that carry meaning as well as sound, and most have two kinds of reading: a Chinese-derived on reading and a native Japanese kun reading. Key idea: A kanji carries a meaning, and most have an on reading from Chinese, used mainly in compounds, and a kun reading from native Japanese, used mainly on its own or with kana endings.

What two kanji make up 休, and what does it mean?

亻 (person) and 木 (tree); it means rest, as in 休む (yasumu). From “First kanji: people, places and nature” →

In plain English: Many early kanji began as pictures of what they mean, and others combine pictures to make new meanings. Key idea: Some common kanji are pictures of what they mean, others combine meaningful parts, and learning the components makes each new kanji easier to remember.

How do you say "Where is the toilet?"

トイレは どこですか (toire wa doko desu ka). From “Questions with ka and question words” →

In plain English: Japanese turns a statement into a question simply by adding か at the end, and question words stay in the same place as the answer would. Key idea: Add か to a polite sentence to make a question, keep the word order, and put question words where the answer will go; never mark a question word with は.

How do you ask "Which one is your bag?"

どれが あなたの かばんですか (dore ga anata no kaban desu ka). From “Kore, sore, are and dore” →

In plain English: Japanese points at things with a set of words built on ko-, so-, a- and do-, which divide space according to where the speaker and the listener are. Key idea: これ is near the speaker, それ near the listener, あれ away from both, and どれ asks which one; when you answer, the viewpoint swaps.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which katakana spells the sound ka? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    カ. カ is ka; it grew from the left part of the kanji 加. ク is ku, タ is ta and ケ is ke.

  2. How is ジュース read? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    jūsu. ジュ is ji with a small yu, giving ju; the bar lengthens it, and ス is su, so jūsu (juice).

  3. How is パーティー (party) read? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    pātī. ティ is the extended katakana for ti, and each bar lengthens the vowel before it, giving pātī.

  4. What does the katakana word カメラ mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Camera. カ ka, メ me and ラ ra spell kamera, the English word camera reshaped into Japanese morae.

  5. Which spelling of "cake" is correct? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    ケーキ. Loanwords mark long vowels with the bar ー, so cake is ke plus a long mark plus ki: ケーキ (kēki).

  6. Why does "test" become テスト (tesuto) in Japanese? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Japanese morae end in vowels, so final consonants gain one. English final consonants and clusters are given extra vowels, usually u, or o after t and d, so test becomes te-su-to.

  7. A learner writes the name "Sue" as スウ instead of スー. Why is this a problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    ウ suggests a separate u vowel instead of a length mark. Loanwords and foreign names mark long vowels with the bar ー, so a standard spelling is スー; スウ would usually be read as two separate vowels.

  8. Why does ビール (beer) have three morae but ビル (building) only two? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    ー adds a full beat to the vowel before it. The long vowel bar counts as one mora, so bi-i-ru has three beats and bi-ru two; length is what separates the two words.

  9. A Japanese colleague says she lives in a マンション. What should an English speaker understand? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She lives in a flat in an apartment block. マンション is wasei-eigo for a block of flats, usually a solid concrete building; it does not mean a grand mansion.

Key takeaways

  • Katakana spells the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana, in sharper shapes, and is used mainly for loanwords, foreign names, sound effects and emphasis.
  • Katakana voices and combines sounds just as hiragana does, but writes long vowels with the bar ー, which counts as a full mora.
  • Loanwords are reshaped into Japanese morae with added vowels, l and r merge into the r row, extended katakana covers sounds such as fa and ti, and many loanwords are clipped or are Japan-made English.
  • A kanji carries a meaning, and most have an on reading from Chinese, used mainly in compounds, and a kun reading from native Japanese, used mainly on its own or with kana endings.
  • Kanji follow standard stroke-order rules (top to bottom, left to right, horizontal before vertical, close the box last), and recurring radicals hint at meaning and organise dictionaries.
  • Number kanji build large numbers in units of ten thousand, the days of the week take the sun, moon and five elements, and time words such as 時, 分 and 半 have a few irregular readings to learn.

Common mistakes in Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence

  • Writing シ and ツ (or ソ and ン) with the strokes in the wrong direction. In print they look alike, but by hand the direction of the long stroke is the only difference: up from the bottom for シ and ン, down from the top for ツ and ソ.
  • Spelling a loanword's long vowel with a vowel letter, as in コオヒイ for coffee. Loanwords take the bar: コーヒー. Vowel letters for length belong to hiragana spelling.
  • Assuming every katakana word means what it does in English. マンション is a flat, not a mansion, and アルバイト comes from German; check the meaning before you use a loanword.
  • Learning one reading per kanji and expecting it everywhere. Learn each kanji in words, such as 山 in 山 (yama) and 富士山 (Fujisan), so that each reading is tied to real vocabulary.
  • Treating a turning stroke as two strokes. In 口 and 田 the top and right side are one stroke that bends at the corner; counting them separately gives the wrong stroke count.
  • Reading 四時 as shiji or yonji, or 九時 as kyūji. The hours four and nine are irregular: yoji and kuji. Likewise April is shigatsu and September kugatsu.

Lessons in this school

  1. Katakana: the basic chart
    Katakana is the second Japanese syllabary, with the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana but sharper, more angular shapes.
  2. Katakana: voiced sounds, combinations and the long vowel mark
    Katakana uses the same dakuten, handakuten, small ya, yu and yo, and small tsu as hiragana, plus one device of its own: the long vowel bar ー.
  3. Loanwords and extended katakana
    Japanese borrows thousands of words, mostly from English, and fits them into its own sound system with extra vowels and some special katakana combinations.
  4. What kanji are: on and kun readings
    Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese that carry meaning as well as sound, and most have two kinds of reading: a Chinese-derived on reading and a native Japanese kun reading.
  5. Strokes, stroke order and radicals
    Every kanji is built from a small set of stroke types written in a standard order, and many share recurring components called radicals.
  6. First kanji: numbers, days and time
    Numbers, days of the week and time words are the first kanji most learners meet, because they appear on every calendar, timetable and price tag.
  7. First kanji: people, places and nature
    Many early kanji began as pictures of what they mean, and others combine pictures to make new meanings.
  8. Okurigana and furigana
    Japanese writing uses kana alongside kanji in two ways: okurigana spell the changing endings of verbs and adjectives, and furigana are small kana printed beside a kanji to show its reading.
  9. X wa Y desu
    The pattern X は Y です (X wa Y desu, "X is Y") is the first Japanese sentence most learners build, and it shows three key features of the language: the topic particle, the polite copula and the verb at the end.
  10. Questions with ka and question words
    Japanese turns a statement into a question simply by adding か at the end, and question words stay in the same place as the answer would.
  11. Negative and past of desu
    です has four basic polite forms: present and past, affirmative and negative.
  12. The particle no
    The particle の (no) links two nouns, with the first describing the second, and it covers English "'s", "of" and many noun-plus-noun phrases.
  13. Kore, sore, are and dore
    Japanese points at things with a set of words built on ko-, so-, a- and do-, which divide space according to where the speaker and the listener are.
  14. Kono, sono, ano and koko, soko, asoko
    The ko-so-a-do pattern extends to words that come before a noun (この, その, あの, どの) and to place words (ここ, そこ, あそこ, どこ).
  15. Nationalities, languages and jobs
    Nationalities and languages in Japanese are built regularly from country names with the suffixes 人 (jin) and 語 (go), and jobs are simple nouns used with です.
  16. Mo: also and too
    The particle も (mo) means "also" or "too", and in negative sentences "not either".

Katakana, First Kanji and the Basic Sentence: questions students ask

How is a long vowel written in a katakana loanword?

With the long vowel bar ー after the vowel, as in コーヒー (kōhī).

What does パソコン come from, and what does it mean?

A clipped form of personal computer; it means a PC or computer.

In 中, which stroke is written last?

The long vertical that pierces the box.

How is 四時 read?

Yoji (four o'clock), not shiji or yonji.
