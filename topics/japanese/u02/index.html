Which katakana is tsu, and how is it different from shi?

ツ is tsu: its short strokes sit across the top and the long stroke falls from the top right; シ (shi) has the short strokes on the left and a long stroke rising from the bottom. From “Katakana: the basic chart” →

What is the difference between an on reading and a kun reading?

An on reading is based on a Chinese pronunciation and is used mainly in compounds; a kun reading is the native Japanese word for the meaning, used mainly on its own. From “What kanji are: on and kun readings” →

What two kanji make up 休, and what does it mean?

亻 (person) and 木 (tree); it means rest, as in 休む (yasumu). From “First kanji: people, places and nature” →

How do you say "Where is the toilet?"

トイレは どこですか (toire wa doko desu ka). From “Questions with ka and question words” →

How do you ask "Which one is your bag?"

どれが あなたの かばんですか (dore ga anata no kaban desu ka). From “Kore, sore, are and dore” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Katakana: the basic chart
    Katakana is the second Japanese syllabary, with the same 46 basic sounds as hiragana but sharper, more angular shapes.
  2. Katakana: voiced sounds, combinations and the long vowel mark
    Katakana uses the same dakuten, handakuten, small ya, yu and yo, and small tsu as hiragana, plus one device of its own: the long vowel bar ー.
  3. Loanwords and extended katakana
    Japanese borrows thousands of words, mostly from English, and fits them into its own sound system with extra vowels and some special katakana combinations.
  4. What kanji are: on and kun readings
    Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese that carry meaning as well as sound, and most have two kinds of reading: a Chinese-derived on reading and a native Japanese kun reading.
  5. Strokes, stroke order and radicals
    Every kanji is built from a small set of stroke types written in a standard order, and many share recurring components called radicals.
  6. First kanji: numbers, days and time
    Numbers, days of the week and time words are the first kanji most learners meet, because they appear on every calendar, timetable and price tag.
  7. First kanji: people, places and nature
    Many early kanji began as pictures of what they mean, and others combine pictures to make new meanings.
  8. Okurigana and furigana
    Japanese writing uses kana alongside kanji in two ways: okurigana spell the changing endings of verbs and adjectives, and furigana are small kana printed beside a kanji to show its reading.
  9. X wa Y desu
    The pattern X は Y です (X wa Y desu, "X is Y") is the first Japanese sentence most learners build, and it shows three key features of the language: the topic particle, the polite copula and the verb at the end.
  10. Questions with ka and question words
    Japanese turns a statement into a question simply by adding か at the end, and question words stay in the same place as the answer would.
  11. Negative and past of desu
    です has four basic polite forms: present and past, affirmative and negative.
  12. The particle no
    The particle の (no) links two nouns, with the first describing the second, and it covers English "'s", "of" and many noun-plus-noun phrases.
  13. Kore, sore, are and dore
    Japanese points at things with a set of words built on ko-, so-, a- and do-, which divide space according to where the speaker and the listener are.
  14. Kono, sono, ano and koko, soko, asoko
    The ko-so-a-do pattern extends to words that come before a noun (この, その, あの, どの) and to place words (ここ, そこ, あそこ, どこ).
  15. Nationalities, languages and jobs
    Nationalities and languages in Japanese are built regularly from country names with the suffixes 人 (jin) and 語 (go), and jobs are simple nouns used with です.
  16. Mo: also and too
    The particle も (mo) means "also" or "too", and in negative sentences "not either".

Key terms

Katakana
The angular Japanese syllabary used chiefly for loanwords, foreign names, onomatopoeia and emphasis
Chōonpu
The long vowel mark ー, used mainly in katakana to lengthen the preceding vowel by one mora
Wasei-eigo
Japan-made English: words built from English parts with a meaning or form not used in English
Kanji
Chinese-derived characters that stand for meaning as well as sound
Stroke order
The standard sequence in which the strokes of a character are written
Yōbi
Day of the week (曜日), added to the day's element kanji, as in 月曜日
Pictograph
A character that began as a picture of its meaning, such as 山 or 川
Okurigana
Hiragana written after a kanji stem to spell the inflecting part of a verb or adjective
Topic
What a sentence is about, marked in Japanese by the particle は
Ka
The sentence-final particle that turns a statement into a question
Ja
The contracted spoken form of では, used in the negative of です
No
The particle linking a describing noun to a main noun, like English 's
Ko-so-a-do
The system of demonstrative words beginning ko-, so-, a- and do-
Rentaishi
A pre-noun word such as この or その that can only appear before a noun
