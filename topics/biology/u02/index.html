Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

State the three principles of cell theory.

All organisms are made of one or more cells; the cell is the basic unit of structure and function; all cells come from pre-existing cells. From “Cell theory” →

In plain English: Cell theory says that every living thing is made of cells, that the cell is the basic unit of life, and that new cells come only from existing cells. Key idea: Every organism is made of cells, and every cell comes from another cell: life is continuous at the cellular level.

Why do Gram-positive bacteria stain purple?

Their thick peptidoglycan layer keeps the crystal violet stain after the decolourising step. From “Bacterial cells” →

In plain English: Bacteria are the most familiar prokaryotes. Key idea: Bacterial walls are made of peptidoglycan; its thickness and the presence of an outer membrane divide bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative.

Name three functions of smooth ER.

Making lipids (phospholipids, cholesterol, steroid hormones), detoxifying drugs and storing calcium ions. From “Endoplasmic reticulum” →

In plain English: The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes spreading through the cytoplasm from the nuclear envelope. Key idea: Rough ER makes and folds proteins for export and membranes; smooth ER makes lipids, detoxifies and stores calcium.

Which transport processes are passive?

Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis. From “Membrane transport” →

In plain English: Substances cross membranes in several ways, which differ in whether they need energy and whether they need a protein. Key idea: Passive transport goes down gradients without energy; active and bulk transport use energy. The route depends on a molecule's size, charge and polarity.

How is a red blood cell adapted for carrying oxygen?

Biconcave shape for a large surface area and flexibility; no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin. From “Cell specialisation” →

In plain English: In a multicellular organism, cells take on different shapes and contents to do particular jobs. Key idea: Specialised cells have structures adapted to particular functions, which makes the organism efficient but makes each cell dependent on the others.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cell Biology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who stated that all cells arise from pre-existing cells? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Rudolf Virchow. Virchow popularised "omnis cellula e cellula" in 1855, building on Remak's work; Hooke named cells, Mendel studied inheritance and Darwin evolution.

  2. Which structure is found in prokaryotic cells? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    70S ribosomes. Prokaryotes have ribosomes but no membrane-bound organelles such as a nucleus, mitochondria or Golgi.

  3. Which feature is found in eukaryotic but not prokaryotic cells? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A nuclear envelope. All cells have ribosomes, a membrane and DNA; only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nuclear envelope.

  4. A student says a light microscope at ×2000 should show ribosomes clearly. Why is this wrong? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ribosomes are about 25 nm across, far below the light microscope's resolution of about 0.2 µm. Resolution, not magnification, decides what can be seen; ribosomes need an electron microscope.

  5. Antibiotic resistance appears in a different bacterial species living in the same gut. What is the most likely route? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A plasmid carrying the resistance gene was transferred between the bacteria. Plasmids can be passed between bacteria, even of different species, through processes such as conjugation.

  6. An organism is single-celled, has a nucleus and mitochondria, and no cell wall. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A eukaryote such as an amoeba. A nucleus and mitochondria make it eukaryotic; being single-celled doesn't make it prokaryotic.

  7. Which observation would most directly challenge the third principle of cell theory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A new cell forming from non-living chemicals in a sealed sterile flask. Only the formation of a cell without a parent cell contradicts "all cells arise from pre-existing cells"; the others are consistent with cell theory.

  8. Why are prokaryotic cells almost never larger than a few micrometres? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Without internal transport systems they depend on diffusion, which is only fast enough when the surface area to volume ratio is large. Diffusion time grows with distance, and the surface area to volume ratio falls as a cell gets bigger, so small size supports fast exchange.

  9. Why do mitochondria have ribosomes that resemble bacterial ribosomes? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are descended from bacteria and kept part of their own protein-making system. Mitochondrial 70S-type ribosomes are a relic of their bacterial ancestry, part of the evidence for endosymbiosis.

Key takeaways

  • Every organism is made of cells, and every cell comes from another cell: life is continuous at the cellular level.
  • Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; their DNA is in a nucleoid, often with plasmids.
  • Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound compartments, which let them be larger and more specialised than prokaryotes.
  • Animal cells have no wall or chloroplasts; their flexible membranes allow movement and engulfing, but make them vulnerable to bursting in dilute solutions.
  • The cell wall, chloroplasts and large vacuole let plant cells photosynthesise and stay rigid through turgor pressure.
  • Fungal cells are eukaryotic with chitin walls and no chloroplasts; fungi feed by external digestion and absorption.

Common mistakes in Cell Biology

  • Confusing magnification with resolution. Enlarging a blurry image more doesn't reveal more detail; only better resolution does.
  • Saying prokaryotes have no DNA organisation or no ribosomes. They have a chromosome and many ribosomes; they lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Thinking "eukaryotic" means "multicellular". Many eukaryotes, such as yeast and amoebae, are single cells.
  • Drawing a large central vacuole in an animal cell. Animal cells have small vesicles and vacuoles, not one large permanent vacuole.
  • Thinking the cell wall controls what enters the cell. The wall is fully permeable; it is the plasma membrane that is selective.
  • Grouping fungi with plants. Fungi don't photosynthesise, have chitin rather than cellulose walls, and are more closely related to animals than to plants.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cell theory
    Cell theory says that every living thing is made of cells, that the cell is the basic unit of life, and that new cells come only from existing cells.
  2. Prokaryotic cells
    Prokaryotes, the bacteria and archaea, are cells without a nucleus or membrane-bound organelles.
  3. Eukaryotic cells
    Eukaryotic cells, found in animals, plants, fungi and protists, keep their DNA in a nucleus and divide their interior into membrane-bound compartments.
  4. Animal cells
    Animal cells are eukaryotic cells without a cell wall or chloroplasts.
  5. Plant cells
    Plant cells share the eukaryotic plan with animal cells but add three features: a cellulose wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole.
  6. Fungal cells
    Fungi, including yeasts, moulds and mushrooms, are eukaryotes with cell walls made of chitin rather than cellulose.
  7. Bacterial cells
    Bacteria are the most familiar prokaryotes.
  8. Cell organelles
    Organelles are the specialised compartments and structures inside eukaryotic cells.
  9. Nucleus and nucleolus
    The nucleus holds almost all of a eukaryotic cell's DNA and controls which genes are used.
  10. Mitochondria
    Mitochondria are the organelles where aerobic respiration releases most of the energy in food, storing it as ATP.
  11. Chloroplasts
    Chloroplasts are the organelles of photosynthesis in plants and algae.
  12. Ribosomes
    Ribosomes are the cell's protein factories: they read the code in messenger RNA and join amino acids in the order it specifies.
  13. Endoplasmic reticulum
    The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes spreading through the cytoplasm from the nuclear envelope.
  14. Golgi apparatus
    The Golgi apparatus is the cell's processing and dispatch centre.
  15. Lysosomes and peroxisomes
    Lysosomes and peroxisomes are small membrane-bound organelles that break things down.
  16. Cytoskeleton
    The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments that gives the cell its shape, holds organelles in place, moves materials around and lets cells crawl, contract and divide.
  17. Cell membranes
    Every cell is bounded by a plasma membrane, and eukaryotic cells are full of internal membranes too.
  18. Membrane structure
    Membranes are built from a double layer of phospholipids with proteins floating in it, a structure described by the fluid mosaic model.
  19. Membrane transport
    Substances cross membranes in several ways, which differ in whether they need energy and whether they need a protein.
  20. Diffusion
    Diffusion is the net movement of particles from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated, driven by their random motion.
  21. Osmosis
    Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a partially permeable membrane.
  22. Facilitated diffusion
    Facilitated diffusion is passive movement down a concentration gradient through specific membrane proteins.
  23. Active transport
    Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient using energy, usually from ATP.
  24. Endocytosis and exocytosis
    Some cargo is far too large for channels or carriers.
  25. Cell specialisation
    In a multicellular organism, cells take on different shapes and contents to do particular jobs.
  26. Stem cells
    Stem cells are unspecialised cells that can divide to renew themselves and can also give rise to specialised cells.
  27. Cell differentiation
    Differentiation is the process by which a cell becomes specialised.
  28. Cell signalling
    Cells constantly send and receive chemical signals that coordinate growth, metabolism and defence.
  29. Cell junctions
    In tissues, cells are physically joined to their neighbours.
  30. Cellular organisation
    Multicellular organisms are organised in levels: specialised cells form tissues, tissues form organs, and organs work together in organ systems.

Cell Biology: questions students ask

What is the difference between magnification and resolution?

Magnification is how much larger the image is; resolution is the smallest separation at which two points can be seen as separate.

Why can transcription and translation be coupled in bacteria?

There is no nuclear envelope separating DNA from ribosomes, so ribosomes can translate mRNA as it is being transcribed.

Why does a red blood cell burst in pure water?

Water enters by osmosis and, with no cell wall to resist expansion, the membrane stretches until it ruptures.

What does the tonoplast surround?

The large permanent vacuole of a plant cell.
