Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is turgor pressure?

The pressure of the cell contents pushing against the cell wall. From “Plant cell structure” →

In plain English: Plant cells share features with animal cells but have distinctive structures. Key idea: Plant cells have a cellulose wall, a large vacuole maintaining turgor, plastids such as chloroplasts and amyloplasts, and plasmodesmata linking cells into the symplast; turgor supports the plant, and its loss causes wilting.

What is the function of pits in xylem?

To allow water to move sideways between vessels. From “Xylem” →

In plain English: Xylem is specialised for transporting water and providing support. Key idea: Xylem vessels and tracheids are dead, lignified cells forming tubes without cytoplasm or end walls; lignin waterproofs and strengthens them against tension, pits allow sideways flow, and wood is secondary xylem.

What is produced when GP is reduced?

Triose phosphate (TP). From “Calvin cycle” →

In plain English: The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugars. Key idea: In the Calvin cycle, RuBisCO fixes CO₂ to RuBP forming GP, GP is reduced to TP using ATP and NADPH, most TP regenerates RuBP and some forms sugars and other molecules; RuBisCO can also cause photorespiration.

What is the role of gibberellin in seed germination?

It stimulates the aleurone layer to produce amylase. From “Gibberellins” →

In plain English: Gibberellins promote stem elongation and seed germination. Key idea: Gibberellins cause stem elongation and promote germination by triggering amylase production in the aleurone layer; they are used in grape production and malting, and dwarfing genes affecting gibberellin response contributed to the Green Revolution.

What is pollination?

The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. From “Pollination” →

In plain English: Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma. Key idea: Pollination transfers pollen from anther to stigma, by self- or cross-pollination; insect-pollinated flowers attract insects with petals, scent and nectar, while wind-pollinated flowers produce much light pollen with exposed anthers and feathery stigmas, and pollinators are vital for many crops.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Plant Biology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which plastid stores starch in plant cells? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Amyloplast. Amyloplasts are starch-storing plastids found in roots and tubers.

  2. Which ground tissue gives rigid support and is often dead? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sclerenchyma. Sclerenchyma cells have thick lignified walls for rigid support.

  3. Which structure protects the growing tip of a root? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The root cap. The root cap shields dividing cells as the root pushes through soil.

  4. Why does a plant wilt when it lacks water? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Its cells lose turgor pressure and become flaccid. Without water, vacuoles shrink and cells stop pressing on walls.

  5. What does the vascular cambium produce? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    New xylem and phloem, increasing stem girth. Cambium is a lateral meristem responsible for secondary growth.

  6. How do mycorrhizal fungi benefit plants? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They increase uptake of phosphate and water. Fungal hyphae extend far into the soil and pass on nutrients.

  7. What is the symplast pathway? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Movement through cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata. The symplast links the cytoplasm of many cells into one pathway.

  8. Why can plants be grown from cuttings? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Meristem cells can produce all plant tissues. Meristematic cells can regenerate roots and shoots from cuttings.

  9. What is the role of the Casparian strip? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It forces water and ions through endodermal cell membranes. Blocking the wall pathway lets the plant control what enters the xylem.

Key takeaways

  • Plant cells have a cellulose wall, a large vacuole maintaining turgor, plastids such as chloroplasts and amyloplasts, and plasmodesmata linking cells into the symplast; turgor supports the plant, and its loss causes wilting.
  • Plants have dermal, vascular and ground tissue systems; ground tissue includes parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma, and meristems allow lifelong primary and secondary growth.
  • Roots have a cap, growth zones, root hairs and layers including the endodermis with a Casparian strip; root systems are tap or fibrous, and root hairs and mycorrhizal fungi increase absorption of water and minerals.
  • Stems support, transport and sometimes store; vascular bundles form a ring in eudicots and are scattered in monocots, and modified stems include tubers, rhizomes, stolons, thorns and tendrils.
  • Leaves have a cuticle, epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll, veins and stomata; they are broad and thin for light and gas exchange, and guard cells open stomata by taking up K⁺ and water and close them in darkness or drought.
  • Xylem carries water and minerals upwards and phloem carries sugars in both directions; their arrangement differs in roots, stems and leaves, and vascular tissue allowed plants to grow tall on land.

Common mistakes in Plant Biology

  • Thinking all plant cells contain chloroplasts. Root cells and many internal cells lack them.
  • Thinking plants stop growing when they reach adult size. Meristems allow growth throughout life.
  • Thinking roots absorb water through the root cap. Most absorption occurs through root hairs in the maturation zone.
  • Calling a potato a root. A potato is a stem tuber with buds at its nodes.
  • Thinking stomata are mostly on the upper surface. In most land plants they are mainly on the lower surface.
  • Thinking phloem only carries sugars downwards. Phloem carries sugars both up and down, from sources to sinks.

Lessons in this school

  1. Plant cell structure
    Plant cells share features with animal cells but have distinctive structures.
  2. Plant tissues
    Plants are built from a few main tissue types.
  3. Roots and root systems
    Roots anchor plants and absorb water and minerals.
  4. Stems
    Stems support plants and transport materials between roots and leaves.
  5. Leaves
    Leaves are the main organs of photosynthesis.
  6. Plant vascular tissue
    Vascular tissue transports water, minerals and sugars through plants.
  7. Xylem
    Xylem is specialised for transporting water and providing support.
  8. Phloem
    Phloem transports sugars and other organic substances.
  9. Water transport
    Water moves from soil to leaves through roots and xylem.
  10. Transpiration
    Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves.
  11. Mineral nutrition
    Plants need mineral ions from the soil for growth.
  12. Photosynthesis
    Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in sugars.
  13. Light-dependent reactions
    The light-dependent reactions capture light energy to make ATP and NADPH.
  14. Calvin cycle
    The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugars.
  15. C3 photosynthesis
    Most plants use C3 photosynthesis, named after its first product.
  16. C4 photosynthesis
    C4 plants concentrate CO₂ around RuBisCO to avoid photorespiration.
  17. CAM photosynthesis
    CAM plants separate CO₂ uptake and the Calvin cycle in time.
  18. Cellular respiration in plants
    Plants respire as well as photosynthesise.
  19. Plant hormones
    Plant hormones coordinate growth and responses to the environment.
  20. Auxins
    Auxins control cell elongation and many growth responses.
  21. Gibberellins
    Gibberellins promote stem elongation and seed germination.
  22. Cytokinins
    Cytokinins promote cell division and delay ageing.
  23. Abscisic acid
    Abscisic acid helps plants respond to stress and controls dormancy.
  24. Ethylene
    Ethylene is a gaseous hormone that controls ripening and ageing.
  25. Tropisms
    Tropisms are growth responses to directional stimuli.
  26. Photoperiodism
    Photoperiodism is the response of plants to day length.
  27. Flowering
    Flowers are the reproductive structures of flowering plants.
  28. Pollination
    Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma.
  29. Fertilisation
    In flowering plants, fertilisation follows pollination.
  30. Seed formation
    Seeds contain an embryo and food store protected by a coat.
  31. Germination
    Germination is the start of growth of a seed into a seedling.
  32. Plant reproduction
    Plants reproduce sexually and asexually.
  33. Plant responses to stress
    Plants cannot move away from danger, so they respond to stress in other ways.
  34. Plant biotechnology
    Plant biotechnology uses scientific techniques to improve and propagate plants.
