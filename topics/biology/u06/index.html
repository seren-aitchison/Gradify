Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who independently proposed natural selection alongside Darwin?

Alfred Russel Wallace. From “Origins of evolutionary thought” →

In plain English: Ideas about how species change developed over centuries before and after Darwin. Key idea: Early naturalists saw species as fixed, geologists showed an ancient Earth, Lamarck proposed inheritance of acquired traits, Darwin and Wallace proposed natural selection in 1858–1859, and the modern synthesis combined selection with genetics.

Why is the sickle cell allele common where malaria occurs?

Heterozygotes are protected against malaria. From “Genetic variation” →

In plain English: Genetic variation is the raw material for evolution. Key idea: Genetic variation comes from mutation, meiosis, random fertilisation, gene flow and horizontal transfer; it is measured by allele frequencies and heterozygosity and maintained by heterozygote advantage, frequency-dependent selection and large population size.

How did drought affect Galápagos finch beaks in 1977?

Larger-beaked birds survived better, increasing average beak size. From “Selection pressures” →

In plain English: Selection pressures are environmental factors that affect survival and reproduction. Key idea: Selection pressures are abiotic or biotic factors affecting survival and reproduction, including human-made ones; strong or changing pressures drive evolution, as in Galápagos finch beaks during drought, and species can coevolve.

What is polyploidy?

Having more than two sets of chromosomes. From “Sympatric speciation” →

In plain English: Sympatric speciation occurs without geographic separation. Key idea: Sympatric speciation occurs without geographic separation, often through polyploidy in plants, where auto- or allopolyploidy creates instant reproductive isolation, or through ecological and behavioural divergence, as in apple maggot flies and cichlids.

What is a molecular clock?

A method using rates of sequence change to estimate when lineages diverged. From “Molecular evolution” →

In plain English: Molecular evolution studies how DNA and protein sequences change over time. Key idea: Sequence differences reflect time since divergence, conserved genes change slowly, neutral theory proposes most molecular change is neutral and spread by drift, and molecular clocks calibrated with fossils estimate divergence times, with dN/dS revealing selection.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Evolution and Natural Selection

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When was On the Origin of Species published? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1859. Darwin published his book in 1859, a year after the joint presentation with Wallace.

  2. Which is an example of a homologous structure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The pentadactyl limb of humans, bats and whales. Shared bone plans reflect common ancestry.

  3. Which type of selection explains the rise of antibiotic resistance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Directional selection. One extreme, resistance, is favoured, shifting the population.

  4. Why is Lamarck's mechanism of evolution not supported? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Characteristics acquired during life are not generally inherited. Changes from use or disuse are not passed on through DNA.

  5. How does biogeography support evolution? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Island species resemble those on the nearest mainland. Colonisation and divergence explain island species.

  6. What changes in a population as a result of natural selection? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Allele frequencies over generations. Selection alters which alleles become common.

  7. What did Darwin's theory lack that the modern synthesis supplied? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A mechanism of inheritance from genetics. Mendelian genetics explained how variation is inherited.

  8. Why is the consistency between different lines of evidence important? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Independent methods agreeing makes the conclusion very strong. Agreement across fossils, anatomy and DNA strengthens common descent.

  9. Why are adaptations often imperfect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Selection can only act on existing variation and trade-offs. Available variation and competing demands limit adaptation.

Key takeaways

  • Early naturalists saw species as fixed, geologists showed an ancient Earth, Lamarck proposed inheritance of acquired traits, Darwin and Wallace proposed natural selection in 1858–1859, and the modern synthesis combined selection with genetics.
  • Evidence for evolution includes the fossil record and transitional forms, homologous and vestigial structures, embryology, molecular similarities, biogeography and observed evolution, all consistent with common descent.
  • Natural selection needs variation, heritability, overproduction and differential survival and reproduction; it changes allele frequencies over generations and can be directional, stabilising or disruptive, acting on existing variation.
  • Adaptations are inherited features improving survival and reproduction in an environment, classed as anatomical, physiological or behavioural; they arise by natural selection, convergent evolution produces similar adaptations in unrelated species, and not every feature is adaptive.
  • Genetic variation comes from mutation, meiosis, random fertilisation, gene flow and horizontal transfer; it is measured by allele frequencies and heterozygosity and maintained by heterozygote advantage, frequency-dependent selection and large population size.
  • Mutation creates new alleles randomly with respect to need; most are neutral or harmful and a few beneficial, large populations and short generations speed adaptation, and gene duplication creates new functions, as shown by experiments such as Luria and Delbrück's.

Common mistakes in Evolution and Natural Selection

  • Describing Darwin's theory as "animals adapt because they need to". Selection acts on existing variation; individuals do not change their genes by need.
  • Saying there is no evidence because transitional fossils are missing. Many transitional fossils, such as Tiktaalik, have been found.
  • Saying individuals evolve during their lifetimes. Populations evolve as allele frequencies change over generations.
  • Assuming every feature is an adaptation. Some traits are by-products, inherited from ancestors or neutral.
  • Thinking meiosis creates new alleles. Meiosis shuffles existing alleles; only mutation creates new ones.
  • Saying the environment causes the mutations an organism needs. Mutations arise randomly; selection then favours useful ones.

Lessons in this school

  1. Origins of evolutionary thought
    Ideas about how species change developed over centuries before and after Darwin.
  2. Evidence for evolution
    Evolution is supported by many independent lines of evidence.
  3. Natural selection
    Natural selection is the main mechanism of adaptive evolution.
  4. Adaptation
    Adaptations are inherited features that improve survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  5. Genetic variation
    Genetic variation is the raw material for evolution.
  6. Mutation and evolution
    Mutations provide the new genetic variation on which evolution depends.
  7. Genetic drift
    Genetic drift is change in allele frequencies due to chance.
  8. Gene flow
    Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations.
  9. Sexual selection
    Sexual selection favours traits that improve mating success.
  10. Selection pressures
    Selection pressures are environmental factors that affect survival and reproduction.
  11. Fitness
    In evolutionary biology, fitness means reproductive success, not physical strength.
  12. Speciation
    Speciation is the formation of new species.
  13. Reproductive isolation
    Reproductive isolation keeps species separate.
  14. Allopatric speciation
    Allopatric speciation occurs when populations are geographically separated.
  15. Sympatric speciation
    Sympatric speciation occurs without geographic separation.
  16. Phylogenetics
    Phylogenetics reconstructs the evolutionary relationships between organisms.
  17. Evolutionary trees
    Evolutionary trees show relationships between groups of organisms.
  18. Common ancestry
    All living organisms share a common ancestor.
  19. Fossil evidence
    Fossils provide direct evidence of past life and evolutionary change.
  20. Molecular evolution
    Molecular evolution studies how DNA and protein sequences change over time.
  21. Human evolution
    Human evolution traces the hominin lineage from our common ancestor with chimpanzees to modern humans.
  22. Evolutionary medicine
    Evolutionary medicine applies evolutionary thinking to health and disease.
  23. Evolution of resistance
    Resistance to antibiotics, pesticides and other treatments evolves by natural selection.
  24. Population genetics
    Population genetics provides the mathematical framework for evolution.

Evolution and Natural Selection: questions students ask

What is a homologous structure?

A structure shared by different species because of common ancestry.

Which type of selection favours intermediate phenotypes?

Stabilising selection.

What is convergent evolution?

Evolution of similar features in unrelated species in similar environments.

What did the Luria-Delbrück experiment show?

That mutations arise randomly, not in response to the environment.
