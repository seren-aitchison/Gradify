Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which four elements make up about 96% of living mass?

Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. From “Chemistry of life” →

In plain English: Living things are built from a small set of elements arranged into large, complex molecules. Key idea: Living things are mainly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, with phosphorus, sulfur and ions; covalent bonds build molecules, weaker bonds shape them, and polymers are made by condensation and broken by hydrolysis.

Which monosaccharides make up sucrose?

Glucose and fructose. From “Disaccharides” →

In plain English: Disaccharides form when two monosaccharides join by a glycosidic bond. Key idea: Disaccharides form by condensation with a glycosidic bond and are hydrolysed by enzymes; maltose is glucose + glucose, sucrose glucose + fructose and lactose glucose + galactose, and sucrose is non-reducing, detected after acid hydrolysis.

How many fused rings does a steroid have?

Four. From “Steroids” →

In plain English: Steroids are lipids with a ring structure rather than fatty acid chains. Key idea: Steroids are lipids with four fused rings; cholesterol regulates membrane fluidity and is the precursor of steroid hormones, vitamin D and bile salts, and steroid hormones cross membranes to bind intracellular receptors that alter gene expression.

Which is the strongest bond in tertiary structure?

The disulfide bridge, a covalent bond. From “Protein tertiary structure” →

In plain English: Tertiary structure is the overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide. Key idea: Tertiary structure is the 3D shape of one polypeptide, stabilised by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions, creating active and binding sites, with hydrophobic cores in soluble proteins.

What does Km represent?

The substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax. From “Enzyme kinetics” →

In plain English: Enzyme kinetics describes how reaction rate depends on substrate concentration. Key idea: Reaction rate rises with substrate concentration until active sites are saturated at Vmax; Km is the substrate concentration giving half Vmax, a low Km means high affinity, and v = Vmax[S] ÷ (Km + [S]).

Quick quiz: test yourself on Biochemistry and Biomolecules

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which element can form four covalent bonds and so build the skeletons of biological molecules? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Carbon. Carbon's four bonds allow chains, rings and branched structures.

  2. Why is water described as polar? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Oxygen pulls electrons more strongly, giving partial charges. Uneven electron sharing gives δ− oxygen and δ+ hydrogens.

  3. Between which atoms do hydrogen bonds typically form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A δ+ hydrogen bonded to O or N and a lone pair on another O or N. Hydrogen bonds need hydrogen attached to an electronegative atom.

  4. What happens in a hydrolysis reaction? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A bond is broken by adding a water molecule. Hydrolysis uses water to split bonds, often catalysed by enzymes.

  5. Which property helps keep lake temperatures stable through the day? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High specific heat capacity. A high specific heat capacity means water temperature changes slowly.

  6. Why does DNA with more G–C pairs separate at a higher temperature? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds rather than two. More hydrogen bonds need more energy to break.

  7. Why are weak interactions such as hydrogen bonds important in large biological molecules? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Many of them together stabilise shape while still allowing changes needed for function. Collectively stable but individually breakable bonds allow both stability and flexibility.

  8. Why does ice floating benefit aquatic life in winter? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It forms an insulating layer so water below stays liquid. Ice on the surface insulates the liquid water where organisms live.

  9. Why is the weakness of individual hydrogen bonds an advantage in DNA? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Strands can be separated for replication and transcription. Weak bonds let enzymes open DNA when needed.

Key takeaways

  • Living things are mainly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, with phosphorus, sulfur and ions; covalent bonds build molecules, weaker bonds shape them, and polymers are made by condensation and broken by hydrolysis.
  • Water is polar and forms hydrogen bonds, making it an excellent solvent with high specific heat capacity, high latent heat of vaporisation, strong cohesion and less dense ice, all of which support life.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between δ+ hydrogen on O or N and lone pairs on other electronegative atoms; they are weak individually but stabilise water, protein secondary structure, DNA base pairing and cellulose, and break with heat.
  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], each unit is a tenfold change, blood is kept near pH 7.4, and buffers of weak acids and conjugate bases, such as carbonic acid–hydrogencarbonate, phosphate and proteins, resist pH changes.
  • Carbohydrates contain C, H and O, are classified as monosaccharides, disaccharides and polysaccharides, and provide energy, energy storage, structure and cell recognition, with structure matched to function.
  • Monosaccharides are classified as trioses, pentoses or hexoses and as aldoses or ketoses; glucose, fructose and galactose are isomers, α- and β-glucose differ at carbon 1, and monosaccharides are reducing sugars detected by Benedict's test.

Common mistakes in Biochemistry and Biomolecules

  • Thinking hydrolysis means "breaking with heat". Hydrolysis breaks bonds by adding water, usually catalysed by enzymes.
  • Saying water molecules are held together by hydrogen bonds within the molecule. Covalent bonds join O and H; hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
  • Calling a hydrogen bond a covalent bond to hydrogen. It is a weaker attraction between molecules or parts of molecules.
  • Thinking pH 4 is twice as acidic as pH 5. It has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration.
  • Thinking all carbohydrates are sweet and soluble. Polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose are neither.
  • Treating α- and β-glucose as unrelated molecules. They are isomers differing only in the position of the –OH on carbon 1.

Lessons in this school

  1. Chemistry of life
    Living things are built from a small set of elements arranged into large, complex molecules.
  2. Water and biological systems
    Water makes up most of the mass of cells, and its unusual properties make life possible.
  3. Hydrogen bonding
    Hydrogen bonds are weak attractions, yet they shape water, proteins and DNA.
  4. pH and biological buffers
    The pH of body fluids and cells must stay within narrow limits.
  5. Carbohydrates
    Carbohydrates provide energy, store energy and build structures.
  6. Monosaccharides
    Monosaccharides are the building blocks of all carbohydrates.
  7. Disaccharides
    Disaccharides form when two monosaccharides join by a glycosidic bond.
  8. Polysaccharides
    Polysaccharides are long chains of sugar units used for storage and structure.
  9. Lipids
    Lipids are a diverse group of molecules that are insoluble in water.
  10. Fatty acids
    Fatty acids are the main components of triglycerides and phospholipids.
  11. Triglycerides
    Triglycerides are the main form of stored fat in plants and animals.
  12. Phospholipids
    Phospholipids form the basic structure of all cell membranes.
  13. Steroids
    Steroids are lipids with a ring structure rather than fatty acid chains.
  14. Proteins
    Proteins carry out most of the work in cells, from catalysis to transport and structure.
  15. Amino acids
    Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  16. Peptide bonds
    Amino acids join through peptide bonds to form polypeptides.
  17. Protein primary structure
    The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  18. Protein secondary structure
    Secondary structure is the regular folding of the polypeptide backbone into helices and sheets.
  19. Protein tertiary structure
    Tertiary structure is the overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide.
  20. Protein quaternary structure
    Some proteins are made of more than one polypeptide chain.
  21. Protein folding
    Newly made polypeptides must fold into precise shapes to function.
  22. Protein denaturation
    Denaturation is the loss of a protein's 3D shape and function.
  23. Enzymes
    Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up reactions in living cells.
  24. Enzyme-substrate interactions
    Enzymes bind their substrates with great specificity.
  25. Enzyme kinetics
    Enzyme kinetics describes how reaction rate depends on substrate concentration.
  26. Enzyme inhibition
    Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity and are important in metabolic control and medicine.
  27. Factors affecting enzyme activity
    Enzyme activity depends on temperature, pH, substrate concentration and enzyme concentration.
  28. Nucleic acids
    Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information.
  29. DNA and RNA
    DNA and RNA are both nucleic acids but differ in structure and role.
  30. ATP and biological energy
    ATP is the main energy currency of cells.

Biochemistry and Biomolecules: questions students ask

Why is water a good solvent for ions?

Its polar molecules surround and separate charged particles.

Why does heating denature proteins?

It breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the folded shape.

What is a buffer made of?

A weak acid and its conjugate base.

How do ribose and deoxyribose differ?

Deoxyribose lacks an oxygen atom at carbon 2.
