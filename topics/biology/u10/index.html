Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Where do T cells mature?

In the thymus. From “Introduction to immunology” →

In plain English: Immunology is the study of how the body defends itself. Key idea: The immune system defends against pathogens, monitors for abnormal cells and maintains tolerance; immune cells are made in bone marrow, T cells mature in the thymus and responses begin in lymph nodes, spleen and other secondary organs.

What is chemotaxis?

Movement towards a chemical signal. From “Neutrophils” →

In plain English: Neutrophils are the most abundant white cells and the first to arrive at infections. Key idea: Neutrophils are abundant, short-lived phagocytes with multi-lobed nuclei that reach infections by chemotaxis, engulf pathogens into phagosomes, kill them with enzymes and reactive oxygen species and can release NETs; pus is mainly dead neutrophils.

What is the role of regulatory T cells?

To suppress immune responses and maintain tolerance. From “T lymphocytes” →

In plain English: T lymphocytes coordinate immune responses and kill infected cells. Key idea: T cells mature in the thymus through positive and negative selection; helper T cells coordinate responses, cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells, regulatory T cells maintain tolerance and memory T cells provide faster responses, all recognising peptides on MHC.

Where are most complement proteins made?

In the liver. From “Complement system” →

In plain English: The complement system is a group of blood proteins that help destroy pathogens. Key idea: Complement is a cascade of liver-made blood proteins activated by classical, lectin and alternative pathways that converge on C3, leading to opsonisation, inflammation and lysis by the membrane attack complex, with host cells protected by regulators.

What is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis?

Adrenaline. From “Allergy” →

In plain English: Allergies are type I hypersensitivity reactions to harmless substances. Key idea: Allergies are type I reactions in which sensitisation produces IgE on mast cells, and re-exposure causes degranulation and histamine release; anaphylaxis needs adrenaline, and early peanut introduction and immunotherapy can reduce allergy.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Immunology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where are all immune cells originally made? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    In the bone marrow. Haematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow give rise to immune cells.

  2. Which receptors do innate cells use to detect pathogens? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Toll-like receptors. Toll-like receptors are pattern recognition receptors.

  3. Which feature is unique to adaptive immunity? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Immunological memory. Memory cells allow faster responses on re-exposure.

  4. Why do lymph nodes swell during infection? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Lymphocytes in them multiply to fight it. Swelling reflects an active immune response in the node.

  5. Which molecule from Gram-negative bacteria is a PAMP? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Lipopolysaccharide. LPS in the outer membrane is detected by TLR4.

  6. How does the secondary response differ from the primary response? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is faster, stronger and longer-lasting. Memory cells produce a rapid, large response.

  7. What happens when immune tolerance fails? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The immune system attacks the body's own cells. Loss of tolerance causes autoimmune disease.

  8. How does innate immunity help start adaptive immunity? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Activated dendritic cells present antigens to T cells. Innate activation of dendritic cells links the two systems.

  9. Why are booster vaccine doses given? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    To strengthen and extend immune memory. Re-exposure increases antibody levels and memory.

Key takeaways

  • The immune system defends against pathogens, monitors for abnormal cells and maintains tolerance; immune cells are made in bone marrow, T cells mature in the thymus and responses begin in lymph nodes, spleen and other secondary organs.
  • Innate immunity is rapid, non-specific and present from birth; it recognises pathogens using pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors that detect PAMPs, triggering phagocytosis, cytokines and inflammation, and helps activate adaptive immunity.
  • Adaptive immunity is specific, diverse and has memory; humoral immunity uses antibodies from B cells and cell-mediated immunity uses T cells, lymphocytes are selected by clonal selection and secondary responses are faster and stronger.
  • Physical barriers such as skin, mucous membranes, mucus and cilia, chemical barriers such as lysozyme, stomach acid and antimicrobial peptides, and protective microbiota prevent most pathogens entering the body.
  • All immune cells come from haematopoietic stem cells; myeloid cells include neutrophils, macrophages and dendritic cells, lymphoid cells include B, T and NK cells, and blood counts help suggest the type of infection.
  • Neutrophils are abundant, short-lived phagocytes with multi-lobed nuclei that reach infections by chemotaxis, engulf pathogens into phagosomes, kill them with enzymes and reactive oxygen species and can release NETs; pus is mainly dead neutrophils.

Common mistakes in Immunology

  • Thinking the immune system only fights infections. It also removes abnormal cells and maintains tolerance to self.
  • Thinking innate immunity is unimportant because it is non-specific. It controls most infections and starts adaptive responses.
  • Thinking adaptive immunity acts immediately. On first exposure it takes days to develop.
  • Thinking barriers are passive walls only. They include active chemicals, flushing and competing microbes.
  • Thinking all white blood cells do the same job. Each type has specialised roles.
  • Thinking pus is a sign the body is failing. It shows neutrophils have been fighting infection.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to immunology
    Immunology is the study of how the body defends itself.
  2. Innate immunity
    Innate immunity provides the first, rapid line of defence.
  3. Adaptive immunity
    Adaptive immunity is specific and improves with exposure.
  4. Physical barriers
    Physical and chemical barriers stop most pathogens entering the body.
  5. Immune cells
    The immune system uses many types of white blood cell.
  6. Neutrophils
    Neutrophils are the most abundant white cells and the first to arrive at infections.
  7. Macrophages
    Macrophages are long-lived phagocytes in tissues.
  8. Dendritic cells
    Dendritic cells are the main link between innate and adaptive immunity.
  9. Natural killer cells
    Natural killer cells destroy infected and cancerous cells without prior sensitisation.
  10. B lymphocytes
    B lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  11. T lymphocytes
    T lymphocytes coordinate immune responses and kill infected cells.
  12. Antibodies
    Antibodies are proteins that bind specific antigens.
  13. Antigen recognition
    Lymphocytes recognise a vast range of antigens.
  14. Antigen presentation
    Antigen presentation displays fragments of proteins to T cells.
  15. MHC molecules
    MHC molecules display peptides to T cells and vary greatly between people.
  16. Cytokines
    Cytokines are signalling proteins that coordinate immune responses.
  17. Complement system
    The complement system is a group of blood proteins that help destroy pathogens.
  18. Inflammation
    Inflammation is the body's response to infection and injury.
  19. Immune memory
    Immune memory allows faster and stronger responses to pathogens met before.
  20. Vaccination
    Vaccination trains the immune system safely to prevent disease.
  21. Hypersensitivity
    Hypersensitivity reactions are harmful immune responses.
  22. Allergy
    Allergies are type I hypersensitivity reactions to harmless substances.
  23. Autoimmunity
    Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system attacks the body's own tissues.
  24. Immunodeficiency
    Immunodeficiency is a weakened immune system that increases infection risk.
  25. Transplant immunology
    Transplanted tissues can be rejected by the immune system.
  26. Cancer immunology
    The immune system can recognise and destroy cancer cells, but tumours can evade it.
  27. Immunotherapy
    Immunotherapy uses the immune system to treat disease, especially cancer.
