Key terms and formulas at a glance

Biology
The scientific study of living organisms and life processes
Metabolism
All the chemical reactions in an organism, including those that release energy and those that build molecules
Tissue
A group of similar cells that work together to carry out a function
Dormancy
A state of very low metabolic activity from which an organism can resume normal life
Taxonomy
The science of naming, describing and classifying organisms
Domain
The highest rank of classification: Bacteria, Archaea or Eukarya
Heterotroph
An organism that obtains organic molecules by feeding on other organisms or their products
Hypothesis
A testable, falsifiable explanation for an observation
Null hypothesis
The hypothesis that there is no effect or no difference; it is what a statistical test assesses
Independent variable
The variable deliberately changed by the experimenter
Negative control
A treatment expected to produce no effect, used to show that an effect depends on the factor tested
Accuracy
How close a measured value is to the true value

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the five unifying ideas of biology.

Cells, genes (inheritance), evolution, energy flow and homeostasis. From “What is biology?” →

In plain English: Biology is the scientific study of living things: what they are made of, how they work, how they develop, how they interact and how they came to be. Key idea: Biology explains living things at every scale, held together by cells, genes, evolution, energy flow and homeostasis.

List the taxonomic ranks from broadest to narrowest.

Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. From “Classification and taxonomy” →

In plain English: With an estimated several million species alive today, biologists need a system to name and organise them. Key idea: Classification uses nested ranks and binomial names, and aims to group organisms by common ancestry.

What is a null hypothesis?

A statement that there is no difference or no relationship between the variables; statistical tests assess whether the data allow it to be rejected. From “Hypotheses and predictions” →

In plain English: A good investigation starts with a clear hypothesis and a precise prediction. Key idea: A hypothesis explains, a prediction states the expected result of a specific test, and the null hypothesis is what a statistical test tries to reject.

Give one reason a correlation between two variables might not mean that one causes the other.

A third (confounding) variable might cause both, or the association might arise by chance. From “Reliability and validity” →

In plain English: A result is reliable if it can be obtained consistently, and valid if it actually measures what it claims to and answers the question asked. Key idea: Reliable means consistent; valid means it measures and tests what it claims; correlation needs extra evidence before it can show cause.

Where should units appear in a results table?

In the column headings, after the quantity (for example "Time / s"), not in the body of the table. From “Graphs and data presentation” →

In plain English: Choosing the right table or graph makes patterns in data clear and honest. Key idea: Match the graph to the data: line graphs for continuous data, bar charts for categories, histograms for frequency distributions, scatter graphs for correlations.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Biology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement is part of cell theory? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cells only arise from existing cells. Cell theory holds that all organisms are made of cells and that new cells come only from the division of existing cells; prokaryotes have no nucleus.

  2. Which is a characteristic of all living organisms? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They respond to stimuli. All organisms detect and respond to changes; many don't move, only some photosynthesise, and many reproduce asexually.

  3. Which is an organ? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The stomach. The stomach contains several tissues (muscle, epithelium, glands) working together; a muscle cell is a cell and a mitochondrion an organelle.

  4. A biologist studies how the population of a bird species changes over 30 years as its habitat warms. Which level of biology is this mainly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Population ecology and evolution. Changes in the size and inherited traits of a population over generations are studied in ecology and evolutionary biology.

  5. A new particle found in a hot spring contains RNA and makes copies of itself only inside bacterial cells. How should it be classified? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Like a virus: biological but not a living organism by the usual criteria. It has genetic material but no cells and can only replicate inside a host, the defining features of a virus.

  6. All the trout, algae, insects and plants in a lake, but not the water or rocks, make up a… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    community. A community is all the living populations in an area; adding the non-living environment would make an ecosystem.

  7. Which example best shows how biology's levels connect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A gene mutation changes an enzyme, altering a cell's metabolism and the organism's survival, and the allele's frequency changes over generations. Only the first traces one cause from the molecular level through cells and organisms to a change in a population.

  8. Which pair of observations would most strongly suggest that a sample from Mars was alive? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It grows in nutrient medium and passes heritable variation to its offspring. Growth with reproduction and inheritance shows metabolism and genetic continuity; carbon compounds, water and shape changes also occur in non-living matter.

  9. Why can't the behaviour of an ecosystem be predicted just by studying each species separately? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Interactions between species and with the environment create emergent properties such as nutrient cycling and food-web stability. Feeding relationships, competition and cycling of matter arise from interactions between parts, which studying parts in isolation misses.

Key takeaways

  • Biology explains living things at every scale, held together by cells, genes, evolution, energy flow and homeostasis.
  • Life is defined by a set of shared characteristics, not by any one of them; viruses have some but not others.
  • Each level is built from the one below and has emergent properties of its own.
  • Use several characteristics together, and remember that an organism can be alive without showing all of them at every moment.
  • Classification uses nested ranks and binomial names, and aims to group organisms by common ancestry.
  • Life has three domains: Bacteria, Archaea (both prokaryotic) and Eukarya, defined largely by ribosomal RNA sequences.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Biology

  • Thinking biology is just memorising names. The names matter less than the mechanisms: how and why living things work as they do.
  • Saying plants don't respire because they photosynthesise. Plant cells respire all the time; in light, photosynthesis also runs, usually faster.
  • Confusing a tissue with an organ. A tissue is one type of cell working together; an organ contains several tissues.
  • Concluding that a dormant seed is dead because it shows no growth. It is alive; its metabolism is very slow until conditions allow germination.
  • Writing "homo Sapiens" or "Homo Sapiens". The genus is capitalised and the species epithet is not: Homo sapiens.
  • Thinking archaea are a kind of bacteria. They are prokaryotes, but they are as different from bacteria as both are from eukaryotes.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is biology?
    Biology is the scientific study of living things: what they are made of, how they work, how they develop, how they interact and how they came to be.
  2. Characteristics of living organisms
    Living things share a set of properties that, taken together, distinguish them from non-living matter.
  3. Levels of biological organisation
    Living things are organised in a hierarchy, from atoms and molecules through cells, tissues, organs and organisms to populations, communities, ecosystems and the biosphere.
  4. The characteristics of life
    How do we know whether a newly discovered organism, a dormant seed or a frozen bacterium is alive?
  5. Classification and taxonomy
    With an estimated several million species alive today, biologists need a system to name and organise them.
  6. The three domains of life
    All living organisms fall into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
  7. The kingdoms of life
    Within the domain Eukarya, organisms are traditionally divided into kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi and protists.
  8. Scientific method
    Science is a way of finding out about the natural world by testing ideas against evidence.
  9. Hypotheses and predictions
    A good investigation starts with a clear hypothesis and a precise prediction.
  10. Variables and experimental design
    A fair experiment changes one factor, measures its effect on another and keeps everything else the same.
  11. Controls and reproducibility
    Controls show that a result is caused by the factor being tested and not by something else.
  12. Accuracy and precision
    Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.
  13. Reliability and validity
    A result is reliable if it can be obtained consistently, and valid if it actually measures what it claims to and answers the question asked.
  14. Biological measurements
    Biologists measure things across an enormous range of scales, from nanometre-sized molecules to whole ecosystems.
  15. Units and conversions
    Science uses the International System of Units (SI) so that measurements can be compared worldwide.
  16. Scientific notation
    Biology deals with numbers from the 10⁻¹⁰ m of an atom to the 10¹³ cells of a human body.
  17. Graphs and data presentation
    Choosing the right table or graph makes patterns in data clear and honest.
  18. Statistical foundations
    Living things vary, so biologists need statistics to summarise data and decide whether differences are real or due to chance.
  19. Sampling methods
    Biologists can rarely measure every individual, so they study samples.
  20. Ethical considerations in biological research
    Biological research can involve animals, human volunteers, human tissue and data, and organisms that could affect the environment.

Foundations of Biology: questions students ask

Why does biology depend on chemistry and physics?

Living things are made of matter and obey physical and chemical laws: enzymes are catalysts, nerve impulses are electrical, transport follows diffusion and fluid flow.

Why is a crystal not alive even though it grows?

It has no cells, no genetic material and no metabolism; it grows by adding matter to its surface, not by controlled chemical reactions.

What is the difference between a population and a community?

A population is one species in an area; a community is all the populations of different species in that area.

Why is a prion not considered alive?

It is a misfolded protein with no genetic material, no cells and no metabolism of its own.
