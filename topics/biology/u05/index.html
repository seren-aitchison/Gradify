Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

In which phase is DNA replicated?

S phase. From “Cell cycle” →

In plain English: The cell cycle is the sequence of events by which a cell grows and divides. Key idea: The cell cycle has interphase (G₁, S and G₂) and M phase (mitosis and cytokinesis), with G₀ as a non-dividing state; DNA doubles in S phase and halves in mitosis, producing identical cells for growth, replacement and repair.

Which division includes crossing over?

Meiosis. From “Comparison of mitosis and meiosis” →

In plain English: Mitosis and meiosis are both forms of nuclear division but have different outcomes and roles. Key idea: Mitosis involves one division without homologous pairing or crossing over and produces two identical diploid cells for growth and repair, while meiosis involves two divisions with pairing and crossing over and produces four varied haploid cells for sexual reproduction.

Why does necrosis cause inflammation?

Cell contents leak out after the membrane ruptures. From “Necrosis” →

In plain English: Necrosis is uncontrolled cell death caused by injury or damage. Key idea: Necrosis is uncontrolled cell death from severe damage, with swelling, rupture and leakage causing inflammation, unlike controlled apoptosis; it occurs in heart attacks and gangrene, and released proteins such as troponin aid diagnosis.

What are induced pluripotent stem cells?

Adult cells reprogrammed to become pluripotent. From “Stem-cell biology” →

In plain English: Stem cells can divide to renew themselves and differentiate into specialised cells. Key idea: Stem cells self-renew and differentiate; they range from totipotent and pluripotent to multipotent and unipotent, are used in bone marrow transplants and research, and iPSCs reprogrammed from adult cells avoid embryo use, while embryonic research is regulated.

What does the neural tube become?

The brain and spinal cord. From “Organogenesis” →

In plain English: Organogenesis is the formation of organs from the germ layers. Key idea: In neurulation, the notochord induces ectoderm to form the neural plate, which folds into the neural tube, while neural crest cells migrate; organs form through induction between tissues, mainly in weeks 3 to 8, when teratogens and folic acid deficiency can cause defects.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cell Division and Development

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which phase includes mitosis and cytokinesis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    M phase. M phase is when the nucleus and cytoplasm divide.

  2. Which event takes place in S phase? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    DNA replication. S stands for synthesis: DNA is replicated in this phase of interphase.

  3. In which stage do chromosomes line up at the equator? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Metaphase. Chromosomes align at the spindle equator in metaphase.

  4. What happens to DNA content during S phase? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It doubles from 2C to 4C. DNA replication produces two sister chromatids per chromosome.

  5. Why does a cell grow during G₁ and G₂? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    So each daughter cell receives enough organelles and cytoplasm. Growth provides materials for two functional cells.

  6. What attaches spindle fibres to chromosomes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Kinetochores at the centromeres. Spindle microtubules bind kinetochore proteins.

  7. Why is interphase described as an active phase rather than a resting phase? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Cells grow, make proteins and replicate DNA during it. Interphase involves growth and preparation for division.

  8. Which part of interphase varies most in length between cell types? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    G₁. G₁ length largely determines how often cells divide.

  9. Why do plant cells form a cell plate rather than a cleavage furrow? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their rigid cell wall prevents the membrane pinching in. A new wall must be built from the middle outward.

Key takeaways

  • The cell cycle has interphase (G₁, S and G₂) and M phase (mitosis and cytokinesis), with G₀ as a non-dividing state; DNA doubles in S phase and halves in mitosis, producing identical cells for growth, replacement and repair.
  • In G₁ the cell grows and makes proteins and organelles, in S phase it replicates DNA and makes histones, and in G₂ it grows further, makes division proteins, duplicates centrosomes and checks DNA, preparing for mitosis.
  • Mitosis proceeds through prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase as spindle fibres attached at centromeres separate sister chromatids; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm by a cleavage furrow in animals and a cell plate in plants, producing identical cells.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous pairs after pairing and crossing over, and meiosis II separates sister chromatids, producing four genetically different haploid cells; halving the chromosome number lets fertilisation restore diploidy.
  • Mitosis involves one division without homologous pairing or crossing over and produces two identical diploid cells for growth and repair, while meiosis involves two divisions with pairing and crossing over and produces four varied haploid cells for sexual reproduction.
  • Cohesin holds sister chromatids until separase cuts it at anaphase, kinetochores attach to opposite poles, the spindle assembly checkpoint delays anaphase until attachments are correct, and errors cause aneuploidy, linked to maternal age and cancer.

Common mistakes in Cell Division and Development

  • Thinking interphase is a resting phase. Cells are very active in interphase, growing and copying DNA.
  • Saying chromosomes are visible as condensed structures during interphase. They are uncondensed chromatin until mitosis.
  • Saying chromosomes pair up in mitosis. Homologous pairing happens in meiosis, not mitosis.
  • Saying sister chromatids separate in meiosis I. Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I; chromatids separate in meiosis II.
  • Saying meiosis produces two cells. It produces four.
  • Assuming chromatids separate as soon as they line up. Anaphase waits for the checkpoint to confirm all attachments.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cell cycle
    The cell cycle is the sequence of events by which a cell grows and divides.
  2. Interphase
    Interphase is the longest part of the cell cycle and prepares the cell for division.
  3. Mitosis
    Mitosis divides the nucleus to produce two genetically identical nuclei.
  4. Meiosis
    Meiosis is the division that produces gametes with half the chromosome number.
  5. Comparison of mitosis and meiosis
    Mitosis and meiosis are both forms of nuclear division but have different outcomes and roles.
  6. Chromosome segregation
    Accurate chromosome segregation ensures each daughter cell receives the correct chromosomes.
  7. Cell-cycle regulation
    The cell cycle is controlled by proteins that drive cells from one phase to the next.
  8. Checkpoints
    Checkpoints are control points where the cell checks whether conditions are right to continue the cycle.
  9. Apoptosis
    Apoptosis is programmed cell death, a controlled process that removes unwanted cells.
  10. Necrosis
    Necrosis is uncontrolled cell death caused by injury or damage.
  11. Cellular senescence
    Senescent cells stop dividing permanently but remain alive.
  12. Cancer biology
    Cancer is uncontrolled cell division caused by changes in genes that regulate the cell cycle.
  13. Tumour development
    Tumours develop in stages from a single altered cell to an invasive cancer.
  14. Stem-cell biology
    Stem cells can divide to renew themselves and differentiate into specialised cells.
  15. Embryonic development
    Embryonic development turns a single cell into a complex organism.
  16. Fertilisation
    Fertilisation is the fusion of a sperm and egg to form a zygote.
  17. Cleavage
    Cleavage is the series of rapid cell divisions that follows fertilisation.
  18. Gastrulation
    Gastrulation reorganises the embryo into germ layers, setting up the body plan.
  19. Organogenesis
    Organogenesis is the formation of organs from the germ layers.
  20. Developmental signalling
    Cells in an embryo communicate using signalling molecules that control their fates.
  21. Cell fate determination
    During development, cells become committed to particular fates.
  22. Tissue formation
    Tissues form as cells of similar types organise and adhere together.
  23. Regeneration
    Some animals can regrow lost body parts, while others, including humans, have limited regeneration.

Cell Division and Development: questions students ask

What is G₀?

A non-dividing state that some cells enter outside the cell cycle.

Why are chromosomes uncondensed during interphase?

So that genes can be transcribed.

How does cytokinesis differ in plant cells?

A cell plate forms between the nuclei, becoming a new wall.

What separates in anaphase I?

Homologous chromosomes.
