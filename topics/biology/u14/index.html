Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a control variable?

A factor kept constant so it does not affect the result. From “Experimental design” →

In plain English: Good experimental design produces reliable answers to biological questions. Key idea: A good experiment starts with a testable question, changes one independent variable, measures a dependent variable, controls others and uses replication, randomisation, blinding and pilot studies, with power analysis to choose sample size.

Which stain shows starch?

Iodine. From “Microscopy” →

In plain English: Microscopy is a core laboratory skill in biology. Key idea: Temporary slides use thin specimens, stains and careful coverslip placement; microscopes are focused from low power with coarse then fine focus, and graticules are calibrated with a stage micrometer to measure cells.

What does smearing on a gel suggest?

Degraded or overloaded DNA. From “Gel electrophoresis” →

In plain English: This lesson focuses on the practical skills of running and analysing agarose gels. Key idea: Agarose gels are poured with wells at the negative end, loaded with samples in loading dye, run and stained; sizes are estimated from a calibration curve of log size against distance, and smears or missing bands indicate problems.

What does a FASTQ file contain?

Sequence reads with quality scores. From “Sequencing” →

In plain English: This lesson focuses on planning a sequencing experiment in the laboratory, from sample to data. Key idea: Sequencing approaches are chosen by question: Sanger for single regions, short reads for many genes or genomes, long reads for structure and amplicon or metagenomic methods for communities; libraries with barcodes produce FASTQ data checked with Phred scores.

What is peer review?

Evaluation of a manuscript by experts before publication. From “Scientific communication” →

In plain English: Scientists share findings through papers, posters, talks and public engagement. Key idea: Scientific papers follow abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion and references; peer review evaluates work before publication, preprints share it early, and communication is adapted for specialist and public audiences.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Experimental Biology and Research Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In the seedling experiment, what is the dependent variable? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Seedling height. Height is what is measured in response to the treatment.

  2. Which is a falsifiable hypothesis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Woodlice prefer damp conditions to dry ones. It makes a claim that could be shown wrong.

  3. In PCR, what is a no-template control? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A negative control checking for contamination. Amplification without template reveals contaminating DNA.

  4. Why are seedlings randomly assigned to trays? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To prevent hidden differences biasing one treatment. Randomisation spreads unknown factors evenly between groups.

  5. What does rejecting the null hypothesis mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The data are unlikely if there were no effect. Rejection gives support for an effect but not proof.

  6. What does it mean if a positive control fails? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The method did not work, so results are unreliable. A failed positive control means negatives cannot be trusted.

  7. Why can't statistics rescue a poorly designed experiment? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Bias and confounding built into the data cannot be removed later. Design flaws distort the data themselves.

  8. Why must hypotheses be falsifiable? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Only claims that could be shown wrong can be tested. Testability depends on possible contrary evidence.

  9. Why is a vehicle control needed when testing a drug dissolved in DMSO? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    DMSO itself might affect the cells. Separating solvent effects from drug effects needs this control.

Key takeaways

  • A good experiment starts with a testable question, changes one independent variable, measures a dependent variable, controls others and uses replication, randomisation, blinding and pilot studies, with power analysis to choose sample size.
  • Hypotheses are testable explanations arising from observations, predictions state expected results, null hypotheses propose no effect and alternative hypotheses an effect, and evidence refines hypotheses without proving them.
  • Negative controls show no effect without the treatment, positive controls show the method works, vehicle controls test the solvent and sham controls test the procedure, all run alongside treatments to make results interpretable.
  • Random, systematic and stratified sampling suit different situations, while opportunistic sampling risks bias; representative, independent and sufficiently large samples give precise estimates with smaller standard errors.
  • Temporary slides use thin specimens, stains and careful coverslip placement; microscopes are focused from low power with coarse then fine focus, and graticules are calibrated with a stage micrometer to measure cells.
  • Histology prepares tissues by fixation, embedding, sectioning and staining, with H&E as the standard stain and special stains and immunohistochemistry for specific components, supporting diagnosis such as cancer grading.

Common mistakes in Experimental Biology and Research Methods

  • Thinking more measurements of the same sample count as replicates. Independent biological samples are needed.
  • Saying an experiment "proved" a hypothesis. Evidence supports or fails to support it; it does not prove it.
  • Thinking a control just means "the normal condition". Different controls answer different questions.
  • Thinking a large sample is always unbiased. A large biased sample is still biased.
  • Using the same graticule value at every magnification. It must be recalibrated for each objective.
  • Thinking stained colours show natural tissue colours. Stains add colour to reveal structures.

Experimental Biology and Research Methods: questions students ask

What is the difference between a hypothesis and a prediction?

A hypothesis is an explanation; a prediction states what should happen if it is true.

What is a positive control?

A condition expected to give a known effect, showing the method works.

What is stratified sampling?

Sampling from defined subgroups in proportion to their size.

Why is a coverslip lowered at an angle?

To avoid trapping air bubbles.
