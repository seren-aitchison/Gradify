Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a gene?

A sequence of DNA that codes for a functional product, usually a polypeptide. From “Introduction to genetics” →

In plain English: Genetics is the study of genes, heredity and variation. Key idea: Genetics studies heredity and variation; genes are DNA sequences coding for products, the central dogma runs from DNA to RNA to protein, and milestones include Mendel, Avery, the double helix and the Human Genome Project.

What is an anticodon?

A three-base sequence on tRNA complementary to an mRNA codon. From “RNA structure” →

In plain English: RNA molecules fold into shapes that suit their roles in gene expression. Key idea: mRNA carries codons with a cap and tail in eukaryotes, tRNA folds into a cloverleaf with an anticodon and amino acid site, rRNA forms and catalyses in ribosomes, and other RNAs such as microRNAs regulate genes.

Which protein blocks the lac operon when lactose is absent?

The lac repressor. From “Operons” →

In plain English: In bacteria, genes for related functions are often grouped in operons and controlled together. Key idea: An operon is a group of genes under one promoter; in the lac operon, the repressor blocks the operator without lactose, allolactose releases it when lactose is present, and CAP-cAMP boosts transcription only when glucose is low.

What is a phenotype?

The observable characteristics of an organism. From “Genotype and phenotype” →

In plain English: Genotype describes the alleles an organism has, while phenotype describes its characteristics. Key idea: Genotype is the set of alleles and phenotype the observable characteristics, determined by genotype, environment and their interaction; discontinuous variation has distinct categories from few genes, while continuous variation shows a range from many genes and environment.

What does 2pq represent in the Hardy-Weinberg equation?

The frequency of heterozygotes. From “Population genetics” →

In plain English: Population genetics studies how allele frequencies vary and change in populations. Key idea: A gene pool contains all alleles in a population; p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1 under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, which requires a large, randomly mating population without mutation, migration or selection, and the equation estimates carrier frequencies.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Genetics and Molecular Biology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does the central dogma describe? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein. DNA is transcribed into RNA, which is translated into protein.

  2. Which evidence showed that amounts of A equal T and G equal C? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Chargaff's rules. Chargaff measured base ratios in DNA from many species.

  3. Which enzyme unwinds the DNA double helix? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Helicase. Helicase breaks hydrogen bonds between bases to separate strands.

  4. Which experiment showed that DNA is the genetic material? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Avery, MacLeod and McCarty's transformation experiments. They showed DNA could transform bacteria, carrying genetic information.

  5. Why can proteins read DNA without separating the strands? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Grooves in the helix expose edges of the bases. Major and minor grooves allow sequence recognition.

  6. Why is the lagging strand made in fragments? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to a 3′ end. Antiparallel strands mean one strand must be built discontinuously.

  7. Why can two people with the same gene variant show different characteristics? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Environment also affects how genes are expressed. Many traits depend on gene-environment interaction.

  8. Why are the bases inside the double helix an advantage? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are protected from chemical damage. The backbone shields the information-carrying bases.

  9. In Meselson and Stahl's experiment, what result after one generation ruled out conservative replication? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    All DNA had intermediate density. Conservative replication would have given separate heavy and light bands.

Key takeaways

  • Genetics studies heredity and variation; genes are DNA sequences coding for products, the central dogma runs from DNA to RNA to protein, and milestones include Mendel, Avery, the double helix and the Human Genome Project.
  • X-ray diffraction and Chargaff's rules supported the double helix; DNA stores information in its base sequence, is stabilised by covalent backbones and hydrogen bonds, and can be copied accurately because each strand is a template for complementary pairing.
  • DNA replication is semi-conservative, as Meselson and Stahl showed; helicase unwinds DNA, primase adds primers, DNA polymerase adds nucleotides 5′ to 3′ with proofreading, the lagging strand forms Okazaki fragments and ligase joins them.
  • DNA is protected by proofreading, mismatch repair, base and nucleotide excision repair and double-strand break repair by homologous recombination or end joining; defects, as in xeroderma pigmentosum and BRCA mutations, increase mutation and cancer risk.
  • Eukaryotic DNA wraps around histones into nucleosomes and chromatin, condensing into chromosomes with sister chromatids and centromeres; prokaryotes have one circular chromosome, and humans have 46 chromosomes, shown in a karyotype.
  • Genes are DNA sequences at specific loci, alleles are different versions of a gene arising by mutation, diploid organisms carry two alleles per gene, homozygous genotypes have identical alleles and heterozygous ones different alleles, and some genes have multiple alleles.

Common mistakes in Genetics and Molecular Biology

  • Thinking all variation is genetic. Many characteristics are affected by both genes and environment.
  • Overlooking Rosalind Franklin's role. Her X-ray data were essential evidence for the double helix.
  • Saying DNA polymerase unwinds DNA. Helicase unwinds the helix; DNA polymerase builds new strands.
  • Assuming all DNA damage becomes a mutation. Most damage is repaired before it can be passed on.
  • Confusing a chromosome with a chromatid. A replicated chromosome has two sister chromatids.
  • Using "gene" and "allele" interchangeably. A gene is the unit; alleles are its versions.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to genetics
    Genetics is the study of genes, heredity and variation.
  2. DNA structure
    The structure of DNA explains how it stores and copies genetic information.
  3. DNA replication
    Before a cell divides, its DNA must be copied accurately.
  4. DNA repair
    DNA is constantly damaged and must be repaired to keep genetic information intact.
  5. Chromosomes
    Chromosomes package DNA so that it fits in cells and can be divided accurately.
  6. Genes and alleles
    Genes come in different versions called alleles.
  7. Genomes
    A genome is all the genetic material of an organism.
  8. RNA structure
    RNA molecules fold into shapes that suit their roles in gene expression.
  9. Transcription
    Transcription copies the information in a gene into RNA.
  10. RNA processing
    In eukaryotes, pre-mRNA must be processed before it can be translated.
  11. Translation
    Translation converts the code in mRNA into a sequence of amino acids.
  12. Genetic code
    The genetic code is the set of rules linking codons to amino acids.
  13. Protein synthesis
    Protein synthesis brings together transcription, processing and translation.
  14. Gene regulation
    Cells control which genes are expressed, when and how much.
  15. Operons
    In bacteria, genes for related functions are often grouped in operons and controlled together.
  16. Epigenetics
    Epigenetic changes alter gene expression without changing the DNA sequence.
  17. Mutations
    Mutations are changes in DNA sequence.
  18. Types of mutation
    Gene mutations take several forms, with very different consequences.
  19. DNA damage
    DNA is damaged thousands of times a day in every cell.
  20. Mendelian genetics
    Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants revealed the basic rules of inheritance.
  21. Dominant and recessive inheritance
    Some alleles mask others in heterozygotes.
  22. Genotype and phenotype
    Genotype describes the alleles an organism has, while phenotype describes its characteristics.
  23. Monohybrid crosses
    A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of one gene.
  24. Dihybrid crosses
    A dihybrid cross follows two genes at once.
  25. Incomplete dominance
    In incomplete dominance, heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype.
  26. Codominance
    In codominance, both alleles in a heterozygote are fully expressed.
  27. Sex-linked inheritance
    Genes on the sex chromosomes are inherited differently in males and females.
  28. Pedigree analysis
    Pedigrees show how a trait is passed through a family.
  29. Population genetics
    Population genetics studies how allele frequencies vary and change in populations.
  30. Genetic linkage
    Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together.
  31. Recombination
    Recombination creates new combinations of alleles.
  32. Chromosomal abnormalities
    Changes in chromosome number or structure can have major effects on development.
  33. Genomics
    Genomics studies whole genomes, using sequencing and computing.
  34. Human genetic variation
    Humans differ from one another at millions of positions in the genome.
  35. Genome-wide association studies
    Genome-wide association studies scan the genome for variants linked to traits and diseases.
