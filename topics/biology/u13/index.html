Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is bioinformatics?

Using computing and statistics to analyse biological data. From “Introduction to bioinformatics” →

In plain English: Bioinformatics uses computing to store, analyse and interpret biological data. Key idea: Bioinformatics combines biology, computing and statistics to manage and analyse large biological datasets, supporting genomics, proteomics, structural and systems biology.

What is an E-value?

The number of hits expected by chance with a given score. From “BLAST” →

In plain English: BLAST searches databases for sequences similar to a query. Key idea: BLAST quickly finds similar sequences using word seeds extended into local alignments; programs include blastn, blastp and blastx, and hits are judged by E-value, percentage identity, coverage and bit score.

Why is ribosomal RNA removed before RNA-seq?

It makes up most RNA and would waste sequencing reads. From “RNA sequencing” →

In plain English: RNA sequencing reads the RNA in a sample to measure expression and discover transcripts. Key idea: RNA-seq extracts RNA, enriches mRNA or removes rRNA, makes cDNA and sequences it; reads are quality checked, aligned, counted and tested statistically, revealing expression, splicing, fusions and new transcripts.

What is homology modelling?

Building a structural model using a related protein of known structure. From “Protein structure prediction” →

In plain English: Computers can now predict protein structures from sequence. Key idea: Protein structures are predicted by homology modelling, ab initio methods and deep learning; AlphaFold predicts many structures with high accuracy and confidence scores, but disordered regions, complexes and mutations remain challenging.

What is an indel?

A small insertion or deletion. From “Variant analysis” →

In plain English: Variant analysis identifies and interprets differences in a genome compared with a reference. Key idea: Variant analysis aligns reads, calls and filters variants into VCF files, distinguishes SNVs, indels and structural variants and classifies them from pathogenic to benign using population frequency, predictions and family evidence.

  1. Which fields does bioinformatics combine? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

  2. Which database stores protein sequences and functions? (Foundation recall)

  3. Which character begins the header line in a FASTA file? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

  4. Why is computing essential for genomics? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Datasets are far too large to analyse by hand. Billions of bases need automated analysis.

  5. How do secondary databases differ from primary ones? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They hold curated or derived information. Curated databases add annotation and checks.

  6. Why is high-GC DNA more stable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    G-C pairs form three hydrogen bonds. More hydrogen bonds hold strands together more strongly.

  7. Why do bioinformaticians need biological knowledge? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    To ask meaningful questions and interpret results. Analysis is only useful when interpreted biologically.

  8. Why did open sharing help during the COVID-19 pandemic? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Scientists worldwide could design tests and vaccines quickly. Rapid access to sequences sped up responses.

  9. Why is gene finding harder in eukaryotes than bacteria? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Introns interrupt coding sequences. Split genes do not form single long ORFs.

Key takeaways

  • Bioinformatics combines biology, computing and statistics to manage and analyse large biological datasets, supporting genomics, proteomics, structural and systems biology.
  • Biological databases store primary data such as sequences in GenBank, ENA and DDBJ and curated information in databases such as UniProt and PDB; entries have accession numbers and links, and open sharing speeds research.
  • DNA sequences are stored in FASTA format and analysed by reverse complementing, translating in six frames to find ORFs and calculating GC content, which affects stability and varies between genomes.
  • Protein sequences use one-letter codes from N- to C-terminus; domains and motifs, classified in Pfam and InterPro, indicate function, and properties such as mass, charge and transmembrane regions can be predicted.
  • Alignment compares sequences globally or locally, scores matches, mismatches and gaps with matrices such as BLOSUM62 using dynamic programming, and measures similarity, which can suggest homology.
  • BLAST quickly finds similar sequences using word seeds extended into local alignments; programs include blastn, blastp and blastx, and hits are judged by E-value, percentage identity, coverage and bit score.

Common mistakes in Bioinformatics and Computational Biology

  • Thinking bioinformatics is only about programming. It needs biological knowledge to ask and interpret the right questions.
  • Thinking database entries are always error-free. Primary data can contain errors, so curated databases add checks.
  • Thinking every ORF is a real gene. Many short ORFs occur by chance and need further evidence.
  • Thinking two proteins with similar functions always have similar sequences. Unrelated proteins can perform similar functions.
  • Saying two sequences are "70% homologous". Homology is all-or-nothing; similarity or identity is given as a percentage.
  • Thinking a large E-value means a strong match. Smaller E-values indicate more significant matches.
