Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the no-miracles argument?

Science's predictive success would be miraculous unless theories were approximately true. From “Scientific realism and instrumentalism” →

In plain English: Scientific realism holds that our best theories describe a real world, including unobservable things like electrons and quarks. Key idea: Realists take theories as true descriptions; instrumentalists see them as predictive tools; structural realism finds a middle way.

What is Bertrand's paradox?

Different reasonable ways of choosing a "random chord" give different probabilities, showing the need to specify sampling. From “Probability and chance” →

In plain English: Probability is everywhere in physics, from statistical mechanics to quantum measurement. Key idea: Probability can mean frequencies, propensities or degrees of belief; physics uses all, and the quantum case raises deep questions.

What is the hole argument?

Einstein's argument that diffeomorphism freedom seemed to make GR indeterministic, resolved by denying spacetime points independent identity. From “The nature of spacetime” →

In plain English: General relativity treats spacetime as a dynamical, curved entity that carries energy and waves. Key idea: In GR spacetime is dynamical and coordinate-free; quantum gravity hints it may emerge from deeper structures.

What is quantum equilibrium?

Particle positions distributed according to |ψ|2, reproducing quantum predictions. From “Pilot-wave theory” →

In plain English: Pilot-wave theory, developed by de Broglie (1927) and revived by David Bohm (1952), says particles always have definite positions, guided by the wavefunction. Key idea: Pilot-wave theory gives definite particle trajectories guided by the wavefunction, restoring determinism with nonlocality.

What did the electroweak theory unify?

The electromagnetic and weak forces. From “Scientific explanation and unification” →

In plain English: Unification, explaining diverse phenomena with fewer principles, has driven physics from Newton's gravity to Maxwell's electromagnetism and the electroweak theory. Key idea: Unification explains by reducing many phenomena to few principles and often predicts new effects, but complete unification remains unproven.

Key takeaways

  • Realists take theories as true descriptions; instrumentalists see them as predictive tools; structural realism finds a middle way.
  • Explaining and predicting are distinct goals; good physics often achieves both, but not always.
  • Laws support counterfactuals and are universal; philosophers debate whether they merely describe patterns or govern events.
  • Determinism means the state fixes the future; relativity limits causal influence to light cones; quantum mechanics complicates both.
  • Probability can mean frequencies, propensities or degrees of belief; physics uses all, and the quantum case raises deep questions.
  • Measurement depends on theory, instruments and standards; agreement of independent methods builds trust, and quantum measurement is philosophically special.

Common mistakes in Philosophy and Foundations of Physics

  • Predictive success doesn't automatically prove truth; history shows successful theories can rest on false entities.
  • A model that predicts well, such as a neural network, may provide little physical understanding.
  • Many textbook "laws" are approximate empirical relations valid only in limited regimes.
  • Determinism and predictability are different: chaotic systems are deterministic but unpredictable.
  • Confusing P(data|hypothesis) with P(hypothesis|data) is the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • "Raw data" never comes without interpretation; instruments and analysis embed assumptions.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Scientific realism and instrumentalism
    Scientific realism holds that our best theories describe a real world, including unobservable things like electrons and quarks.
  2. Explanation and prediction in physics
    Physics both predicts what will happen and explains why it happens.
  3. Laws of nature
    Laws of nature, like Newton's gravitation or the conservation of energy, seem to express how the universe must behave.
  4. Causality and determinism
    Causality concerns how events bring about others; determinism holds that the present state fixes the entire future.
  5. Probability and chance
    Probability is everywhere in physics, from statistical mechanics to quantum measurement.
  6. Measurement and observation
    Measurement connects theory to the world, but it is never simple.
  7. The problem of induction
    Physics generalises from finite observations to universal laws, but logic alone cannot justify this leap.
  8. Reductionism and emergence
    Reductionism holds that complex phenomena can be explained by simpler underlying parts and laws.
  9. Space and time
    What are space and time?
  10. The nature of spacetime
    General relativity treats spacetime as a dynamical, curved entity that carries energy and waves.
  11. Interpretations of quantum mechanics
    Quantum mechanics predicts experimental results with extraordinary accuracy, yet physicists disagree about what it says reality is like.
  12. Copenhagen interpretation
    The Copenhagen interpretation, developed by Bohr, Heisenberg and others in the 1920s, became the standard view taught in textbooks.
  13. Many-worlds interpretation
    The many-worlds interpretation, proposed by Hugh Everett in 1957, takes the quantum wavefunction literally and denies collapse.
  14. Pilot-wave theory
    Pilot-wave theory, developed by de Broglie (1927) and revived by David Bohm (1952), says particles always have definite positions, guided by the wavefunction.
  15. Objective collapse theories
    Objective collapse theories modify quantum mechanics so that wavefunctions collapse spontaneously and physically, rarely for single particles but rapidly for large objects.
  16. Quantum measurement problem
    The quantum measurement problem asks how definite outcomes arise from quantum superpositions, given that the Schrödinger equation never produces a single result.
  17. Locality and nonlocality
    Locality means things are influenced only by their immediate surroundings, with influences spreading no faster than light.
  18. Bell's theorem
    Bell's theorem (1964) proves that no theory based on local hidden variables can reproduce all predictions of quantum mechanics.
  19. Scientific explanation and unification
    Unification, explaining diverse phenomena with fewer principles, has driven physics from Newton's gravity to Maxwell's electromagnetism and the electroweak theory.
  20. Symmetry and physical law
    Symmetry is one of physics's deepest principles: laws stay the same under certain transformations.
  21. Model dependence and idealisation
    Physics relies on models that simplify reality: frictionless planes, point masses, ideal gases, perfect crystals.
  22. Underdetermination of theory by evidence
    Underdetermination says that evidence alone may not decide between rival theories: different theories can fit the same data.
  23. The limits of scientific knowledge
    Physics has explained a vast range of phenomena, but are there limits to what it can know?

Philosophy and Foundations of Physics: questions students ask

What is the deductive-nomological model?

Explanation by logically deducing a phenomenon from general laws and initial conditions.

What is the best-system account of laws?

Laws are the axioms of the deductive system best balancing simplicity and strength in describing the world.

What is Laplace's demon?

A hypothetical intellect that could predict the entire future from complete knowledge of the present state and laws.

What is the Born rule?

The probability of a measurement outcome equals the squared magnitude of the corresponding amplitude.
