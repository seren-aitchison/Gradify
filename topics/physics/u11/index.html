Common mistakes in Acoustics and Sound

  • Sound travels faster in warm air, not in "thicker" air; humidity raises it slightly too.
  • Microphones measure pressure; intensity also needs the particle velocity (or an assumption of plane waves).
  • Decibels don't add arithmetically: 80 dB + 80 dB = 83 dB.
  • Doubling intensity doesn't double perceived loudness; you need about 10 times the intensity.
  • Spectra and waveforms are two views of the same signal; very different-looking waveforms can sound identical if only the phases differ.
  • For a closed pipe, the first overtone is the 3rd harmonic, not the 2nd.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Sound wave propagation
    Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave: regions of compression and rarefaction travel through a medium.
  2. Sound pressure and intensity
    Sound can be measured by its pressure amplitude (what microphones detect) or its intensity (power per unit area).
  3. Decibel scale
    The decibel scale measures sound level logarithmically, matching the ear's roughly logarithmic response and compressing a trillion-fold range into 0–120 dB.
  4. Pitch, loudness and timbre
    Pitch, loudness and timbre are how we perceive sound.
  5. Frequency spectra
    A frequency spectrum shows how much of each frequency a sound contains.
  6. Harmonics and overtones
    Most musical sounds contain a fundamental plus higher frequencies.
  7. Resonance in air columns
    Air in a tube resonates at frequencies where standing sound waves fit its length.
  8. Musical instruments and acoustics
    Every instrument couples a vibrating source to a resonator and a radiator.
  9. Doppler effect
    The Doppler effect is the change in observed frequency when the source and observer move relative to each other.
  10. Beats and interference
    Sound waves interfere like all waves: overlapping coherent sounds reinforce or cancel at different places, and nearly equal frequencies beat.
  11. Acoustic impedance
    Acoustic impedance is a medium's resistance to sound: the product of its density and sound speed.
  12. Reflection and absorption of sound
    When sound hits a surface, some reflects, some is absorbed, and some is transmitted.
  13. Reverberation
    Reverberation is the persistence of sound in a room after the source stops, as countless reflections decay.
  14. Architectural acoustics
    Architectural acoustics designs spaces so sound is clear, balanced and free of defects, and so unwanted noise is kept out.
  15. Ultrasound
    Ultrasound is sound above the range of human hearing (> 20 kHz).
  16. Infrasound
    Infrasound is sound below about 20 Hz, too low to hear as a tone.
  17. Sonar
    Sonar (sound navigation and ranging) uses sound in water to detect, locate and image objects.
  18. Acoustic levitation
    Intense standing sound waves can hold small objects suspended in air at their pressure nodes.
  19. Noise pollution
    Noise is unwanted sound.
  20. Psychoacoustics
    Psychoacoustics studies how we perceive sound.
  21. Bioacoustics
    Bioacoustics studies sound production, hearing and communication in animals, and how sound is used to study them.

Acoustics and Sound: questions students ask

How does sound intensity relate to pressure amplitude?

I = p02/(2ρv), proportional to pressure amplitude squared.

What sets the timbre of a note?

Its spectrum of harmonics and how it changes over time, especially the attack.

What is the difference between a harmonic and an overtone?

Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental; overtones are any resonances above it.

Why use two resonances to measure the speed of sound?

The difference removes the unknown end correction.
