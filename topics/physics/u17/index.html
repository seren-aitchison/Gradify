Key terms and formulas at a glance

B in tesla (T); 1 T = 1 N A-1 m-1; 1 G = 10-4 T
F = qv × B; |F| = qvB sin θ
F = q(E + v × B)
v = E/B
velocity selector: undeflected particles
F = IL × B; |F| = BIL sin θ
F/L = μ0I1I2/(2πd)
τ = NIAB sin θ = m × B
m = NIA magnetic moment
qvB = mv2/r ⇒ r = mv/(qB) = p/(qB)

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the sources of magnetic fields?

Moving charges (currents), intrinsic magnetic moments (spin) and changing electric fields. From “Magnetic fields” →

In plain English: A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or moving charge where other moving charges and magnets feel a force.

Why are Helmholtz coils useful?

They produce a very uniform field in the region between them. From “Biot–Savart law” →

In plain English: The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field produced by a small element of current.

Why does a magnet fall slowly through a copper pipe?

Eddy currents induced in the pipe produce fields opposing its motion. From “Lenz's law” →

In plain English: Lenz's law says an induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that caused it.

State Curie's law.

Paramagnetic susceptibility is inversely proportional to temperature: χ = C/T. From “Paramagnetism” →

In plain English: Paramagnetic materials have atoms with unpaired electrons whose moments align partly with an applied field, giving weak attraction.

Which Maxwell equation says there are no magnetic monopoles?

∇·B = 0. From “Maxwell's equations” →

In plain English: Maxwell's four equations describe all classical electric and magnetic phenomena: how charges and currents create fields, how changing fields create each other, and therefore how light propagates.

  2. An electron moving east in a field pointing north is pushed: (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Up (out of the ground). F = −e(v × B): v × B is down, so force is up.

  3. Thomson measured the electron's: (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Charge-to-mass ratio. e/m using crossed fields.

  4. Earth's magnetic field strength at the surface is about: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    50 μT. Tens of microtesla.

  5. A charged particle moves at right angles to a magnetic field. What is its path? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A circle. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, giving circular motion.

  6. The magnetic force on a charge depends on: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Its charge, speed and angle to B. qvB sin θ.

Common mistakes in Magnetism and Electromagnetism

  • Earth's geographic North Pole is near a magnetic south pole (it attracts compass north poles).
  • A magnetic force can't speed up or slow down a charge; it only changes direction. Accelerators use electric fields to add energy.
  • Whether a force is "electric" or "magnetic" depends on the observer's frame; the total Lorentz force transforms consistently.
  • A wire parallel to the field feels no force however large the current.
  • For relativistic particles use r = p/(qB) with the relativistic momentum γmv.
  • The Biot–Savart law is for steady currents; for time-varying fields, retardation effects (Jefimenko's equations) matter.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Magnetic fields
    A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or moving charge where other moving charges and magnets feel a force.
  2. Magnetic forces on charges
    A magnetic field exerts a force on a moving charge, perpendicular to both its velocity and the field.
  3. Lorentz force
    The Lorentz force is the total electromagnetic force on a charged particle: the electric force plus the magnetic force.
  4. Magnetic forces on current-carrying wires
    A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force because the moving charges in it do.
  5. Motion of charged particles in magnetic fields
    In a uniform magnetic field a charged particle moves in a circle (or helix), with a radius set by its momentum and a frequency independent of its speed.
  6. Biot–Savart law
    The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field produced by a small element of current.
  7. Ampère's law
    Ampère's law relates the magnetic field circulating around a closed loop to the current passing through it.
  8. Magnetic dipoles
    A current loop, a bar magnet, and an electron all behave as magnetic dipoles: they produce a characteristic field and twist to align with external fields.
  9. Magnetic flux
    Magnetic flux measures the total magnetic field passing through a surface.
  10. Electromagnetic induction
    Electromagnetic induction is the production of an EMF by a changing magnetic flux.
  11. Faraday's law
    Faraday's law states that the EMF induced in a circuit equals the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage through it.
  12. Lenz's law
    Lenz's law says an induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that caused it.
  13. Inductance
    Inductance is the property of a circuit that opposes changes in current, by producing an EMF proportional to the rate of change.
  14. Self-induction and mutual induction
    Self-induction is a changing current inducing an EMF in its own circuit; mutual induction is a changing current in one circuit inducing an EMF in another nearby.
  15. Energy stored in magnetic fields
    Building up a current in an inductor stores energy in its magnetic field.
  16. Magnetic materials
    Materials respond to magnetic fields because their atoms carry magnetic moments from electron orbits and spins.
  17. Diamagnetism
    Diamagnetism is a weak repulsion from magnetic fields shown by all materials.
  18. Paramagnetism
    Paramagnetic materials have atoms with unpaired electrons whose moments align partly with an applied field, giving weak attraction.
  19. Ferromagnetism
    In ferromagnets, a quantum-mechanical exchange interaction makes neighbouring spins align, producing strong spontaneous magnetisation in regions called domains.
  20. Hysteresis
    Hysteresis is the lagging of a ferromagnet's magnetisation behind the applied field.
  21. Electromagnets
    An electromagnet is a coil of wire, usually around an iron core, that becomes magnetic when current flows.
  22. Solenoids and toroids
    A solenoid is a long helical coil; a toroid is a coil wound around a ring.
  23. Electric motors and generators
    Motors turn electrical energy into motion using the force on currents in magnetic fields; generators do the reverse using induction.
  24. Maxwell's equations
    Maxwell's four equations describe all classical electric and magnetic phenomena: how charges and currents create fields, how changing fields create each other, and therefore how light propagates.
  25. Displacement current
    Displacement current is Maxwell's addition to Ampère's law: a changing electric field produces a magnetic field just as a real current does.
  26. Electromagnetic potentials
    Instead of working with E and B directly, electromagnetism can be written in terms of a scalar potential V and a vector potential A.
  27. Gauge transformations
    The electromagnetic potentials can be changed by a gauge transformation without altering the physical fields.
  28. Electromagnetic boundary conditions
    At the boundary between two materials, Maxwell's equations require particular components of E, D, B and H to match.
  29. Electromagnetic momentum and energy
    Electromagnetic fields carry energy and momentum.

Magnetism and Electromagnetism: questions students ask

Why does the magnetic force do no work?

It's always perpendicular to the velocity.

How does a velocity selector work?

Crossed E and B fields balance for particles with v = E/B, which pass undeflected.

Why does the cyclotron frequency not depend on speed (non-relativistically)?

Faster particles move on proportionally larger circles, taking the same time per orbit.

What is the field of a long straight wire?

B = μ0I/(2πd), circling the wire.
