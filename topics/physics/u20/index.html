Lessons in this unit

  1. Origins of quantum theory
    Quantum theory began around 1900 when classical physics failed to explain how hot objects glow, how light ejects electrons, and why atoms are stable with sharp spectral lines.
  2. Blackbody radiation
    Classical physics predicted a hot cavity would radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths.
  3. Photoelectric effect
    When light shines on a metal, electrons are emitted only if the light's frequency exceeds a threshold, however intense it is.
  4. Compton scattering
    When X-rays scatter off electrons, their wavelength increases by an amount depending on the scattering angle.
  5. Wave-particle duality
    Light and matter both show wave and particle behaviour.
  6. de Broglie wavelength
    De Broglie proposed that every particle has a wavelength inversely proportional to its momentum.
  7. Double-slit experiments
    Sending particles one at a time through two slits builds up an interference pattern dot by dot.
  8. Heisenberg uncertainty principle
    The uncertainty principle states that certain pairs of quantities, like position and momentum, can't both have precisely defined values.
  9. Wavefunctions
    The wavefunction ψ is the mathematical object describing a quantum system's state.
  10. Probability amplitudes
    In quantum mechanics, each way an event can happen has a complex probability amplitude.
  11. Born rule
    The Born rule connects quantum theory to experiment: the probability of a measurement outcome is the squared magnitude of the corresponding amplitude.
  12. Schrödinger equation
    The Schrödinger equation governs how quantum states evolve in time.
  13. Time-dependent and time-independent Schrödinger equations
    For potentials that don't change in time, the Schrödinger equation separates.
  14. Quantum states and observables
    A quantum state contains everything that can be predicted about a system.
  15. Operators and eigenvalues
    Operators act on quantum states to produce new states.
  16. Expectation values
    The expectation value of an observable is the average of many measurements on identically prepared systems.
  17. Hilbert spaces
    A Hilbert space is the complete vector space with an inner product in which quantum states live.
  18. Superposition
    The superposition principle says that if two states are possible, any normalised complex combination of them is also a possible state.
  19. Quantum measurement
    Measurement in quantum mechanics gives a definite outcome with Born-rule probabilities and leaves the system in a corresponding state.
  20. Commutators
    The commutator of two operators measures whether the order of applying them matters.
  21. Compatible and incompatible observables
    Compatible observables can have definite values at the same time and share a set of eigenstates; incompatible observables can't.
  22. Infinite and finite potential wells
    A particle confined in a potential well can only have certain energies.
  23. Quantum tunnelling
    A quantum particle can pass through a barrier higher than its energy, because its wavefunction decays inside the barrier but doesn't vanish.
  24. Quantum harmonic oscillator
    The quantum harmonic oscillator has evenly spaced energy levels and a nonzero ground-state energy.
  25. Quantum barriers and scattering
    Quantum scattering describes how particles bounce off or pass through potentials.
  26. Angular momentum
    In quantum mechanics angular momentum is quantised in both magnitude and direction.
  27. Spin
    Spin is an intrinsic angular momentum carried by particles, with no classical analogue as literal rotation.
  28. Addition of angular momentum
    When two angular momenta combine (orbital and spin, or two particles' spins), the total takes a range of quantum numbers.
  29. Identical particles
    Identical quantum particles are truly indistinguishable: swapping two can't change any measurable result.
  30. Bosons and fermions
    All particles are either bosons (integer spin, sociable: any number can share a state) or fermions (half-integer spin, antisocial: one per state).
  31. Pauli exclusion principle
    No two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state.
  32. Hydrogen atom
    Hydrogen, one electron bound to a proton, is the only atom the Schrödinger equation solves exactly.
  33. Orbital angular momentum
    An electron moving around a nucleus has orbital angular momentum, quantised by l and m.
  34. Approximation methods
    Most quantum problems can't be solved exactly.
  35. Perturbation theory
    Perturbation theory finds approximate energies and states when a small extra term is added to a Hamiltonian we can solve.
  36. Variational method
    The variational method estimates a ground-state energy by guessing a trial wavefunction with adjustable parameters and minimising its energy.
  37. WKB approximation
    The WKB approximation solves the Schrödinger equation when the potential varies slowly compared with the wavelength.
  38. Density matrices
    The density matrix describes quantum states including classical uncertainty about which state a system is in.
  39. Mixed and pure states
    A pure state is a single quantum state vector; a mixed state is a statistical mixture, arising from classical ignorance or from entanglement with something else.
  40. Quantum entanglement
    Entangled particles share a joint quantum state that can't be written as separate states for each.
  41. Decoherence
    Decoherence is the loss of quantum coherence when a system interacts with its environment, which effectively measures it.
  42. Quantum information fundamentals
    Quantum information theory studies how information is stored, processed and transmitted using quantum systems.
