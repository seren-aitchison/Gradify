Common mistakes in Electric Circuits

  • Current isn't "used up" in a component; the same current enters and leaves. Energy is what's transferred.
  • Resistance depends on shape; resistivity doesn't. Stretching a wire to twice its length (same volume) quadruples its resistance.
  • V = IR defines resistance for any component at a point; Ohm's law is the claim that R stays constant.
  • High conductivity doesn't always mean high mobility: metals have huge carrier densities but modest mobilities.
  • Adding resistors in parallel decreases total resistance and increases total current drawn.
  • Watch signs: going through a resistor along the current is a drop (−IR); through a cell from − to + is a rise (+ε).

Lessons in this unit

  1. Electric current
    Electric current is the rate of flow of charge.
  2. Resistance and resistivity
    Resistance measures how much a component opposes current: the voltage needed per ampere.
  3. Ohm's law
    Ohm's law states that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it, at constant temperature.
  4. Conductivity
    Conductivity σ is the reciprocal of resistivity: how readily a material carries current.
  5. Series and parallel circuits
    Components in series share the same current; in parallel they share the same voltage.
  6. Kirchhoff's laws
    Kirchhoff's laws express conservation of charge and energy in circuits.
  7. Electromotive force
    Electromotive force (EMF) is the energy a source gives each coulomb of charge passing through it.
  8. Internal resistance
    Every real source has internal resistance, so its terminal voltage drops when it supplies current.
  9. Electrical power and energy
    Electrical power is the rate at which energy is transferred by a current.
  10. Resistor networks
    Complex resistor networks can often be simplified by combining series and parallel parts, using symmetry, or converting between star and delta shapes.
  11. Capacitor charging and discharging
    When a capacitor charges or discharges through a resistor, the current doesn't change instantly: voltage and charge follow exponential curves with time constant RC.
  12. RC circuits
    RC circuits combine resistors and capacitors to shape signals: they set delays, smooth ripple, block DC, and filter frequencies.
  13. RL circuits
    An inductor opposes changes in current by generating a back EMF.
  14. RLC circuits
    An RLC circuit combines resistance, inductance and capacitance.
  15. Transient responses
    A transient is a circuit's temporary response when something changes suddenly, like a switch closing.
  16. Alternating current
    Alternating current (AC) reverses direction periodically, usually sinusoidally.
  17. Root-mean-square values
    The root-mean-square (rms) value of an alternating quantity is the steady DC value that would deliver the same average power to a resistor.
  18. Impedance and reactance
    Impedance generalises resistance to AC: it includes the resistance and the reactances of capacitors and inductors, which depend on frequency and shift the phase between voltage and current.
  19. Phasor diagrams
    Phasors represent sinusoidal quantities as rotating arrows: length for amplitude, angle for phase.
  20. Resonance in AC circuits
    A series RLC circuit resonates when the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.
  21. Transformers
    A transformer changes AC voltage using electromagnetic induction between two coils on an iron core.
  22. Rectification
    Rectification converts AC into DC using diodes, which conduct one way only.
  23. Filters and frequency response
    Filters pass some frequencies and block others.
  24. Electrical measurement
    Measuring voltage, current and resistance accurately means choosing the right instrument and connecting it so it disturbs the circuit as little as possible.
  25. Circuit analysis methods
    Beyond series and parallel rules, systematic methods solve any linear circuit: nodal and mesh analysis, superposition, and source transformations.
  26. Thevenin and Norton equivalents
    Any linear network seen from two terminals behaves like a single voltage source with a series resistance (Thevenin) or a current source with a parallel resistance (Norton).
  27. Operational amplifiers
    An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain differential amplifier on a chip.
  28. Semiconductor circuit fundamentals
    Semiconductor devices, chiefly diodes and transistors, control current in ways resistors can't: one-way flow, amplification and switching.

Electric Circuits: questions students ask

Why does a lamp light immediately when drift speeds are so slow?

The electric field propagates at near the speed of light, starting electrons moving everywhere at once.

Why does metal resistance rise with temperature?

Increased lattice vibrations scatter electrons more often.

Why is a filament lamp non-ohmic?

Its temperature, and so resistance, increases with current.

What is mobility?

Drift speed per unit electric field.
