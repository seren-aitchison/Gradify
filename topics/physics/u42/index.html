Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a particle in QFT?

A quantised excitation of a field mode. From “Quantum fields and particles” →

In plain English: In quantum field theory, the fundamental objects are fields filling all of space, and particles are their quantised excitations. Key idea: Fields are fundamental; particles are quantised ripples, all identical because they come from the same field.

What sets the range of a force mediated by a massive particle?

The Compton wavelength ħ/(mc). From “Propagators” →

In plain English: A propagator gives the amplitude for a particle to travel from one spacetime point to another. Key idea: Propagators are Green's functions with causal boundary conditions; their poles are physical masses, and exchange gives forces.

Why do IR divergences cancel?

Virtual loop divergences cancel against real emission of unresolvable soft or collinear particles. From “Divergences and regularisation” →

In plain English: Loop diagrams in QFT often give infinite integrals because virtual particles can have arbitrarily large momenta. Key idea: Regulate divergences to compute consistently; dimensional regularisation is preferred because it respects gauge symmetry.

What is the weak mixing angle?

θW, describing how W3 and B mix into Z and γ; sin2θW ≈ 0.231. From “Electroweak theory” →

In plain English: Electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak force in an SU(2) × U(1) gauge theory. Key idea: Electroweak theory unites EM and weak forces; mixing of W3 and B gives the Z and photon.

What explains universality in critical phenomena?

Different systems flow to the same RG fixed point. From “Renormalisation group” →

In plain English: The renormalisation group describes how a theory's couplings and effective description change with the scale at which it is probed. Key idea: The RG tracks how theories change with scale; fixed points govern universality and asymptotic behaviour.

Key takeaways

  • Fields are fundamental; particles are quantised ripples, all identical because they come from the same field.
  • Creation and annihilation operators build Fock space; commutators give bosons, anticommutators give fermions.
  • Promote fields to operators with canonical commutators; mode expansions then give creation and annihilation operators.
  • Path integrals sum over all field histories; derivatives of Z[J] give correlation functions and Feynman diagrams.
  • Propagators are Green's functions with causal boundary conditions; their poles are physical masses, and exchange gives forces.
  • Feynman rules turn diagrams into amplitudes: propagators for lines, couplings for vertices, integrals for loops.

Common mistakes in Quantum Field Theory

  • The particle concept is observer-dependent in general; the field is the more basic object.
  • Using commutators for fermion fields leads to inconsistencies (negative energies or acausality); spin and statistics are tied.
  • The infinite zero-point energy is removed by normal ordering; only differences in vacuum energy are observable (except perhaps in gravity).
  • Gauge theories need gauge fixing; without it, the path integral overcounts and diverges.
  • Virtual particles in propagators don't satisfy E2 = p2c2 + m2c4; they are mathematical terms, not observable particles.
  • Relative signs between diagrams with identical fermions matter; missing a minus sign breaks the Pauli principle.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Quantum fields and particles
    In quantum field theory, the fundamental objects are fields filling all of space, and particles are their quantised excitations.
  2. Creation and annihilation operators
    Creation and annihilation operators add or remove a quantum of a field mode.
  3. Canonical quantisation
    Canonical quantisation turns a classical field theory into a quantum one by promoting fields and conjugate momenta to operators with equal-time commutation relations.
  4. Path-integral quantisation
    Path-integral quantisation computes quantum amplitudes by integrating over all possible field configurations, each weighted by eiS/ħ.
  5. Propagators
    A propagator gives the amplitude for a particle to travel from one spacetime point to another.
  6. Feynman rules
    Feynman rules translate diagrams into mathematical expressions for scattering amplitudes.
  7. Scattering amplitudes
    Scattering amplitudes are the central predictions of QFT: complex numbers whose squared magnitudes give probabilities of particles scattering into particular final states.
  8. Perturbation theory
    Perturbation theory in QFT expands amplitudes in powers of small couplings, with each order corresponding to Feynman diagrams with more vertices and loops.
  9. Divergences and regularisation
    Loop diagrams in QFT often give infinite integrals because virtual particles can have arbitrarily large momenta.
  10. Renormalisation
    Renormalisation turns divergent loop calculations into finite predictions by expressing results in terms of measured quantities.
  11. Gauge invariance
    In quantum field theory, gauge invariance is the principle that fixes how matter couples to force carriers.
  12. Quantum electrodynamics
    Quantum electrodynamics is the quantum field theory of electrons, positrons and photons.
  13. Quantum chromodynamics
    Quantum chromodynamics is the SU(3) gauge theory of quarks and gluons, describing the strong force that binds quarks into protons, neutrons and other hadrons.
  14. Electroweak theory
    Electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak force in an SU(2) × U(1) gauge theory.
  15. Spontaneous symmetry breaking
    Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the laws are symmetric but the lowest-energy state is not.
  16. Higgs mechanism
    The Higgs mechanism gives mass to the W and Z bosons through spontaneous breaking of electroweak gauge symmetry by the Higgs field.
  17. Effective field theory
    Effective field theory describes physics at a chosen energy scale using only the relevant fields, with heavier physics summarised by higher-dimension operators.
  18. Renormalisation group
    The renormalisation group describes how a theory's couplings and effective description change with the scale at which it is probed.
  19. Anomalies
    An anomaly is a classical symmetry broken by quantum effects.
  20. Non-Abelian gauge theories
    Non-Abelian gauge theories, or Yang–Mills theories, use symmetry groups whose transformations don't commute.
  21. Lattice field theory
    Lattice field theory discretises spacetime into a grid so quantum field theories can be computed numerically from first principles.
  22. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime
    Quantum field theory in curved spacetime studies quantum fields on a classical, curved background geometry.

Quantum Field Theory: questions students ask

What does the number operator a†a measure?

The number of quanta in that mode.

What is normal ordering?

Placing all annihilation operators to the right of creation operators, removing the zero-point constant.

What are Grassmann variables used for?

Representing fermion fields in path integrals; they anticommute.

What does unitarity of the S-matrix guarantee?

Probabilities sum to one; it implies the optical theorem.
