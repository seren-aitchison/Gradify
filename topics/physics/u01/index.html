Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the four main aims of physics?

To describe, explain, predict and unify natural phenomena using as few principles as possible. From “Scope and aims of physics” →

In plain English: Physics is the study of matter, energy, space and time, and of the laws that connect them. Key idea: A good physical theory is economical (few assumptions), general (wide range of validity), quantitative and falsifiable.

What does the Buckingham π theorem say?

n variables with k independent dimensions can be combined into n − k independent dimensionless groups that the physics depends on. From “Dimensional analysis” →

In plain English: Every physical quantity has dimensions: powers of the base dimensions mass [M], length [L], time [T], current [I], temperature [Θ], amount [N] and luminous intensity [J].

What is the order of magnitude of the Earth's radius in metres?

107 m (6.4 × 106 m). From “Estimation and order-of-magnitude calculations” →

In plain English: An order-of-magnitude estimate gets the answer to within a factor of about 10 using rough inputs.

What is the Born rule?

The probability density for finding a particle at x is |ψ(x)|2. From “Deterministic and probabilistic models” →

In plain English: A deterministic model gives one future from a given present; a probabilistic model gives a distribution of possible outcomes.

What was the ultraviolet catastrophe?

The classical prediction that a black body emits infinite power at short wavelengths. From “Classical and modern physics” →

In plain English: Classical physics (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism before 1900) works superbly for everyday scales. Key idea: Modern physics doesn't make classical physics wrong in its domain; it shows where that domain ends. Engineers still design bridges with Newton.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Physics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes the aim of physics? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    To explain the widest range of phenomena with the fewest principles. Physics seeks economical, general, testable laws, not certainty or mere cataloguing.

  2. Which of these is an extensive quantity? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Internal energy. Extensive quantities add when systems combine; energy does, the others don't.

  3. Which is NOT an SI base unit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Newton. The newton is derived: kg m s-2.

  4. Which regime needs both quantum mechanics and special relativity? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High-energy particle collisions. When particles move near c and quantum effects matter, you need quantum field theory.

  5. 3.2 m is converted to millimetres. What happens? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The number is multiplied by 1000; the quantity is unchanged. Number and unit change inversely so the product (the quantity) is invariant.

  6. What is the tesla in SI base units? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    kg s-2 A-1. From F = BIL: B = N/(A m) = kg m s-2/(A m) = kg s-2 A-1.

  7. The metre is defined by fixing which quantity? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The speed of light. Since 1983 c is exact, so the metre is the distance light travels in 1/299 792 458 s.

  8. A drag force depends on ρ, v, a size L and viscosity μ. How many dimensionless groups govern it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    2. Five variables (F, ρ, v, L, μ) with three dimensions (M, L, T) give 5 − 3 = 2 groups: a drag coefficient and the Reynolds number.

  9. Two forces of 5 N give a resultant of 5 N. What is the angle between them? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    120°. R2 = 25 + 25 + 50 cos θ = 25, so cos θ = −1/2 and θ = 120°.

Key takeaways

  • A good physical theory is economical (few assumptions), general (wide range of validity), quantitative and falsifiable.
  • An operational definition says how to measure a quantity. Physics only admits quantities that have one.
  • Since 2019 no SI unit depends on a physical artefact; all are defined by exact constants, so they can be realised anywhere with the right experiment.
  • Constants with dimensions can be set to 1 by choosing units (natural units: c = ħ = 1). Dimensionless constants cannot.
  • "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (George Box). A model is judged by how well it predicts in its domain, not by being literally true.
  • Idealisations are not errors. They are controlled approximations, and a good physicist states them and checks their limits.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Physics

  • Physics does not "prove" theories true. It tests them, and a theory survives only as long as no reliable measurement contradicts it within its domain.
  • Do not treat a unit as optional. "The length is 3.2" is meaningless; units carry half the information.
  • The radian and steradian are dimensionless derived units. Leaving "rad" out of ω in s-1 is legal but can hide errors between angular frequency ω and frequency f.
  • A dimensionally correct equation can still be wrong (it may miss a factor of 2π, or a whole term). Dimensional analysis can rule things out, never fully rule them in.
  • Squared and cubed units trip people up: 1 mm2 = 10-6 m2, not 10-3 m2.
  • Speed and velocity are not interchangeable: average speed over a round trip is positive, but average velocity is zero.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Scope and aims of physics
    Physics is the study of matter, energy, space and time, and of the laws that connect them.
  2. Physical quantities and observables
    A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number times a unit.
  3. SI base and derived units
    The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units by fixing exact values of seven constants of nature.
  4. Dimensional analysis
    Every physical quantity has dimensions: powers of the base dimensions mass [M], length [L], time [T], current [I], temperature [Θ], amount [N] and luminous intensity [J].
  5. Unit conversions and prefixes
    SI prefixes scale units by powers of ten; conversions multiply by factors equal to one.
  6. Scalar and vector quantities
    A scalar has magnitude only; a vector has magnitude and direction and adds by the parallelogram rule.
  7. Significant figures and scientific notation
    Significant figures carry the precision of a measurement; scientific notation (a × 10n, 1 ≤ a < 10) makes size and precision explicit.
  8. Estimation and order-of-magnitude calculations
    An order-of-magnitude estimate gets the answer to within a factor of about 10 using rough inputs.
  9. Physical constants
    Physical constants are quantities that appear in the laws of nature and have the same value everywhere.
  10. Mathematical modelling
    A mathematical model represents a physical system with variables and equations so its behaviour can be predicted.
  11. Idealisation and simplifying assumptions
    Idealisation replaces a messy real system with a simpler one that keeps the essential physics: point masses, frictionless surfaces, ideal gases, thin lenses.
  12. Deterministic and probabilistic models
    A deterministic model gives one future from a given present; a probabilistic model gives a distribution of possible outcomes.
  13. Conservation laws
    A conservation law states that some quantity of an isolated system stays constant however the system changes.
  14. Symmetry and invariance
    A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the laws of physics unchanged.
  15. Physical laws, principles and theories
    Physicists use these words for different things.
  16. Classical and modern physics
    Classical physics (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism before 1900) works superbly for everyday scales.
  17. Microscopic and macroscopic descriptions
    A macroscopic description uses a few bulk variables (pressure, temperature, volume); a microscopic one tracks the constituents (molecules, electrons).
  18. Uncertainty and measurement limitations
    No measurement is exact.
  19. Scientific reasoning and falsifiability
    Science advances by proposing hypotheses that make risky predictions and testing them against observation.

Foundations of Physics: questions students ask

What is an operational definition?

A definition of a quantity by the procedure used to measure it.

How many SI base units are there?

Seven: s, m, kg, A, K, mol, cd.

Is work a scalar or a vector?

A scalar (the dot product of force and displacement), though it can be negative.

How many significant figures are in 0.00340?

Three (3, 4 and the trailing 0).
