Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is capacity factor?

Actual energy produced divided by the maximum possible at rated power over the same time. From “Energy conversion and conservation” →

In plain English: Energy can't be created or destroyed, only converted between forms, but every conversion wastes some as low-grade heat. Key idea: Energy is conserved but degraded; multiply efficiencies along chains and respect Carnot limits for heat engines.

Which turbine suits high head and low flow?

The Pelton wheel. From “Hydroelectric power” →

In plain English: Hydroelectric power converts the gravitational potential energy of water into electricity using turbines. Key idea: Hydropower ∝ flow × head; it is efficient and flexible, and pumped storage dominates grid storage.

What is the Lawson criterion?

A minimum triple product of density, temperature and confinement time needed for net fusion energy. From “Nuclear fusion energy” →

In plain English: Fusion power aims to reproduce the Sun's energy source on Earth by fusing light nuclei, mainly deuterium and tritium, at temperatures above 100 million kelvin. Key idea: Fusion needs high temperature, density and confinement time (Lawson criterion); ignition has been shown but net electricity hasn't yet.

What is a Ragone plot?

A chart of specific energy versus specific power for storage technologies. From “Supercapacitors” →

In plain English: Supercapacitors store energy electrostatically at electrode–electrolyte interfaces, delivering power quickly and surviving hundreds of thousands of cycles. Key idea: Supercapacitors trade energy density for power and cycle life; energy ∝ CV2.

Why are flow batteries suited to long duration?

Energy is stored in tanks of electrolyte, scalable independently of power. From “Grid storage” →

In plain English: Grid storage stores electricity when supply exceeds demand and releases it later, balancing variable wind and solar. Key idea: Storage matches supply and demand across timescales; a mix of technologies is needed for a renewable grid.

Key takeaways

  • Energy is conserved but degraded; multiply efficiencies along chains and respect Carnot limits for heat engines.
  • PV converts photons above Eg into current; Shockley–Queisser limits single junctions to ~34%, tandems go further.
  • Solar thermal turns sunlight into heat; concentration reaches power-plant temperatures and molten salt enables storage.
  • Wind power ∝ v3 and rotor area; Betz caps extraction at 59%, so bigger rotors and taller towers win.
  • Hydropower ∝ flow × head; it is efficient and flexible, and pumped storage dominates grid storage.
  • Tides offer predictable power from dense, slow water; waves offer high power density but tough engineering.

Common mistakes in Renewable Energy and Energy Physics

  • Don't confuse power (kW) with energy (kWh); a 1 kW device running for 3 hours uses 3 kWh.
  • Solar panels lose efficiency when hot; sunny but hot days don't always give peak output.
  • Concentrating systems need direct sunlight; cloudy, diffuse conditions give little output.
  • Doubling wind speed gives eight times the power; small errors in wind speed estimates give big errors in yield.
  • Hydro is renewable but not impact-free; ecological and social effects can be large.
  • Tidal power is predictable but not constant; output varies with the tidal cycle.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Energy conversion and conservation
    Energy can't be created or destroyed, only converted between forms, but every conversion wastes some as low-grade heat.
  2. Solar photovoltaic physics
    Solar cells convert sunlight directly into electricity using the photovoltaic effect in semiconductors.
  3. Solar thermal systems
    Solar thermal systems capture sunlight as heat, for hot water, space heating, industrial processes or electricity generation.
  4. Wind energy
    Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity.
  5. Hydroelectric power
    Hydroelectric power converts the gravitational potential energy of water into electricity using turbines.
  6. Tidal and wave energy
    Tidal and wave energy harness the ocean's motion: tides driven by the Moon and Sun, and waves driven by wind.
  7. Geothermal energy
    Geothermal energy uses heat from Earth's interior, originally from formation and ongoing radioactive decay, for electricity and direct heating.
  8. Nuclear fission energy
    Nuclear fission power splits heavy nuclei like uranium-235, releasing about 200 MeV per fission, millions of times more energy per kilogram than burning fuel.
  9. Nuclear fusion energy
    Fusion power aims to reproduce the Sun's energy source on Earth by fusing light nuclei, mainly deuterium and tritium, at temperatures above 100 million kelvin.
  10. Hydrogen energy systems
    Hydrogen is an energy carrier, not a source: it must be produced using energy, then stored, transported and converted back.
  11. Fuel cells
    Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly into electricity by electrochemical reactions, without combustion.
  12. Batteries and electrochemical energy storage
    Batteries store energy chemically and release it as electricity through redox reactions.
  13. Supercapacitors
    Supercapacitors store energy electrostatically at electrode–electrolyte interfaces, delivering power quickly and surviving hundreds of thousands of cycles.
  14. Thermoelectric energy conversion
    Thermoelectric devices convert heat directly into electricity (Seebeck effect) or use electricity to pump heat (Peltier effect), with no moving parts.
  15. Heat pumps
    Heat pumps move heat from a cold place to a warm one using work, delivering three to four units of heat per unit of electricity.
  16. Energy efficiency
    Energy efficiency means getting the same service, such as warmth, light, motion or computation, with less energy.
  17. Grid storage
    Grid storage stores electricity when supply exceeds demand and releases it later, balancing variable wind and solar.
  18. Energy transmission and losses
    Electricity travels from power plants to users through transmission and distribution networks.
  19. Energy systems modelling
    Energy systems models simulate how energy is supplied, converted, stored and used across a region over time.
  20. Life-cycle energy analysis
    Life-cycle analysis counts energy use and emissions over a product's whole life, from extracting raw materials to manufacturing, operation and disposal.
  21. Energy security and sustainability
    Energy security means reliable, affordable access to energy; sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming future generations or the climate.

Renewable Energy and Energy Physics: questions students ask

What is the Shockley–Queisser limit?

The maximum efficiency (~33.7%) of a single-junction solar cell under unconcentrated sunlight.

Why use selective coatings?

High solar absorption with low thermal emission reduces heat loss.

What is the Betz limit?

The maximum 59.3% of wind kinetic energy flux a turbine can extract.

What is pumped-storage hydro?

Storing energy by pumping water uphill and later releasing it through turbines.
