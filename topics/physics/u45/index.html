What does the Hall–Petch relation describe?

Yield strength increasing as grain size decreases, σy ∝ d−1/2. From “Structure-property relationships” →

In plain English: A material's properties, such as strength, conductivity, colour and magnetism, come from its structure at every scale: the bonding between atoms, the crystal lattice, defects, grains and microstructure. Key idea: Properties follow from structure at many scales, and processing controls structure.

What is pearlite?

Alternating layers of ferrite and cementite formed from austenite at the eutectoid. From “Phase diagrams” →

In plain English: Phase diagrams map which phases are stable at each temperature and composition. Key idea: Phase diagrams show stable phases; the lever rule gives phase fractions and the phase rule counts freedoms.

What is coercivity?

The reverse field needed to reduce magnetisation to zero. From “Magnetic properties” →

In plain English: Materials respond to magnetic fields in different ways: weakly opposing (diamagnetism), weakly aligning (paramagnetism), or strongly ordering (ferro-, ferri- and antiferromagnetism). Key idea: Magnetism comes from electron moments; exchange interaction produces spontaneous order in ferro-, ferri- and antiferromagnets.

What does (BH)max measure?

The maximum magnetic energy a permanent magnet can supply per unit volume. From “Magnetic materials” →

In plain English: Magnetic materials are engineered for specific jobs: soft magnets that switch easily in transformers and motors, hard magnets that hold strong fields in electric vehicles and wind turbines, and thin f Key idea: Soft magnets minimise loss for switching; hard magnets maximise stored energy; thin films enable memory and sensing.

What is osseointegration?

Direct structural and functional connection between living bone and an implant surface. From “Biomaterials” →

In plain English: Biomaterials are materials designed to interact with the body: implants, prosthetics, scaffolds and drug-delivery systems. Key idea: Biomaterials must meet mechanical needs while being biocompatible, durable and suited to the body's environment.

Key takeaways

  • Properties follow from structure at many scales, and processing controls structure.
  • Bonding sets strength and conductivity; lattice structure sets packing, slip and anisotropy.
  • Dislocations make metals ductile; blocking them makes metals strong.
  • Diffusion follows Fick's laws with Arrhenius temperature dependence; distances scale as √(Dt).
  • Phase diagrams show stable phases; the lever rule gives phase fractions and the phase rule counts freedoms.
  • Transformations proceed by nucleation and growth (or shear); their kinetics, captured in TTT diagrams, set microstructures.

Common mistakes in Materials Physics

  • The same composition can give wildly different properties depending on processing and microstructure.
  • Close-packed structures (FCC, HCP) have the same packing fraction but different ductility because of slip-system geometry.
  • A perfect, dislocation-free crystal (like a whisker) can be enormously strong; real metals are weak because of dislocations.
  • Doubling the diffusion distance needs four times the time.
  • Phase diagrams describe equilibrium; fast processing can produce structures not on the diagram.
  • Larger undercooling doesn't always speed transformation; at low temperature diffusion becomes too slow.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Structure-property relationships
    A material's properties, such as strength, conductivity, colour and magnetism, come from its structure at every scale: the bonding between atoms, the crystal lattice, defects, grains and microstructure.
  2. Atomic bonding and crystal structure
    Atoms bond through ionic, covalent, metallic and van der Waals interactions, and their arrangement into crystal lattices determines density, slip systems, cleavage and much more.
  3. Defects and dislocations
    No crystal is perfect.
  4. Diffusion in solids
    Atoms in solids move by hopping between sites, driven by random thermal motion and concentration gradients.
  5. Phase diagrams
    Phase diagrams map which phases are stable at each temperature and composition.
  6. Phase transformations
    Phase transformations change a material's structure: solidification, precipitation, eutectoid reactions and martensitic changes.
  7. Mechanical properties
    Mechanical properties describe how materials respond to forces: stiffness, strength, ductility, hardness, toughness and resistance to fatigue and creep.
  8. Thermal properties
    Thermal properties describe how materials store heat, conduct it and change size with temperature.
  9. Electrical properties
    Electrical properties range over more than 25 orders of magnitude, from superconductors to insulators.
  10. Magnetic properties
    Materials respond to magnetic fields in different ways: weakly opposing (diamagnetism), weakly aligning (paramagnetism), or strongly ordering (ferro-, ferri- and antiferromagnetism).
  11. Optical properties
    A material's optical properties, how it absorbs, reflects, transmits and emits light, come from its electrons and their band structure.
  12. Dielectric materials
    Dielectrics are insulators that polarise in electric fields, storing energy and reducing field strength inside.
  13. Semiconductors
    Semiconductors have band gaps small enough that their conductivity can be controlled by temperature, doping, light and electric fields.
  14. Superconductors
    Superconductors carry current with zero resistance and expel magnetic fields below a critical temperature.
  15. Magnetic materials
    Magnetic materials are engineered for specific jobs: soft magnets that switch easily in transformers and motors, hard magnets that hold strong fields in electric vehicles and wind turbines, and thin f
  16. Metamaterials
    Metamaterials are engineered structures whose properties come from their designed architecture rather than chemistry.
  17. Nanostructured materials
    Nanostructured materials have features between about 1 and 100 nm, where surface effects and quantum confinement change properties dramatically.
  18. Thin films and coatings
    Thin films, layers from a few atoms to several micrometres thick, give surfaces new functions: anti-reflection, hardness, corrosion protection, conductivity and the active layers of chips, solar cells and displays.
  19. Amorphous materials
    Amorphous materials lack long-range crystalline order while keeping short-range order between neighbouring atoms.
  20. Biomaterials
    Biomaterials are materials designed to interact with the body: implants, prosthetics, scaffolds and drug-delivery systems.
  21. Smart materials
    Smart materials change a property, such as shape, colour, stiffness or electrical output, in a controlled way in response to stimuli like stress, temperature, light or fields.
  22. Shape-memory alloys
    Shape-memory alloys like nickel–titanium remember a shape: deform them when cold and they spring back on heating, or stretch them several percent at body temperature and they recover instantly.
  23. Materials under extreme conditions
    Extreme pressures, temperatures, radiation and strain rates transform materials, creating new phases and revealing fundamental physics.
  24. Materials characterisation techniques
    Characterisation techniques probe materials from atomic to macroscopic scales.

Materials Physics: questions students ask

Why are FCC metals typically ductile?

They have 12 slip systems on close-packed planes.

What is a Burgers vector?

The vector describing the magnitude and direction of lattice distortion caused by a dislocation.

Why is interstitial diffusion faster than substitutional?

Interstitial atoms don't need a vacancy next to them and have lower activation energies.

What is a eutectic?

A composition where liquid transforms directly into two solid phases at the lowest melting temperature.
