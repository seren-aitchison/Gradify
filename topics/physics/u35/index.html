Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What tissue property does ultrasound image?

Changes in acoustic impedance, which produce reflections. From “Physics of diagnostic imaging” →

In plain English: Diagnostic imaging uses waves and particles to see inside the body without cutting it open. Key idea: Every imaging modality is a physical measurement; choosing one means choosing which tissue property you want to see.

Why is Tc-99m so widely used?

Ideal 140 keV gamma, 6 h half-life, no beta emission, and it is made on site from a Mo-99 generator. From “Nuclear medicine” →

In plain English: Nuclear medicine images and treats disease using radioactive tracers given to the patient. Key idea: Nuclear medicine images physiology: where the tracer goes, and how fast it gets there and leaves.

Why does the Doppler shift fall at 90° insonation?

cos 90° = 0, so there is no velocity component along the beam. From “Doppler ultrasound” →

In plain English: Doppler ultrasound measures the frequency shift of echoes from moving blood cells to find how fast and in which direction blood flows. Key idea: The Doppler shift measures velocity along the beam; angle matters as much as frequency.

What isotope is used in most HDR afterloaders?

Iridium-192. From “Brachytherapy” →

In plain English: Brachytherapy places sealed radioactive sources inside or next to a tumour, delivering a high dose locally with a steep fall-off that spares surrounding tissue. Key idea: Brachytherapy's steep 1/r2 fall-off gives dose concentration that external beams struggle to match.

What does DQE measure?

How efficiently a detector preserves the signal-to-noise ratio of the incoming photons. From “Medical detector systems” →

In plain English: Medical detectors convert X-rays, gamma rays and particles into electrical signals. Key idea: Detector choice is a balance of stopping power, light or charge yield, speed and cost.

  1. Which modality shows function rather than anatomy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    PET. PET maps where a metabolic tracer accumulates.

  2. The maximum photon energy from an 80 kV tube is: (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    80 keV. Emax = eV.

  3. Fat has a Hounsfield value of roughly: (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    −100. Fat attenuates slightly less than water.

  4. Detectability of a lesion is best described by: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Contrast-to-noise ratio. CNR combines contrast and noise.

  5. Anti-scatter grids remove photons from: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Compton scattering. Scattered photons blur the image.

  6. Each CT detector reading measures: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A line integral of μ. ln(I0/I) = ∫μ dl.

  7. Mammography uses low kV because: (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Photoelectric contrast between soft tissues is higher. Low energies enhance small differences in Z and density.

  8. MRI position encoding uses: (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Magnetic field gradients. Gradients make the frequency and phase depend on position.

  9. Imaging a thyroid near the skin would best use: (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    12 MHz. Shallow targets allow high frequency for resolution.

Key takeaways

  • Every imaging modality is a physical measurement; choosing one means choosing which tissue property you want to see.
  • Image contrast comes from differences in μ, which depends strongly on Z through the photoelectric effect.
  • CT turns many projections into a map of attenuation coefficients, expressed in Hounsfield units.
  • MRI signal comes from precessing proton magnetisation, and contrast comes from T1, T2 and proton density.
  • Nuclear medicine images physiology: where the tracer goes, and how fast it gets there and leaves.
  • PET's coincidence detection gives electronic collimation, making it far more sensitive than single-photon imaging.

Common mistakes in Medical Physics

  • A sharper image is not always a better one. If noise rises with resolution, the CNR can fall and lesions become harder to see.
  • Raising kV improves penetration but reduces contrast, because the photoelectric effect falls steeply with energy.
  • HU values depend on beam energy and calibration; they are not universal constants of the tissue.
  • T2 is always shorter than or equal to T1; T2* is shorter still.
  • Effective half-life is always shorter than both the physical and biological half-lives.
  • Attenuation correction is essential. Both photons must escape, so the total path through the body matters, and an uncorrected image under-represents deep structures.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Physics of diagnostic imaging
    Diagnostic imaging uses waves and particles to see inside the body without cutting it open.
  2. X-ray production and imaging
    X-rays are produced when fast electrons strike a metal target, creating a continuous bremsstrahlung spectrum topped with characteristic lines.
  3. Computed tomography
    Computed tomography rotates an X-ray source and detector around the patient, measuring attenuation along thousands of lines.
  4. Magnetic resonance imaging
    Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnetic field and radiofrequency pulses to make hydrogen nuclei in the body emit signals.
  5. Nuclear medicine
    Nuclear medicine images and treats disease using radioactive tracers given to the patient.
  6. Positron emission tomography
    Positron emission tomography detects pairs of 511 keV photons from positron annihilation.
  7. Single-photon emission computed tomography
    Single-photon emission computed tomography rotates gamma cameras around the patient to build 3D images from single gamma photons.
  8. Ultrasound imaging
    Ultrasound imaging sends short pulses of high-frequency sound into the body and listens for echoes from tissue boundaries.
  9. Doppler ultrasound
    Doppler ultrasound measures the frequency shift of echoes from moving blood cells to find how fast and in which direction blood flows.
  10. Radiation therapy
    Radiation therapy uses ionising radiation to kill cancer cells by damaging their DNA, while sparing normal tissue as much as possible.
  11. Ionising radiation dosimetry
    Dosimetry measures and calculates how much energy ionising radiation deposits in matter and what that means biologically.
  12. Radiation protection
    Radiation protection keeps exposures to patients, staff and the public as low as reasonably achievable while allowing the benefits of radiation.
  13. Medical accelerators
    Medical linear accelerators accelerate electrons to several MeV using microwaves, then turn them into therapeutic X-ray beams or use them directly.
  14. Brachytherapy
    Brachytherapy places sealed radioactive sources inside or next to a tumour, delivering a high dose locally with a steep fall-off that spares surrounding tissue.
  15. Radiobiological modelling
    Radiobiological models predict how cells, tumours and normal tissues respond to radiation dose, dose per fraction and time.
  16. Image reconstruction
    Image reconstruction turns raw measurements, such as projections, k-space samples or coincidence counts, into images.
  17. Image registration and analysis
    Image registration aligns images from different times, patients or modalities so they can be compared or fused.
  18. Medical detector systems
    Medical detectors convert X-rays, gamma rays and particles into electrical signals.
  19. Optical medical imaging
    Optical imaging uses visible and near-infrared light to see tissue structure and chemistry at high resolution without ionising radiation.
  20. Functional imaging
    Functional imaging measures what tissue is doing rather than what it looks like: blood flow, metabolism, oxygenation, diffusion and neural activity.
  21. Quality assurance in medical physics
    Quality assurance makes sure imaging and therapy equipment performs consistently and safely, so every patient gets the intended image quality or dose.
  22. Radiation safety and clinical risk
    Radiation safety in hospitals combines physics, regulation and human factors to prevent accidents and manage risk.

Medical Physics: questions students ask

What fraction of electron energy in an X-ray tube becomes X-rays?

About 1%; the rest is heat.

What does helical CT mean?

The table moves continuously while the gantry rotates, so the beam traces a spiral through the patient.

What does a 180° refocusing pulse undo?

Dephasing from static field inhomogeneities, recovering a spin echo.

Why are PET photons 511 keV?

Each carries the rest energy of an electron (or positron), mec2 = 511 keV.
