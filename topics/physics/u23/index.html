What are magic numbers?

Numbers of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) giving closed shells and extra stability. From “Nuclear structure” →

In plain English: The nucleus is a tightly bound cluster of protons and neutrons held together by the strong nuclear force.

What sets the range of the nuclear force in Yukawa's theory?

The mass of the exchanged particle (pion): r ≈ ħ/(mc). From “Nuclear forces” →

In plain English: The nuclear force binds protons and neutrons despite the electric repulsion of protons.

What must be conserved in a nuclear reaction equation?

Nucleon number, charge, energy, momentum (and lepton and baryon numbers). From “Nuclear reaction equations” →

In plain English: Nuclear reactions are written like chemical equations, but must conserve mass number, charge, energy and momentum.

What does k = 1 mean?

Each generation has the same number of neutrons: a steady chain reaction. From “Chain reactions” →

In plain English: In a chain reaction, each fission releases neutrons that cause more fissions.

What are the three principles of radiation protection?

Time, distance and shielding (under ALARA). From “Radiation shielding” →

In plain English: Shielding reduces radiation exposure by placing material between the source and people.

Common mistakes in Nuclear Physics

  • The nucleus isn't a static bag of balls; nucleons move rapidly (~0.25c) in quantum orbits.
  • Neutrons are stable inside most nuclei, because decay there would leave the nucleus in a higher energy state.
  • Nuclear "size" depends on what's probed: charge radius and matter radius can differ (neutron skins in heavy nuclei).
  • Binding energy is energy released when a nucleus forms, so more binding means less mass, not more.
  • The mass defect isn't missing matter; the energy was released when the nucleus formed, and mass and energy are equivalent.
  • The nuclear force isn't a fundamental force itself; it's the leftover of the strong force between colour-neutral nucleons, like van der Waals forces between neutral molecules.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Nuclear structure
    The nucleus is a tightly bound cluster of protons and neutrons held together by the strong nuclear force.
  2. Protons, neutrons and nucleons
    Protons and neutrons, together called nucleons, are the building blocks of nuclei.
  3. Nuclear size and density
    Nuclei are a few femtometres across, and their radius grows as the cube root of the number of nucleons.
  4. Nuclear binding energy
    Binding energy is the energy needed to pull a nucleus apart into separate protons and neutrons.
  5. Mass defect
    A nucleus weighs less than the sum of its separate protons and neutrons.
  6. Nuclear forces
    The nuclear force binds protons and neutrons despite the electric repulsion of protons.
  7. Nuclear stability
    Only certain combinations of protons and neutrons are stable.
  8. Radioactive decay
    Unstable nuclei spontaneously transform into more stable ones, emitting radiation.
  9. Alpha, beta and gamma decay
    The three classic types of radioactive decay emit helium nuclei (alpha), electrons or positrons (beta) or high-energy photons (gamma).
  10. Decay chains and half-life
    Many radioactive nuclei decay into products that are themselves radioactive, forming decay chains that end at a stable isotope.
  11. Nuclear reaction equations
    Nuclear reactions are written like chemical equations, but must conserve mass number, charge, energy and momentum.
  12. Nuclear cross-sections
    A cross-section measures how likely a nuclear reaction is, expressed as an effective target area.
  13. Nuclear scattering
    Scattering particles off nuclei reveals nuclear sizes, shapes, forces and excited states.
  14. Nuclear fission
    In fission, a heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 splits into two medium nuclei plus neutrons, releasing about 200 MeV.
  15. Nuclear fusion
    Fusion joins light nuclei into heavier ones, releasing energy because the products are more tightly bound.
  16. Chain reactions
    In a chain reaction, each fission releases neutrons that cause more fissions.
  17. Nuclear reactors
    A nuclear reactor sustains a controlled fission chain reaction, using the heat to make steam and generate electricity.
  18. Neutron moderation
    Fission neutrons are born fast (~2 MeV) but U-235 fissions far more readily with slow (thermal, ~0.025 eV) neutrons.
  19. Nuclear energy and power
    Nuclear power generates about 10% of the world's electricity with very low carbon emissions.
  20. Radiation detection
    Radiation detectors turn invisible ionising radiation into measurable signals by collecting the charge or light it produces.
  21. Radiation shielding
    Shielding reduces radiation exposure by placing material between the source and people.
  22. Nuclear spectroscopy
    Nuclear spectroscopy measures the energies of radiation emitted by nuclei to map their energy levels, spins and shapes.
  23. Nuclear magnetic resonance
    Nuclei with spin behave like tiny magnets that precess in a magnetic field.
  24. Nuclear astrophysics
    Nuclear astrophysics explains how stars shine and how the chemical elements were made.
  25. Nuclear waste and radiation safety
    Nuclear technology produces radioactive waste and exposes people to radiation, so it's governed by strict safety principles.

Nuclear Physics: questions students ask

Why is the neutron's magnetic moment evidence of structure?

A neutral point particle wouldn't have one; it comes from its charged quarks.

Why is nuclear density roughly constant?

The strong force saturates: nucleons interact only with near neighbours, packing like a liquid.

Where does binding energy per nucleon peak?

Near iron and nickel (A ≈ 56–62).

What is the mass defect?

The difference between the mass of separated nucleons and the nucleus.
