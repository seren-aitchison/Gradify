Why does stratospheric temperature increase with height?

Ozone absorbs solar UV, heating it from within. From “Atmospheric structure” →

In plain English: Earth's atmosphere is divided into layers by how temperature changes with height.

Why do solar and terrestrial radiation hardly overlap?

The Sun (~5800 K) emits short wavelengths; Earth (~255–288 K) emits long infrared. From “Solar and terrestrial radiation” →

In plain English: Earth receives short-wavelength radiation from the hot Sun and emits long-wavelength infrared from its cool surface and atmosphere.

Why does supercooled water exist in clouds?

Ice formation needs ice nuclei, which are scarce; pure droplets can stay liquid to −38 °C. From “Cloud formation and microphysics” →

In plain English: Clouds form when rising air cools to its dew point and water vapour condenses on tiny particles.

What restores atmospheric gravity waves?

Buoyancy in a stably stratified atmosphere. From “Atmospheric waves” →

In plain English: The atmosphere supports many kinds of waves, from sound to planetary-scale Rossby waves.

Why is ice–albedo feedback positive?

Melting ice exposes darker surfaces that absorb more sunlight, causing more warming. From “Climate feedback mechanisms” →

In plain English: Feedbacks amplify or dampen the climate's response to a forcing.

Common mistakes in Atmospheric Physics and Climate

  • The thermosphere's high temperature means fast molecules, not much heat: you'd freeze there because there's too little gas to warm you.
  • Pressure differences, not absolute pressure, drive winds; a "high" is only a few per cent above average.
  • Humid air is lighter, not heavier, than dry air at the same temperature and pressure.
  • An atmosphere opaque at a wavelength still emits at it; what escapes comes from the upper layers.
  • Solar variability is too small to explain recent warming; satellite measurements show no upward trend in solar output since the 1980s.
  • Water vapour isn't ignored in climate science; it's included as a feedback amplifying CO2-driven warming.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Atmospheric structure
    Earth's atmosphere is divided into layers by how temperature changes with height.
  2. Atmospheric pressure and temperature
    Atmospheric pressure is the weight of air above per unit area; it falls roughly exponentially with height.
  3. Gas laws in the atmosphere
    Air behaves almost as an ideal gas, so its pressure, density and temperature obey the gas law.
  4. Radiative transfer
    Radiative transfer describes how radiation is absorbed, emitted and scattered as it passes through the atmosphere.
  5. Solar and terrestrial radiation
    Earth receives short-wavelength radiation from the hot Sun and emits long-wavelength infrared from its cool surface and atmosphere.
  6. Greenhouse gases
    Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation, warming the surface.
  7. Atmospheric energy balance
    Earth's climate settles where absorbed sunlight balances emitted infrared.
  8. Convection and atmospheric stability
    Whether a rising parcel of air keeps rising or sinks back depends on how its temperature compares with its surroundings.
  9. Cloud formation and microphysics
    Clouds form when rising air cools to its dew point and water vapour condenses on tiny particles.
  10. Precipitation physics
    Cloud droplets are too small to fall; precipitation requires them to grow a million-fold in volume.
  11. Atmospheric circulation
    The atmosphere moves heat from the tropics to the poles.
  12. Fluid dynamics of the atmosphere
    The atmosphere is a thin, rotating, stratified fluid.
  13. Weather systems
    Weather systems are the moving patterns of pressure, wind, cloud and precipitation that make daily weather: mid-latitude cyclones and fronts, anticyclones, tropical cyclones and thunderstorms.
  14. Atmospheric waves
    The atmosphere supports many kinds of waves, from sound to planetary-scale Rossby waves.
  15. Turbulence and boundary layers
    The atmospheric boundary layer is the lowest ~1–2 km of air, directly influenced by the surface.
  16. Aerosols and atmospheric optics
    Aerosols are tiny particles suspended in air, from sea salt and dust to soot and sulfate.
  17. Climate modelling
    Climate models simulate the physics of the atmosphere, oceans, ice and land on computers, solving equations for fluid motion, radiation and chemistry.
  18. Climate feedback mechanisms
    Feedbacks amplify or dampen the climate's response to a forcing.
  19. Ocean-atmosphere interactions
    The ocean and atmosphere exchange heat, water, momentum and gases, coupling them into one system.
  20. Climate sensitivity
    Equilibrium climate sensitivity is how much global temperature eventually rises for a doubling of atmospheric CO2.
  21. Remote sensing of the atmosphere
    Satellites and ground instruments measure the atmosphere remotely by analysing radiation it emits, absorbs or scatters.
  22. Atmospheric electricity and lightning
    The atmosphere carries a global electric circuit: thunderstorms charge the ionosphere, and current leaks back through fair-weather air.

Atmospheric Physics and Climate: questions students ask

What is the atmospheric scale height?

The height over which pressure falls by a factor of e, about 8 km.

What is potential temperature?

The temperature air would have if brought adiabatically to 1000 hPa.

What is optical depth?

A measure of how much radiation is attenuated along a path: I = I0e−τ.

Why are N2 and O2 not greenhouse gases?

Their vibrations don't change their dipole moment, so they don't absorb infrared.
