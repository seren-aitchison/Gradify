Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a cyclic coordinate?

A coordinate absent from the Lagrangian; its conjugate momentum is conserved. From “Lagrangian mechanics” →

In plain English: Lagrangian mechanics reformulates Newton's laws using energy rather than forces. Key idea: Write T − V in convenient coordinates and the Euler–Lagrange equations give the motion; cyclic coordinates give conservation laws.

What is Hamilton's principal function?

The action S as a function of endpoints, solving the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. From “Hamilton–Jacobi theory” →

In plain English: Hamilton–Jacobi theory seeks a canonical transformation that makes the new Hamiltonian zero, so all new variables are constant. Key idea: Hamilton–Jacobi theory turns mechanics into a wave-like PDE; its solution is the action, linking classical and quantum physics.

How did the Eightfold Way predict the Ω−?

The baryon decuplet had one missing member, whose properties were predicted from SU(3). From “Representation theory” →

In plain English: A representation assigns matrices to group elements so that the group's multiplication is preserved. Key idea: Physical states organise into irreducible representations; their dimensions predict degeneracies and multiplets.

What does Wick rotation do?

It converts real time to imaginary time, turning quantum amplitudes into statistical weights e−SE/ħ. From “Path integrals” →

In plain English: Feynman's path integral says a quantum amplitude is the sum over every possible path, each weighted by eiS/ħ. Key idea: Sum over all paths with phase eiS/ħ; classical physics emerges where the phase is stationary.

What does the Born approximation give?

A scattering amplitude proportional to the Fourier transform of the potential. From “Scattering theory” →

In plain English: Scattering theory describes what happens when particles collide and fly apart. Key idea: Scattering measures cross-sections; theory computes amplitudes via Born, partial waves or the S-matrix.

Key takeaways

  • Write T − V in convenient coordinates and the Euler–Lagrange equations give the motion; cyclic coordinates give conservation laws.
  • Hamilton's equations are first-order in (q, p); Poisson brackets encode dynamics and lead to quantum commutators.
  • Nature makes the action stationary; from this one principle come the laws of motion across physics.
  • Pick generalised coordinates matching the constraints and symmetry; use multipliers if you need constraint forces.
  • Canonical transformations reshape phase space without changing physics; action–angle variables make integrable motion trivial.
  • Hamilton–Jacobi theory turns mechanics into a wave-like PDE; its solution is the action, linking classical and quantum physics.

Common mistakes in Mathematical and Theoretical Physics

  • L = T − V, not T + V; the total energy is a different quantity.
  • H equals total energy only when coordinates are time-independent and the potential doesn't depend on velocity.
  • "Least" action is a misnomer; the action is stationary and may be a saddle point.
  • Rolling without slipping on a plane is non-holonomic; you cannot simply eliminate a coordinate.
  • Not every change of variables is canonical; check that Poisson brackets are preserved.
  • The HJ equation is nonlinear and usually only solvable by separation in special coordinates.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Lagrangian mechanics
    Lagrangian mechanics reformulates Newton's laws using energy rather than forces.
  2. Hamiltonian mechanics
    Hamiltonian mechanics describes motion with coordinates and momenta treated as equal partners.
  3. Principle of least action
    The principle of least (stationary) action says a system follows the path that makes the action S = ∫L dt stationary.
  4. Generalised coordinates
    Generalised coordinates are any set of independent variables that fully specify a system's configuration.
  5. Canonical transformations
    Canonical transformations change phase-space variables (q, p) to new ones (Q, P) while preserving the form of Hamilton's equations.
  6. Hamilton–Jacobi theory
    Hamilton–Jacobi theory seeks a canonical transformation that makes the new Hamiltonian zero, so all new variables are constant.
  7. Symplectic geometry
    Symplectic geometry is the mathematical language of Hamiltonian mechanics.
  8. Noether's theorem
    Noether's theorem states that every continuous symmetry of the action gives a conserved quantity.
  9. Continuous and discrete symmetries
    Symmetries are transformations that leave physical laws unchanged.
  10. Lie groups and Lie algebras
    Lie groups are continuous symmetry groups, like rotations, whose elements depend smoothly on parameters.
  11. Representation theory
    A representation assigns matrices to group elements so that the group's multiplication is preserved.
  12. Tensor calculus
    Tensors are quantities that transform in a definite way under coordinate changes, so equations written with them hold in every coordinate system.
  13. Differential geometry
    Differential geometry studies curves, surfaces and higher-dimensional spaces using calculus.
  14. Manifolds and curvature
    A manifold is a space that looks flat locally but may be curved or twisted globally, like the Earth's surface.
  15. Classical field theory
    Classical field theory treats physical quantities as continuous fields spread through space and time, such as electromagnetic, gravitational and fluid fields.
  16. Quantum field theory
    Quantum field theory combines quantum mechanics and special relativity by treating particles as excitations of underlying fields.
  17. Path integrals
    Feynman's path integral says a quantum amplitude is the sum over every possible path, each weighted by eiS/ħ.
  18. Feynman diagrams
    Feynman diagrams are pictorial shorthand for terms in perturbative calculations of particle interactions.
  19. Renormalisation
    Renormalisation handles infinities that appear in quantum field theory loop calculations by absorbing them into measurable parameters like mass and charge.
  20. Gauge theories
    Gauge theories describe forces arising from local symmetries: demanding that a phase or internal rotation can vary from point to point forces the existence of gauge fields.
  21. Effective field theories
    Effective field theories describe physics at a given energy scale using only the relevant degrees of freedom, with unknown high-energy effects packaged into a few parameters.
  22. Scattering theory
    Scattering theory describes what happens when particles collide and fly apart.
  23. Green's functions
    A Green's function is the response of a linear system to a point source.
  24. Perturbative and non-perturbative methods
    Perturbation theory expands answers in powers of a small parameter, giving most of physics' precise predictions.
  25. Solvable models
    Exactly solvable models are idealised systems whose behaviour can be computed completely.
  26. Integrable systems
    Integrable systems have as many independent conserved quantities as degrees of freedom, so their motion is regular and solvable rather than chaotic.
  27. Mathematical methods for boundary-value problems
    Boundary-value problems ask for solutions of differential equations that satisfy conditions on the edges of a region.

Mathematical and Theoretical Physics: questions students ask

How is the Hamiltonian obtained from the Lagrangian?

By a Legendre transform: H = Σpq̇ − L.

How does quantum mechanics explain the principle of stationary action?

Paths near the stationary one interfere constructively, while others cancel.

What is a holonomic constraint?

A constraint expressible as an equation among coordinates (and time).

What is an adiabatic invariant?

A quantity, such as the action, that stays nearly constant when parameters change slowly.
