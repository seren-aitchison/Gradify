What are orbital elements?

Six numbers describing an orbit's size, shape, orientation and position. From “Celestial mechanics” →

In plain English: Celestial mechanics applies gravity and the laws of motion to planets, moons, stars and spacecraft.

What determines a star's position on the main sequence?

Mainly its mass. From “Main-sequence stars” →

In plain English: Main-sequence stars fuse hydrogen into helium in their cores.

What is a kilonova?

A transient powered by radioactive decay of r-process elements made in a neutron-star merger. From “Neutron star mergers” →

In plain English: When two neutron stars spiral together and merge, they emit gravitational waves, a gamma-ray burst and a glowing kilonova rich in heavy elements.

What is interstellar extinction?

Dimming of starlight by dust absorption and scattering, stronger at shorter wavelengths. From “Cosmic dust” →

In plain English: Cosmic dust is made of tiny solid grains of silicates, carbon and ices that make up about 1% of the interstellar medium's mass.

What did Schmidt discover about 3C 273?

Its spectrum was highly redshifted, implying great distance and enormous luminosity. From “Quasars” →

In plain English: Quasars are the most luminous active galactic nuclei, shining with up to thousands of times the light of a whole galaxy from a region smaller than the Solar System.

Common mistakes in Astrophysics

  • Planets don't orbit the Sun's centre exactly; they orbit the Solar System's centre of mass, which is sometimes outside the Sun.
  • More fuel doesn't mean a longer life: massive stars burn their fuel disproportionately fast.
  • Star formation is inefficient: only a few per cent of a molecular cloud's mass becomes stars.
  • Losing energy makes stars hotter, not cooler: a consequence of gravity's negative heat capacity.
  • The Sun's core power density is low (~280 W m-3); its enormous output comes from its enormous volume.
  • Spectral class tracks temperature, not mass directly: giants and dwarfs can share a spectral type.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Celestial mechanics
    Celestial mechanics applies gravity and the laws of motion to planets, moons, stars and spacecraft.
  2. Stellar structure and evolution
    Stars are balls of plasma held up by pressure against gravity, powered by nuclear fusion in their cores.
  3. Star formation
    Stars form when cold, dense regions of molecular clouds collapse under their own gravity.
  4. Hydrostatic equilibrium in stars
    A stable star balances the inward pull of gravity with the outward push of pressure at every depth.
  5. Nuclear fusion in stars
    Stars shine by fusing light nuclei into heavier ones in their cores.
  6. Stellar spectra and classification
    A star's spectrum reveals its temperature, composition, surface gravity, motion and magnetic field.
  7. Main-sequence stars
    Main-sequence stars fuse hydrogen into helium in their cores.
  8. Red giants and supergiants
    When a star exhausts hydrogen in its core, the core contracts and heats while the outer layers swell enormously and cool.
  9. White dwarfs
    A white dwarf is the hot, dense core left behind when a low- or medium-mass star dies.
  10. Neutron stars
    A neutron star is the collapsed core of a massive star: about 1.4–2 solar masses packed into a sphere ~20–25 km across.
  11. Pulsars
    Pulsars are rotating neutron stars that sweep beams of radiation across Earth like a lighthouse, producing extremely regular pulses.
  12. Black holes
    Black holes are regions where gravity is so strong that nothing escapes.
  13. Supernovae
    Supernovae are stellar explosions briefly outshining entire galaxies.
  14. Neutron star mergers
    When two neutron stars spiral together and merge, they emit gravitational waves, a gamma-ray burst and a glowing kilonova rich in heavy elements.
  15. Binary star systems
    Most stars are in binary or multiple systems.
  16. Accretion discs
    When gas falls towards a star or black hole with angular momentum, it forms a flattened, rotating accretion disc.
  17. Exoplanets
    Exoplanets are planets orbiting other stars.
  18. Planetary systems
    Planetary systems form from discs of gas and dust around young stars.
  19. Interstellar medium
    The interstellar medium (ISM) is the gas, dust, cosmic rays and magnetic fields between the stars.
  20. Molecular clouds
    Molecular clouds are cold, dense regions of the interstellar medium where hydrogen exists as molecules.
  21. Cosmic dust
    Cosmic dust is made of tiny solid grains of silicates, carbon and ices that make up about 1% of the interstellar medium's mass.
  22. Nebulae
    Nebulae are clouds of gas and dust in space, glowing, reflecting or blocking light.
  23. Star clusters
    Star clusters are groups of stars born together from the same cloud, sharing age and composition.
  24. Galaxies and galaxy formation
    Galaxies are vast systems of stars, gas, dust and dark matter bound by gravity.
  25. Galaxy dynamics
    Galaxy dynamics studies how stars and gas move under gravity within galaxies.
  26. Dark matter in galaxies
    Galaxies rotate too fast for the visible matter alone to hold them together.
  27. Active galactic nuclei
    Active galactic nuclei (AGN) are galaxy centres outshining their entire galaxies, powered by gas falling into supermassive black holes.
  28. Quasars
    Quasars are the most luminous active galactic nuclei, shining with up to thousands of times the light of a whole galaxy from a region smaller than the Solar System.
  29. Gravitational lensing
    Mass bends light, so galaxies and clusters act as lenses that magnify, distort and multiply images of objects behind them.
  30. High-energy astrophysics
    High-energy astrophysics studies X-rays, gamma rays, cosmic rays and neutrinos from the most violent places in the universe: black holes, neutron stars, supernovae and gamma-ray bursts.
  31. Cosmic rays
    Cosmic rays are high-energy charged particles, mostly protons and nuclei, arriving from space.
  32. Neutrino astronomy
    Neutrinos pass almost unhindered through matter, so they carry information straight from the cores of stars, supernovae and the most violent cosmic accelerators.
  33. Gravitational-wave astronomy
    Gravitational-wave astronomy observes ripples in spacetime from merging black holes and neutron stars, opening a window on the universe independent of light.
  34. Multi-messenger astronomy
    Multi-messenger astronomy combines light, gravitational waves, neutrinos and cosmic rays from the same event.

Astrophysics: questions students ask

What decides a star's fate?

Mainly its initial mass.

What is the Jeans mass?

The minimum mass a cloud needs for gravity to overcome thermal pressure and collapse.

What balances gravity in a main-sequence star?

The gas (and radiation) pressure gradient.

What are the two main hydrogen-burning processes?

The proton–proton chain and the CNO cycle.
