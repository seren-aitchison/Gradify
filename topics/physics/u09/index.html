Why use stress and strain instead of force and extension?

They describe the material itself, independent of the sample's dimensions. From “Stress and strain” →

In plain English: Stress is the internal force per unit area in a material; strain is the fractional deformation it produces.

Why did Newton's speed of sound in air come out too low?

He assumed isothermal compression; sound is adiabatic, with K = γp. From “Bulk modulus” →

In plain English: The bulk modulus measures resistance to uniform compression: the pressure needed per fractional change in volume.

What does the area of a hysteresis loop represent?

Energy dissipated per cycle as heat. From “Elastic energy” →

In plain English: Work done deforming a material elastically is stored as elastic potential energy, released when it springs back.

Why are real materials weaker than theory predicts?

Flaws and cracks concentrate stress. From “Fracture and failure” →

In plain English: Materials fail by yielding (permanent deformation), fracture (cracking apart), buckling (sudden instability under compression) or fatigue.

What does σij mean?

The i-component of force per unit area on a surface whose normal points along j. From “Stress tensors” →

In plain English: Stress inside a material can't be described by a single number: the force on a small surface depends on the surface's orientation.

Common mistakes in Elasticity and Solid Mechanics

  • Strain has no units; percentages are common (0.05% here).
  • Stiffness (E) isn't strength. Glass is stiff but brittle; rubber is flexible but tough.
  • Shear strain is an angle (in radians, for small values), not a fractional length change.
  • A gas's bulk modulus depends on how it's compressed: isothermally K = p, adiabatically K = γp. Newton got the speed of sound wrong by assuming isothermal.
  • Rubber doesn't obey Hooke's law beyond small strains; its stiffness comes from entropy of tangled polymer chains, not bond stretching.
  • Elastic limit and limit of proportionality are different: a material can be non-linear yet still elastic (rubber).

Lessons in this unit

  1. Stress and strain
    Stress is the internal force per unit area in a material; strain is the fractional deformation it produces.
  2. Young's modulus
    Young's modulus is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear elastic region.
  3. Shear modulus
    The shear (rigidity) modulus relates shear stress to shear strain: how a material resists being skewed.
  4. Bulk modulus
    The bulk modulus measures resistance to uniform compression: the pressure needed per fractional change in volume.
  5. Hooke's law
    Hooke's law states that extension is proportional to the applied force, up to a limit.
  6. Elastic and plastic deformation
    Elastic deformation is reversible: atoms are displaced but return when the load is removed.
  7. Poisson's ratio
    When you stretch a material it usually gets thinner.
  8. Elastic energy
    Work done deforming a material elastically is stored as elastic potential energy, released when it springs back.
  9. Bending and torsion
    When a beam bends, one side stretches and the other compresses, with a neutral axis between.
  10. Beam deflection
    Engineers predict how much beams sag under loads using the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation.
  11. Pressure and material deformation
    Materials under pressure compress, and their structure can change: pressure vessels must contain it safely, rocks deep in the Earth deform plastically, and high-pressure physics creates new phases like metallic hydrogen.
  12. Fracture and failure
    Materials fail by yielding (permanent deformation), fracture (cracking apart), buckling (sudden instability under compression) or fatigue.
  13. Viscoelasticity
    Viscoelastic materials behave partly like elastic solids and partly like viscous fluids: their response depends on how fast and how long you load them.
  14. Crystal elasticity
    In crystals, elastic properties depend on direction because atoms are arranged in regular lattices.
  15. Mechanical waves in solids
    Solids carry several kinds of elastic wave: compressional (P), shear (S) and surface waves.
  16. Stress tensors
    Stress inside a material can't be described by a single number: the force on a small surface depends on the surface's orientation.
  17. Continuum mechanics
    Continuum mechanics treats solids and fluids as continuous media described by fields (density, velocity, stress, strain) instead of individual atoms.
  18. Material stability
    A structure or material is stable if small disturbances die away and unstable if they grow.
  19. Fatigue and fracture mechanics
    Repeated loading well below a material's strength can grow microscopic cracks until a part fails: fatigue causes most mechanical failures.

Elasticity and Solid Mechanics: questions students ask

What does Young's modulus measure?

A material's stiffness in tension: stress per unit strain in the elastic region.

Why can't S-waves travel through liquid?

Liquids have zero shear modulus.

Why do most solids obey Hooke's law for small strains?

Interatomic potentials are approximately quadratic near equilibrium.

What carries plastic deformation in metals?

The movement of dislocations.
