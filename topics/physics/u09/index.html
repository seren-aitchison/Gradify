Lessons in this unit

  1. Stress and strain
    Stress is the internal force per unit area in a material; strain is the fractional deformation it produces.
  2. Young's modulus
    Young's modulus is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear elastic region.
  3. Shear modulus
    The shear (rigidity) modulus relates shear stress to shear strain: how a material resists being skewed.
  4. Bulk modulus
    The bulk modulus measures resistance to uniform compression: the pressure needed per fractional change in volume.
  5. Hooke's law
    Hooke's law states that extension is proportional to the applied force, up to a limit.
  6. Elastic and plastic deformation
    Elastic deformation is reversible: atoms are displaced but return when the load is removed.
  7. Poisson's ratio
    When you stretch a material it usually gets thinner.
  8. Elastic energy
    Work done deforming a material elastically is stored as elastic potential energy, released when it springs back.
  9. Bending and torsion
    When a beam bends, one side stretches and the other compresses, with a neutral axis between.
  10. Beam deflection
    Engineers predict how much beams sag under loads using the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation.
  11. Pressure and material deformation
    Materials under pressure compress, and their structure can change: pressure vessels must contain it safely, rocks deep in the Earth deform plastically, and high-pressure physics creates new phases like metallic hydrogen.
  12. Fracture and failure
    Materials fail by yielding (permanent deformation), fracture (cracking apart), buckling (sudden instability under compression) or fatigue.
  13. Viscoelasticity
    Viscoelastic materials behave partly like elastic solids and partly like viscous fluids: their response depends on how fast and how long you load them.
  14. Crystal elasticity
    In crystals, elastic properties depend on direction because atoms are arranged in regular lattices.
  15. Mechanical waves in solids
    Solids carry several kinds of elastic wave: compressional (P), shear (S) and surface waves.
  16. Stress tensors
    Stress inside a material can't be described by a single number: the force on a small surface depends on the surface's orientation.
  17. Continuum mechanics
    Continuum mechanics treats solids and fluids as continuous media described by fields (density, velocity, stress, strain) instead of individual atoms.
  18. Material stability
    A structure or material is stable if small disturbances die away and unstable if they grow.
  19. Fatigue and fracture mechanics
    Repeated loading well below a material's strength can grow microscopic cracks until a part fails: fatigue causes most mechanical failures.
