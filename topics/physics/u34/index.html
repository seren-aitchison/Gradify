What is kBT at body temperature?

About 4.3 × 10-21 J, or ~4 pN nm. From “Physical principles of living systems” →

In plain English: Living things obey the same physical laws as everything else, but they operate far from equilibrium, at scales where thermal noise matters, using energy flows to build and maintain order.

How do cells drive unfavourable reactions?

By coupling them to favourable ones, usually ATP hydrolysis. From “Thermodynamics of biological systems” →

In plain English: Living systems obey the laws of thermodynamics, using free energy from food or light to drive the reactions that build and maintain them.

What is the Nernst potential?

The membrane voltage at which an ion's diffusive and electrical driving forces balance. From “Electrophysiology” →

In plain English: Electrophysiology studies electrical activity in cells and tissues: membrane potentials, ion currents, nerve impulses and heartbeats.

What is mechanotransduction?

The process by which cells sense and respond to mechanical forces. From “Cellular mechanics” →

In plain English: Cells are mechanical objects: they have shape, stiffness and the ability to move, divide and exert forces.

What is GFP?

Green fluorescent protein from jellyfish, used to tag proteins genetically. From “Fluorescence microscopy” →

In plain English: Fluorescence microscopy labels molecules with fluorescent tags and images them with high contrast.

Common mistakes in Biophysics

  • Life doesn't violate the second law; it decreases entropy locally by increasing it more in the environment.
  • Muscles are weak in mechanical advantage but fast; the body trades force for speed and range of motion.
  • Blood flow in large arteries is pulsatile and not exactly Poiseuille; the r4 rule is still a good guide for small vessels.
  • Diffusion doesn't "push" molecules in a direction; net flux arises statistically from concentration differences.
  • Osmosis moves water, not solute; water flows towards higher solute concentration.
  • Metabolic "calories" are kilocalories: a food Calorie = 4.184 kJ.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Physical principles of living systems
    Living things obey the same physical laws as everything else, but they operate far from equilibrium, at scales where thermal noise matters, using energy flows to build and maintain order.
  2. Biomechanics
    Biomechanics applies mechanics to living bodies: how bones carry loads, muscles produce force, joints move and animals walk, run, swim and fly.
  3. Biological fluid dynamics
    Fluids flow throughout living things: blood in vessels, air in lungs, water around swimming cells and fish.
  4. Diffusion and transport
    Diffusion, the random spreading of molecules, moves substances within cells and across short distances in the body.
  5. Osmosis and membrane transport
    Cell membranes control what enters and leaves cells.
  6. Thermodynamics of biological systems
    Living systems obey the laws of thermodynamics, using free energy from food or light to drive the reactions that build and maintain them.
  7. Molecular motors
    Molecular motors are proteins that turn chemical energy into mechanical work, walking along tracks, spinning or contracting.
  8. Protein dynamics
    Proteins aren't rigid: they fold, flex, breathe and change shape to function.
  9. DNA mechanics
    DNA is a long, stiff-but-flexible polymer storing genetic information.
  10. Membrane biophysics
    Cell membranes are thin, fluid sheets of lipids with embedded proteins.
  11. Electrophysiology
    Electrophysiology studies electrical activity in cells and tissues: membrane potentials, ion currents, nerve impulses and heartbeats.
  12. Ion channels
    Ion channels are protein pores that let specific ions cross membranes rapidly, opening and closing in response to voltage, chemicals or force.
  13. Nerve impulses and action potentials
    Neurons send signals as action potentials: brief, all-or-nothing spikes of membrane voltage that travel along axons.
  14. Neural signal processing
    Neurons combine thousands of inputs, transform them and encode information in spike patterns.
  15. Muscle contraction
    Muscles contract when myosin motors pull on actin filaments, sliding them past each other.
  16. Cellular mechanics
    Cells are mechanical objects: they have shape, stiffness and the ability to move, divide and exert forces.
  17. Tissue mechanics
    Tissues combine cells and extracellular matrix into materials with remarkable mechanical properties, from soft brain to stiff bone.
  18. Medical imaging physics
    Medical imaging uses physics to see inside the body without surgery: X-rays and CT for structure, MRI for soft tissue, ultrasound for real-time images, and nuclear medicine for function.
  19. Radiation biology
    Radiation biology studies how ionising radiation damages cells and tissues.
  20. Optical microscopy
    Optical microscopes magnify small structures using lenses and visible light.
  21. Fluorescence microscopy
    Fluorescence microscopy labels molecules with fluorescent tags and images them with high contrast.
  22. Single-molecule methods
    Single-molecule techniques watch and manipulate individual biomolecules rather than averages over billions.
  23. Biological networks
    Cells process information through networks of interacting genes, proteins and metabolites.
  24. Statistical physics of living systems
    Statistical physics helps explain how collective behaviour in biology emerges from many interacting parts: flocks of birds, bacterial colonies, protein folding, neural activity and evolution itself.
  25. Computational biophysics
    Computational biophysics uses simulations and data analysis to study biological molecules and systems, from atomic motions of proteins to whole-cell models.

Biophysics: questions students ask

Why are most human limb levers third-class?

Muscles attach close to joints, giving speed and range at the cost of force.

Why does arterial narrowing matter so much?

Flow depends on the fourth power of radius.

Why do large organisms need circulatory systems?

Diffusion is far too slow over large distances (time ∝ distance squared).

What is osmotic pressure?

The pressure needed to stop water flowing across a semipermeable membrane into a more concentrated solution.
