State the weak equivalence principle.

All bodies fall with the same acceleration in a gravitational field, regardless of their composition. From “Equivalence principle” →

In plain English: The equivalence principle says that, locally, the effects of gravity are indistinguishable from those of acceleration.

What is the photon sphere?

The radius (1.5rs for a non-rotating black hole) where light can orbit. From “Geodesics” →

In plain English: A geodesic is the straightest possible path in curved spacetime.

What is the ergosphere?

The region outside a rotating black hole's horizon where nothing can remain stationary relative to distant stars. From “Kerr black holes” →

In plain English: The Kerr solution describes a rotating black hole, defined by just mass and spin.

What is the Shapiro delay?

The extra time for signals passing near a massive body due to spacetime curvature. From “Light bending and gravitational lensing” →

In plain English: Light follows curved paths near massive objects.

What does the FLRW metric assume?

A homogeneous, isotropic universe (the cosmological principle). From “Cosmological solutions” →

In plain English: Applying general relativity to the whole universe, assuming it's uniform on large scales, gives expanding or contracting models.

Common mistakes in General Relativity

  • The equivalence is only local: over large regions tidal forces (differences in gravity) reveal true curvature.
  • Special relativity handles acceleration perfectly well; general relativity is needed for gravity (curved spacetime), not for acceleration itself.
  • The "rubber sheet" picture of curved space shows spatial curvature, but in reality it's mostly time curvature that makes things fall.
  • The metric's components depend on coordinates; curvature (computed from them) is what's physically meaningful.
  • Geodesics in spacetime aren't geodesics in space; a straight spacetime path can look like an orbit in space.
  • Curvature isn't the same as non-zero Christoffel symbols; those can appear in flat space with curvilinear coordinates.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Equivalence principle
    The equivalence principle says that, locally, the effects of gravity are indistinguishable from those of acceleration.
  2. Accelerated reference frames
    Accelerated frames experience inertial forces that mimic gravity.
  3. Gravity as spacetime curvature
    General relativity describes gravity not as a force but as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
  4. Metric tensors
    The metric tensor defines distances and times in spacetime, determining its geometry.
  5. Geodesics
    A geodesic is the straightest possible path in curved spacetime.
  6. Curvature and the Riemann tensor
    The Riemann curvature tensor measures how spacetime is curved: how much a vector changes when carried around a small loop, and how nearby geodesics converge or diverge.
  7. Einstein field equations
    Einstein's field equations relate spacetime curvature to the energy and momentum within it.
  8. Stress-energy tensor
    The stress–energy tensor describes the density and flow of energy and momentum in spacetime.
  9. Schwarzschild solution
    The Schwarzschild solution describes spacetime outside a spherical, non-rotating, uncharged mass.
  10. Kerr black holes
    The Kerr solution describes a rotating black hole, defined by just mass and spin.
  11. Gravitational time dilation
    Clocks deeper in a gravitational potential run slower than clocks higher up.
  12. Gravitational redshift
    Light climbing out of a gravitational well loses energy and is shifted to longer wavelengths.
  13. Perihelion precession
    General relativity predicts that orbits around a massive body aren't closed ellipses: the point of closest approach slowly advances.
  14. Light bending and gravitational lensing
    Light follows curved paths near massive objects.
  15. Black holes and event horizons
    A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.
  16. Singularities
    Singularities are places where general relativity predicts infinite curvature and the theory breaks down.
  17. Gravitational waves
    Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature that travel at the speed of light, produced by accelerating masses.
  18. Binary systems and gravitational radiation
    Two compact objects orbiting each other emit gravitational waves, losing energy and spiralling together.
  19. Cosmological solutions
    Applying general relativity to the whole universe, assuming it's uniform on large scales, gives expanding or contracting models.
  20. Friedmann equations
    The Friedmann equations govern how the universe's expansion rate changes over time, given its contents: matter, radiation, dark energy and curvature.
  21. Tests of general relativity
    General relativity has passed every experimental test for over a century, from the solar system to merging black holes.
  22. Numerical relativity
    Numerical relativity solves Einstein's equations on supercomputers when no exact solution exists, most famously for merging black holes and neutron stars.
  23. Quantum gravity challenges
    Quantum mechanics and general relativity are both spectacularly successful but don't fit together.

General Relativity: questions students ask

Why must light bend in gravity, by the equivalence principle?

In an accelerating frame light appears to curve; by equivalence it must curve in gravity too.

What is the Sagnac effect?

A time difference for light travelling opposite ways round a rotating loop, used in optical gyroscopes.

How does general relativity describe gravity?

As the curvature of spacetime by mass and energy, with free particles following geodesics.

What does the metric tensor determine?

Spacetime intervals: distances, times and the geometry of spacetime.
