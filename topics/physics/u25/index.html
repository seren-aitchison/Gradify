Key terms and formulas at a glance

x′ = x − vt; y′ = y; z′ = z; t′ = t
u′ = u − v
velocity addition
x′ = γ(x − vt); t′ = γ(t − vx/c2); y′ = y; z′ = z
γ = 1/√(1 − v2/c2)
x = γ(x′ + vt′); t = γ(t′ + vx′/c2)
inverse
v/c = tanh φ; γ = cosh φ
s2 = (cΔt)2 − Δx2 − Δy2 − Δz2
Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c2)

Why were Maxwell's equations a problem for Newtonian mechanics?

They aren't invariant under Galilean transformations and imply a fixed speed of light. From “Historical foundations of relativity” →

In plain English: By 1900, Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism clashed: Maxwell's equations gave light a fixed speed, but in which frame?

How is the metre defined?

The distance light travels in vacuum in 1/299 792 458 of a second. From “Speed of light invariance” →

In plain English: The speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer, whatever their motion or the source's motion.

What do you get adding v to the speed of light?

c. From “Relativistic velocity addition” →

In plain English: Velocities don't simply add in relativity.

What is relativistic momentum?

p = γmv. From “Relativistic momentum” →

In plain English: Momentum in relativity is p = γmv, which grows without limit as an object approaches c.

What is the transverse Doppler effect?

A redshift from time dilation of a source moving perpendicular to the line of sight. From “Relativistic Doppler effect” →

In plain English: The relativistic Doppler effect is the change in frequency of light from a moving source, including time dilation.

Common mistakes in Special Relativity

  • Einstein didn't need the Michelson–Morley result specifically; his reasoning started from the symmetry of electromagnetism.
  • Galilean relativity isn't "wrong" for everyday use; its errors are far below anything measurable at normal speeds.
  • "Observing" in relativity isn't seeing; seeing includes light-travel delays, which produce different visual effects from length contraction (Terrell rotation).
  • Relativity doesn't say "everything is relative": c, the spacetime interval, proper time and physical laws are absolute.
  • Light slows in materials (v = c/n), but c, the vacuum speed, is the invariant one.
  • The vx/c2 term in t′ is easy to forget; it's the source of the relativity of simultaneity.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Historical foundations of relativity
    By 1900, Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism clashed: Maxwell's equations gave light a fixed speed, but in which frame?
  2. Galilean transformations
    Galilean transformations convert positions and times between frames moving at constant velocity, assuming time is absolute.
  3. Inertial reference frames
    An inertial frame is one in which a free object moves in a straight line at constant speed.
  4. Postulates of special relativity
    Special relativity rests on two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer.
  5. Speed of light invariance
    The speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer, whatever their motion or the source's motion.
  6. Lorentz transformations
    The Lorentz transformations convert space and time coordinates between inertial frames moving relative to each other, keeping the speed of light the same in both.
  7. Relativity of simultaneity
    Events that happen at the same time in one inertial frame generally happen at different times in another.
  8. Time dilation
    A clock moving relative to you ticks more slowly than one at rest beside you, by the factor γ.
  9. Length contraction
    An object moving relative to you is shorter along its direction of motion than when measured at rest, by the factor γ.
  10. Relativistic velocity addition
    Velocities don't simply add in relativity.
  11. Spacetime diagrams
    Spacetime diagrams plot time against position, showing events as points and objects' histories as worldlines.
  12. Minkowski spacetime
    Minkowski spacetime is the four-dimensional geometry of special relativity, combining three dimensions of space and one of time.
  13. Proper time
    Proper time is the time measured by a clock carried along a worldline.
  14. Four-vectors
    Four-vectors combine a time-like component and three space components into objects that transform by Lorentz transformations.
  15. Relativistic momentum
    Momentum in relativity is p = γmv, which grows without limit as an object approaches c.
  16. Mass-energy equivalence
    Mass and energy are equivalent: any system's rest mass corresponds to an energy E = mc2.
  17. Relativistic energy
    A moving object's total energy is its rest energy multiplied by γ.
  18. Energy-momentum relation
    The energy–momentum relation links a particle's total energy, momentum and mass, valid at any speed.
  19. Relativistic collisions
    In high-energy collisions, energy and momentum are conserved as four-vectors, but rest mass isn't: kinetic energy can turn into new particles.
  20. Relativistic Doppler effect
    The relativistic Doppler effect is the change in frequency of light from a moving source, including time dilation.
  21. Relativistic electromagnetism
    Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of one electromagnetic field that transforms between frames.
  22. Causality and light cones
    The light cone of an event divides spacetime into its causal future, causal past and the elsewhere region it can never influence.
  23. Twin paradox
    One twin travels to a distant star at high speed and returns younger than the twin who stayed home.
  24. Relativistic particle dynamics
    Relativistic dynamics describes how forces change momentum and energy at any speed.

Special Relativity: questions students ask

What does the Galilean transformation assume about time?

That it's absolute: t′ = t.

What is an inertial frame?

One in which free objects move in straight lines at constant speed.

What does the second postulate imply about simultaneity?

Events simultaneous in one frame need not be in another.

What evidence shows light's speed is independent of the source's motion?

Pion decay photons, binary star observations and modern cavity experiments.
