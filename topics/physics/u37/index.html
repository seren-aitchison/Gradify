Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What normalisation condition must a qubit's amplitudes satisfy?

|α|2 + |β|2 = 1. From “Qubits and quantum states” →

In plain English: A qubit is the quantum version of a bit: a two-level system that can be in 0, 1 or any superposition of both. Key idea: A qubit's power is not that it "is both 0 and 1", but that complex amplitudes can interfere across an exponentially large space.

Write the Bell state |Φ+⟩.

(|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. From “Entanglement” →

In plain English: Entanglement is a correlation between quantum systems so strong that the state of the whole cannot be described by states of the parts. Key idea: Entangled states are wholes that cannot be split into parts; each part alone looks random.

What is amplitude amplification?

Repeatedly boosting the amplitude of marked states, the technique behind Grover's algorithm. From “Quantum algorithms” →

In plain English: Quantum algorithms use superposition, interference and entanglement to solve certain problems faster than any known classical method. Key idea: Quantum algorithms win by engineering interference, and only for problems with the right structure.

What is randomised benchmarking?

Running random Clifford sequences of increasing length to extract average gate fidelity. From “Quantum noise and decoherence” →

In plain English: Decoherence is the loss of quantum behaviour as a qubit becomes entangled with its environment. Key idea: Decoherence is entanglement with the environment; T1 and T2 time-limit every computation.

What is entanglement purification?

Using several imperfect entangled pairs to distil fewer higher-fidelity pairs. From “Quantum networks” →

In plain English: Quantum networks connect quantum processors and sensors by distributing entanglement between nodes. Key idea: Quantum networks distribute entanglement, using swapping and purification to build long-distance links from short ones.

Key takeaways

  • A qubit's power is not that it "is both 0 and 1", but that complex amplitudes can interfere across an exponentially large space.
  • Every pure qubit state is a point on the Bloch sphere; every single-qubit gate is a rotation of it.
  • Quantum gates are reversible unitary operations; single-qubit rotations plus CNOT are enough to build anything.
  • Circuits make quantum algorithms concrete; depth and two-qubit gate counts decide whether today's hardware can run them.
  • Measurement gives probabilistic classical outcomes and disturbs the state; choose the basis to reveal what you need.
  • Entangled states are wholes that cannot be split into parts; each part alone looks random.

Common mistakes in Quantum Information and Quantum Computing

  • You cannot read out all 2n amplitudes; measurement gives only n bits. Algorithms must use interference to make the right answer likely.
  • Orthogonal states are opposite on the sphere, at 180°, not at 90° as in ordinary vector space.
  • Clifford circuits alone, however large, offer no quantum speed-up; non-Clifford gates like T are needed.
  • Mathematically, a circuit A then B is the operator BA, written in reverse order.
  • Measuring a qubit in superposition does not reveal the amplitudes; it gives a single outcome each time.
  • Entanglement cannot transmit information faster than light; classical communication is still needed.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Qubits and quantum states
    A qubit is the quantum version of a bit: a two-level system that can be in 0, 1 or any superposition of both.
  2. Bloch sphere
    The Bloch sphere represents any pure single-qubit state as a point on a unit sphere.
  3. Quantum gates
    Quantum gates are unitary operations that transform qubit states reversibly.
  4. Quantum circuits
    Quantum circuits are the standard language for describing quantum computations: qubits are wires, gates act on them in time order from left to right, and measurements read out results.
  5. Quantum measurement
    Measurement is how quantum information becomes classical.
  6. Entanglement
    Entanglement is a correlation between quantum systems so strong that the state of the whole cannot be described by states of the parts.
  7. Bell inequalities
    Bell inequalities test whether nature can be explained by local hidden variables, pre-existing values with no faster-than-light influences.
  8. Quantum teleportation
    Quantum teleportation transfers an unknown quantum state from one place to another using a shared entangled pair and two bits of classical communication.
  9. Quantum cryptography
    Quantum cryptography uses the laws of physics, rather than the difficulty of maths problems, to secure communication.
  10. Quantum key distribution
    Quantum key distribution (QKD) lets two parties share a secret random key over an insecure channel, with any eavesdropping revealed by errors.
  11. Quantum algorithms
    Quantum algorithms use superposition, interference and entanglement to solve certain problems faster than any known classical method.
  12. Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm
    The Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm decides with a single query whether a function is constant or balanced, where a deterministic classical algorithm may need exponentially many queries.
  13. Grover's algorithm
    Grover's algorithm searches an unstructured list of N items for a marked one using about √N queries instead of N.
  14. Shor's algorithm
    Shor's algorithm factors large integers in polynomial time on a quantum computer, exponentially faster than the best known classical algorithms.
  15. Quantum error correction
    Quantum error correction protects fragile quantum information by spreading one logical qubit across many physical qubits.
  16. Quantum noise and decoherence
    Decoherence is the loss of quantum behaviour as a qubit becomes entangled with its environment.
  17. Fault-tolerant quantum computing
    Fault-tolerant quantum computing performs arbitrarily long computations reliably using error-corrected logical qubits, even though every physical component is imperfect.
  18. Quantum complexity
    Quantum complexity theory classifies problems by the resources a quantum computer needs to solve them.
  19. Quantum simulation
    Quantum simulation uses controllable quantum systems to model other quantum systems, such as molecules, materials and field theories, that are exponentially hard for classical computers.
  20. Quantum communication
    Quantum communication sends quantum states between distant parties, enabling secure key distribution, teleportation and distributed quantum computing.
  21. Quantum networks
    Quantum networks connect quantum processors and sensors by distributing entanglement between nodes.
  22. Physical implementations of qubits
    Many physical systems can act as qubits, each with distinct strengths in coherence, speed, connectivity and scalability.
  23. Superconducting qubits
    Superconducting qubits are tiny electrical circuits cooled near absolute zero that behave like artificial atoms.
  24. Trapped-ion systems
    Trapped-ion quantum computers store qubits in the internal states of individual ions held by electromagnetic fields in vacuum.
  25. Neutral-atom quantum computing
    Neutral-atom quantum computers hold individual atoms in arrays of tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers.
  26. Photonic quantum computing
    Photonic quantum computing encodes qubits in single photons or squeezed light, which travel at the speed of light, barely decohere and work at room temperature.

Quantum Information and Quantum Computing: questions students ask

Where do mixed states lie?

Inside the sphere; the maximally mixed state is at the centre.

What does CNOT do?

It flips the target qubit if and only if the control qubit is |1⟩.

What is phase kickback?

When a controlled operation's target is an eigenstate, the eigenvalue's phase appears on the control qubit.

How do you measure a qubit in the X basis on hardware that measures Z?

Apply a Hadamard first, then measure in Z.
