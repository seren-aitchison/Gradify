What is the Feynman technique?

Explaining a concept simply to reveal gaps in your own understanding, then studying to fill them. From “Explaining physical concepts clearly” →

In plain English: Explaining physics clearly means connecting abstract ideas to what the listener already knows, using precise language, concrete examples and well-chosen analogies. Key idea: Clear explanation starts from the audience, uses examples and precise language, and acknowledges where analogies fail.

What is the first step in modelling?

Understanding the situation and identifying what's being asked. From “Translating physical situations into mathematical models” →

In plain English: Modelling turns a real situation into mathematics: deciding what matters, what can be neglected, which laws apply and which variables describe the system. Key idea: Modelling means idealising wisely, applying laws, solving and validating; compare effect sizes to decide what to neglect.

How do you find the exponent of a power law from data?

Plot log y against log x; the gradient is the exponent. From “Interpreting graphs and experimental data” →

In plain English: Graphs reveal relationships hidden in tables of numbers. Key idea: Gradients and areas carry physical meaning; linearise data to extract constants and quantify uncertainties.

What is a preprint?

A paper shared publicly (e.g. on arXiv) before formal peer review. From “Peer review and research critique” →

In plain English: Peer review is how scientists evaluate each other's work before publication, checking methods, analysis and conclusions. Key idea: Peer review evaluates methods, evidence and conclusions; constructive critique strengthens science, while replication guards against errors peer review misses.

Why is non-ionising radiation unable to damage DNA directly?

Its photons have too little energy to break chemical bonds or ionise atoms. From “Science communication and misinformation” →

In plain English: Misinformation about physics, such as claims about radiation, 5G, climate or free energy, spreads quickly online. Key idea: Counter misinformation with clear physics, prebunking, truth sandwiches and respect for audiences.

Key takeaways

  • Clear explanation starts from the audience, uses examples and precise language, and acknowledges where analogies fail.
  • Diagrams encode physics visually; correct, labelled, uncluttered diagrams make reasoning easier and errors obvious.
  • Start from fundamental laws, state assumptions, build step by step, then check units and limits.
  • Modelling means idealising wisely, applying laws, solving and validating; compare effect sizes to decide what to neglect.
  • Break problems into estimable factors, use powers of ten, and check that the answer is sensible.
  • Break complex problems into steps, choose the right principle for each, solve symbolically, then check.

Common mistakes in Physics Education and Communication

  • Simplifying is good; saying something false is not. Avoid explanations that create misconceptions to be unlearned later.
  • Don't include forces that don't exist (like a separate "centripetal force" alongside tension); centripetal force is the net inward force.
  • Every derived formula has assumptions; using it outside them gives wrong answers.
  • A model is only as good as its assumptions; always check whether neglected effects really are small.
  • Estimation is about the order of magnitude, not precision; state that results are rough.
  • Mixing up which principle applies where (e.g. using energy conservation through an inelastic collision) is a common error.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Explaining physical concepts clearly
    Explaining physics clearly means connecting abstract ideas to what the listener already knows, using precise language, concrete examples and well-chosen analogies.
  2. Scientific diagrams and representations
    Diagrams turn physical situations into visual models: free-body diagrams, field lines, ray diagrams, circuit diagrams, energy-level diagrams and graphs.
  3. Deriving equations from first principles
    Deriving equations from first principles means starting with fundamental laws and logically building to a result.
  4. Translating physical situations into mathematical models
    Modelling turns a real situation into mathematics: deciding what matters, what can be neglected, which laws apply and which variables describe the system.
  5. Estimation and order-of-magnitude reasoning
    Order-of-magnitude estimation gives quick, approximate answers using rough numbers and simple physics.
  6. Solving multi-step problems
    Real physics problems usually need several steps, combining principles in sequence.
  7. Interpreting graphs and experimental data
    Graphs reveal relationships hidden in tables of numbers.
  8. Demonstrations and practical investigations
    Demonstrations and hands-on investigations make physics tangible, letting learners see principles in action and practise scientific methods.
  9. Laboratory report writing
    A laboratory report communicates what you did, what you found and what it means, clearly enough that someone else could reproduce and judge your work.
  10. Scientific presentations
    Scientific presentations, from class talks to conference posters, communicate results to audiences quickly.
  11. Peer review and research critique
    Peer review is how scientists evaluate each other's work before publication, checking methods, analysis and conclusions.
  12. Communicating uncertainty
    Every measurement and prediction has uncertainty, and communicating it well is essential for honest science and good decisions.
  13. Public engagement with physics
    Public engagement shares physics with society through talks, festivals, museums, media and citizen science, and listens to public concerns.
  14. Science communication and misinformation
    Misinformation about physics, such as claims about radiation, 5G, climate or free energy, spreads quickly online.
  15. Teaching and assessing conceptual understanding
    Understanding physics means more than plugging numbers into formulas: it means grasping concepts deeply enough to explain and apply them in new situations.
  16. Identifying misconceptions
    Learners bring intuitive ideas about the physical world, many of them reasonable but incorrect.
  17. Interdisciplinary communication
    Physicists increasingly work with biologists, engineers, clinicians, economists, data scientists and policymakers.

Physics Education and Communication: questions students ask

What goes on a free-body diagram?

Only the forces acting on the chosen object, each from a real interaction.

What assumption gives the simple pendulum formula?

Small angles, so sin θ ≈ θ.

What is a Fermi problem?

An estimation problem solved by breaking it into rough, guessable factors.

Why solve symbolically before substituting numbers?

It reduces arithmetic errors, shows structure and allows checking units and limits.
