Common mistakes in Electromagnetic Waves

  • EM waves don't need a medium; the 19th-century "luminiferous ether" was abandoned after Michelson–Morley and special relativity.
  • "Light-year" is a distance, not a time.
  • B in a wave is numerically small in tesla, but its energy density equals E's: don't conclude that the magnetic part is negligible.
  • The wave equation in vacuum is non-dispersive: all frequencies travel at c.
  • All EM waves travel at c in vacuum; only frequency and wavelength differ.
  • Radio waves aren't sound; they're EM waves that carry sound information after demodulation.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Electromagnetic wave propagation
    Electromagnetic waves are self-sustaining oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that travel through space at the speed of light.
  2. Speed of light
    The speed of light in vacuum, c = 299 792 458 m s-1, is a universal constant: the same for all observers and the maximum speed for any signal.
  3. Electric and magnetic field relationships
    In an electromagnetic wave, the electric and magnetic fields are locked together: perpendicular to each other and the direction of travel, in phase, and with sizes in the fixed ratio E/B = c.
  4. Electromagnetic wave equation
    Combining Maxwell's equations in empty space gives a wave equation for E and B, with wave speed 1/√(μ0ε0) = c.
  5. Electromagnetic spectrum
    Electromagnetic waves range from radio waves kilometres long to gamma rays smaller than nuclei.
  6. Radio waves
    Radio waves have the longest wavelengths in the EM spectrum.
  7. Microwaves
    Microwaves span wavelengths from about 1 mm to 1 m.
  8. Infrared radiation
    Infrared radiation lies between visible light and microwaves.
  9. Visible light
    Visible light is the narrow band of EM wavelengths, about 400–700 nm, that human eyes detect.
  10. Ultraviolet radiation
    Ultraviolet radiation has wavelengths shorter than violet light, 10–400 nm.
  11. X-rays
    X-rays are high-energy photons (about 100 eV to 100 keV) produced by decelerating electrons and by inner-shell electron transitions.
  12. Gamma rays
    Gamma rays are the most energetic photons, emitted by atomic nuclei, particle annihilations and the most violent events in the universe.
  13. Polarisation
    The polarisation of an electromagnetic wave is the direction in which its electric field oscillates.
  14. Reflection and transmission
    When an EM wave meets a boundary, part reflects and part transmits.
  15. Refraction
    Refraction bends light as it crosses into a medium of different refractive index, because its speed changes.
  16. Dispersion
    Dispersion is the variation of a medium's refractive index with frequency.
  17. Absorption
    Matter absorbs electromagnetic waves when their frequency matches a way for the material to take up energy: electronic transitions, molecular vibrations and rotations, or free charges.
  18. Electromagnetic wave intensity
    The intensity of an electromagnetic wave is the average power it carries per unit area.
  19. Poynting vector
    The Poynting vector S = E × B/μ0 describes the direction and rate of electromagnetic energy flow.
  20. Radiation pressure
    Electromagnetic waves carry momentum, so they exert pressure on surfaces that absorb or reflect them.
  21. Waveguides
    A waveguide is a structure that confines and guides electromagnetic waves: hollow metal pipes for microwaves, optical fibres for light.
  22. Antennas and radiation patterns
    An antenna converts electrical signals into electromagnetic waves and back.
  23. Near-field and far-field behaviour
    Close to an antenna or source, electromagnetic fields store energy and fall off rapidly; far away, they form radiating waves that fall as 1/r.
  24. Electromagnetic interference
    Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is unwanted electromagnetic energy disrupting electronic devices.
  25. Electromagnetic shielding
    Shielding blocks electromagnetic fields from entering or leaving a region.
  26. Microwave engineering
    Microwave engineering designs circuits and systems at frequencies (~300 MHz–300 GHz) where wavelengths are comparable to component sizes.
  27. Radio-frequency physics
    Radio-frequency (RF) physics covers electromagnetic phenomena from kHz to GHz: how RF fields interact with matter, plasmas and biological tissue, and how they're generated, transmitted and detected.

Electromagnetic Waves: questions students ask

Why is the speed of light exact?

Since 1983 the metre has been defined from it.

How are E and B related in an EM wave in vacuum?

Perpendicular, in phase, with E = cB.

Why do AM/shortwave signals reflect off the ionosphere while FM doesn't?

Frequencies below the plasma frequency reflect; FM frequencies are above it.

Why must X-ray telescopes be in space?

The atmosphere absorbs X-rays.
