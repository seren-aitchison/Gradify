Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the three-pass approach to reading papers?

Skim for relevance, read for understanding of results, then study methods in depth. From “Reading and evaluating scientific papers” →

In plain English: Reading research papers efficiently and critically is a core professional skill. Key idea: Read papers in passes, extract claims and evidence, and evaluate critically for significance, systematics and reproducibility.

What is a confounding variable?

An uncontrolled factor that affects the outcome and could mimic or hide the effect studied. From “Designing experiments” →

In plain English: Experimental design determines whether a study can answer its question convincingly. Key idea: Design experiments to control variables, quantify and minimise uncertainties, avoid bias and fit available resources.

What are the three main forms of research misconduct?

Fabrication, falsification and plagiarism. From “Research ethics and integrity” →

In plain English: Research integrity means honesty, rigour, transparency and accountability in how research is done and reported. Key idea: Integrity means honest, rigorous, transparent research; misconduct and questionable practices damage science and trust.

What should a poster's title achieve?

Be readable from a distance and convey the main point. From “Research presentations and posters” →

In plain English: Presenting research at group meetings, conferences and seminars builds your reputation, invites feedback and spreads ideas. Key idea: Tailor talks to the audience, tell one clear story, design visuals for readability and handle questions honestly.

What is the critical path?

The longest sequence of dependent tasks, which determines the minimum project duration. From “Project management” →

In plain English: Project management keeps research on track: defining goals, planning tasks, managing time, resources and risks, and communicating progress. Key idea: Plan scope, tasks, schedule, resources and risks; monitor progress and adapt, with contingency for research uncertainty.

Key takeaways

  • Read papers in passes, extract claims and evidence, and evaluate critically for significance, systematics and reproducibility.
  • Search systematically with databases, keywords and citation chaining; systematic reviews and meta-analyses combine evidence transparently.
  • Good research questions are specific, answerable, significant, feasible and original; refine them through literature and discussion.
  • Models connect assumptions to predictions; develop them iteratively, test against data and state their validity.
  • Design experiments to control variables, quantify and minimise uncertainties, avoid bias and fit available resources.
  • A strong proposal has a clear significant question, credible methods with preliminary evidence, a realistic plan and identified risks.

Common mistakes in Research and Professional Physics Skills

  • A published paper isn't automatically correct; independent confirmation matters.
  • Searching only one database or recent papers can miss key work; document your search so others can repeat it.
  • Questions that are too broad lead to unfocused projects; too narrow ones may lack significance.
  • Too many free parameters can fit anything; a good model predicts beyond the data it was tuned on.
  • An experiment without sufficient statistical power can't detect the effect it seeks, wasting effort.
  • Vague proposals ("we will study X") fail; reviewers need specific goals and evidence of feasibility.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Reading and evaluating scientific papers
    Reading research papers efficiently and critically is a core professional skill.
  2. Literature searches and systematic reviews
    A literature search finds what is already known before starting research, avoiding duplication and building on others' work.
  3. Formulating research questions
    A good research question is specific, answerable, significant and feasible.
  4. Developing theoretical models
    Theoretical models explain observations and make predictions using physical principles and mathematics.
  5. Designing experiments
    Experimental design determines whether a study can answer its question convincingly.
  6. Writing research proposals
    A research proposal persuades others that a project is worth doing, feasible and well planned.
  7. Grant applications and project planning
    Research is funded through competitive grants from government agencies, charities and industry.
  8. Laboratory management
    Running a laboratory involves more than doing experiments: managing equipment, budgets, safety, people, data and schedules.
  9. Research ethics and integrity
    Research integrity means honesty, rigour, transparency and accountability in how research is done and reported.
  10. Data management and documentation
    Research data management keeps data organised, safe, documented and shareable throughout and after a project.
  11. Version control and reproducible workflows
    Version control systems like git track every change to code and documents, enabling collaboration, rollback and reproducibility.
  12. Collaboration across disciplines
    Modern physics research is collaborative, from small teams to collaborations of thousands.
  13. Research presentations and posters
    Presenting research at group meetings, conferences and seminars builds your reputation, invites feedback and spreads ideas.
  14. Scientific writing and publishing
    Scientific writing communicates research precisely and clearly so others can understand, evaluate and build on it.
  15. Peer review
    Peer review is how experts evaluate research for publication, funding and facility time.
  16. Intellectual property and technology transfer
    Physics research can produce inventions with commercial value.
  17. Project management
    Project management keeps research on track: defining goals, planning tasks, managing time, resources and risks, and communicating progress.
  18. Research impact and public engagement
    Research impact is the difference research makes beyond academia: to the economy, society, health, policy, culture and the environment.
  19. Communicating with industry and policymakers
    Physicists increasingly advise industry and government.
  20. Careers in academia, industry and scientific computing
    A physics education opens many careers: academic research, industry R&D, engineering, data science, finance, medicine, teaching, policy and entrepreneurship.
