Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

When is momentum conserved?

When the net external force on the system is zero (or negligible during a brief impact). From “Identifying relevant physical principles” →

In plain English: The hardest step in many physics problems is deciding which principles apply. Key idea: Read problems for cues, classify by underlying principle, and check which quantities are conserved at each stage.

What forces appear on a book resting on a table?

Weight (down) and normal force (up), equal and opposite. From “Drawing free-body and field diagrams” →

In plain English: Free-body diagrams isolate one object and show every force on it, while field diagrams map electric, magnetic or gravitational fields. Key idea: Isolate the object, draw only real forces acting on it, resolve along sensible axes; field lines and equipotentials are perpendicular.

Why must the argument of an exponential be dimensionless?

Exponentials are power series in their argument; terms of different powers could not add unless dimensionless. From “Checking units and dimensions” →

In plain English: Dimensional analysis checks whether equations make sense and can even predict how quantities depend on each other. Key idea: Check that every term has consistent dimensions; dimensional analysis also reveals scaling laws up to dimensionless constants.

What is a normal mode?

A pattern of motion where all parts oscillate at the same frequency with fixed relative amplitudes. From “Solving coupled equations” →

In plain English: Many physical systems involve several interacting quantities described by coupled equations: masses on springs, circuits with multiple loops, predator–prey populations and simultaneous force balances. Key idea: Write coupled equations in matrix form; normal modes from eigenvalues decouple oscillating systems.

What are Sobol indices?

Variance-based measures of how much each input (and interactions) contributes to output variance. From “Sensitivity analysis” →

In plain English: Sensitivity analysis asks how much a result changes when inputs change. Key idea: Sensitivity shows which inputs dominate; for power laws, relative sensitivity equals the exponent.

Key takeaways

  • Read problems for cues, classify by underlying principle, and check which quantities are conserved at each stage.
  • Define the system deliberately; internal forces cancel, and boundaries determine how energy and momentum are exchanged.
  • Align coordinates with motion and symmetry; switch frames (CM, rotating) when it simplifies the physics.
  • Isolate the object, draw only real forces acting on it, resolve along sensible axes; field lines and equipotentials are perpendicular.
  • Conservation laws relate initial and final states directly; check conditions (no external force, torque or non-conservative work).
  • Build equations from fundamental laws plus assumptions; solve the resulting ODEs and check limits.

Common mistakes in Advanced Problem-Solving Skills

  • Surface similarity can mislead; two inclined-plane problems may need completely different principles.
  • Mixing systems mid-problem (counting a force as both internal and external) leads to double-counting.
  • Mixing sign conventions between steps (e.g. up positive, then down positive) is a classic error.
  • "ma" is not a force; it's the result of the net force.
  • Angular momentum is conserved about a point only if external torque about that point is zero; choose the point carefully.
  • Using a constant-acceleration formula when forces depend on velocity or position gives wrong answers.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Identifying relevant physical principles
    The hardest step in many physics problems is deciding which principles apply.
  2. Defining systems and boundaries
    Choosing the system, what's included and what's outside, determines which forces are internal or external, which energies are counted and which quantities are conserved.
  3. Choosing coordinate systems
    The right coordinate system can make a problem simple: aligning axes with motion, using polar coordinates for circles and spherical coordinates for spheres.
  4. Drawing free-body and field diagrams
    Free-body diagrams isolate one object and show every force on it, while field diagrams map electric, magnetic or gravitational fields.
  5. Applying conservation laws
    Conservation laws, of energy, momentum, angular momentum and charge, are among the most powerful problem-solving tools because they connect initial and final states without needing details in between.
  6. Deriving equations from fundamental laws
    Many problems require building your own equations from fundamental laws rather than using memorised formulas.
  7. Checking units and dimensions
    Dimensional analysis checks whether equations make sense and can even predict how quantities depend on each other.
  8. Evaluating limiting cases
    Checking what an answer does in extreme or special cases, like zero mass, infinite time or a vanishing angle, is a powerful test.
  9. Estimating numerical answers
    Before calculating, estimate roughly what the answer should be.
  10. Analysing approximations
    Approximations make problems tractable: small angles, weak fields, low speeds, thin films, ideal gases.
  11. Solving coupled equations
    Many physical systems involve several interacting quantities described by coupled equations: masses on springs, circuits with multiple loops, predator–prey populations and simultaneous force balances.
  12. Selecting appropriate mathematical techniques
    Physics problems use a toolkit of mathematical techniques: calculus, vectors, differential equations, series, complex numbers, linear algebra and Fourier analysis.
  13. Comparing analytical and numerical solutions
    Analytical solutions give exact formulas and insight; numerical solutions handle problems no formula can.
  14. Sensitivity analysis
    Sensitivity analysis asks how much a result changes when inputs change.
  15. Physical interpretation of mathematical results
    A mathematical answer is only half the job: you must understand what it means physically.
  16. Error checking and independent verification
    Even experts make mistakes, so good physicists check their work systematically and seek independent verification.
  17. Communicating assumptions and limitations
    Every physics result rests on assumptions and has limitations.

Advanced Problem-Solving Skills: questions students ask

What is an isolated system?

One with no exchange of energy or matter with its surroundings.

Why use tilted axes on an incline?

So acceleration lies along one axis, avoiding coupled components.

When is mechanical energy conserved?

When only conservative forces do work.

What equation describes hydrostatic pressure?

dp/dz = −ρg, giving p = p0 + ρgh for constant density.
