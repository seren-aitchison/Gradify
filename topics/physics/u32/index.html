How was Earth's liquid outer core discovered?

S-waves can't pass through it, creating an S-wave shadow. From “Earth's internal structure” →

In plain English: Earth is layered: a thin rocky crust, a thick solid but slowly flowing mantle, a liquid iron outer core and a solid iron inner core.

How do seismologists locate an earthquake?

Using S–P time differences at several stations to triangulate. From “Seismology” →

In plain English: Seismology studies earthquakes and the seismic waves they create.

How can the solid mantle convect?

Rock deforms slowly by creep over geological time, behaving as a very viscous fluid. From “Mantle convection” →

In plain English: Although solid, Earth's mantle flows slowly by creep, carrying heat from the core to the surface in convection currents.

What do gravity surveys detect?

Variations in subsurface density. From “Gravity and magnetic surveys” →

In plain English: Gravity and magnetic surveys map small variations in Earth's gravitational and magnetic fields caused by underground rocks of different density and magnetism.

What is geostrophic balance?

Balance between Coriolis force and pressure gradient force, making flows parallel to isobars. From “Geophysical fluid dynamics” →

In plain English: Geophysical fluid dynamics studies large-scale flows of the atmosphere, oceans and Earth's core, where rotation and density stratification dominate.

Common mistakes in Geophysics

  • The mantle isn't liquid magma; it's solid rock that flows slowly like a very viscous material over millions of years.
  • Isostasy means mountains have low-density roots; their gravity anomalies are smaller than their visible mass suggests.
  • The geodynamo isn't a permanent magnet; Earth's core is far above the Curie temperature of iron.
  • Geothermal heat flow per square metre is tiny (~0.1 W) compared with sunlight (~200 W average); useful only where concentrated or extracted over large volumes.
  • The Richter scale saturates for large earthquakes; moment magnitude is used for big events.
  • Soft sediments amplify shaking; damage depends on local geology as well as magnitude and distance.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Earth's internal structure
    Earth is layered: a thin rocky crust, a thick solid but slowly flowing mantle, a liquid iron outer core and a solid iron inner core.
  2. Earth's gravitational field
    Earth's gravity varies slightly over its surface because of rotation, shape and density differences underground.
  3. Earth's magnetic field
    Earth has a magnetic field generated by convection of liquid iron in its outer core.
  4. Geothermal energy
    Earth's interior is hot, from primordial heat of formation and radioactive decay.
  5. Seismology
    Seismology studies earthquakes and the seismic waves they create.
  6. Earthquake waves
    Earthquakes radiate several kinds of seismic wave: fast compressional P-waves, slower shear S-waves through the body of the Earth, and surface waves that do most of the shaking damage.
  7. Earthquake mechanisms
    Most earthquakes happen when stress builds up on faults until the rocks suddenly slip, releasing stored elastic energy.
  8. Plate tectonics
    Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into about 15 major plates that move a few centimetres a year over the mantle.
  9. Mantle convection
    Although solid, Earth's mantle flows slowly by creep, carrying heat from the core to the surface in convection currents.
  10. Earth's rotation
    Earth spins once every sidereal day, but its rotation isn't perfectly steady: the length of day varies, the axis wobbles and precesses, and tides are gradually slowing it down.
  11. Paleomagnetism
    Paleomagnetism studies Earth's ancient magnetic field recorded in rocks.
  12. Geophysical surveying
    Geophysical surveying uses physical measurements at or above the surface to investigate what lies beneath: rock layers, minerals, water, archaeology and hazards, without digging.
  13. Gravity and magnetic surveys
    Gravity and magnetic surveys map small variations in Earth's gravitational and magnetic fields caused by underground rocks of different density and magnetism.
  14. Electrical resistivity methods
    Resistivity methods pass current into the ground through electrodes and measure the voltages that result.
  15. Ground-penetrating radar
    Ground-penetrating radar sends short radio pulses into the ground and records echoes from boundaries between materials.
  16. Rock mechanics
    Rock mechanics studies how rocks deform and break under stress.
  17. Geophysical fluid dynamics
    Geophysical fluid dynamics studies large-scale flows of the atmosphere, oceans and Earth's core, where rotation and density stratification dominate.
  18. Earth's atmosphere and oceans
    The atmosphere and oceans are thin fluid layers on Earth's surface that store and transport heat, water and carbon.
  19. Planetary geophysics
    Planetary geophysics applies physics to other worlds, revealing their interiors, magnetic fields, surfaces and histories from spacecraft gravity, magnetic, seismic and radar data.
  20. Natural hazard monitoring
    Geophysics monitors natural hazards (earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunamis, landslides) to warn people and reduce risk.

Geophysics: questions students ask

What is the geoid?

The gravitational equipotential surface approximating mean sea level.

What generates Earth's magnetic field?

A dynamo driven by convection in the liquid iron outer core.

Where does Earth's internal heat come from?

Radioactive decay and primordial heat from formation.

Why don't S-waves pass through the outer core?

Liquids have no shear rigidity.
