Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is helioseismology?

Studying the Sun's interior by analysing surface oscillation modes. From “Solar physics” →

In plain English: The Sun is a G2V main-sequence star powered by hydrogen fusion in its core. Key idea: The Sun fuses hydrogen in its core; its magnetic dynamo drives sunspots, flares and CMEs on an 11-year cycle.

Why does AM radio reach further at night?

The absorbing D layer disappears after sunset. From “Ionospheres” →

In plain English: The ionosphere is the layer of the upper atmosphere ionised by solar extreme ultraviolet and X-rays. Key idea: The ionosphere's electron density sets which radio waves reflect and how much GPS signals are delayed.

Why is Venus hotter than Mercury despite being further from the Sun?

Its thick CO2 atmosphere traps heat via a runaway greenhouse effect. From “Planetary atmospheres” →

In plain English: Planetary atmospheres range from Venus's crushing CO2 hothouse to Mars's thin cold air and Jupiter's deep hydrogen weather layers. Key idea: Atmospheres follow hydrostatic balance; radiation, composition and greenhouse gases set surface temperature.

What are Kirkwood gaps?

Gaps in the asteroid belt at orbital resonances with Jupiter. From “Comets and asteroids” →

In plain English: Comets and asteroids are leftover building blocks of the solar system. Key idea: Asteroids and comets are primitive leftovers; resonances sculpt the asteroid belt, and solar heating gives comets their tails.

Why is JWST at Sun–Earth L2?

It keeps Sun, Earth and Moon on one side for shielding, with a stable thermal environment. From “Orbital dynamics” →

In plain English: Orbital dynamics describes the motion of planets, moons and spacecraft under gravity. Key idea: Kepler's laws and vis-viva describe orbits; Hohmann transfers and gravity assists move spacecraft efficiently.

Key takeaways

  • The Sun fuses hydrogen in its core; its magnetic dynamo drives sunspots, flares and CMEs on an 11-year cycle.
  • The solar wind is the supersonic expansion of the corona, dragging the magnetic field into a spiral.
  • The heliosphere is the solar wind's bubble, bounded by the termination shock and heliopause, which shield us from many cosmic rays.
  • Magnetospheres balance planetary field pressure against the solar wind; reconnection drives their dynamics.
  • The ionosphere's electron density sets which radio waves reflect and how much GPS signals are delayed.
  • Space weather from flares, particles and CMEs threatens grids, satellites, navigation and people; monitoring at L1 gives short warnings.

Common mistakes in Space Physics and Planetary Science

  • The corona is far hotter than the photosphere; heat doesn't simply flow outward from hot to cold here.
  • The solar wind is not just fast particles; it carries magnetic field, which controls how it interacts with planets.
  • The heliopause isn't the edge of the solar system gravitationally; the Oort cloud extends tens of thousands of AU further.
  • The magnetosphere doesn't block all solar particles; reconnection lets energy and plasma in, especially with southward IMF.
  • Higher frequencies pass through the ionosphere; HF skywave works only below the maximum usable frequency.
  • A CME's impact depends strongly on its magnetic orientation; southward fields cause the biggest storms.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Solar physics
    The Sun is a G2V main-sequence star powered by hydrogen fusion in its core.
  2. Solar wind
    The solar wind is a continuous outflow of plasma from the Sun's corona, carrying the Sun's magnetic field throughout the solar system.
  3. Heliosphere
    The heliosphere is the vast bubble carved into interstellar space by the solar wind.
  4. Magnetospheres
    A magnetosphere is the region where a planet's magnetic field dominates over the solar wind.
  5. Ionospheres
    The ionosphere is the layer of the upper atmosphere ionised by solar extreme ultraviolet and X-rays.
  6. Space weather
    Space weather is the changing conditions in space, driven by the Sun, that affect technology and people.
  7. Cosmic rays
    Cosmic rays are high-energy charged particles, mostly protons and nuclei, arriving from the Sun, the galaxy and beyond.
  8. Radiation belts
    Earth's radiation belts are doughnut-shaped regions where the magnetic field traps energetic electrons and protons.
  9. Planetary atmospheres
    Planetary atmospheres range from Venus's crushing CO2 hothouse to Mars's thin cold air and Jupiter's deep hydrogen weather layers.
  10. Planetary interiors
    Planetary interiors are layered by density: metal cores, rocky or icy mantles and crusts.
  11. Planetary magnetic fields
    Planetary magnetic fields are generated by dynamos: convecting, electrically conducting fluids in rotating planets.
  12. Planetary rings
    Planetary rings are flat discs of countless orbiting particles, from dust to house-sized boulders.
  13. Moons and tidal heating
    Moons are shaped by tidal forces from their planets, which lock their rotation, alter their orbits and, through flexing, generate internal heat.
  14. Comets and asteroids
    Comets and asteroids are leftover building blocks of the solar system.
  15. Meteorites and impact physics
    Meteorites are pieces of asteroids, the Moon and Mars that survive passage through the atmosphere.
  16. Planetary formation
    Planets form in discs of gas and dust around young stars.
  17. Exoplanet detection
    Thousands of planets have been found orbiting other stars, using methods that detect tiny wobbles, dips in starlight, gravitational lensing and direct images.
  18. Orbital dynamics
    Orbital dynamics describes the motion of planets, moons and spacecraft under gravity.
  19. Spacecraft propulsion physics
    Spacecraft propulsion changes velocity by expelling mass at high speed.
  20. Remote sensing
    Remote sensing gathers information about planets from a distance, using reflected sunlight, emitted heat, radar and other signals.
  21. Planetary exploration instrumentation
    Planetary missions carry instruments that measure composition, structure, fields and environment on other worlds.
  22. Habitability and astrobiology interfaces
    Astrobiology asks where life could exist beyond Earth and how to detect it.

Space Physics and Planetary Science: questions students ask

Where does the fast solar wind come from?

Coronal holes, regions of open magnetic field.

Where did Voyager 1 cross the heliopause?

About 121 AU, in 2012.

What is the Dungey cycle?

Magnetospheric convection driven by dayside and nightside magnetic reconnection.

What was the Carrington event?

An extreme 1859 geomagnetic storm that disrupted telegraphs worldwide.
