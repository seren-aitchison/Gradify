Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is W for air?

About 34 eV per ion pair. From “Ionisation chambers” →

In plain English: An ionisation chamber measures radiation by collecting the electrons and ions it creates in a gas. Key idea: Ion chambers collect all ionisation charge without multiplication, giving a stable, energy-proportional signal ideal for dosimetry.

Why do HPGe detectors need cooling?

Ge's small band gap (0.67 eV) causes large thermal leakage current at room temperature. From “Semiconductor radiation detectors” →

In plain English: Semiconductor detectors collect electron–hole pairs created by radiation in a reverse-biased diode. Key idea: Low energy per carrier pair gives semiconductors superb energy resolution; HPGe is the gold standard for gamma spectroscopy.

What is a sampling calorimeter?

Alternating layers of dense absorber and active material, measuring a fraction of the shower energy. From “Calorimeters” →

In plain English: Calorimeters measure particle energy by absorbing it completely and sampling the resulting shower. Key idea: Calorimeters absorb particles to measure energy; resolution improves as 1/√E, limited at high energy by the constant term.

Why does the LHC need a trigger?

Collision rates and data volumes far exceed what can be stored. From “Trigger systems” →

In plain English: Trigger systems decide in microseconds which collisions to keep, since detectors produce far more data than can be stored. Key idea: Triggers select rare interesting events in real time through fast hardware and software stages; what isn't triggered is lost.

What causes escape peaks?

After pair production, one or both 511 keV annihilation photons escape the detector. From “Radiation spectroscopy” →

In plain English: Radiation spectroscopy measures the energy distribution of emitted gamma rays, X-rays, alpha and beta particles to identify isotopes and study nuclear structure. Key idea: Spectra identify isotopes by their characteristic lines; peak areas with efficiency calibration give activity.

Key takeaways

  • Ion chambers collect all ionisation charge without multiplication, giving a stable, energy-proportional signal ideal for dosimetry.
  • GM tubes give large identical pulses ideal for counting, but no energy information and significant dead time.
  • Scintillators turn energy into light; light yield sets energy resolution, density sets efficiency, decay time sets speed.
  • Low energy per carrier pair gives semiconductors superb energy resolution; HPGe is the gold standard for gamma spectroscopy.
  • PMTs give enormous low-noise gain and fast timing; SiPMs are taking over where size and magnetic fields matter.
  • Silicon trackers give micrometre hit positions; in a magnetic field, curvature gives momentum and extrapolation gives vertices.

Common mistakes in Nuclear and Particle Detection Methods

  • Ion chamber signals are tiny (pA–nA); electrometer leakage and environmental corrections matter.
  • GM counters saturate in very high fields and can read low or even zero ("fold-back") at extreme dose rates.
  • The Compton continuum can hide weak photopeaks; larger or denser crystals improve peak-to-Compton ratios.
  • Germanium must be cooled; at room temperature its small band gap gives huge leakage currents.
  • PMT gain is very sensitive to supply voltage and magnetic fields; calibrate and shield.
  • Momentum resolution degrades at high pT because tracks curve less; larger B and longer lever arms help.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Ionisation chambers
    An ionisation chamber measures radiation by collecting the electrons and ions it creates in a gas.
  2. Geiger–Müller counters
    A Geiger–Müller tube produces a large, uniform pulse each time radiation triggers an avalanche in its gas.
  3. Scintillation detectors
    Scintillation detectors convert radiation energy into flashes of visible light, which photodetectors turn into electrical pulses.
  4. Semiconductor radiation detectors
    Semiconductor detectors collect electron–hole pairs created by radiation in a reverse-biased diode.
  5. Photomultiplier tubes
    A photomultiplier tube converts a faint flash of light into a measurable electrical pulse by turning photons into electrons and multiplying them in a chain of dynodes.
  6. Silicon tracking detectors
    Silicon tracking detectors measure where charged particles pass with micrometre precision.
  7. Time projection chambers
    A time projection chamber is a large gas or liquid volume where charged particles leave ionisation trails that drift to a readout plane.
  8. Calorimeters
    Calorimeters measure particle energy by absorbing it completely and sampling the resulting shower.
  9. Cherenkov detectors
    A charged particle moving faster than light in a medium emits Cherenkov radiation, a cone of blue light like an optical sonic boom.
  10. Time-of-flight measurements
    Time-of-flight systems measure how long a particle takes to travel a known distance.
  11. Particle identification
    Particle identification (PID) determines what kind of particle left a signal: electron, muon, pion, kaon, proton, photon, neutron or neutrino.
  12. Trigger systems
    Trigger systems decide in microseconds which collisions to keep, since detectors produce far more data than can be stored.
  13. Event reconstruction
    Event reconstruction turns millions of raw detector signals into physics objects: tracks, vertices, electrons, photons, muons, jets and missing energy.
  14. Background suppression
    Background suppression removes ordinary events that mimic the signal being searched for.
  15. Detector calibration
    Calibration translates raw detector signals into physical quantities such as energy, position and time.
  16. Radiation spectroscopy
    Radiation spectroscopy measures the energy distribution of emitted gamma rays, X-rays, alpha and beta particles to identify isotopes and study nuclear structure.
  17. Accelerator instrumentation
    Accelerator instrumentation measures the beam's position, size, intensity, energy and losses so operators can steer, focus and protect a machine.
  18. Collider data analysis
    Collider data analysis turns petabytes of recorded collisions into physics measurements: cross-sections, masses, couplings and searches for new particles.
  19. Statistical significance of discoveries
    Particle physicists claim a discovery when the chance that background alone produced the observed excess is below about 1 in 3.5 million: five sigma.

Nuclear and Particle Detection Methods: questions students ask

What is a quench gas for?

It stops the self-sustaining discharge after each pulse.

Why is LaBr3(Ce) valued?

High light yield and fast decay give excellent energy resolution (~3% at 662 keV) and timing.

What is the Fano factor?

A factor (~0.1 for Si and Ge) by which pair-creation fluctuations are smaller than Poisson.

What is quantum efficiency for a PMT?

The fraction of incident photons producing photoelectrons at the photocathode.
