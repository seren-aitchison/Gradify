Why are molecular rotational energies so much smaller than electronic ones?

They scale with the electron-to-nucleus mass ratio; nuclei are heavy and rotate slowly. From “Molecular energy levels” →

In plain English: Molecules store energy in electronic states, vibrations and rotations, each on a very different scale.

Why does longer conjugation shift absorption to the red?

The electrons' "box" is longer, so energy gaps are smaller. From “Electronic molecular transitions” →

In plain English: Electronic transitions move a molecule's electrons between molecular orbitals, absorbing UV or visible light.

What makes a vibration Raman active?

A change in the molecule's polarisability during the vibration. From “Raman spectroscopy” →

In plain English: Raman spectroscopy shines monochromatic light on a sample and detects the tiny fraction scattered at shifted frequencies.

What does molecular dynamics simulate?

The motion of atoms by integrating Newton's equations with interatomic forces. From “Molecular dynamics” →

In plain English: Molecular dynamics (MD) simulates the motion of atoms by solving Newton's equations with forces from interatomic potentials.

Why are supersonic beams useful for spectroscopy?

They cool molecules' internal states to a few kelvin, simplifying spectra. From “Molecular beams” →

In plain English: A molecular beam is a stream of molecules travelling in one direction through a vacuum with few collisions.

Common mistakes in Molecular Physics

  • Vibrational and rotational energies aren't independent exactly; vibration changes the bond length and so the rotational constant.
  • Homonuclear molecules (N2, O2, H2) have no permanent dipole, so no pure rotational absorption spectrum; they're seen by Raman spectroscopy.
  • Not every vibration absorbs infrared light; symmetric vibrations with no dipole change are IR-inactive (but often Raman-active).
  • The colour we see is complementary to what's absorbed: chlorophyll absorbs red and blue, so leaves look green.
  • Covalent bonds aren't "two electrons sitting between atoms"; they're delocalised quantum states with lower total energy.
  • Symmetry predicts which transitions are zero, not how strong the allowed ones are.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Molecular energy levels
    Molecules store energy in electronic states, vibrations and rotations, each on a very different scale.
  2. Rotational spectra
    Molecules with a permanent electric dipole moment absorb and emit microwaves as they change rotational state.
  3. Vibrational spectra
    Molecules vibrate like masses on springs.
  4. Electronic molecular transitions
    Electronic transitions move a molecule's electrons between molecular orbitals, absorbing UV or visible light.
  5. Molecular bonding from a physical perspective
    Chemical bonds form because sharing or transferring electrons lowers the total energy of nuclei and electrons.
  6. Molecular symmetry
    A molecule's symmetry (its rotations, reflections and inversions that leave it unchanged) determines which vibrations are infrared or Raman active, which orbitals can mix and which transitions are allowed.
  7. Molecular orbitals
    Molecular orbitals are one-electron wavefunctions spread over a whole molecule, built from combinations of atomic orbitals.
  8. Raman spectroscopy
    Raman spectroscopy shines monochromatic light on a sample and detects the tiny fraction scattered at shifted frequencies.
  9. Infrared spectroscopy
    Infrared spectroscopy measures which infrared wavelengths a substance absorbs, revealing its molecular vibrations.
  10. Fluorescence and phosphorescence
    After absorbing light, a molecule can re-emit it.
  11. Molecular collisions
    Molecules constantly collide in gases and liquids, exchanging energy and sometimes reacting.
  12. Molecular dynamics
    Molecular dynamics (MD) simulates the motion of atoms by solving Newton's equations with forces from interatomic potentials.
  13. Energy transfer
    Energy moves between molecules, and within them, through collisions, radiation and near-field coupling.
  14. Non-radiative transitions
    Excited molecules often return to lower states without emitting light, turning the energy into heat through vibrations.
  15. Photodissociation
    Photodissociation is the breaking of a molecule by absorbing a photon.
  16. Molecular beams
    A molecular beam is a stream of molecules travelling in one direction through a vacuum with few collisions.
  17. Ultrafast molecular dynamics
    Ultrafast lasers with femtosecond pulses let scientists watch molecules vibrate, bonds break and electrons move in real time.
  18. Quantum molecular physics
    Molecules are quantum systems with rich internal structure: electronic, vibrational, rotational and spin states.
  19. Spectroscopic selection rules
    Molecular selection rules determine which transitions between electronic, vibrational and rotational states absorb or emit light.

Molecular Physics: questions students ask

What is the rotational energy of a rigid rotor?

EJ = BJ(J + 1), with B = ħ2/(2I).

What determines a vibration's frequency?

The bond's force constant and the reduced mass: ω = √(k/μ).

Why does sharing electrons lower energy in a covalent bond?

Delocalisation lowers kinetic energy and electrons attract both nuclei.

What is the mutual exclusion rule?

In molecules with an inversion centre, vibrations can't be both IR and Raman active.
