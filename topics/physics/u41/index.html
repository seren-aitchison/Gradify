Give an example of a tensor field.

Stress in a material, the metric of spacetime, or the electromagnetic field tensor. From “Scalar, vector and tensor fields” →

In plain English: A field assigns a quantity to every point in space and time: a number for a scalar field, an arrow for a vector field, or a matrix-like object for a tensor field. Key idea: Fields are classified by how they transform; scalars, vectors and tensors keep equations consistent in any frame.

What is the conjugate momentum density of a scalar field?

π = ∂ℒ/∂φ̇ = φ̇ for a standard kinetic term. From “Hamiltonian field theory” →

In plain English: Hamiltonian field theory treats a field and its conjugate momentum density as canonical variables at every point. Key idea: Fields become canonical variables at every point; ℋ gives energy density, and brackets lead to quantisation.

What is the Poynting vector?

S = E × H, the energy flux of the electromagnetic field. From “Conservation laws” →

In plain English: Conservation laws state that certain quantities, such as energy, momentum, angular momentum and charge, stay constant in isolated systems. Key idea: Conservation laws are continuity equations: what flows out of a region must reduce what's inside.

What is the group velocity of deep-water waves relative to phase velocity?

Half the phase velocity. From “Classical wave fields” →

In plain English: Wave fields carry energy and information through space as oscillations of a field. Key idea: Wave fields obey wave equations; their dispersion relation sets speeds, and superposition gives interference.

What is a Neumann boundary condition?

The normal derivative of the field is specified at the boundary. From “Boundary conditions” →

In plain English: Boundary conditions specify how fields behave at edges and interfaces, completing a field problem. Key idea: Boundary conditions complete field problems; they quantise modes and fix reflection and transmission.

Key takeaways

  • Fields are classified by how they transform; scalars, vectors and tensors keep equations consistent in any frame.
  • Field equations connect fields to their sources; linear ones allow superposition, nonlinear ones allow self-interaction.
  • A Lagrangian density encodes a field theory; vary the action to get field equations.
  • Fields become canonical variables at every point; ℋ gives energy density, and brackets lead to quantisation.
  • Symmetries constrain theories; how they are broken explains much of the structure of matter and forces.
  • Each continuous symmetry yields a locally conserved current; integrating gives a conserved charge.

Common mistakes in Classical Field Theory

  • The magnetic field is a pseudovector: under a mirror reflection it does not flip like velocity does.
  • Gauss's law is a constraint, not an evolution equation; initial data must satisfy it.
  • ℒ is a density; the Lagrangian is its integral over space.
  • In gauge theories not every field component has a conjugate momentum; constraints must be handled carefully.
  • A spontaneously broken symmetry is still a symmetry of the laws; only the ground state isn't symmetric.
  • Conservation is local: charges flow continuously, they cannot teleport.

Lessons in this unit

  1. Scalar, vector and tensor fields
    A field assigns a quantity to every point in space and time: a number for a scalar field, an arrow for a vector field, or a matrix-like object for a tensor field.
  2. Field equations
    Field equations are partial differential equations that determine how fields vary in space and time, given their sources.
  3. Lagrangian field theory
    Lagrangian field theory derives field equations from a Lagrangian density integrated over spacetime.
  4. Hamiltonian field theory
    Hamiltonian field theory treats a field and its conjugate momentum density as canonical variables at every point.
  5. Field symmetries
    Symmetries of a field theory are transformations of the fields or spacetime that leave the action unchanged.
  6. Noether currents
    Each continuous symmetry of a field theory gives a Noether current, a four-vector jμ whose divergence vanishes.
  7. Conservation laws
    Conservation laws state that certain quantities, such as energy, momentum, angular momentum and charge, stay constant in isolated systems.
  8. Electromagnetic field theory
    Electromagnetism is the prototype classical field theory: a gauge field Aμ, a Lagrangian, Maxwell's equations as field equations, and conservation of energy, momentum and charge.
  9. Stress-energy tensors
    The stress-energy tensor Tμν collects energy density, momentum density, energy flux and stress (pressure and shear) into one symmetric object.
  10. Classical wave fields
    Wave fields carry energy and information through space as oscillations of a field.
  11. Gauge invariance
    Gauge invariance means that certain changes to the potentials leave all physical fields and predictions unchanged.
  12. Green's functions for field equations
    Green's functions solve field equations for point sources, so any source distribution can be handled by superposition.
  13. Boundary conditions
    Boundary conditions specify how fields behave at edges and interfaces, completing a field problem.
  14. Radiation from sources
    Accelerating charges radiate electromagnetic waves, and oscillating masses radiate gravitational waves.
  15. Continuum field models
    Continuum models treat matter as smooth fields rather than discrete atoms: density, velocity, displacement and order parameters.

Classical Field Theory: questions students ask

What does Helmholtz's theorem state?

A well-behaved vector field is determined by its divergence and curl.

What makes Einstein's equations nonlinear?

Gravitational field energy itself gravitates; the metric appears in products in the curvature.

What is the Lagrangian density of a vibrating string?

½μ(∂y/∂t)2 − ½T(∂y/∂x)2.

What does Goldstone's theorem predict?

A massless boson for each spontaneously broken continuous global symmetry generator.
