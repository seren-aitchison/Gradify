Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a complement?

A good used together with another, such as printers and ink. From “Demand” →

In plain English: Demand is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. Key idea: The law of demand says quantity demanded falls as price rises; changes in income, related prices, tastes, expectations and population shift the demand curve, while a change in the good's own price moves along it.

What is the free-rider problem?

People can benefit from a public good without paying, so markets underprovide it. From “Market failure” →

In plain English: Markets do not always allocate resources efficiently. Key idea: Market failure arises from externalities, public goods, information failure and merit and demerit goods; governments respond with taxes, subsidies, regulation, permits, information and direct provision, though interventions can also fail.

What is the maximum fine for breaching UK competition law?

Up to 10% of a business's worldwide turnover. From “Competition policy” →

In plain English: Competition policy protects consumers from anti-competitive behaviour. Key idea: The Competition Act 1998 bans anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance, with fines of up to 10% of worldwide turnover; the CMA enforces it, reviews mergers, studies markets and now regulates firms with strategic market status in digital markets.

What is economic inactivity?

Not working and not seeking work, such as students, carers or people with long-term illness. From “Unemployment and the labour market” →

In plain English: The labour market determines the availability and cost of workers. Key idea: Unemployment is measured using the ILO definition and the labour force; it can be frictional, structural, cyclical or seasonal, and wages reflect labour demand and supply, with a legal floor set by the National Living Wage.

What does SPICED stand for?

Strong Pound, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer. From “Exchange rates” →

In plain English: The value of the pound affects exporters, importers and multinational firms. Key idea: Floating exchange rates are set by supply and demand for currency; a strong pound makes imports cheaper and exports dearer, and firms manage the risk with forward contracts, options, sterling invoicing and natural hedging.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Economics for Business

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What happens to quantity demanded when price rises, other things equal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It falls. Applying the standard result: This is the law of demand.

  2. Which change would shift a supply curve to the left? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A rise in production costs. Applying the standard result: Higher costs reduce supply.

  3. Demand is price inelastic. What happens to total revenue when price rises? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It rises. Quantity falls proportionately less than price rises.

  4. Incomes rise and demand for a good falls. What kind of good is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An inferior good. Applying the standard result: Demand falls as income rises.

  5. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity when demand rises? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Both rise. Applying the standard result: Higher demand raises both.

  6. Which product is likely to have the most price-elastic demand? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    One brand of baked beans among many. Many close substitutes make demand elastic.

  7. The price of coffee rises sharply. What happens to the demand for tea? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It shifts right, as tea is a substitute. Applying the standard result: Buyers switch to the substitute.

  8. A new government subsidy is paid to producers of solar panels. What happens? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Supply shifts right; price falls and quantity rises. Applying the standard result: Subsidies lower producers' costs.

  9. Why might a government tax cigarettes heavily? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Demand is inelastic, so revenue is raised and consumption falls slightly. Applying the standard result: Inelastic demand sustains tax revenue.

Key takeaways

  • The law of demand says quantity demanded falls as price rises; changes in income, related prices, tastes, expectations and population shift the demand curve, while a change in the good's own price moves along it.
  • Supply rises with price and shifts with costs, technology, taxes, subsidies and the number of firms; equilibrium is where demand meets supply, and shortages or surpluses move price towards it.
  • PED compares the percentage change in quantity demanded with the percentage change in price; it depends on substitutes, necessity, income share, time and habit, and determines whether price changes raise or lower revenue.
  • Market failure arises from externalities, public goods, information failure and merit and demerit goods; governments respond with taxes, subsidies, regulation, permits, information and direct provision, though interventions can also fail.
  • Perfect competition, with many price-taking firms and free entry, drives profits to normal levels and achieves efficiency; monopoly restricts output and raises prices, maximising profit where MR = MC, while natural monopolies are regulated.
  • Oligopolies are dominated by a few interdependent firms, measured by concentration ratios; firms may compete on price, rely on non-price competition or collude, and game theory, including the prisoner's dilemma and Nash equilibrium, explains their strategic choices.

Common mistakes in Economics for Business

  • Saying "demand fell" when price rose. A price rise causes a fall in quantity demanded, a movement along the curve.
  • Thinking a rise in price always shifts supply. A price change moves along the supply curve; only other factors shift it.
  • Assuming higher prices always raise revenue. If demand is elastic, revenue falls.
  • Assuming government intervention always corrects market failure. Poorly designed policy can create new inefficiencies.
  • Assuming monopolies always charge the highest possible price. They choose the price that maximises profit, where MR = MC.
  • Assuming oligopolists always collude. Rivalry, distrust and competition law often push them into competition.

Lessons in this school

  1. Demand
    Demand is the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price.
  2. Supply and market equilibrium
    Supply shows how much producers will offer at each price; together with demand it determines market prices.
  3. Price elasticity of demand
    Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price changes.
  4. Market failure
    Markets do not always allocate resources efficiently.
  5. Perfect competition and monopoly
    Market structure shapes prices, profits and efficiency.
  6. Oligopoly and game theory
    Many important markets, from supermarkets to mobile networks, are dominated by a few large firms.
  7. Competition policy
    Competition policy protects consumers from anti-competitive behaviour.
  8. Economic growth and the business cycle
    The level of economic activity affects every business.
  9. Inflation
    Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level.
  10. Unemployment and the labour market
    The labour market determines the availability and cost of workers.
  11. Interest rates and monetary policy
    The Bank of England uses interest rates to keep inflation on target.
  12. Fiscal policy and taxation
    Government spending and taxation shape demand and the business environment.
  13. Exchange rates
    The value of the pound affects exporters, importers and multinational firms.
  14. International trade and protectionism
    Trade lets countries specialise and exchange.
  15. Behavioural economics for business
    Real people do not always behave as rational economic models assume.

Economics for Business: questions students ask

What happens when price is above equilibrium?

There is a surplus, and price tends to fall.

Why is demand for petrol inelastic in the short run?

There are few immediate substitutes for most drivers.

What is normal profit?

The minimum profit needed to keep a firm in its industry.

What is interdependence in oligopoly?

Each firm's best decision depends on how its rivals react.
