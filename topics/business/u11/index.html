Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which philosopher is most associated with the categorical imperative?

Immanuel Kant. From “Ethical theories in business” →

In plain English: Business decisions often raise questions of right and wrong that law alone does not answer. Key idea: Consequentialism judges outcomes, deontology judges duties, virtue ethics judges character and justice theories judge fairness; each sheds light on business dilemmas but none gives a mechanical answer.

Who developed stakeholder theory in 1984?

R. Edward Freeman. From “Shareholder and stakeholder theories of the firm” →

In plain English: Whose interests should a company serve? Key idea: Shareholder theory puts owners first, stakeholder theory seeks value for all affected groups, and UK law takes a middle path of enlightened shareholder value in which directors serve shareholders while having regard to other stakeholders.

Which section requires directors to declare an interest in a proposed transaction?

Section 177. From “Directors' duties” →

In plain English: Company directors have legal duties set out in the Companies Act 2006. Key idea: The Companies Act 2006 sets seven general duties, owed to the company, covering proper use of powers, promoting success, independent judgement, care and skill, avoiding conflicts, refusing third-party benefits and declaring interests, with remedies and disqualification for breaches.

Who coined the triple bottom line?

John Elkington, in 1994. From “Sustainability and the triple bottom line” →

In plain English: Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising the future. Key idea: Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations; the triple bottom line measures people, planet and profit, the SDGs give global goals, and the circular economy keeps resources in use, though measurement and trade-offs remain challenges.

What are the three pillars of the UN Guiding Principles?

Protect, respect and remedy. From “Modern slavery and supply chain ethics” →

In plain English: Global supply chains can hide forced labour and serious abuses. Key idea: Modern slavery includes forced labour, servitude and trafficking hidden in complex supply chains; the Modern Slavery Act 2015 requires larger firms to publish annual statements, and responsible firms use due diligence, mapping, fair purchasing and remedy rather than audits alone.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Ethics, ESG and Corporate Governance

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which ethical theory judges an action mainly by its consequences? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Utilitarianism. Utilitarianism is a form of consequentialism that weighs outcomes for everyone affected.

  2. Which stage of Rest's model involves deciding which action is morally right? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Moral judgement. Judgement comes after awareness and before intention and behaviour.

  3. Which responsibility forms the base of Carroll's pyramid? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Economic. Carroll argued that profitability is the foundation that makes other responsibilities possible.

  4. A manager refuses to mislead a customer even though a small lie would close a profitable sale. Which theory best explains this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Deontology, because lying breaks a duty. Deontology holds that some acts, such as lying, are wrong regardless of their outcome.

  5. An employee says "everyone in the industry inflates expense claims". What is this an example of? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Moral disengagement. Justifying wrongdoing by pointing to others is a classic disengagement mechanism.

  6. A bank lets staff take paid days to volunteer with local charities. Which layer of Carroll's pyramid is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Philanthropic. Voluntary contributions to the community are philanthropic responsibilities.

  7. What is the most common criticism of using utilitarianism alone to make business decisions? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can justify harming a minority if the majority gains. Adding up total benefit can override the rights of the few who bear the costs.

  8. Staff in one branch repeatedly mis-sell products to meet aggressive targets. What is the most effective first response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Review the targets and incentives that create the pressure. When many people behave badly, the system is usually the cause and must be changed.

  9. Which example best illustrates creating shared value rather than traditional CSR? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Redesigning packaging to cut waste and material costs. Shared value improves social outcomes and the firm's competitiveness at the same time.

Key takeaways

  • Consequentialism judges outcomes, deontology judges duties, virtue ethics judges character and justice theories judge fairness; each sheds light on business dilemmas but none gives a mechanical answer.
  • Ethical action needs awareness, judgement, intention and behaviour; situational pressures such as targets and culture often matter as much as individual values, and simple tests can help people check a decision.
  • CSR covers economic, legal, ethical and philanthropic responsibilities; supporters see business benefits and a licence to operate, critics such as Friedman see a misuse of shareholders' funds, and shared value tries to link social progress to competitive advantage.
  • Shareholder theory puts owners first, stakeholder theory seeks value for all affected groups, and UK law takes a middle path of enlightened shareholder value in which directors serve shareholders while having regard to other stakeholders.
  • Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled; it addresses the separation of ownership and control through an accountable board, independent non-executives, committees and principles of transparency and fairness.
  • The UK Corporate Governance Code, issued by the FRC, applies to listed companies on a comply or explain basis and covers leadership and purpose, division of responsibilities, composition and evaluation, audit and risk, and remuneration.

Common mistakes in Ethics, ESG and Corporate Governance

  • Assuming legal means ethical. Law sets a minimum; many unethical practices are lawful and ethics asks what a business should do, not only what it must do.
  • Blaming unethical behaviour only on a few "bad apples". Incentives, culture and leadership (the "barrel") strongly shape what ordinary people do.
  • Equating CSR with charity donations. Philanthropy is only the top layer of Carroll's pyramid; how a firm makes its money matters more than how it gives some away.
  • Thinking section 172 makes directors accountable to all stakeholders equally. It requires them to have regard to other stakeholders while acting for the benefit of members.
  • Thinking non-executive directors are part-time managers. Their role is independent oversight and challenge, not running the business day to day.
  • Thinking the Code is law and non-compliance is illegal. It is applied through the listing rules on a comply or explain basis; a reasoned explanation is acceptable.
