Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why do lenders read a company's accounts?

To judge whether the business can repay loans and interest. From “Why managers need finance” →

In plain English: Every management decision has a financial side. Key idea: Many groups rely on accounting information; financial accounting reports the past to outsiders, management accounting supports internal decisions, and because profit follows the accruals basis, a profitable business can still run out of cash.

Why is depreciation added back in the indirect method?

It is an expense that does not involve any cash payment. From “The statement of cash flows” →

In plain English: The statement of cash flows explains how cash changed over a period. Key idea: The statement of cash flows splits cash movements into operating, investing and financing; the indirect method adds back non-cash items and adjusts for working capital, and the pattern of flows shows how a business funds itself.

What does a high P/E ratio usually indicate?

Investors expect strong future earnings growth. From “Gearing and investor ratios” →

In plain English: How a business is financed affects its risk and the returns to shareholders. Key idea: Gearing and interest cover measure financial risk from borrowing, while EPS, P/E, dividend yield and dividend cover help investors judge earnings, valuation and income.

Why flex a budget before comparing it with actual results?

So that costs are compared at the same level of activity. From “Variance analysis” →

In plain English: Variance analysis compares actual results with the budget to explain what went right or wrong. Key idea: Budgets are flexed to actual activity before comparison; price and volume or usage variances then explain the difference, and investigating the causes of significant, often linked, variances is what makes the analysis useful.

Why does ARR deduct depreciation?

It is based on accounting profit, not cash flow. From “Investment appraisal: payback and ARR” →

In plain English: Capital investment decisions commit large sums for years. Key idea: Payback measures how quickly the investment is recovered and ARR measures average accounting profit on average investment; both are simple, but neither allows for the time value of money.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Finance for Managers

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which user group is most interested in whether loans will be repaid? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Lenders. Applying the standard result: Lenders assess repayment ability.

  2. Which figure is revenue minus cost of sales? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Gross profit. Applying the standard result: Gross profit comes first.

  3. Which is a current asset? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Trade receivables. Receivables turn into cash within a year.

  4. How does management accounting differ from financial accounting? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is internal and forward-looking with no set format. Management accounting serves internal decisions.

  5. A machine costs £50,000, has a residual value of £10,000 and a life of 5 years. What is the annual straight-line depreciation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    £8,000. Applying the standard result: (50,000 − 10,000) ÷ 5.

  6. Which is a non-current liability? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A five-year bank loan. Applying the standard result: It is due after more than a year.

  7. A fast-growing firm reports record profits but cannot pay its suppliers. What is the most likely cause? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Cash tied up in stock and slow-paying customers. Applying the standard result: Growth can absorb cash despite profit.

  8. Rent of £12,000 is paid on 1 October for the next 12 months; the year ends 31 December. What rent expense is recognised this year? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    £3,000. Three months belong to this year; nine are prepaid.

  9. A business pays £20,000 cash to a supplier it owed. What happens to the statement? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Cash and trade payables both fall by £20,000. Both sides fall equally, so it still balances.

Key takeaways

  • Many groups rely on accounting information; financial accounting reports the past to outsiders, management accounting supports internal decisions, and because profit follows the accruals basis, a profitable business can still run out of cash.
  • The income statement subtracts cost of sales, operating expenses, finance costs and tax from revenue to reach profit for the year; accruals and depreciation ensure expenses are matched to the period that benefits.
  • The statement of financial position lists assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time and always satisfies assets = equity + liabilities; retained profits link it to the income statement.
  • The statement of cash flows splits cash movements into operating, investing and financing; the indirect method adds back non-cash items and adjusts for working capital, and the pattern of flows shows how a business funds itself.
  • Gross and operating margins show how much of each £1 of sales becomes profit, while ROCE measures profit generated on the long-term capital; ratios are meaningful only when compared over time or with similar businesses.
  • Current and acid-test ratios measure the ability to meet short-term debts, while receivables, inventory and payables days show how efficiently working capital is managed; together they give the cash conversion cycle.

Common mistakes in Finance for Managers

  • Treating profit as money in the bank. Profit measures performance; cash measures the ability to pay bills now.
  • Including the purchase price of a new machine as an expense. Only that year's depreciation appears in the income statement; the machine is an asset.
  • Thinking equity is cash the business holds. Equity is a claim on the net assets, which may be tied up in buildings, stock and receivables.
  • Adding an increase in receivables. More receivables means cash not yet collected, so it is deducted.
  • Comparing margins across different industries. A 3% margin is normal for a supermarket but poor for a consultancy.
  • Assuming a higher current ratio is always better. A very high ratio may mean idle cash or excess stock.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why managers need finance
    Every management decision has a financial side.
  2. The income statement
    The income statement shows whether a business made a profit over a period.
  3. The statement of financial position
    The statement of financial position, or balance sheet, shows what a business owns and owes at one moment.
  4. The statement of cash flows
    The statement of cash flows explains how cash changed over a period.
  5. Profitability ratios
    Ratios turn raw figures into comparable measures.
  6. Liquidity and efficiency ratios
    A business must be able to pay its bills and manage its working capital.
  7. Gearing and investor ratios
    How a business is financed affects its risk and the returns to shareholders.
  8. Costs and break-even analysis
    Knowing how costs behave helps managers set prices and targets.
  9. Contribution and short-term decisions
    Many short-term decisions depend on contribution rather than full cost.
  10. Budgeting
    A budget turns plans into numbers.
  11. Variance analysis
    Variance analysis compares actual results with the budget to explain what went right or wrong.
  12. Cash flow forecasting
    A cash flow forecast predicts when money will come in and go out.
  13. Sources of finance
    Businesses fund themselves from many sources, each with different costs, risks and control implications.
  14. Investment appraisal: payback and ARR
    Capital investment decisions commit large sums for years.
  15. Net present value
    Net present value is the most widely recommended appraisal method because it allows for the time value of money.
  16. Working capital management
    Working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day operations.
  17. The cost of capital
    The cost of capital is the return investors require, and it sets the hurdle rate for new investments.

Finance for Managers: questions students ask

Why is an unpaid March electricity bill a March expense?

The accruals concept matches expenses to the period in which they are incurred.

What is equity?

The owners' residual interest: total assets minus total liabilities.

What does a falling gross margin with steady sales suggest?

Lower prices or higher direct costs, such as purchase prices.

Why does the acid-test ratio exclude inventory?

Stock may take time to sell, so it is the least liquid current asset.
