Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is operations management?

Managing the resources that create and deliver products and services. From “What is operations management?” →

In plain English: Operations management is about how organisations create and deliver their goods and services. Key idea: Operations management manages the resources that create and deliver products and services; it drives cost, customer experience and capability, and is judged on quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost.

How is efficiency calculated?

Actual output divided by effective capacity. From “Capacity management” →

In plain English: Capacity management matches an operation's ability to produce with demand. Key idea: Capacity is measured as design and effective capacity, giving utilisation and efficiency; operations manage fluctuating demand by level capacity, chase demand or demand management; Little's law links arrivals, time and queue size.

What does DMAIC stand for?

Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. From “Six Sigma” →

In plain English: Six Sigma is a data-driven approach to reducing variation and defects. Key idea: Six Sigma reduces variation using data and the DMAIC method, targeting 3.4 defects per million opportunities; DPMO and process capability measure performance.

In ABC analysis, what proportion of value do A items typically account for?

About 80%. From “Inventory management” →

In plain English: Inventory protects against uncertainty but ties up cash. Key idea: Inventory buffers uncertainty and supports batching and seasonality but costs money and risks obsolescence; ABC analysis focuses control on high-value items, and reorder points with safety stock prevent stock-outs.

What is reverse logistics?

Managing the flow of returned, repaired or recycled goods back through the supply chain. From “Logistics” →

In plain English: Logistics moves and stores goods through supply chains. Key idea: Logistics combines transport modes, warehousing, cross-docking and last-mile delivery, often through 3PL providers; containerisation revolutionised trade, and reverse logistics supports returns and the circular economy.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Operations and Supply Chain Management

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which performance objective means delivering on time as promised? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Dependability. Dependability is about keeping delivery promises.

  2. In a hospital, which is a transformed resource? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Patients. Applying the standard result: Patients are changed by the operation.

  3. Which process type is used to build a cruise ship? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Project. A ship is a unique, large product built as a project.

  4. A budget airline's main operations priority is most likely to be which? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cost. Low fares depend on tightly controlled costs.

  5. Which combination of the four Vs usually gives the lowest unit cost? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High volume, low variety, low variation, low visibility. Standardised, high-volume, hidden operations are cheapest per unit.

  6. Which layout groups similar equipment, as in a hospital's X-ray department? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Functional layout. Similar activities are grouped in one place.

  7. How can improving quality reduce an operation's costs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Fewer errors mean less rework, waste and complaint handling. Internal quality improvements cut failure costs.

  8. A bank moves many services online. How does this change its four Vs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Visibility falls and volume can rise, lowering unit costs. Online processes are less visible and more standardised.

  9. A furniture maker produces many varied, made-to-order pieces using an assembly line. What problem is likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The line lacks the flexibility for high variety, raising costs. High variety and low volume suit jobbing and functional layouts.

Key takeaways

  • Operations management manages the resources that create and deliver products and services; it drives cost, customer experience and capability, and is judged on quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost.
  • Operations transform inputs, materials, information and customers, using facilities and staff, into outputs; the four Vs of volume, variety, variation and visibility explain differences in cost and design.
  • Process types range from project and jobbing to mass and continuous in manufacturing, and professional to mass services; layouts are fixed-position, functional, cell or product; the right choice matches volume and variety.
  • Capacity is measured as design and effective capacity, giving utilisation and efficiency; operations manage fluctuating demand by level capacity, chase demand or demand management; Little's law links arrivals, time and queue size.
  • Quality can be defined as conformance, fitness for use or meeting expectations; prevention, appraisal, internal and external failure make up the costs of quality, and prevention-focused assurance and systems such as ISO 9001 reduce total costs.
  • TQM makes quality everyone's responsibility, focusing on customers, processes, data and continuous improvement; Deming, Juran and Crosby shaped it, and the PDCA cycle structures improvement.

Common mistakes in Operations and Supply Chain Management

  • Thinking operations only applies to factories. Hospitals, banks, schools and online platforms all have operations functions.
  • Ignoring customers as an input. In services, customers are often transformed resources, which makes operations more visible and variable.
  • Choosing an assembly line for a highly varied, low-volume product. Line layouts suit standardised, high-volume work.
  • Aiming for 100% utilisation in services. Near full utilisation, queues and waiting times grow sharply with any variation in demand.
  • Relying on inspection to achieve quality. Inspection finds defects after they occur; prevention stops them happening.
  • Treating TQM as a one-off project. It is a long-term culture of continuous improvement involving everyone.

    Operations management is about how organisations create and deliver their goods and services.
  2. The transformation model and the four Vs

    The way work flows through an operation depends on its process type and layout.
  4. Capacity management
    Capacity management matches an operation's ability to produce with demand.
  5. Quality management
    Quality is central to customer satisfaction and cost.
  6. Total quality management
    Total quality management (TQM) makes quality the responsibility of everyone in an organisation.
  7. Six Sigma
    Six Sigma is a data-driven approach to reducing variation and defects.
  8. Lean production
    Lean production, developed at Toyota, aims to deliver value with minimum waste.

  10. Inventory management
    Inventory protects against uncertainty but ties up cash.
  11. Just-in-time
    Just-in-time (JIT) aims to produce and deliver exactly what is needed, when it is needed.
  12. Procurement and supplier relationships
    Procurement obtains the goods and services an organisation needs, and supplier relationships shape cost, quality and risk.
  13. Logistics
    Logistics moves and stores goods through supply chains.

    Supply chains face disruptions from natural disasters, pandemics, conflicts and accidents.
  15. Productivity and efficiency
    Productivity measures how well inputs are turned into outputs.
  16. Project management
    Projects are temporary endeavours with defined goals.

Operations and Supply Chain Management: questions students ask

What are transforming resources?

Facilities and staff that act on the transformed resources.

What is a fixed-position layout?

A layout where the product stays in place and resources come to it.

What is the difference between quality control and quality assurance?

Quality control detects defects; quality assurance prevents them through system design.

What does kaizen mean?

Continuous improvement through many small changes.
