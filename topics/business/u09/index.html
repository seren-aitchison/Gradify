Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What did Schumpeter mean by creative destruction?

Innovation by entrepreneurs replaces old products, firms and industries with new ones. From “What is entrepreneurship?” →

In plain English: Entrepreneurship is the process of discovering, evaluating and exploiting opportunities to create new value. Key idea: Entrepreneurship is the discovery, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities; Schumpeter stresses innovation and creative destruction, Kirzner alertness to missed opportunities.

Name the nine blocks of the business model canvas.

Customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure. From “The business model canvas” →

In plain English: A business model describes how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value. Key idea: The business model canvas maps how a venture creates, delivers and captures value across nine blocks, helping founders test assumptions and compare models.

How long does a UK patent last?

Up to 20 years, subject to renewal fees. From “Protecting ideas: intellectual property” →

In plain English: Intellectual property rights let creators and businesses control and profit from their ideas. Key idea: Patents protect inventions for up to 20 years, trade marks protect brand signs indefinitely if renewed, designs protect appearance, copyright protects original works automatically, and trade secrets protect confidential know-how.

What is the innovator's dilemma?

Successful firms fail because they rationally focus on current customers and high margins, ignoring disruptive innovations. From “Disruptive innovation” →

In plain English: Disruptive innovation explains how small entrants with simpler, cheaper products can displace established leaders. Key idea: Disruptive innovations start as simpler, cheaper offerings for overlooked customers and improve until they displace incumbents, who rationally focus on their best customers; the term is often misapplied.

What are the five stages of design thinking?

Empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test. From “Design thinking” →

In plain English: Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems and developing innovations. Key idea: Design thinking is a human-centred, iterative approach: empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test, or discover, define, develop and deliver in the Double Diamond, balancing desirability, feasibility and viability.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Entrepreneurship and Innovation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is intrapreneurship? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Entrepreneurial behaviour inside an established organisation. Intrapreneurs innovate within large firms rather than starting new ones.

  2. Which is one of Drucker's sources of innovation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Demographic change. Population changes create new needs and markets.

  3. What does MVP stand for? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Minimum viable product. An MVP is the simplest product that tests a key assumption.

  4. Which example best fits Schumpeter's idea of the entrepreneur? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A firm introducing streaming that displaces DVD rental. Schumpeter's entrepreneur introduces new combinations that destroy old industries.

  5. Why are people with industry experience more likely to spot opportunities? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Prior knowledge helps them see problems and unmet needs. Shane's research shows prior knowledge shapes which opportunities people see.

  6. Which reasoning starts from the resources an entrepreneur already has? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Effectual reasoning. Effectuation begins with "who I am, what I know and whom I know".

  7. Which statement about new firms is best supported by evidence? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A small share of fast-growing firms creates many new jobs. Job creation is concentrated in a minority of high-growth firms.

  8. An entrepreneur has a clever product idea but no evidence anyone will pay. What should she do first? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Test demand cheaply with potential customers. Testing demand before committing resources reduces risk.

  9. In which case is detailed upfront planning most necessary? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Developing a new medicine needing regulatory approval. Regulated, capital-intensive products cannot easily be tested in small, cheap steps.

Key takeaways

  • Entrepreneurship is the discovery, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities; Schumpeter stresses innovation and creative destruction, Kirzner alertness to missed opportunities.
  • Opportunities arise from change and unmet needs; entrepreneurs recognise them through prior knowledge and networks, then test them for real demand, market size, advantage and feasibility.
  • Effectuation starts from available means and affordable loss; the lean start-up tests assumptions with an MVP through build–measure–learn and pivots when evidence demands.
  • The business model canvas maps how a venture creates, delivers and captures value across nine blocks, helping founders test assumptions and compare models.
  • A business plan sets out the product, market, operations, team, finances and risks; break-even output equals fixed costs divided by contribution per unit.
  • Start-ups combine savings, debt, equity and grants; equity investors such as angels and VCs share risk in exchange for ownership, and SEIS and EIS encourage investment through tax relief.

Common mistakes in Entrepreneurship and Innovation

  • Equating entrepreneurship with owning any small business. The defining features are opportunity, innovation and value creation under uncertainty.
  • Confusing an idea with an opportunity. An idea becomes an opportunity only if customers will pay and the venture can deliver profitably.
  • Thinking an MVP means a poor-quality product. It is the smallest test of a key assumption, and must still deliver enough value to measure real behaviour.
  • Filling in the canvas once and treating it as fact. Each block is a hypothesis to be tested and revised.
  • Treating the plan's forecasts as certain. They are estimates and should be tested and revised.
  • Thinking equity finance is free money. Founders give up a share of future profits and some control.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is entrepreneurship?
    Entrepreneurship is the process of discovering, evaluating and exploiting opportunities to create new value.
  2. Opportunity recognition and the entrepreneurial mindset
    Entrepreneurs succeed by spotting and shaping opportunities.
  3. Effectuation and the lean start-up
    Entrepreneurs often cannot predict the future, so they act, learn and adapt.

    A business model describes how an organisation creates, delivers and captures value.
  5. The business plan
    A business plan sets out what a venture will do, for whom, and how it will make money.

    New ventures need money before they earn it.
  7. Protecting ideas: intellectual property
    Intellectual property rights let creators and businesses control and profit from their ideas.

    Innovation means turning new ideas into value, and it takes many forms.

    Disruptive innovation explains how small entrants with simpler, cheaper products can displace established leaders.
  11. Diffusion of innovations
    New products spread through a population over time at different speeds.
  12. Open innovation
    Firms no longer rely only on their own laboratories for new ideas.

    Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems and developing innovations.
  14. Scaling a business
    Scaling means growing revenue much faster than costs.
  15. Social entrepreneurship
    Social entrepreneurs use business methods to tackle social and environmental problems.

Entrepreneurship and Innovation: questions students ask

What is the affordable loss principle?

Commit only what you can afford to lose, rather than chasing expected returns.

What is a freemium model?

A free basic service with paid premium features.

What is contribution per unit?

Selling price minus variable cost per unit.
