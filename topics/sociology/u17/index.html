Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between a research design and a research method?

A design is the overall plan for what evidence is collected, from whom and when; a method is the technique used to collect it. From “Research design” →

In plain English: A research design is the overall plan that links a research question to the evidence needed to answer it: what will be studied, how, with whom and when. Key idea: A research design is the plan connecting a question to evidence; theory, practicality and ethics together determine which design fits.

What is triangulation?

Using more than one method, data source, researcher or theory to study the same question. From “Mixed methods” →

In plain English: Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative methods in one study, to gain both breadth and depth. Key idea: Mixed methods combine quantitative breadth with qualitative depth, and triangulation tests whether different kinds of evidence agree.

What is thick description?

Describing behaviour along with its meaning in context, not just what happened. From “Ethnography” →

In plain English: Ethnography is the in-depth study of a culture or community over an extended period, combining observation, conversation and documents to produce a detailed account of how life is lived. Key idea: Ethnography produces detailed, contextual accounts of social worlds through long-term immersion and careful fieldnotes.

Why might recorded crime rise without more crime occurring?

Changes in reporting, recording rules or police priorities can increase recorded crime. From “Official statistics” →

In plain English: Official statistics are numerical data collected by governments and public bodies on topics such as population, crime, employment and health. Key idea: Official statistics are valuable large-scale data, but many are socially constructed by decisions about what to count and record.

Name three sources of bias in sociological research.

Any three of sampling, measurement, interviewer or observer, researcher values, sponsorship, publication. From “Bias” →

In plain English: Bias is systematic distortion in research. Key idea: Bias is systematic distortion from sampling, measurement, researchers, sponsors and publication; transparency and design are the main safeguards.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Following the same group of people over several years is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Longitudinal design. Repeated measurement of the same units over time defines a longitudinal design.

  2. A testable prediction about a relationship between variables is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Hypothesis. Hypotheses state predictions that data can support or fail to support.

  3. Which classic study is usually cited as an example of positivist sociology? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Durkheim's Suicide. Durkheim used official statistics to find social causes of suicide rates.

  4. A researcher with little funding wants to understand how members of one food bank volunteer team see their work. Which design fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A qualitative case study. A single setting, a question about meaning and limited resources suit a case study.

  5. Which research question is most researchable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How do parents at one school choose secondaries?. It names a population, setting and process that can be studied.

  6. A researcher wants to measure how voting differs by social class across Britain. Which method best fits a positivist approach? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A large structured survey with a representative sample. Measuring patterns across a population calls for standardised, representative data.

  7. Why is starting from a favourite method a weak way to design research? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The method may not fit the question being asked. The question should determine the design; a mismatched method gives weak evidence.

  8. What is wrong with the question "Why are benefit claimants lazy?" (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It assumes a contested conclusion. Loaded questions build in assumptions and bias the research.

  9. What is the strongest interpretivist objection to positivism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Measurement can miss meanings. Interpretivists argue that understanding meaning, not just counting behaviour, is essential.

Key takeaways

  • A research design is the plan connecting a question to evidence; theory, practicality and ethics together determine which design fits.
  • A research question focuses a topic into something answerable; hypotheses suit studies that test predictions, while exploratory studies keep questions open.
  • Positivism treats society as an objective reality to be measured and explained, favouring large-scale, quantitative, replicable research.
  • Interpretivism seeks to understand social action from participants' viewpoints, using qualitative methods that prioritise validity and meaning.
  • Realism holds that social structures and mechanisms are real even when unobservable, and explains outcomes by mechanisms operating in particular contexts.
  • Quantitative research measures patterns in large samples reliably, enabling generalisation, comparison and hypothesis testing.

Common mistakes in Research Methods

  • Choosing a method first and then finding a question to fit it. The question should drive the design.
  • Writing questions that contain their own answer, such as "Why are young people addicted to phones?". Good questions don't assume the conclusion.
  • Equating positivism with "using numbers". Positivism is a set of assumptions about knowledge; interpretivists sometimes use numbers too.
  • Assuming interpretivist research is "just opinion". It follows systematic procedures, and its quality is judged by credibility, transparency and reflexivity.
  • Thinking realism is just "common sense realism" about the world being real. Critical realism makes specific claims about layers of reality and causal mechanisms.
  • Treating numbers as automatically objective. Every figure depends on definitions, measurement and who was counted.

Lessons in this school

  1. Research design
    A research design is the overall plan that links a research question to the evidence needed to answer it: what will be studied, how, with whom and when.
  2. Research questions and hypotheses
    Sociological research begins with a question.
  3. Positivism
    Positivism holds that sociology should study society scientifically, as natural sciences study the physical world, seeking objective facts and law-like patterns.
  4. Interpretivism
    Interpretivism holds that sociology must understand the meanings people give to their actions, because human behaviour differs from the behaviour of natural objects.
  5. Realism
    Realism (critical realism) argues that society has real structures and mechanisms that may not be directly observable, so research must go beyond observed patterns and participants' accounts.
  6. Quantitative research
    Quantitative research collects numerical data to measure social phenomena, identify patterns and test relationships.
  7. Qualitative research
    Qualitative research collects non-numerical data, such as words, observations and documents, to understand meanings, experiences and processes in depth.
  8. Mixed methods
    Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative methods in one study, to gain both breadth and depth.
  9. Surveys
    Surveys collect standardised information from many people, usually by questionnaire or structured interview.
  10. Questionnaire design
    The quality of survey data depends on the questions asked.
  11. Structured interviews
    A structured interview is a survey delivered by an interviewer: the same questions, in the same order and wording, for every respondent.
  12. Semi-structured interviews
    Semi-structured interviews use a guide of key questions but allow the interviewer to follow up and respondents to answer in their own words.
  13. Unstructured interviews
    An unstructured interview is a guided conversation in which the respondent largely leads, allowing researchers to explore experiences in great depth.
  14. Focus groups
    Focus groups are group interviews in which participants discuss a topic together, guided by a moderator.
  15. Participant observation
    In participant observation, the researcher joins a group or setting, taking part in its activities while observing.
  16. Ethnography
    Ethnography is the in-depth study of a culture or community over an extended period, combining observation, conversation and documents to produce a detailed account of how life is lived.
  17. Case studies
    A case study investigates one case, such as a community, organisation, event or policy, in depth and in its context, often with several methods.
  18. Experiments in sociology
    Experiments are rare in sociology but powerful when they can be used, especially field experiments that test discrimination in real life.
  19. Social network analysis
    Social network analysis (SNA) studies the patterns of relationships between people, groups or organisations, using measures such as density and centrality.
  20. Content analysis
    Content analysis systematically analyses the content of media and documents.
  21. Discourse analysis
    Discourse analysis studies how language constructs social reality, identities and power, rather than simply describing them.
  22. Secondary data
    Secondary data are data collected by someone else, such as official statistics, existing surveys, documents and archives, that sociologists reanalyse for new questions.
  23. Official statistics
    Official statistics are numerical data collected by governments and public bodies on topics such as population, crime, employment and health.
  24. Longitudinal research
    Longitudinal research follows the same people, households or units over time, showing how lives change and in what order events happen.
  25. Comparative research
    Comparative research compares societies, regions, institutions or time periods to understand similarities and differences and test explanations.
  26. Probability sampling
    In probability sampling every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection, which allows sample results to be generalised with measurable precision.
  27. Non-probability sampling
    Non-probability sampling selects participants without giving everyone a known chance of selection.
  28. Reliability
    Reliability concerns consistency: whether a method would produce the same results if repeated.
  29. Validity
    Validity concerns whether research captures what it claims to: a true picture of the social reality studied.
  30. Generalisability
    Generalisability is how far findings from a study apply beyond the people and settings studied.
  31. Bias
    Bias is systematic distortion in research.
  32. Positionality
    Positionality is the researcher's social position, such as class, gender, ethnicity, age and experience, and how it shapes access, relationships and interpretation in research.
  33. Reflexivity
    Reflexivity means researchers critically reflecting on how they, their methods and their assumptions shape the research and its findings.
  34. Informed consent
    Informed consent means participants agree to take part freely, understanding what the research involves.
  35. Anonymity and confidentiality
    Anonymity and confidentiality protect participants from being identified and from harm.
  36. Researching vulnerable groups
    Some participants are vulnerable because of age, capacity, circumstances or power relations.
  37. Interpreting findings
    Interpreting findings means deciding what the evidence shows and does not show.
  38. Disseminating research
    Research matters only if it reaches people who can use or challenge it.

Research Methods: questions students ask

What makes a good research question?

It is clear, focused, researchable with available resources and connected to sociological debates.

Why do positivists prefer large representative samples?

So that patterns found can be generalised to the wider population.

What do interpretivists aim to understand?

The meanings people give to their actions, seen from their point of view.

What are the three layers of reality in critical realism?

The empirical (observed), the actual (events) and the real (structures and mechanisms).
