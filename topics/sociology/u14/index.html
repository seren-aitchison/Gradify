Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How did Giddens define globalisation?

The intensification of worldwide social relations linking distant places so local events are shaped far away. From “What is globalisation?” →

In plain English: Globalisation is the growing interconnectedness of the world through flows of goods, money, people, information and ideas. Key idea: Globalisation intensifies worldwide connections and compresses time and space; hyperglobalists, sceptics and transformationalists disagree on its extent; it has long roots but new speed and scale.

How many international migrants were there in 2024?

About 303 million, around 3.7% of the world's population. From “Global migration” →

In plain English: Migration is a central part of globalisation. Key idea: About 303 million people (3.7%) were international migrants in 2024; push–pull, structural and network theories explain migration; remittances and brain drain show its mixed effects.

What is cultural imperialism?

The domination of one culture's media and values, especially Western, over others. From “Cultural homogenisation” →

In plain English: Cultural homogenisation is the idea that globalisation makes cultures more alike. Key idea: Homogenisation theorists argue global brands and media make cultures alike; cultural imperialism stresses Western dominance; critics point to active audiences, localisation and non-Western flows.

Which body enforces the Online Safety Act 2023?

Ofcom. From “Social media and society” →

In plain English: Social media platforms let people create and share content and connect with others. Key idea: Social media shape identity and relationships; they spread information and misinformation; the Online Safety Act 2023 regulates platforms; evidence on well-being is mixed.

What did Oxfam (2020) estimate about the richest 10%?

They were responsible for 52% of cumulative carbon emissions from 1990 to 2015. From “Consumerism and the environment” →

In plain English: High levels of consumption, especially in rich countries, drive resource use and emissions. Key idea: Consumerism drives resource use and emissions; the richest 10% caused 52% of cumulative emissions from 1990 to 2015 (Oxfam); green consumption helps but may individualise responsibility.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Globalisation and Social Change

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. The view that globalisation is creating a borderless world is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Hyperglobalist. Hyperglobalists see states losing power to markets.

  2. A company building a factory in another country is making… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Foreign direct investment. Investing in businesses or assets abroad is foreign direct investment (FDI).

  3. Supreme authority within a territory is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sovereignty. Sovereignty is supreme territorial authority.

  4. A researcher shows most trade happens within regions, not globally, and states still set key rules. This supports… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The sceptical view. Sceptics doubt the world economy is truly global.

  5. A developing country privatises utilities and cuts tariffs as a condition of an IMF loan. This reflects… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Washington Consensus. Market reforms tied to loans reflect the Washington Consensus.

  6. EU members agree to make trade policy jointly through EU institutions. This is an example of… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pooled sovereignty. Sharing decision-making in common institutions pools sovereignty.

  7. What is distinctive about contemporary globalisation compared with earlier eras? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its speed, scale and density of connections. Digital technology intensifies older connections.

  8. How has economic globalisation been linked to political discontent in rich countries? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Slow income growth for lower-middle groups. Losers of globalisation may turn to protest politics.

  9. Why might global civil society be unequal? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Well-funded NGOs from rich countries may dominate. Money and organisational resources shape whose voices are heard in global debates.

Key takeaways

  • Globalisation intensifies worldwide connections and compresses time and space; hyperglobalists, sceptics and transformationalists disagree on its extent; it has long roots but new speed and scale.
  • Economic globalisation integrates trade, investment, finance and production; neoliberalism and institutions like the IMF drove it; gains were uneven, as the elephant curve shows.
  • Political globalisation includes international organisations, regional bodies, international law and global civil society; states' sovereignty is transformed rather than erased; legitimacy and power remain contested.
  • Culture travels through media, migration and markets; Appadurai's scapes describe disjunctive flows; homogenisation, hybridisation and polarisation all occur, shaped by power.
  • Container shipping, aviation and the internet drove globalisation; 73.6% of people used the internet in 2025; the digital divide persists; technology can both reduce and reinforce inequality.
  • About 303 million people (3.7%) were international migrants in 2024; push–pull, structural and network theories explain migration; remittances and brain drain show its mixed effects.

Common mistakes in Globalisation and Social Change

  • Treating globalisation as a single, inevitable force. It is uneven and shaped by policy.
  • Assuming economic globalisation benefits everyone equally. Gains and losses are unevenly spread.
  • Assuming globalisation makes states powerless. States create and enforce many global rules.
  • Assuming cultural globalisation means Americanisation only. Flows run in many directions.
  • Assuming technology spreads equally. Access, skills and power shape who benefits.
  • Assuming most international migrants are refugees. Most move for work, family or study.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is globalisation?
    Globalisation is the growing interconnectedness of the world through flows of goods, money, people, information and ideas.
  2. Economic globalisation
    Economic globalisation is the integration of national economies through trade, investment, finance and production.
  3. Political globalisation
    Political globalisation is the growth of political authority, cooperation and activism beyond the nation state.
  4. Cultural globalisation
    Cultural globalisation is the worldwide flow of ideas, media, products and practices.
  5. Technological globalisation
    Technology has driven globalisation by making communication, transport and coordination faster and cheaper.
  6. Global migration
    Migration is a central part of globalisation.
  7. Global inequality and development
    Development is the process by which societies improve living standards and capabilities.
  8. Global capitalism
    Global capitalism describes a world economy in which capital, production and markets are organised across borders.
  9. Transnational corporations
    Transnational corporations (TNCs) operate in many countries and control much of world trade and investment.
  10. Global supply chains
    A global supply chain is the network of firms and workers that produce and deliver a product across countries.
  11. Cultural homogenisation
    Cultural homogenisation is the idea that globalisation makes cultures more alike.
  12. Cultural hybridisation
    Cultural hybridisation is the mixing of cultures to create new forms.
  13. Glocalisation
    Glocalisation describes how global products and ideas are adapted to local contexts.
  14. Digital society
    In a digital society, digital technologies shape everyday life, work, relationships and power.
  15. The network society
    Castells argued that information technology has produced a network society in which networks are the main form of social organisation.
  16. Social media and society
    Social media platforms let people create and share content and connect with others.
  17. Global social movements
    Global social movements organise across borders on issues such as climate change, trade justice and human rights.
  18. Climate change and society
    Climate change is a social as well as a physical problem: human activity causes it, and its impacts and solutions are shaped by inequality and politics.
  19. Environmental sociology
    Environmental sociology studies the relationship between society and the natural environment.
  20. Sustainable development
    Sustainable development aims to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  21. Consumerism and the environment
    High levels of consumption, especially in rich countries, drive resource use and emissions.
  22. Risk society
    Beck argued that late modern societies are increasingly organised around producing and managing risks they have created themselves.
  23. Modernity
    Modernity is the social order that emerged with industrialisation, capitalism, the nation state and Enlightenment thought.
  24. Postmodernity
    Postmodernity describes a proposed new era following modernity, marked by media saturation, consumer culture, fragmented identities and distrust of grand theories.
  25. Social transformation
    Social transformation refers to deep, long-term change in social structures and relationships.
  26. Future societies
    Sociologists study trends to think about possible futures, while recognising uncertainty.
