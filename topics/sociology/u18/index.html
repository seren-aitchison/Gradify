Key terms and formulas at a glance

Aggregate data
Data describing groups or places rather than individuals
Median
The middle value when data are ordered
Interquartile range
The spread of the middle 50% of values (Q3 − Q1)
Frequency distribution
A table or graph showing how many cases fall into each category or range
Population pyramid
A chart showing the age and sex structure of a population
Conditional probability
The probability of an event given that another has occurred
Sampling distribution
The distribution of an estimate across all possible random samples of a given size
Null hypothesis
The hypothesis of no difference or association in the population
p-value
The probability of a result at least as extreme as that observed, if the null hypothesis were true
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for a population figure based on a sample
Percentage-point difference
The arithmetic difference between two percentages
Correlation coefficient
A number from −1 to +1 showing the strength and direction of an association

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is aggregate data?

Data describing groups or places, such as a country's unemployment rate, rather than individuals. From “Types of social data” →

In plain English: Social data come in different forms: categorical or numerical, individual or aggregate, survey or administrative, structured or unstructured. Key idea: The type of data, its level of measurement and whether it describes individuals or groups determine which statistics and conclusions are appropriate.

What is conditional probability?

The probability of one event given that another has occurred. From “Probability for social research” →

In plain English: Probability underpins sampling and inference in social research. Key idea: Probabilities and conditional probabilities describe life chances, but interpreting them requires attention to base rates.

What is the difference between a percentage-point change and a percentage change?

Percentage points are the arithmetic difference between percentages; a percentage change is relative to the starting value. From “Effect sizes” →

In plain English: Effect sizes measure how big a difference or association is. Key idea: Effect sizes describe how large differences and associations are; percentage points, ratios and odds ratios each answer slightly different questions.

What does the Human Development Index combine?

Life expectancy, education and income. From “Social indicators” →

In plain English: Social indicators are statistics used to measure social conditions and track progress, from unemployment rates to life expectancy and wellbeing. Key idea: Social indicators measure social conditions; composite indices and wellbeing measures address the limits of GDP but involve value choices.

What is a survey weight?

A value adjusting each respondent's influence so the sample better represents the population. From “Survey data” →

In plain English: Large social surveys are the workhorses of quantitative sociology. Key idea: Large social surveys give high-quality, representative data for secondary analysis, provided weighting, survey design and question consistency are handled correctly.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Social Statistics and Data Science

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Housing tenure (owner, private renter, social renter) is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nominal. The tenure categories have no inherent order, so the variable is nominal.

  2. In a positively skewed distribution, the mean is usually… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Higher than the median. The long right tail pulls the mean upwards.

  3. The spread of the middle 50% of values is the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The IQR. The interquartile range is Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle half of the data.

  4. A researcher attaches each respondent's local area poverty rate to their survey record. What kind of variable is the poverty rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Aggregate data. It describes the area, not the individual.

  5. A news story says "the average worker earns £45,000", using the mean. What is the main problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High earners inflate the mean above typical pay. Skewed pay makes the mean unrepresentative of a typical worker.

  6. Two countries have the same median income, but one has a much larger 90/10 ratio. What does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Greater income inequality in that country. A larger ratio means a bigger gap between high and low incomes.

  7. Why shouldn't social class categories be averaged? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are categorical, not quantities. Class categories classify positions; averaging them treats labels as numbers.

  8. Why is the UK relative poverty line based on the median rather than the mean? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The median isn't pulled up by very high incomes. Using the mean would raise the line whenever top incomes rose, even if typical incomes didn't.

  9. Why might researchers report both the 90/10 ratio and the top 1% share? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They capture different parts of the distribution. The 90/10 ignores the very top; the top 1% share focuses on it.

Key takeaways

  • The type of data, its level of measurement and whether it describes individuals or groups determine which statistics and conclusions are appropriate.
  • The mean uses all values but is distorted by extremes; the median is the standard typical value for skewed social data such as income.
  • Measures of dispersion describe spread; ratio measures such as the 90/10 or 20/20 ratio describe inequality between parts of a distribution.
  • Frequency distributions summarise how cases are spread across categories, using counts, percentages and cumulative percentages.
  • Good visualisations match chart type to data and question, and show patterns honestly with clear axes, labels and sources.
  • Probabilities and conditional probabilities describe life chances, but interpreting them requires attention to base rates.

Common mistakes in Social Statistics and Data Science

  • Treating class categories as numbers and averaging them. Ordinal and nominal categories aren't quantities.
  • Reporting "average income" without saying whether it is the mean or the median. The two can differ greatly.
  • Using the standard deviation alone to describe highly skewed income. Percentile-based measures are more informative.
  • Comparing raw counts between groups of different sizes. Use percentages.
  • Using a pie chart for many categories or for change over time. Pie charts work only for a few parts of one whole.
  • Concluding that because a group has a higher rate of an outcome, most of its members experience it. A higher rate can still be a small proportion.

Lessons in this school

  1. Types of social data
    Social data come in different forms: categorical or numerical, individual or aggregate, survey or administrative, structured or unstructured.
  2. Central tendency
    Measures of central tendency summarise typical values.
  3. Dispersion
    Dispersion describes how spread out values are.
  4. Frequency distributions
    A frequency distribution shows how many cases fall into each category or range.
  5. Visualising social data
    Good charts make social patterns visible and honest.
  6. Probability for social research
    Probability underpins sampling and inference in social research.
  7. Sampling distributions
    If a survey were repeated many times with new random samples, the results would vary.
  8. Hypothesis testing
    Hypothesis testing assesses whether patterns in sample data are strong enough to infer patterns in the population.
  9. Statistical significance
    Statistical significance tells us whether a result would be surprising if there were no real effect, not whether it matters.
  10. Confidence intervals
    Confidence intervals give a range of plausible values for a population figure, showing how precise a survey estimate is.
  11. Effect sizes
    Effect sizes measure how big a difference or association is.
  12. Correlation
    Correlation measures how strongly two numerical variables are related.
  13. Regression
    Regression models how an outcome changes with one or more explanatory variables, allowing sociologists to estimate associations while holding other factors constant.
  14. Categorical data
    Much social data is categorical: class, ethnicity, tenure, voting.
  15. Chi-square tests
    The chi-square test of independence checks whether an association in a crosstabulation is larger than sampling chance would produce.
  16. Social indicators
    Social indicators are statistics used to measure social conditions and track progress, from unemployment rates to life expectancy and wellbeing.
  17. Measuring inequality: the Gini coefficient
    The Gini coefficient summarises inequality in a single number from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality).
  18. Measuring poverty
    How poverty is measured shapes how much of it we see.
  19. Demographic analysis
    Demographic analysis measures and explains population size, structure and change through births, deaths and migration.
  20. Census data
    A census counts every person and household in a country at one point in time.
  21. Survey data
    Large social surveys are the workhorses of quantitative sociology.
  22. Social network analysis in practice
    This lesson takes social network analysis from concepts to practice: collecting network data, choosing measures and interpreting results, with classic and contemporary examples.
  23. Computational sociology
    Computational sociology uses computers to model and analyse social processes, from agent-based simulations to large-scale analysis of digital data.
  24. Digital trace data
    Digital trace data are records left by online and digital activity: posts, searches, clicks, transactions and phone movements.
  25. Reproducible research
    Reproducible research means others can follow and repeat every step from raw data to published findings.

Social Statistics and Data Science: questions students ask

Why is median income usually reported rather than mean income?

Income is positively skewed, so the mean is pulled up by high incomes and doesn't represent a typical household.

What is the 90/10 ratio?

The income at the 90th percentile divided by the income at the 10th percentile.

What is a cumulative percentage?

The percentage of cases at or below a given category.

Which chart shows the age and sex structure of a population?

A population pyramid.
