Lessons in this school

  1. Types of social data
    Social data come in different forms: categorical or numerical, individual or aggregate, survey or administrative, structured or unstructured.
  2. Central tendency
    Measures of central tendency summarise typical values.
  3. Dispersion
    Dispersion describes how spread out values are.
  4. Frequency distributions
    A frequency distribution shows how many cases fall into each category or range.
  5. Visualising social data
    Good charts make social patterns visible and honest.
  6. Probability for social research
    Probability underpins sampling and inference in social research.
  7. Sampling distributions
    If a survey were repeated many times with new random samples, the results would vary.
  8. Hypothesis testing
    Hypothesis testing assesses whether patterns in sample data are strong enough to infer patterns in the population.
  9. Statistical significance
    Statistical significance tells us whether a result would be surprising if there were no real effect, not whether it matters.
  10. Confidence intervals
    Confidence intervals give a range of plausible values for a population figure, showing how precise a survey estimate is.
  11. Effect sizes
    Effect sizes measure how big a difference or association is.
  12. Correlation
    Correlation measures how strongly two numerical variables are related.
  13. Regression
    Regression models how an outcome changes with one or more explanatory variables, allowing sociologists to estimate associations while holding other factors constant.
  14. Categorical data
    Much social data is categorical: class, ethnicity, tenure, voting.
  15. Chi-square tests
    The chi-square test of independence checks whether an association in a crosstabulation is larger than sampling chance would produce.
  16. Social indicators
    Social indicators are statistics used to measure social conditions and track progress, from unemployment rates to life expectancy and wellbeing.
  17. Measuring inequality: the Gini coefficient
    The Gini coefficient summarises inequality in a single number from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality).
  18. Measuring poverty
    How poverty is measured shapes how much of it we see.
  19. Demographic analysis
    Demographic analysis measures and explains population size, structure and change through births, deaths and migration.
  20. Census data
    A census counts every person and household in a country at one point in time.
  21. Survey data
    Large social surveys are the workhorses of quantitative sociology.
  22. Social network analysis in practice
    This lesson takes social network analysis from concepts to practice: collecting network data, choosing measures and interpreting results, with classic and contemporary examples.
  23. Computational sociology
    Computational sociology uses computers to model and analyse social processes, from agent-based simulations to large-scale analysis of digital data.
  24. Digital trace data
    Digital trace data are records left by online and digital activity: posts, searches, clicks, transactions and phone movements.
  25. Reproducible research
    Reproducible research means others can follow and repeat every step from raw data to published findings.

Key terms

Aggregate data
Data describing groups or places rather than individuals
Median
The middle value when data are ordered
Interquartile range
The spread of the middle 50% of values (Q3 − Q1)
Frequency distribution
A table or graph showing how many cases fall into each category or range
Population pyramid
A chart showing the age and sex structure of a population
Conditional probability
The probability of an event given that another has occurred
Sampling distribution
The distribution of an estimate across all possible random samples of a given size
Null hypothesis
The hypothesis of no difference or association in the population
p-value
The probability of a result at least as extreme as that observed, if the null hypothesis were true
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for a population figure based on a sample
Percentage-point difference
The arithmetic difference between two percentages
Correlation coefficient
A number from −1 to +1 showing the strength and direction of an association
Regression coefficient
The predicted change in the outcome for a one-unit change in a predictor
Crosstabulation
A table showing the joint distribution of two categorical variables
