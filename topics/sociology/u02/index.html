Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three social changes that gave rise to sociology.

Any three of political revolution, industrialisation, urbanisation, secularisation and the spread of science. From “The origins of sociology” →

In plain English: Sociology emerged in 19th-century Europe as thinkers tried to understand the upheavals of revolution, industrialisation and urbanisation. Key idea: Sociology arose to explain the transition from traditional to modern society: revolution, industrial capitalism, cities and secularisation.

What is the superstructure in Marx's theory?

Institutions and ideas such as law, politics, religion and education, shaped by the economic base. From “Marx: historical materialism” →

In plain English: Historical materialism is Marx's theory that the way a society produces its material needs shapes its social relations, politics and ideas, and that history moves through conflict between classes. Key idea: For Marx, the economic base shapes society's institutions and ideas, and history moves through class conflict as forces and relations of production come into contradiction.

What are the two defining features of a social fact?

It is external to individuals and constraining on them. From “Durkheim: social facts” →

In plain English: Durkheim argued that sociology has its own subject matter, social facts, which must be studied as things and explained by other social facts. Key idea: Durkheim defined social facts as external and constraining, and argued they should be studied objectively and explained by other social facts.

What two social dimensions did Durkheim use to explain suicide rates?

Integration and regulation. From “Durkheim's Suicide” →

In plain English: In Suicide (1897), Durkheim set out to show that even the most personal act has social causes, by analysing official suicide statistics across groups and countries. Key idea: Durkheim explained variations in suicide rates by levels of social integration and regulation, producing four types of suicide.

What are Weber's three dimensions of inequality?

Class (market situation), status (social honour) and party (political power). From “Weber: class, status and party” →

In plain English: Weber argued that inequality has three distinct dimensions: class (economic position), status (social honour) and party (political power). Key idea: Weber's three dimensions, class, status and party, can vary independently, giving a multidimensional account of inequality.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Classical Sociological Theory

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In which century did sociology emerge as a discipline? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The 19th century. Sociology emerged in 19th-century Europe as thinkers responded to industrialisation and revolution.

  2. Which Enlightenment thinker compared laws and governments across societies in The Spirit of the Laws? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Montesquieu. Montesquieu's 1748 work used comparison to explain differences between societies.

  3. Which writer documented living conditions in Manchester's industrial slums in 1845? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Friedrich Engels. Engels's The Condition of the Working Class in England described Manchester's slums.

  4. A thinker in 1860 studies why people moving from villages to cities feel cut off from community. Which change is this thinker responding to? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Urbanisation. Rapid movement from countryside to cities was a defining change of the 19th century.

  5. Who coined the term sociology? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Auguste Comte. Auguste Comte coined the term sociology in the 1830s.

  6. A thinker argues that society must be rebuilt using science rather than religious authority, but worries about the loss of shared values. Which two influences does this combine? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Enlightenment reason and conservative concern for order. This combination is typical of Comte and Durkheim.

  7. Which is the strongest criticism of the standard story of sociology's origins? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It treats European development as universal and ignores colonialism and non-European thinkers. Postcolonial critics argue the canon misrepresents how modernity was made and who contributed to social thought.

  8. Which statement best evaluates the Enlightenment's legacy for sociology? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It gave sociology scientific ambitions and ideals of rights, but its universal claims excluded many people. A balanced view recognises both its contribution (reason, evidence, rights) and its exclusions.

  9. Which evidence most challenges Parsons's view that industrialisation created the nuclear family? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Historical records showing nuclear households were common before industrialisation. Laslett's and Anderson's work suggests the link between industrialisation and nuclear families is not straightforward.

Key takeaways

  • Sociology arose to explain the transition from traditional to modern society: revolution, industrial capitalism, cities and secularisation.
  • Sociology combines the Enlightenment's faith in reason and science with the conservative reaction's concern for order, community and the social bonds that hold society together.
  • Industrialisation reshaped work, class, cities, family and community, and each classical sociologist interpreted these changes through a different central concept.
  • Comte founded positivism: society should be studied scientifically, through observation, to discover laws that could guide social reorganisation.
  • For Marx, the economic base shapes society's institutions and ideas, and history moves through class conflict as forces and relations of production come into contradiction.
  • Marx defined class by ownership of the means of production; class conflict is built into capitalism, but ideology can prevent workers from recognising their shared interests.

Common mistakes in Classical Sociological Theory

  • Thinking sociology began with a single founder or date. Comte named it, but several thinkers in different countries developed sociological ideas at once.
  • Treating the Enlightenment as a single agreed doctrine. Its thinkers disagreed widely, about religion, politics and human nature.
  • Assuming industrialisation affected everyone the same way. Effects differed by class, gender, region and between countries.
  • Confusing Comte's positivism with logical positivism, a 20th-century philosophy of science developed by the Vienna Circle. They share a name and a respect for science but differ.
  • Thinking Marx said ideas never matter. He argued the economy is ultimately decisive; Engels later wrote that the superstructure also influences the base.
  • Equating Marx's classes with income groups. For Marx, class is about ownership and control of production, not how much someone earns.

Lessons in this school

  1. The origins of sociology
    Sociology emerged in 19th-century Europe as thinkers tried to understand the upheavals of revolution, industrialisation and urbanisation.
  2. The Enlightenment and social thought
    The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement that championed reason, science and progress.
  3. Industrialisation and social change
    Industrialisation transformed work, family, cities and class.
  4. Comte and positivism
    Auguste Comte named sociology and argued it should be a positive science, discovering laws of society as natural sciences discover laws of nature.
  5. Marx: historical materialism
    Historical materialism is Marx's theory that the way a society produces its material needs shapes its social relations, politics and ideas, and that history moves through conflict between classes.
  6. Marx: class conflict
    Marx argued that the history of all societies so far has been the history of class struggle.
  7. Marx: capitalism
    Marx's analysis of capitalism explains profit as the result of exploitation, through the concept of surplus value, and argues that capitalism is dynamic but prone to crisis.
  8. Marx: alienation
    Alienation is Marx's term for the way capitalism estranges workers from their work, their products, their own humanity and each other.
  9. Engels
    Friedrich Engels was Marx's closest collaborator and an important social researcher in his own right.
  10. Durkheim: social facts
    Durkheim argued that sociology has its own subject matter, social facts, which must be studied as things and explained by other social facts.
  11. Durkheim: social solidarity
    Durkheim's central question was what holds society together.
  12. Mechanical and organic solidarity
    Durkheim distinguished two types of solidarity: mechanical, based on likeness in simple societies, and organic, based on interdependence in complex ones.
  13. Durkheim: anomie
    Anomie is Durkheim's term for a condition of normlessness, in which rapid change weakens the rules that regulate people's desires.
  14. Durkheim's Suicide
    In Suicide (1897), Durkheim set out to show that even the most personal act has social causes, by analysing official suicide statistics across groups and countries.
  15. Weber: social action
    Max Weber defined sociology as the study of social action: behaviour to which individuals attach meaning and which takes account of others.
  16. Weber: Verstehen
    Verstehen, German for "understanding", is Weber's term for grasping the meaning of social action from the actor's point of view.
  17. Weber: rationalisation
    Rationalisation is Weber's term for the long-term process by which calculation, efficiency and rules replace tradition, emotion and magic in social life.
  18. Weber: bureaucracy
    Weber saw bureaucracy as the most technically efficient form of organisation and the characteristic institution of modern rational society.
  19. Weber: class, status and party
    Weber argued that inequality has three distinct dimensions: class (economic position), status (social honour) and party (political power).
  20. Simmel
    Georg Simmel studied the forms of social interaction that recur across different settings, from the significance of group size to the experience of city life and money.
  21. W. E. B. Du Bois
    W. E. B. Du Bois was a pioneering American sociologist who studied race, inequality and Black urban life with rigorous empirical methods.
  22. Harriet Martineau
    Harriet Martineau was a British writer and social theorist who wrote one of the first books on sociological method, translated Comte and analysed slavery, marriage and women's position.
  23. Classical debates compared
    The founders disagreed about what holds society together, what drives change, how society should be studied and whether modernity is progress.

Classical Sociological Theory: questions students ask

What question did Durkheim ask about modern society?

What holds a society together when people no longer share the same traditions.

What did the conservative reaction to the French Revolution stress?

Tradition, community, religion and authority as foundations of social order.

What did the 1851 census show for the first time?

More people in England and Wales lived in towns than in the countryside.

What are Comte's three stages of thought?

Theological, metaphysical and positive (scientific).
