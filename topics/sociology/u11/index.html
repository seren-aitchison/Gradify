Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How did Weber define power?

The chance of realising one's will even against the resistance of others. From “Power” →

In plain English: Power is the ability to get what you want, even against resistance. Key idea: Power is getting your way despite resistance; Lukes adds agenda-setting and preference-shaping to visible decisions; pluralist, elite and Marxist views disagree on who holds power.

What did Anderson mean by imagined communities?

Nations whose members never meet most others but imagine a shared bond. From “Nation states” →

In plain English: A nation state combines a state with a nation, a community that sees itself as sharing an identity. Key idea: Nation states join state and nation; Gellner and Anderson see nations as modern constructions, Smith stresses older roots; devolution and globalisation challenge nation states.

What is descriptive representation?

Representatives sharing the characteristics of those they represent. From “Political inequality” →

In plain English: Political inequality means some groups have more influence over decisions than others. Key idea: Representation is unequal by gender, class and ethnicity; money and access shape influence; Gilens and Page found US policy tracked elite preferences.

What did Putnam mean by Bowling Alone?

Declining group participation reducing social capital. From “Civil society” →

In plain English: Civil society is the space between the state, the market and the family where people organise voluntarily. Key idea: Civil society is voluntary organisation between state and market; Tocqueville and Putnam see it as strengthening democracy, Gramsci as a site of hegemony and resistance.

What is the democratic deficit?

A lack of democratic accountability in institutions such as global or regional bodies. From “Global governance” →

In plain English: Global governance is the set of institutions, rules and networks that manage problems crossing national borders. Key idea: Global governance includes the UN, IMF, World Bank, WTO and EU; liberals, realists and critical theorists disagree on its role; critics highlight a democratic deficit.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Politics and Power

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Power accepted as legitimate is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Authority. Weber distinguished legitimate authority from coercion.

  2. Obeying a hereditary chief because "it has always been this way" reflects… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Traditional authority. Custom is the basis of traditional authority.

  3. A system where people obey because they believe rulers have the right to rule has… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Legitimacy. Belief in the right to rule is legitimacy.

  4. A government avoids ever debating a proposal for a wealth tax by keeping it out of parliamentary business. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Non-decision-making. Keeping issues off the agenda is the second dimension of power.

  5. After its founder dies, a religious movement creates a written constitution and elected council. Weber would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Routinisation of charisma. Turning charisma into rules is routinisation.

  6. A government fails to fund public services during a recession and loses public support. Habermas would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A legitimation crisis. Failing to meet economic and welfare demands erodes mass loyalty to the state.

  7. Why is Lukes's third dimension criticised? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is hard to observe and assumes false consciousness. Claims of hidden power can be hard to test.

  8. Why might charisma in modern politics be called "mediated"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Media and image management help create it. Charisma can be built through media presentation.

  9. Why doesn't low trust necessarily mean a crisis of democracy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Critical citizens may still back democracy itself. People can distrust politicians but value democracy.

Key takeaways

  • Power is getting your way despite resistance; Lukes adds agenda-setting and preference-shaping to visible decisions; pluralist, elite and Marxist views disagree on who holds power.
  • Weber's authority types are traditional, charismatic and legal-rational; charisma is routinised over time; real systems mix the types.
  • Legitimacy comes from procedures, performance and values; Habermas describes legitimation crisis; ONS 2023 found low trust in government but higher trust in courts.
  • Weber defines the state by its monopoly of legitimate force; pluralist, Marxist, feminist and state-centred theories disagree about whose interests it serves.
  • Nation states join state and nation; Gellner and Anderson see nations as modern constructions, Smith stresses older roots; devolution and globalisation challenge nation states.
  • Marshall described civil, political and social rights; citizenship includes some and excludes others; debates continue over conditionality and who counts as a citizen.

Common mistakes in Politics and Power

  • Measuring power only through visible decisions. Agenda-setting and shaping beliefs are also power.
  • Treating ideal types as perfect descriptions. They are models for comparison.
  • Equating low trust with rejecting democracy. Critical citizens may support democracy while distrusting politicians.
  • Treating the state as only the government. It includes courts, police, military and bureaucracy.
  • Assuming every state contains one nation. Many are multinational.
  • Assuming everyone living in a country has full citizenship rights. Migrants and others may have fewer.

Lessons in this school

  1. Power
    Power is the ability to get what you want, even against resistance.
  2. Authority: traditional, charismatic and legal-rational
    Authority is power that people accept as legitimate.
  3. Legitimacy
    Legitimacy is the belief that those in power have the right to rule.
  4. The state
    The state is the set of institutions that make and enforce binding rules over a territory.
  5. Nation states
    A nation state combines a state with a nation, a community that sees itself as sharing an identity.
  6. Citizenship and rights
    Citizenship is membership of a political community, bringing rights and duties.
  7. Democracy
    Democracy means rule by the people, but it takes many forms.
  8. Political participation
    Political participation includes all the ways people try to influence politics, from voting to boycotts.
  9. Political inequality
    Political inequality means some groups have more influence over decisions than others.
  10. Political ideologies
    A political ideology is a set of ideas about how society is and should be organised.
  11. Social movements
    Social movements are sustained, organised collective efforts to bring about or resist social change.
  12. Collective action
    Collective action is people acting together to achieve shared goals.
  13. Civil society
    Civil society is the space between the state, the market and the family where people organise voluntarily.
  14. Protest
    Protest is public action to express opposition or demand change.
  15. Political institutions
    Political institutions are the formal and informal rules and organisations through which power is exercised.
  16. Bureaucracy and the state
    Modern states rely on large bureaucracies to make and deliver policy.
  17. Global governance
    Global governance is the set of institutions, rules and networks that manage problems crossing national borders.
  18. Power and knowledge
    Knowledge is not neutral: who produces it, and whose knowledge counts, is shaped by power.
  19. Political surveillance
    Political surveillance is the monitoring of political activity by states and other actors.
  20. Political sociology
    Political sociology studies the social bases of politics: how class, education, age and identity shape political attitudes and behaviour.
  21. Political change
    Political systems change through reform, revolution, democratisation and backsliding.

Politics and Power: questions students ask

What is legal-rational authority?

Authority based on rules and offices, where people obey the position rather than the person.

What is legitimacy?

The belief that those in power have the right to rule.

How did Weber define the state?

A community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory.

What are Marshall's three elements of citizenship?

Civil, political and social rights.
