Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a substantive definition of religion?

A definition based on the content of belief, such as belief in God or the supernatural. From “Defining religion” →

In plain English: Sociologists study religion as a social phenomenon, not to judge whether beliefs are true. Key idea: Substantive definitions focus on beliefs, functional definitions on what religion does, constructionist definitions on how people define it; each has strengths and blind spots.

What is a theodicy?

An explanation of why suffering and evil exist. From “Weberian approaches to religion” →

In plain English: Weber argued religious ideas can shape economic and social change. Key idea: Weber argued Calvinist predestination and asceticism helped produce the spirit of capitalism; theodicies shape action; ideas are one cause of change among others.

What did Heelas and Woodhead mean by subjective life?

Living by inner experience rather than external roles and authorities. From “Religious authority” →

In plain English: Religious authority is the recognised right to interpret beliefs and guide others. Key idea: Religious authority can be charismatic, traditional or legal-rational; individualism, diversity and scandals have weakened it; digital media reshape rather than eliminate it.

What is civil religion?

A belief system that attaches sacred qualities to the nation and unites people across faiths. From “Civil religion” →

In plain English: Civil religion is a set of beliefs, symbols and rituals that sacralise the nation and unite citizens across different faiths. Key idea: Bellah's civil religion sacralises the nation and unites people across faiths; Britain's national rituals combine Christian and civic elements; critics warn of exclusion and legitimation.

What is a theodicy of disprivilege?

A religious explanation of suffering, often with a promise of reward, that appeals to deprived groups. From “Religion and inequality” →

In plain English: Religion intersects with class, ethnicity and other inequalities. Key idea: Census 2021 shows inequalities between religious groups in housing and employment; Weber linked deprivation to theodicies of disprivilege; religious hate crime rose in 2026.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Religion

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Durkheim defined religion in terms of beliefs and practices relating to… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sacred things. Durkheim centred religion on sacred things set apart from the everyday.

  2. Durkheim argued that worshipping a totem is really worshipping… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Society itself. The totem symbolised the clan, so worship reinforced society.

  3. The belief that monarchs rule by God's will is an example of religion… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Legitimating inequality. Divine right made inequality seem God-given.

  4. A researcher counts only groups that believe in a god as religious. This uses a… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Substantive definition. Defining by belief content is substantive.

  5. A community holds a large funeral ritual after a tragedy, and people say they feel closer afterwards. Functionalists would say this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Strengthens solidarity after a crisis. Shared ritual helps a society cope with loss and renews solidarity among members.

  6. Priests in a poor region help farmworkers organise against landlords. A neo-Marxist would see this as… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Religion acting with relative autonomy. Clergy acting as organic intellectuals challenge power.

  7. Why does the choice of definition affect secularisation research? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Narrow definitions may find decline while broad ones find change. What counts as religion shapes the trend found.

  8. Why is functionalism less convincing in religiously diverse societies? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Religion may divide groups rather than unite them. Diversity can produce conflict as well as solidarity.

  9. Why does the US civil rights movement challenge classical Marxist views? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Churches helped organise resistance to inequality. Black churches were central to the movement.

Key takeaways

  • Substantive definitions focus on beliefs, functional definitions on what religion does, constructionist definitions on how people define it; each has strengths and blind spots.
  • Durkheim saw religion as society worshipping itself and creating solidarity; Malinowski and Parsons stressed coping and values; critics point to conflict, diversity and power.
  • Marx saw religion as ideology and the opium of the people; neo-Marxists stress hegemony, relative autonomy and hope; liberation theology shows religion can resist oppression.
  • Weber argued Calvinist predestination and asceticism helped produce the spirit of capitalism; theodicies shape action; ideas are one cause of change among others.
  • Secularisation is the declining social significance of religion; the 2021 Census showed Christian identity falling to 46.2% and no religion rising to 37.2%; critics argue religion is changing and globally strong.
  • Religion has dimensions of belief, belonging and practice that don't always align; each measure has limitations; lived religion highlights everyday practice.

Common mistakes in Religion

  • Assuming religion always means belief in God. Many traditions and definitions don't require it.
  • Assuming religion always unites. In diverse societies it can also divide.
  • Equating Marxist views with dismissing religion as unimportant. Marx saw it as a powerful response to suffering.
  • Thinking Weber said religion alone caused capitalism. He said it was one necessary factor among several.
  • Treating census identity as a measure of practice. Identity, belief and practice can differ.
  • Using one measure, such as attendance, to stand for all of religion. Belief and belonging may tell a different story.

Lessons in this school

  1. Defining religion
    Sociologists study religion as a social phenomenon, not to judge whether beliefs are true.
  2. Functionalist approaches to religion
    Functionalists explain religion by the functions it performs for society and individuals.
  3. Marxist approaches to religion
    Marxists see religion as ideology that helps maintain class inequality.
  4. Weberian approaches to religion
    Weber argued religious ideas can shape economic and social change.
  5. Secularisation
    Secularisation is the decline in the social significance of religion.
  6. Belief and practice
    Religion involves both what people believe and what they do.
  7. Religious institutions
    Religious organisations take different forms, from large established churches to small sects.
  8. Religious authority
    Religious authority is the recognised right to interpret beliefs and guide others.
  9. Religious identity
    Religion can be a key source of identity, especially for minority communities.
  10. Religious pluralism
    Religious pluralism describes societies with many religions and the acceptance of that diversity.
  11. Fundamentalism
    Fundamentalism describes movements that seek to return to what they see as the basic truths of a religion, often in reaction to modern change.
  12. Civil religion
    Civil religion is a set of beliefs, symbols and rituals that sacralise the nation and unite citizens across different faiths.
  13. Religion and social change
    Is religion a conservative force or a force for change?
  14. Religion and politics
    Religion and politics interact through laws, parties, voting and public debate.
  15. Religion and gender
    Religion has often been shaped by patriarchy, yet women are more religious than men in many societies.
  16. Religion and inequality
    Religion intersects with class, ethnicity and other inequalities.
  17. Religion and globalisation
    Globalisation spreads religions across borders, connects believers worldwide and changes how religion is practised.
  18. New religious movements
    New religious movements (NRMs) are religious or spiritual groups that have emerged relatively recently.
  19. Contemporary religious change
    Religion in contemporary societies is changing in form: becoming more individual, spiritual, mediated and diverse.

Religion: questions students ask

What did Durkheim mean by collective effervescence?

The shared excitement and unity produced by collective ritual.

Why did Marx call religion "the opium of the people"?

Because it eased the pain of exploitation while hiding its causes.

What is predestination?

The Calvinist belief that God has already chosen who will be saved.

How did Wilson define secularisation?

The process by which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance.
