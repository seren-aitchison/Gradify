Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the organic analogy in functionalism?

Society is compared to a living body, with institutions working together like organs to keep the whole stable. From “Functionalism” →

In plain English: Functionalism sees society as a system of interrelated parts, each contributing to the stability and survival of the whole. Key idea: Functionalism explains institutions by the functions they serve for society as a whole, assuming value consensus and a tendency towards stability.

Name two ideological state apparatuses.

Any two of education, the family, the media, religion. From “Neo-Marxism” →

In plain English: Neo-Marxists revised Marx to explain why revolution didn't happen in advanced capitalist societies, giving more weight to culture, ideology and the state. Key idea: Neo-Marxists explain capitalism's stability through ideology and consent (hegemony) as well as economic power, giving more scope to culture and agency.

What is the natural attitude?

The everyday stance of taking the social world for granted as real and shared. From “Phenomenology” →

In plain English: Phenomenological sociology studies how people experience and understand the everyday world, using shared, taken-for-granted categories. Key idea: Phenomenological sociology studies how people experience the world through taken-for-granted typifications and stocks of shared knowledge.

What did the Combahee River Collective Statement argue?

That Black women face interlocking systems of oppression that must all be dismantled. From “Black feminism” →

In plain English: Black feminism analyses how racism, sexism and class oppression combine in Black women's lives, and criticises feminisms that treat white middle-class women's experience as universal. Key idea: Black feminism analyses interlocking oppressions of race, gender and class and challenges feminism to recognise differences among women.

What is habitus?

Durable dispositions acquired from social position that shape how people think, perceive and act. From “Bourdieu: habitus, capital and field” →

In plain English: Pierre Bourdieu explained how social inequality is reproduced through taste, education and everyday dispositions, using the concepts of habitus, capital and field. Key idea: Bourdieu explained the reproduction of inequality through habitus, cultural capital and competition within fields, legitimised as merit.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Contemporary Sociological Theory

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which assumption is central to functionalism? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Social order rests on shared values. Functionalists see value consensus as the basis of order.

  2. In AGIL, which function is mainly met by the economy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Adaptation. The economy obtains and distributes resources from the environment.

  3. An intended, recognised consequence of an institution is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Manifest function. Manifest functions are intended and recognised.

  4. A sociologist argues that religion persists because it binds people to shared values. Which perspective is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Functionalism. Explaining an institution by its integrative function is functionalist.

  5. What analogy did functionalists use for society? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A living organism with interdependent parts. Each institution was seen as an organ with a function for the whole.

  6. A teacher marks every pupil's work by the same criteria, regardless of personal relationships. Which pattern variable is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Universalism. Applying the same standards to all is universalism.

  7. What is the teleological criticism of functionalism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It explains an institution's existence by its effects. Saying something exists because it is useful confuses consequences with causes.

  8. What was C. Wright Mills' main criticism of Parsons? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    His grand theory was abstract and remote from real problems. Mills satirised Parsons' abstraction in The Sociological Imagination.

  9. Why did Merton favour theories of the middle range? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They can be tested against evidence. Limited theories connect more directly with empirical research.

Key takeaways

  • Functionalism explains institutions by the functions they serve for society as a whole, assuming value consensus and a tendency towards stability.
  • Parsons modelled society as a system meeting four functional needs (AGIL), with modern roles governed by universalistic, achievement-based norms.
  • Merton made functionalism more flexible and empirical: institutions have intended and unintended consequences, some harmful, and needs can be met in alternative ways.
  • Conflict theory explains society through inequality, power and struggle between groups, with Marxist and non-Marxist versions.
  • Neo-Marxists explain capitalism's stability through ideology and consent (hegemony) as well as economic power, giving more scope to culture and agency.
  • Critical theory criticises capitalist modernity's culture and reason with the aim of emancipation, influencing media studies and social philosophy.

Common mistakes in Contemporary Sociological Theory

  • Assuming functionalists claim every institution is good. Merton recognised dysfunctions, and functions can benefit the system while harming individuals.
  • Treating AGIL as describing four separate buildings or organisations. They are functional problems that institutions help solve, often several at once.
  • Treating latent functions as secret or sinister. They are simply consequences that aren't intended or recognised.
  • Equating conflict theory only with Marxism. Weber, Dahrendorf, Coser and Mills developed conflict theories without Marx's economic focus.
  • Thinking hegemony means simple brainwashing. Gramsci stressed active, negotiated consent that can be challenged.
  • Assuming critical theorists dismissed all popular culture as worthless without argument. Their critique concerned how culture is produced and how it shapes consciousness.

Lessons in this school

  1. Functionalism
    Functionalism sees society as a system of interrelated parts, each contributing to the stability and survival of the whole.
  2. Parsons
    Talcott Parsons built the most ambitious functionalist theory of society, the social system.
  3. Merton: manifest and latent functions
    Robert Merton revised functionalism into a more flexible, testable approach.
  4. Conflict theory
    Conflict theory sees society as shaped by inequality and struggle between groups with different interests and power.
  5. Neo-Marxism
    Neo-Marxists revised Marx to explain why revolution didn't happen in advanced capitalist societies, giving more weight to culture, ideology and the state.
  6. Critical theory and the Frankfurt School
    Critical theory, developed by the Frankfurt School, combined Marx, Weber and Freud to criticise modern capitalist society, its culture and its forms of reason.
  7. Symbolic interactionism: Mead and Blumer
    Symbolic interactionism explains society as built from interaction, in which people create and act on shared meanings.
  8. Goffman and dramaturgy
    Erving Goffman studied the fine detail of everyday interaction, from self-presentation to stigma and total institutions.
  9. Ethnomethodology
    Ethnomethodology studies the methods people use to make sense of everyday life and produce social order moment by moment.
  10. Phenomenology
    Phenomenological sociology studies how people experience and understand the everyday world, using shared, taken-for-granted categories.
  11. Social constructionism
    Social constructionism argues that much of what we treat as natural or objective, from gender to mental illness, is created through social processes.
  12. Liberal feminism
    Liberal feminism seeks equal rights and opportunities for women and men through legal and policy reform, education and changing attitudes.
  13. Radical feminism
    Radical feminism argues that patriarchy, men's domination of women, is the most fundamental form of inequality, rooted in family, sexuality and violence as well as public life.
  14. Marxist feminism
    Marxist feminism links women's oppression to capitalism: women's unpaid domestic labour and position in the labour market serve capitalist interests.
  15. Black feminism
    Black feminism analyses how racism, sexism and class oppression combine in Black women's lives, and criticises feminisms that treat white middle-class women's experience as universal.
  16. Intersectionality
    Intersectionality analyses how categories such as race, gender, class, sexuality and disability combine to shape distinctive experiences of privilege and disadvantage.
  17. Postmodernism
    Postmodernism argues that contemporary societies have moved beyond modernity: grand theories have lost credibility, identities are fluid and media images blur with reality.
  18. Poststructuralism
    Poststructuralism holds that meaning is unstable and produced through language and discourse, and that subjects are shaped by the discourses that describe them.
  19. Foucault
    Michel Foucault analysed how power operates through knowledge, institutions and everyday practices, shaping how people are seen and how they see themselves.
  20. Bourdieu: habitus, capital and field
    Pierre Bourdieu explained how social inequality is reproduced through taste, education and everyday dispositions, using the concepts of habitus, capital and field.
  21. Giddens and structuration theory
    Anthony Giddens' structuration theory argues that structure and agency are not opposites but two sides of the same process.
  22. Bauman
    Zygmunt Bauman analysed how modern society has become "liquid": relationships, identities and institutions are fluid, temporary and uncertain.
  23. Beck
    Ulrich Beck argued that late modern societies are "risk societies", organised around manufactured risks produced by modernisation itself.
  24. Theoretical synthesis
    Contemporary sociologists often try to combine insights from different theories rather than choose one.

Contemporary Sociological Theory: questions students ask

What does AGIL stand for?

Adaptation, goal attainment, integration and latency (pattern maintenance).

What is a latent function?

An unintended and unrecognised consequence of a social practice.

What did Dahrendorf see as the main basis of conflict in modern societies?

Authority relations within organisations, between those who command and those who obey.

What is hegemony?

Domination achieved through consent, by shaping culture and common sense, as well as through force.
