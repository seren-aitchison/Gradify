Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define sociology.

The systematic study of social life, social groups, institutions and societies. From “What is sociology?” →

In plain English: Sociology is the systematic study of social life: how societies are organised, how they change and how they shape, and are shaped by, the people in them. Key idea: Sociology explains social patterns by looking beyond individual choices to the social structures, relationships and cultures that shape them.

Name a theory that tries to link micro and macro levels.

Giddens's structuration theory (or Bourdieu's habitus and field). From “Micro and macro sociology” →

In plain English: Sociologists study social life at different scales: micro sociology studies face-to-face interaction; macro sociology studies large-scale structures and whole societies. Key idea: Micro sociology studies interaction and meaning; macro sociology studies structures and whole societies; many questions need both.

What did Tönnies mean by Gesellschaft?

Association: impersonal relationships based on contract and self-interest, typical of modern cities and markets. From “Social relationships” →

In plain English: Social life is made of relationships: between friends, family members, colleagues, strangers and citizens. Key idea: Social relationships range from primary to secondary and from community to association; modern societies mix both.

Name four agents of secondary socialisation.

Any four of education, peer groups, media, religion, the workplace. From “Secondary socialisation” →

In plain English: Secondary socialisation continues throughout life as people learn the norms of wider society through school, peer groups, the media, religion and work. Key idea: Secondary socialisation continues through life, through education, peers, media, religion and work, building on and sometimes challenging primary socialisation.

What does the Thomas theorem state?

If people define situations as real, they are real in their consequences. From “Social interaction” →

In plain English: Social interaction is how people act towards and respond to one another. Key idea: Interaction creates and sustains social reality through shared meanings and performances; definitions of situations have real consequences.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Sociology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the best definition of sociology? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The systematic study of social life, groups and institutions. Sociology studies social life and institutions; psychology, economics and history have different main focuses.

  2. Which professional body represents sociologists in the UK? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The British Sociological Association. The BSA, founded in 1951, publishes journals and the Statement of Ethical Practice.

  3. Mills's sociological imagination connects… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Biography and history. It links individual lives to wider social structure and historical change.

  4. Drop-out rates are higher among students who work over 20 hours a week. Which explanation is most sociological? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Financial pressure linked to background shapes students' ability to study. A sociological explanation links the pattern to social conditions, here financial circumstances, rather than to individual traits alone.

  5. A researcher studies how care leavers, ex-prisoners and ex-service personnel are over-represented among homeless people. Which discipline's focus is this mainly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Sociology. Explaining an outcome through institutions and social pathways is a sociological focus.

  6. Thousands of graduates in a region can't find jobs after a factory closes. In Mills's terms this is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A public issue. Mass unemployment caused by economic change is a public issue, explained by social structure.

  7. Which is the strongest reply to the claim that sociology is "just common sense"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Common sense is contradictory and untested, while sociology checks claims against systematic evidence. The difference is method: sociology tests claims and reveals patterns invisible from individual experience.

  8. Which is the strongest argument that sociology's several competing theories are a strength? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Each theory reveals aspects of social life that others miss. Different theories highlight consensus or conflict, structure or meaning; combining them can give a fuller picture, while evidence still decides between specific claims.

  9. Which is the most accurate criticism of the sociological imagination? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is a perspective rather than a testable theory, so it needs evidence to apply. Mills offers a way of thinking; whether a trouble is really an issue must be shown with evidence.

Key takeaways

  • Sociology explains social patterns by looking beyond individual choices to the social structures, relationships and cultures that shape them.
  • Sociology is a discipline with shared concepts, competing theories and a range of methods, distinguished by its focus on social structures, institutions and relationships.
  • The sociological imagination connects personal troubles to public issues, showing how private lives are shaped by social structure and history.
  • Micro sociology studies interaction and meaning; macro sociology studies structures and whole societies; many questions need both.
  • Sociology holds that individuals and society make each other: society shapes individuals through social facts and socialisation, while individuals sustain and change society through interaction.
  • Structure shapes and constrains action, and action reproduces or changes structure: most contemporary sociologists study how the two interact.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Sociology

  • Thinking a sociological explanation denies individual choice. It shows how choices are made within social conditions, which makes some choices easier or more likely than others.
  • Treating sociology as only about "society" in the abstract. Much sociology studies concrete things: a school, a hospital ward, a workplace, a neighbourhood.
  • Using "public issue" to mean "a problem that's in the news". Mills meant a problem rooted in how society is organised, whether or not it gets attention.
  • Assuming micro means "unimportant" or macro means "more scientific". Each answers different questions with appropriate methods.
  • Treating "society" as a thing with a mind of its own. Sociologists use the word for patterned relationships and institutions, not a separate being.
  • Writing "structure determines everything" or "people are free to choose anything". Both extremes fail to explain why patterns are strong but not absolute.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is sociology?
    Sociology is the systematic study of social life: how societies are organised, how they change and how they shape, and are shaped by, the people in them.
  2. Sociology as a discipline
    Sociology is an academic discipline with its own concepts, theories, methods and professional bodies.
  3. The sociological imagination
    C. Wright Mills called the ability to connect personal experience to wider social forces "the sociological imagination".
  4. Micro and macro sociology
    Sociologists study social life at different scales: micro sociology studies face-to-face interaction; macro sociology studies large-scale structures and whole societies.
  5. The individual and society
    Is society just a collection of individuals, or something more?
  6. Structure and agency
    How far are our lives shaped by social structures, and how far by our own choices?
  7. Social institutions
    Institutions are the established patterns of social life that organise how societies meet their needs: the family, education, religion, the economy, politics and law, health and the media.
  8. Social relationships
    Social life is made of relationships: between friends, family members, colleagues, strangers and citizens.
  9. Norms and values
    Values are shared ideas about what is good and desirable; norms are the expected behaviours that follow from them.
  10. Culture
    Culture is the shared way of life of a group: its values, norms, beliefs, language, knowledge and material objects.
  11. Primary socialisation
    Primary socialisation is the first and most intensive stage of learning the culture of a society, usually within the family.
  12. Secondary socialisation
    Secondary socialisation continues throughout life as people learn the norms of wider society through school, peer groups, the media, religion and work.
  13. Roles and status
    Status is a social position; a role is the behaviour expected of someone in that position.
  14. Identity
    Identity is our sense of who we are and how others see us.
  15. Groups and communities
    Much of social life happens in groups: families, friendship circles, teams, organisations and communities.
  16. Social interaction
    Social interaction is how people act towards and respond to one another.
  17. Sociological research: an introduction
    Sociologists study society using a range of methods, from large surveys to long-term participant observation.
  18. Contemporary sociology
    Contemporary sociology studies a world reshaped by globalisation, digital technology, climate change, migration and changing inequalities.
  19. Applying sociology
    Sociology is used in policy, public services, business, campaigning and everyday citizenship.

Foundations of Sociology: questions students ask

How did the UK's total fertility rate change between 1964 and 2024?

It fell from 2.93 to 1.55 children per woman (World Bank data).

How does sociology's focus differ from psychology's?

Sociology focuses on groups, institutions and social structure; psychology on individual mind and behaviour.

Who coined the term "sociological imagination", and when?

C. Wright Mills, in 1959.

What does micro sociology study?

Small-scale, face-to-face social interaction and the meanings people create.
