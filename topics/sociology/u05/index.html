Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is social stratification?

A structured, lasting ranking of groups with unequal access to resources, power and prestige. From “Social stratification” →

In plain English: Social stratification is the division of a society into layers with unequal access to resources, power and prestige. Key idea: Stratification is a structured, lasting ranking of groups; functionalists see it as motivating talent, while conflict theorists see it as reflecting power and ownership.

What is a mobility table?

A table comparing people's class origins with their class destinations. From “Social mobility” →

In plain English: Social mobility is movement between positions in a stratification system. Key idea: Social mobility is movement between positions, measured by comparing origins and destinations in mobility tables.

What is the difference between income and wealth?

Income is a flow of money over time; wealth is a stock of assets minus debts. From “Wealth inequality” →

In plain English: Wealth is the stock of assets a household owns, minus its debts. Key idea: Wealth is a stock of assets; in Great Britain in 2018–2020 the top 10% of households held 43% of wealth, and inheritance and returns on capital help it persist.

What is the ecological fallacy?

Wrongly inferring individual characteristics from area-level data. From “Deprivation” →

In plain English: Deprivation is lacking things that are widely regarded as necessities. Key idea: Deprivation is going without necessities; the IMD measures area deprivation across seven domains, but area data doesn't describe every individual.

How far behind were disadvantaged pupils by the end of secondary school in 2019, according to EPI?

Around 18 months of learning. From “Educational inequality” →

In plain English: Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education. Key idea: Educational outcomes differ strongly by disadvantage; material resources, cultural capital and school processes all contribute.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Social Stratification and Inequality

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who argued that stratification motivates talented people to fill important roles? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Davis and Moore. Davis and Moore offered the classic functionalist theory in 1945.

  2. Feudal Europe's nobility, clergy and commoners formed… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An estate system. Estates were groups with different legal rights.

  3. In Marx's theory, the class that owns the means of production is the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Bourgeoisie. The bourgeoisie owns capital; the proletariat sells its labour.

  4. A society where most people stay in the layer they were born into is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A closed system. Little movement between layers marks a closed system.

  5. A ruler claims authority comes directly from God. This is an example of… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Legitimation. Claiming divine authority presents the hierarchy as sacred, which legitimates it.

  6. Workers vote for policies that favour employers because they believe it is "common sense". Gramsci would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Hegemony. Ruling ideas accepted as common sense are hegemonic.

  7. Which is the strongest criticism of functionalist stratification theory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It assumes rewards match importance. Rewards may reflect power rather than functional importance.

  8. How does meritocratic ideology legitimate class inequality? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It presents inequality as deserved. Claiming rewards reflect merit makes inequality seem fair.

  9. Why did Wright develop the idea of contradictory class locations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Many jobs don't fit a two-class model. Managers and professionals sit between owners and workers.

Key takeaways

  • Stratification is a structured, lasting ranking of groups; functionalists see it as motivating talent, while conflict theorists see it as reflecting power and ownership.
  • Slavery, caste, estate and class systems differ in openness and legitimation; modern class systems are legally open but still shaped by birth.
  • Marxism defines class by ownership and sees exploitation and conflict at its core; neo-Marxists adapt it to complex modern occupations.
  • Weber saw stratification as class, status and party, with class defined by market situation shaping life chances.
  • Class is measured through occupation or through combined capitals; whether class has declined as an identity or as a predictor is debated.
  • Social mobility is movement between positions, measured by comparing origins and destinations in mobility tables.

Common mistakes in Social Stratification and Inequality

  • Treating stratification as differences between individuals. It refers to structured inequality between groups, built into institutions.
  • Assuming slavery and caste are only historical. Forced labour and caste discrimination persist today.
  • Assuming Marxist class analysis is only about factory workers. Neo-Marxists apply it to managers, professionals and service workers.
  • Thinking Weber rejected the importance of property. He saw property as central, but not the only basis of class.
  • Concluding class has disappeared because people talk about it less. Class still strongly predicts health, education and income.
  • Assuming lots of movement means equal chances. Many people may move while chances remain very unequal.

Lessons in this school

  1. Social stratification
    Social stratification is the division of a society into layers with unequal access to resources, power and prestige.
  2. Social hierarchy
    Societies have organised hierarchy in different ways: slavery, caste, estates and class.
  3. Marxist theories of class
    Marx argued class is defined by relationship to the means of production and that class conflict drives history.
  4. Weberian theories of class
    Weber saw stratification as having three dimensions: class, status and party.
  5. Contemporary class analysis
    Contemporary class analysis asks how class should be measured today.
  6. Social mobility
    Social mobility is movement between positions in a stratification system.
  7. Absolute and relative mobility
    Absolute mobility is how many people move; relative mobility is how unequal people's chances are.
  8. Intergenerational and intragenerational mobility
    Intergenerational mobility compares children with parents; intragenerational mobility follows changes within one person's career.
  9. Meritocracy
    A meritocracy rewards people according to ability and effort rather than background.
  10. Life chances
    Life chances are the opportunities a person has to improve their quality of life.
  11. Wealth inequality
    Wealth is the stock of assets a household owns, minus its debts.
  12. Income inequality
    Income inequality is how unevenly income is shared across a population.
  13. Absolute poverty
    Absolute poverty is lacking the resources to meet basic physical needs.
  14. Relative poverty
    Relative poverty is having too little to take part in the normal life of one's society.
  15. Social exclusion
    Social exclusion is being shut out from the social, economic and political life of society.
  16. Deprivation
    Deprivation is lacking things that are widely regarded as necessities.
  17. Status
    Status is social honour or prestige.
  18. Occupational inequality
    Occupational inequality is unequal access to jobs, pay and progression.
  19. Housing inequality
    Housing inequality is unequal access to secure, affordable and decent homes.
  20. Health inequality
    Health inequalities are systematic differences in health between social groups.
  21. Educational inequality
    Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education.
  22. Digital inequality
    Digital inequality is unequal access to and use of digital technologies.
  23. Regional inequality
    Regional inequality is unequal economic and social conditions between parts of a country.
  24. Global inequality
    Global inequality is inequality between countries and between all the world's people.
  25. Social reproduction
    Social reproduction is how inequality is passed from one generation to the next.
  26. Welfare and inequality
    Welfare states redistribute resources and protect people against risks.

What makes a caste system closed?

Status is fixed at birth, with restrictions on marriage and occupation.

What is surplus value?

The value workers produce beyond their wages, taken by owners as profit.

What are Weber's three dimensions of stratification?

Class, status and party.

What are Bourdieu's three forms of capital?

Economic, cultural and social capital.
