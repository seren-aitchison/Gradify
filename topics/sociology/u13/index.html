Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is time discipline?

Training workers to organise their activity by the clock rather than by tasks or seasons. From “Industrial society” →

In plain English: Industrial society is organised around factory production, wage labour and mass employment in manufacturing. Key idea: Industrial society centres on factory production, wage labour and clock time; Taylorism and Fordism rationalised work; classical sociologists analysed exploitation, solidarity and rationalisation.

What did Braverman mean by deskilling?

Management separating planning from doing, reducing the skill in workers' jobs. From “The division of labour” →

In plain English: The division of labour is how work is split into specialised tasks and roles. Key idea: The division of labour is technical, social, gendered and international; Durkheim saw solidarity, Marx and Braverman control and deskilling; gendered divisions shape pay gaps.

What did Sennett mean by the corrosion of character?

Flexible, short-term work undermining long-term commitment, loyalty and identity. From “Occupational identity” →

In plain English: Work shapes how people see themselves and how others see them. Key idea: Work provides status, purpose and identity; occupational socialisation and communities build shared identities; flexible work may weaken them, though meaning persists.

What is a zero-hours contract?

A contract with no guaranteed hours of work. From “Precarity” →

In plain English: Precarity describes work that is insecure, unpredictable and lacking protections. Key idea: Precarious work includes temporary, agency and zero-hours work; globalisation, deregulation and union decline drove its growth; it harms security and well-being; policy responses are contested.

What is a buyer-driven commodity chain?

A supply chain led by retailers and brands that design and market products made by suppliers. From “Global production” →

In plain English: Many goods are produced through chains of firms spread across several countries. Key idea: Global production splits design and manufacture across countries; buyer-driven chains concentrate value in brands; Rana Plaza exposed dangerous conditions; binding agreements are more effective than voluntary codes.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Work and Economy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Taylor's method of breaking jobs into simple, timed tasks is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Scientific management. Taylor called his approach scientific management.

  2. Flexible production for niche markets using IT and multi-skilled workers is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Post-Fordism. Post-Fordism replaced mass production with flexibility.

  3. The value workers create beyond their wages is, for Marx… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Surplus value. Marx called this surplus value, the source of profit.

  4. A factory introduces bells, clocking-in and fines for lateness to replace flexible working rhythms. Thompson would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Imposing time discipline. Clock-based control of workers' activity is what Thompson called time discipline.

  5. A company keeps a small team of permanent engineers and hires agency staff when demand rises. This illustrates… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The flexible firm. Core and periphery workers define the flexible firm.

  6. A country relies on flexible labour markets, weak unions and shareholder finance. Hall and Soskice would classify it as… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A liberal market economy. Market coordination defines liberal market economies.

  7. Why have feminists criticised classical accounts of industrial work? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They ignored unpaid domestic labour, mostly done by women. Domestic labour was left out of many accounts.

  8. Why do critics say post-industrial labour markets are polarised? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Growth is at the top and bottom, with fewer middle jobs. Middle-skill jobs have declined relative to others.

  9. What does the varieties of capitalism approach suggest about economic outcomes? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Institutions and political choices shape them. Different institutions produce different outcomes.

Key takeaways

  • Industrial society centres on factory production, wage labour and clock time; Taylorism and Fordism rationalised work; classical sociologists analysed exploitation, solidarity and rationalisation.
  • Employment shifted from manufacturing to services; Bell stressed knowledge work; post-Fordism and the flexible firm describe new production; critics highlight routine service work and polarisation.
  • Capitalism combines private ownership, markets, wage labour and profit; Marx saw exploitation, liberals efficiency; liberal and coordinated market economies differ; financialisation reshapes priorities.
  • The labour market is measured by employment, unemployment and inactivity; in mid-2026 UK employment was 75.3% and unemployment 4.7%; dual and segmented labour market theories explain unequal job quality.
  • The division of labour is technical, social, gendered and international; Durkheim saw solidarity, Marx and Braverman control and deskilling; gendered divisions shape pay gaps.
  • Marx saw alienation from product, process, species-being and others; Blauner linked it to technology in an inverted U-curve; Hochschild extended it to emotional labour in services.

Common mistakes in Work and Economy

  • Assuming industrial work replaced all other work. Domestic and agricultural work continued, often unrecognised.
  • Assuming all service jobs are skilled knowledge work. Many are routine and closely monitored.
  • Treating capitalism as identical everywhere. Institutions create very different varieties.
  • Assuming falling unemployment always means more people in work. Inactivity may have risen instead.
  • Treating the division of labour as purely technical. It also reflects power, gender and global inequality.
  • Treating alienation as just job dissatisfaction. For Marx it is an objective condition of capitalist work.

Work and Economy mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Industrial society
    Industrial society is organised around factory production, wage labour and mass employment in manufacturing.
  2. Post-industrial society
    Post-industrial society is one where services and knowledge, rather than manufacturing, dominate employment.
  3. Capitalism
    Capitalism is an economic system based on private ownership, markets and wage labour, aimed at profit.
  4. Labour markets
    A labour market is where workers sell their labour and employers buy it.
  5. The division of labour
    The division of labour is how work is split into specialised tasks and roles.
  6. Alienation at work
    Alienation describes workers' estrangement from their work, its products, other people and themselves.
  7. Workplace hierarchy
    Workplaces are organised into hierarchies of authority, pay and status.
  8. Bureaucracy and organisations
    Most paid work happens inside formal organisations.
  9. Professionalisation
    Professions such as medicine and law hold high status, autonomy and rewards.
  10. Occupational identity
    Work shapes how people see themselves and how others see them.
  11. Work and gender
    Men and women experience paid and unpaid work differently.
  12. Work and class
    Occupation is the main basis for measuring social class.
  13. Work and ethnicity
    People from different ethnic groups have different experiences of employment, pay and progression.
  14. Unemployment
    Unemployment means being without paid work while looking for it.
  15. Precarity
    Precarity describes work that is insecure, unpredictable and lacking protections.
  16. The gig economy
    The gig economy is work arranged through digital platforms for short tasks or "gigs".
  17. Automation and artificial intelligence
    Automation and artificial intelligence (AI) are changing which tasks humans do at work.
  18. Workplace surveillance
    Employers increasingly use technology to monitor workers.
  19. Trade unions and industrial relations
    Trade unions are organisations of workers that bargain collectively with employers.
  20. Global production
    Many goods are produced through chains of firms spread across several countries.
  21. Consumer society
    In consumer societies, buying goods and services is central to everyday life and identity.
  22. Economic inequality
    Economic inequality concerns the unequal distribution of income and wealth.
  23. Welfare capitalism
    Welfare states protect people against risks such as unemployment, illness and old age.
  24. The future of work
    Work is changing through technology, demography, climate policy and new ways of organising.
