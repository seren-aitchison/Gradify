Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between crime and deviance?

Crime breaks the criminal law; deviance breaks social norms. From “Crime and deviance” →

In plain English: Crime is behaviour that breaks the criminal law; deviance is behaviour that breaks social norms. Key idea: Crime breaks the law and deviance breaks norms; police data and the CSEW measure crime differently; the CSEW estimated 9.7 million incidents in the year ending June 2026.

Name Merton's five adaptations.

Conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism and rebellion. From “Strain theory” →

In plain English: Strain theory explains crime as a response to the gap between socially approved goals and the legitimate means to achieve them. Key idea: Merton's strain arises from a gap between success goals and legitimate means, producing five adaptations; Agnew broadened strain to negative experiences and emotions.

What are left realism's three causes of crime?

Relative deprivation, subculture and marginalisation. From “Realist criminology” →

In plain English: Realist criminologists argue crime is a real problem that harms people, especially the most vulnerable, and that practical solutions are needed. Key idea: Right realists focus on control, rational choice and broken windows; left realists link crime to relative deprivation, subculture and marginalisation, using the square of crime.

Name four types of corporate crime.

Financial, consumer, employee and environmental crimes. From “Corporate crime” →

In plain English: Corporate crime is illegal or harmful acts committed by organisations for their benefit. Key idea: Corporate crime includes financial, consumer, employee and environmental offences; it causes major harm but is hard to prosecute; Grenfell shows the consequences of corporate and regulatory failure.

Name the five purposes of sentencing in the Sentencing Act 2020.

Punishment, reduction of crime, reform and rehabilitation, protection of the public, and reparation. From “Punishment” →

In plain English: Punishment is the deliberate imposition of pain or loss on offenders. Key idea: Sentencing aims include punishment, crime reduction, rehabilitation, protection and reparation; Durkheim, Marxists and Foucault explain punishment socially; certainty deters more than severity.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Crime and Deviance

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Crime that is never reported or recorded is called… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The dark figure of crime. It is the hidden crime outside official statistics.

  2. Who argued that social groups create deviance by making rules? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Howard Becker. Becker made this argument in Outsiders (1963).

  3. Durkheim's term for normlessness during rapid change is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Anomie. Anomie describes the weakening of shared rules during rapid social change.

  4. Police recorded crime rises after new recording rules are introduced, while the CSEW is stable. What is the likely explanation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Recording practices changed. Changes in recording can raise police figures without more crime.

  5. Why is homicide used to compare crime between countries? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is defined and recorded more consistently than most crimes. Most crimes are defined and recorded differently, but homicide is usually recorded.

  6. A campaign group successfully lobbies for a new law banning a substance. Becker would call them… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Moral entrepreneurs. Campaigners for new rules are moral entrepreneurs.

  7. Why do sociologists ask whose crimes are counted? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Some crimes are under-recorded. Official data can hide crimes of the powerful.

  8. Which is the main criticism of the social construction approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Some harms are near-universal. Near-universal condemnation suggests limits to relativism.

  9. Which is the main criticism of functionalist theory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can't explain who offends. It explains crime's existence, not who commits it.

Key takeaways

  • Crime breaks the law and deviance breaks norms; police data and the CSEW measure crime differently; the CSEW estimated 9.7 million incidents in the year ending June 2026.
  • Crime is constructed through laws, enforcement and reactions that vary across time and place; moral entrepreneurs and power shape definitions.
  • Durkheim argued crime is normal and has functions such as boundary maintenance and change; anomie explains rises in crime during rapid change.
  • Marxists see capitalism as criminogenic and law as serving ruling-class interests through selective enforcement; neo-Marxists add a fully social theory.
  • Interactionists see deviance as created through interaction and reaction; primary deviance becomes secondary through labelling; agencies' decisions shape statistics.
  • Labels can become master statuses and lead to deviant careers; control can amplify deviance; reintegrative shaming offers an alternative.

Common mistakes in Crime and Deviance

  • Treating police recorded crime as an exact measure of crime. Much crime is never reported or recorded.
  • Assuming "socially constructed" means crime causes no real harm. Constructed categories still describe harmful acts with real victims.
  • Reading functionalism as approving of crime. It argues some crime is inevitable and has social effects, not that crime is good.
  • Assuming Marxists only explain working-class crime. They also stress crimes of the powerful.
  • Thinking interactionists deny that anyone commits crime. They focus on how reactions shape who is defined as criminal.
  • Assuming every labelled person becomes more deviant. Some reject labels; effects depend on context.

Lessons in this school

  1. Crime and deviance
    Crime is behaviour that breaks the criminal law; deviance is behaviour that breaks social norms.
  2. The social construction of crime
    Crime is socially constructed: what counts as crime depends on laws, enforcement and social reactions that change over time and differ between places.
  3. Functionalist theories of crime
    Functionalists argue crime is normal and can even be useful for society.
  4. Marxist theories of crime
    Marxists argue crime and the law reflect the interests of the ruling class under capitalism.
  5. Interactionist theories of crime
    Interactionists study how deviance is created through social interaction and reactions.
  6. Labelling theory
    Labelling theory explains how being labelled as deviant can create a deviant identity and further deviance.
  7. Strain theory
    Strain theory explains crime as a response to the gap between socially approved goals and the legitimate means to achieve them.
  8. Subcultural theories
    Subcultural theories explain crime as a collective response by groups whose values differ from the mainstream.
  9. Differential association
    Differential association theory proposes that criminal behaviour is learned through interaction with others.
  10. Social learning and crime
    Social learning theory explains crime through observation, imitation and reinforcement.
  11. Control theory
    Control theories ask not why people commit crime but why most people don't.
  12. Feminist criminology
    Feminist criminology examines how gender shapes offending, victimisation and justice, and criticises "malestream" criminology.
  13. Critical criminology
    Critical criminology examines how power, inequality and the state shape crime, laws and justice.
  14. Realist criminology
    Realist criminologists argue crime is a real problem that harms people, especially the most vulnerable, and that practical solutions are needed.
  15. Postmodern criminology
    Postmodern criminology questions grand theories of crime and examines how crime is shaped by identity, consumption, media and risk in late modern society.
  16. Crime and social class
    Official statistics suggest working-class people are more likely to be convicted of crime.
  17. Crime and gender
    Men commit most recorded crime, especially violence.
  18. Crime and ethnicity
    Some ethnic minority groups are over-represented in the criminal justice system.
  19. Youth crime
    Young people, especially young men, are over-represented in recorded crime, but most grow out of offending.
  20. White-collar crime
    White-collar crime is crime committed by respectable people in the course of their occupations.
  21. Corporate crime
    Corporate crime is illegal or harmful acts committed by organisations for their benefit.
  22. Organised crime
    Organised crime is serious crime planned and carried out by groups for profit.
  23. Cybercrime
    Cybercrime is crime committed using computers and the internet.
  24. Victimology
    Victimology studies victims of crime: who becomes a victim, how victimisation affects people and how the justice system treats victims.
  25. Policing
    The police are the main formal agency of social control and the gateway to the criminal justice system.
  26. The criminal justice system
    The criminal justice system (CJS) is the set of agencies that respond to crime, from police to prisons.
  27. Prisons
    Prison is the most severe punishment used in the UK.
  28. Punishment
    Punishment is the deliberate imposition of pain or loss on offenders.
  29. Rehabilitation
    Rehabilitation aims to change offenders so they stop offending.
  30. Social control
    Social control covers all the ways societies encourage conformity and respond to deviance.
  31. Surveillance
    Surveillance is the monitoring of people's behaviour to control, manage or protect them.
  32. Moral panics
    A moral panic is an exaggerated public reaction to a group or behaviour seen as a threat to society.
  33. Crime and the media
    The media shape how people understand crime.
  34. Crime prevention
    Crime prevention aims to stop crime before it happens.

Crime and Deviance: questions students ask

What is a moral entrepreneur?

Someone who campaigns to create new rules or laws.

Why did Durkheim think crime is normal?

Not everyone is equally committed to shared values, so some crime exists in all societies.

What does criminogenic mean?

Tending to cause crime.

What is the difference between primary and secondary deviance?

Primary deviance isn't publicly labelled; secondary deviance results from being labelled.
