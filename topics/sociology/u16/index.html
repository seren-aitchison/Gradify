Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the social determinants of health?

The social and economic conditions, such as income, education, housing and work, that shape health. From “Social determinants of health” →

In plain English: Health is shaped by the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age. Key idea: Social determinants include income, education, housing, work and environment; Dahlgren and Whitehead's model shows layers of influence; outer conditions shape individual behaviour.

What is biographical disruption?

Chronic illness disrupting routines, relationships, future plans and sense of self. From “The experience of illness” →

In plain English: Sociologists study how people experience and make sense of illness, especially long-term conditions. Key idea: Bury's biographical disruption and Williams's narrative reconstruction explain how illness affects identity; Corbin and Strauss describe illness, everyday and biographical work; Frank identifies restitution, chaos and quest narratives.

What are the rights and duties of Parsons's sick role?

Exemption from normal duties and no blame, but a duty to want to get well and seek competent help. From “Patients and power” →

In plain English: Relationships between patients and doctors involve power and communication. Key idea: Parsons's sick role sets rights and duties for sick people; models range from paternalism to shared decision-making; the expert patient and Montgomery ruling increase patient power, but inequalities remain.

What percentage of UK adults smoked cigarettes in 2025?

9.8%. From “Health behaviours” →

In plain English: Health behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and exercise influence health, but they are shaped by social circumstances. Key idea: UK smoking fell to 9.8% in 2025 but remains higher in deprived groups; behaviours are shaped by stress, habitus and commercial factors; structural policies often work better than individual campaigns.

What is multimorbidity?

Having two or more long-term health conditions. From “Ageing and health” →

In plain English: Older people use health services more, but ageing is not the same as illness. Key idea: Life expectancy at 65 was 21.2 (women) and 18.7 (men) years in 2022–24; multimorbidity is common; disadvantage accumulates over the life course; ageism can lead to under-treatment.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Health and Medicine

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Smoking, diet and exercise are examples of… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Lifestyle factors. Individual behaviours are lifestyle factors.

  2. Ill health causing people to move down the class structure is the… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Social selection explanation. Illness causing downward mobility is selection.

  3. Studying how the medical profession gained its power is sociology… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Of medicine. Studying medicine as an institution is sociology of medicine.

  4. A worker in insecure night shifts eats irregularly and sleeps poorly. The rainbow model would explain this through… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Working conditions shaping lifestyle. Outer conditions shape individual behaviour.

  5. A government gives every family support but offers extra help to those in the poorest areas. Marmot would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Proportionate universalism. Universal provision scaled to need is proportionate universalism.

  6. A researcher argues that dangerous workplaces and pollution from factories cause illness for profit. This is a… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Marxist perspective. Linking ill health to capitalism is Marxist.

  7. Why do critics say the social determinants approach can be hard to act on? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is so broad that priorities are unclear. Its breadth can make it hard to decide which policies to target first.

  8. Why do cultural and structural explanations interact? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Behaviours like smoking are shaped by circumstances. Material conditions shape health behaviours.

  9. Why do sociologists argue understanding power improves health care? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Power shapes access, diagnosis and experience. Recognising power helps address inequality in care.

Key takeaways

  • Social determinants include income, education, housing, work and environment; Dahlgren and Whitehead's model shows layers of influence; outer conditions shape individual behaviour.
  • In 2018–2020, the life expectancy gap by deprivation was 9.7 years for males and 7.9 for females, with healthy life expectancy gaps of about 19 years; the Black Report's four explanations remain influential; Marmot recommended proportionate universalism.
  • Medical sociology studies health, illness and care as social; functionalist, interactionist, Marxist, feminist and Foucauldian perspectives offer different insights.
  • The biomedical model treats disease as biological; the social model stresses social causes and meanings (McKeown, Illich); the biopsychosocial model combines both.
  • Bury's biographical disruption and Williams's narrative reconstruction explain how illness affects identity; Corbin and Strauss describe illness, everyday and biographical work; Frank identifies restitution, chaos and quest narratives.
  • Medicalisation defines non-medical problems as medical; drivers include the profession, pharmaceutical companies and consumers; it can bring help but also over-diagnosis and neglect of social causes.

Common mistakes in Health and Medicine

  • Assuming health depends mainly on individual choices and health care. Social conditions shape both.
  • Explaining health inequalities only by individual lifestyles. Lifestyles are shaped by material conditions.
  • Treating illness as purely biological. Its meaning, distribution and treatment are social.
  • Rejecting medicine entirely. The social model challenges biomedicine's limits, not its achievements.
  • Assuming all chronic illness disrupts identity in the same way. Experiences vary by age, class and prior hardship.
  • Assuming medicalisation is always imposed by doctors. Patients and companies also drive it.

Lessons in this school

  1. Social determinants of health
    Health is shaped by the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age.
  2. Health inequalities
    Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable differences in health between social groups.
  3. Medical sociology
    Medical sociology studies health, illness and health care as social phenomena.
  4. Medical and social models of health
    The biomedical model sees disease as a biological fault to be fixed by medicine, while social models stress social causes and meanings.
  5. The experience of illness
    Sociologists study how people experience and make sense of illness, especially long-term conditions.
  6. Medicalisation
    Medicalisation is the process by which non-medical problems come to be defined and treated as medical.
  7. The medical profession
    The medical profession has high status, autonomy and power over health care.
  8. Healthcare institutions
    Healthcare institutions organise how care is funded and delivered.
  9. Patients and power
    Relationships between patients and doctors involve power and communication.
  10. Disability and the social model
    Disability can be understood as an individual impairment or as a product of social barriers.
  11. Stigma and health
    Stigma is a mark of disgrace that sets a person apart.
  12. Mental health and social control
    Mental health is an area where medicine has the power to define abnormality and detain people.
  13. Health behaviours
    Health behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and exercise influence health, but they are shaped by social circumstances.
  14. Public health
    Public health aims to protect and improve the health of whole populations.
  15. Health policy
    Health policy shapes how health care is funded, organised and delivered.
  16. Global health
    Global health studies health issues that cross borders and inequalities between countries.
  17. Ageing and health
    Older people use health services more, but ageing is not the same as illness.
  18. Gender and health
    Men and women differ in health, illness and use of services.
  19. Race and health inequalities
    Health differs between ethnic groups in ways linked to deprivation, racism and access to care.
  20. Digital healthcare
    Digital technology is changing how people access and receive health care.

Health and Medicine: questions students ask

What is healthy life expectancy?

The number of years a person is expected to live in good health.

What is the difference between sociology in and of medicine?

In medicine helps solve medical problems; of medicine studies medicine as a social institution.

What is iatrogenesis?

Harm caused by medical treatment.

What is medicalisation?

The process by which non-medical problems become defined and treated as medical problems.
