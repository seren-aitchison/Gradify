Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between sex and gender?

Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender to social roles and expectations. From “Sex and gender” →

In plain English: Sociologists distinguish sex, usually referring to biological characteristics, from gender, the social meanings attached to being a woman or a man. Key idea: Sociologists distinguish biological sex from socially constructed gender; cross-cultural and historical change shows gender roles are shaped by society.

What did Moss-Racusin et al. find?

Faculty rated an identical CV as more competent and offered more pay when it was named John. From “Gender stereotypes” →

In plain English: Gender stereotypes are generalised beliefs about the characteristics of women and men. Key idea: Gender stereotypes are descriptive and prescriptive beliefs that shape self-perception, choices and judgements; structural changes can reduce their effects.

What is dual-systems theory?

Walby's view that patriarchy and capitalism are two interacting systems. From “Feminist theory” →

In plain English: Feminist theory explains gender inequality and how to change it. Key idea: Feminist theory includes liberal, radical, Marxist and Black feminism, which explain gender inequality differently; intersectional critiques broadened it.

What is a symmetrical family?

A family in which partners' roles in paid work, housework and leisure are similar. From “Gender and the family” →

In plain English: Families are central sites where gender roles, power and care are organised. Key idea: The domestic division of labour remains unequal despite change; women bear more responsibility and emotion work, and power over money is often unequal.

What is heteronormativity?

The assumption that heterosexuality and binary gender are normal, with institutions built around it. From “Heteronormativity” →

In plain English: Heteronormativity is the assumption that heterosexuality is normal and natural, and the social arrangements built on it. Key idea: Heteronormativity treats heterosexuality as the default, shaping everyday interaction, institutions and law, and can cause minority stress.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Gender and Sexuality

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who popularised the sex–gender distinction in British sociology? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ann Oakley. Oakley's 1972 book set out the distinction.

  2. Who wrote about "doing gender"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    West and Zimmerman. West and Zimmerman's 1987 article introduced the idea.

  3. Calling a little girl "pretty" and a boy "strong" is an example of… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Verbal appellations. Gendered descriptions are verbal appellations.

  4. In one society both men and women are expected to be gentle and nurturing. What does this suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Gender expectations are culturally shaped. Variation across societies points to social shaping.

  5. A job requiring long, inflexible hours assumes workers have no caring duties. Acker would call this… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A gendered organisation. The "ideal worker" assumption is gendered.

  6. Adults offer a doll to a baby dressed in pink and a hammer to the same baby in blue. This shows… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Expectations shape treatment. The same baby received different treatment.

  7. Why has Mead's fieldwork been criticised? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Her findings may be overstated. Later anthropologists questioned the neatness of her contrasts.

  8. Which is a criticism of strong social constructionism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It may underplay biology. Critics argue biology also shapes some patterns.

  9. Why might peer policing be powerful in gender socialisation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Children face mockery for breaking norms. Fear of being teased by friends encourages children to conform to gender norms.

Key takeaways

  • Sociologists distinguish biological sex from socially constructed gender; cross-cultural and historical change shows gender roles are shaped by society.
  • Gender is constructed through everyday interaction, gendered institutions and language; constructionism explains change but debates continue about biological influences.
  • Gender is learned through manipulation, canalisation, language and the influence of schools, peers and media, but children actively interpret and negotiate gender norms.
  • Traditional gender roles assigned paid work to men and care to women; functionalists saw complementarity, feminists inequality; roles are changing but unpaid work remains unequal.
  • Gender stereotypes are descriptive and prescriptive beliefs that shape self-perception, choices and judgements; structural changes can reduce their effects.
  • Gender identity is distinct from sex and sexual orientation; it is recognised in UK law in specific ways, measured in the Census 2021, and the subject of active debate.

Common mistakes in Gender and Sexuality

  • Assuming every difference between women and men is either fully biological or fully social. Most outcomes involve both.
  • Thinking "socially constructed" means "not real". Social constructs have real effects on people's lives.
  • Assuming gender socialisation is a one-way process. Children actively learn, police and challenge gender norms.
  • Assuming equal employment means equal roles. Unpaid work and care are still unevenly shared.
  • Assuming stereotypes only affect people who believe them. They also shape institutions, judgements and self-perception unconsciously.
  • Confusing gender identity with sexual orientation. They are different aspects of identity.

Lessons in this school

  1. Sex and gender
    Sociologists distinguish sex, usually referring to biological characteristics, from gender, the social meanings attached to being a woman or a man.
  2. The social construction of gender
    Social constructionists argue that gender is produced through social processes rather than simply expressing biology.
  3. Gender socialisation
    Gender socialisation is the process through which people learn gender norms and roles.
  4. Gender roles
    Gender roles are the behaviours and responsibilities expected of women and men.
  5. Gender stereotypes
    Gender stereotypes are generalised beliefs about the characteristics of women and men.
  6. Gender identity in society
    Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender.
  7. Gender performativity: Butler
    Judith Butler argued that gender is performative: it is produced through repeated acts rather than expressing an inner essence.
  8. Masculinities
    Sociologists study masculinities: the different ways of being a man.
  9. Hegemonic masculinity
    Connell's concept of hegemonic masculinity explains how one form of masculinity becomes dominant and legitimates men's power.
  10. Feminist theory
    Feminist theory explains gender inequality and how to change it.
  11. Patriarchy
    Patriarchy is a system of social structures and practices in which men dominate women.
  12. Gender inequality
    Gender inequality is unequal access to resources, power and opportunities based on gender.
  13. Gender and work
    Work is a central site of gender inequality, from pay and segregation to harassment and care.
  14. Gender and education
    Gender shapes educational achievement, subject choices and experiences.
  15. Gender and the family
    Families are central sites where gender roles, power and care are organised.
  16. Gender and politics
    Gender shapes who holds political power, how political issues are defined and how people participate.
  17. Gender and the media
    Media representations shape and reflect ideas about gender.
  18. Sexuality as a social phenomenon
    Sociologists study sexuality not only as biology but as something shaped by society, culture, law and history.
  19. Sexual identities
    Sexual identity is how people understand and describe their own sexuality.
  20. Heteronormativity
    Heteronormativity is the assumption that heterosexuality is normal and natural, and the social arrangements built on it.
  21. LGBTQ+ sociology
    LGBTQ+ sociology studies the lives, communities, movements and inequalities of lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans and queer people.
  22. Gender and intersectionality
    Intersectionality examines how gender combines with race, class, sexuality, disability and other social divisions.
  23. Gender-based violence
    Gender-based violence is violence directed at people because of their gender, or that affects one gender disproportionately.
  24. Current debates in gender and sexuality
    Gender and sexuality remain areas of active social and political debate.

Gender and Sexuality: questions students ask

What does "doing gender" mean?

Producing gender through everyday behaviour, with others holding people accountable to norms.

What is manipulation in Oakley's account?

Encouraging or discouraging behaviour according to a child's gender.

What are Parsons's instrumental and expressive roles?

Instrumental is the breadwinner role; expressive is the caring, emotional role.

What is the difference between descriptive and prescriptive stereotypes?

Descriptive say what women and men are like; prescriptive say what they should be like.
