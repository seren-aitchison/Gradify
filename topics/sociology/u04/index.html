Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between material and non-material culture?

Material culture is physical objects; non-material culture is ideas, beliefs, values and norms. From “Material and non-material culture” →

In plain English: Culture is the shared way of life of a group, including both objects and ideas. Key idea: Culture includes material objects and non-material ideas, which shape each other; non-material culture can lag behind technological change.

What is incorporation?

The mainstream absorbing and neutralising a counterculture, for example by commodifying its style. From “Countercultures” →

In plain English: A counterculture rejects key values and norms of the dominant culture and offers an alternative. Key idea: Countercultures oppose central mainstream values; they may be incorporated through commodification or controlled through law, and some of their values become mainstream.

What are Hall's three decoding positions?

Dominant, negotiated and oppositional. From “The media” →

In plain English: The media is a powerful agent of socialisation, shaping knowledge, norms and identities. Key idea: Media socialisation ranges from direct effects to active interpretation; representations and agenda-setting shape norms, and digital media adds new dynamics.

What is the difference between sex and gender?

Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender to social roles and expectations. From “Gender identity” →

In plain English: Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender. Key idea: Gender is learned and performed through socialisation and interaction; masculinities form a hierarchy; understandings of gender identity are changing and debated.

What is consumer culture?

A culture in which buying and using goods is central to identity and social life. From “Consumer culture” →

In plain English: Consumer culture is a culture in which buying and using goods is central to identity and social life. Key idea: Consumer culture makes consumption central to identity; critics see ideology and exclusion, while others see freedom and creativity.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Culture, Identity and Socialisation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an example of non-material culture? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A belief in fairness. Beliefs are ideas rather than physical objects, so they are non-material culture.

  2. A belief that honesty is important is an example of a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Value. Values are broad ideas about what is desirable.

  3. A court fine is an example of a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Formal negative sanction. Courts are official bodies applying punishments, so a fine is a formal negative sanction.

  4. Laws on online privacy develop years after social media spreads. This illustrates… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cultural lag. Norms and laws catching up slowly with new technology is cultural lag.

  5. Younger people raised in economic security care more about the environment than older generations. Which theory explains this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Postmaterialism. Inglehart linked security to postmaterialist values.

  6. A student who pushes into a queue gets tutted at. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An informal negative sanction. Tutting is everyday disapproval from ordinary people: an informal negative sanction.

  7. Which is a criticism of Ogburn's idea of cultural lag? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ideas can drive technological change. Values and beliefs can also lead change, rather than always lagging behind.

  8. Why do conflict theorists question value consensus? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dominant values may serve powerful groups. Shared values may reflect power rather than agreement.

  9. Why did Garfinkel break norms deliberately? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    To reveal hidden rules of interaction. Breaching shows norms that usually go unnoticed.

Key takeaways

  • Culture includes material objects and non-material ideas, which shape each other; non-material culture can lag behind technological change.
  • Values are shared ideas about what is desirable; they differ across societies along traditional–secular and survival–self-expression dimensions and change with security.
  • Norms range from folkways to mores to laws and are enforced by formal and informal, positive and negative sanctions; they vary and change.
  • Subcultures have distinctive values and norms within a wider culture; the CCCS saw youth subcultures as resistance, while post-subcultural theorists stress fluid neo-tribes.
  • Countercultures oppose central mainstream values; they may be incorporated through commodification or controlled through law, and some of their values become mainstream.
  • Culture is transmitted vertically, horizontally and obliquely through socialisation and language; digital globalisation is changing how culture spreads.

Common mistakes in Culture, Identity and Socialisation

  • Treating culture as only "high culture" such as art and opera. Sociologically, culture is a whole way of life, including everyday practices.
  • Assuming everyone in a society holds the same values. Values vary by age, class, religion and region.
  • Assuming norms are only laws. Most norms are unwritten and enforced informally.
  • Assuming subcultures always reject the mainstream. Many share most mainstream values and differ in style or interests.
  • Calling every distinctive group a counterculture. Most groups are subcultures that share mainstream values.
  • Assuming culture is passed on unchanged. Each generation reinterprets and changes what it receives.

Lessons in this school

  1. Material and non-material culture
    Culture is the shared way of life of a group, including both objects and ideas.
  2. Values
    Values are shared ideas about what is good, right and desirable.
  3. Norms and sanctions
    Norms are shared rules for behaviour, and sanctions are the rewards and punishments that enforce them.
  4. Subcultures
    A subculture is a group with distinctive values and norms within a wider culture.
  5. Countercultures
    A counterculture rejects key values and norms of the dominant culture and offers an alternative.
  6. Cultural transmission
    Cultural transmission is how culture is passed between people and across generations.
  7. The family as an agent of socialisation
    The family is usually the first and most influential agent of socialisation.
  8. Education as an agent of socialisation
    Schools teach not only subjects but also values, norms and ways of behaving.
  9. Peer groups
    Peer groups are people of similar age and status.
  10. The media
    The media is a powerful agent of socialisation, shaping knowledge, norms and identities.
  11. Religion as an agent of socialisation
    Religion teaches beliefs, values and moral codes and shapes identity and community.
  12. Personal and social identity
    Identity is how people see themselves and how others see them.
  13. National identity
    National identity is a sense of belonging to a nation.
  14. Ethnic identity
    Ethnic identity is a sense of belonging to a group sharing culture, ancestry or history.
  15. Gender identity
    Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender.
  16. Class identity
    Class identity is how people see their own social class and how class shapes their sense of self.
  17. Cultural identity
    Cultural identity is a sense of belonging based on shared culture, such as language, traditions, religion or lifestyle.
  18. Hybrid identities
    Hybrid identities combine elements from different cultures into new forms.
  19. Globalised identities
    Globalisation connects people across the world and creates new sources of identity.
  20. Consumer culture
    Consumer culture is a culture in which buying and using goods is central to identity and social life.
  21. Popular culture
    Popular culture is the culture enjoyed by large numbers of people, such as music, television, sport and social media.
  22. Cultural change
    Cultures change through technology, migration, social movements and economic change.
  23. Cultural diversity
    Cultural diversity is the existence of many cultures within a society.
  24. Multiculturalism
    Multiculturalism refers both to cultural diversity and to policies that recognise and support it.

Culture, Identity and Socialisation: questions students ask

What is the difference between values and norms?

Values are broad principles; norms are specific rules that put them into practice.

What is the difference between folkways and mores?

Folkways are everyday customs; mores carry strong moral significance.

What is a subculture?

A group with distinctive values, norms or styles within a wider culture.

How does a counterculture differ from a subculture?

A counterculture actively opposes central mainstream values; a subculture mainly differs from them.
