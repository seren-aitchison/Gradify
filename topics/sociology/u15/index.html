Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is urbanisation?

Growth in the share of a population living in towns and cities. From “Urbanisation” →

In plain English: Urbanisation is the growth in the share of people living in towns and cities. Key idea: 57.8% of the world's population was urban in 2025 (83.3% in the UK); industrialisation drove early urbanisation, while migration and natural increase drive it today; informal settlements are a major challenge.

What is the index of dissimilarity?

The share of a group that would need to move for it to be evenly spread across areas. From “Segregation” →

In plain English: Residential segregation is the separation of groups into different neighbourhoods by income, ethnicity or religion. Key idea: Segregation separates groups by income, ethnicity or religion; it results from constraint and choice; British evidence suggests growing dispersal despite claims of "parallel lives".

Name two causes of suburbanisation in Britain.

Railways, mass housebuilding, new towns, car ownership, rising incomes (any two). From “Suburbanisation” →

In plain English: Suburbanisation is the growth of residential areas on the edges of cities. Key idea: Suburbs grew with railways, housebuilding, new towns and cars; they were seen as uniform and family-centred but are increasingly diverse; counter-urbanisation moves people from cities to rural areas.

Why do death rates fall in stage 2?

Better food, sanitation, clean water and medicine. From “The demographic transition” →

In plain English: The demographic transition model describes how birth and death rates change as societies develop. Key idea: The demographic transition moves from high to low birth and death rates; death rates fall first, causing growth; birth rates fall with development; fertility ranged from 4.4 (Nigeria) to 0.75 (South Korea) in 2024.

What happened to the UK's non-UK-born population between mid-2022 and mid-2024?

It rose from 11.5 million to 13.1 million. From “Migration and population” →

In plain English: Migration changes the size, age structure and diversity of populations. Key idea: Migration affects population size, age structure and diversity; net migration fell to 171,000 in 2025; measurement has shifted to administrative data and remains uncertain.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Urban Sociology and Population

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A city with more than 10 million people is called a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Megacity. A megacity is defined as an urban area with more than 10 million residents.

  2. Simmel's term for city dwellers' reserved detachment is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The blasé attitude. Simmel described the blasé attitude in the metropolis.

  3. A new group moving into an area and replacing the previous residents is… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Invasion and succession. Park and Burgess described invasion and succession.

  4. Families leave drought-hit farmland for a city where relatives say there is work. This illustrates… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Rural–urban migration. Moving from countryside to city is rural–urban migration.

  5. A researcher finds that residents of an inner-city neighbourhood know their neighbours well and rely on extended family. This supports… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The idea of urban villages. Gans's urban villages show community in cities.

  6. An inner-city area of cheap rented housing near the centre has high population turnover and new migrants. Burgess would call it… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The zone in transition. Cheap housing near the centre defines the zone in transition.

  7. Why do critics question the "planet of slums" framing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It may overlook residents' agency and improvements. Residents build networks and economies that change settlements.

  8. How did Fischer challenge Wirth's view of the city? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Urban size allows subcultures to form and thrive. Fischer saw size enabling, not destroying, community.

  9. Why do critics say the concentric model ignores power? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Planning, markets and racism shape where people live. Residential patterns reflect constraints, not just competition.

Key takeaways

  • 57.8% of the world's population was urban in 2025 (83.3% in the UK); industrialisation drove early urbanisation, while migration and natural increase drive it today; informal settlements are a major challenge.
  • Tönnies contrasted community and association; Simmel described the blasé attitude; Wirth linked size, density and heterogeneity to weak ties; studies of urban villages show community persists.
  • The Chicago School saw cities as ecosystems; Burgess's concentric zones and invasion and succession explained urban patterns; critics stress power, markets and racism.
  • Cities combine concentrated wealth and deprivation; Harvey and Lefebvre link inequality to capital and power; neighbourhood effects research suggests place shapes life chances.
  • Segregation separates groups by income, ethnicity or religion; it results from constraint and choice; British evidence suggests growing dispersal despite claims of "parallel lives".
  • Gentrification brings wealthier residents and investment into working-class areas; the rent gap and middle-class preferences explain it; displacement and its measurement are central debates.

Common mistakes in Urban Sociology and Population

  • Assuming urbanisation always follows industrialisation. In many places, cities grew faster than industrial jobs.
  • Assuming cities have no community. Urban villages and subcultures show otherwise.
  • Treating urban patterns as natural. Policy, markets and discrimination shape them.
  • Assuming deprived areas are caused by their residents' choices. Markets, policy and history shape places.
  • Assuming segregation is always chosen. Discrimination, income and policy constrain choices.
  • Equating gentrification with improvement for everyone. Existing residents may be displaced.

    Classical sociologists saw the modern city as a new form of social life.

  4. Urban inequality
    Cities concentrate both wealth and poverty.
  5. Segregation
    Residential segregation is the separation of groups into different neighbourhoods by income, ethnicity or religion.
  6. Gentrification
    Gentrification is the movement of wealthier residents and investment into working-class areas, changing their character.
  7. Housing
    Housing shapes health, wealth, family life and opportunity.

  15. Fertility
    Fertility refers to the number of children people have.
  16. Mortality
    Mortality refers to deaths in a population.
  17. Population ageing

Urban Sociology and Population: questions students ask

What is the difference between Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft?

Gemeinschaft is close community based on kinship; Gesellschaft is impersonal association based on contracts.

What is the concentric zone model?

Burgess's model of the city as rings around a central business district.

What does the Index of Multiple Deprivation measure?

Deprivation in small areas across income, employment, health, education, crime, housing and environment.

What is the rent gap?

The difference between an area's current rent and its potential rent after investment.
