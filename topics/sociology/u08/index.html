Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How does the ONS define a household?

One person or a group of people living together and sharing cooking facilities and a living space. From “What is a family?” →

In plain English: Defining the family is harder than it seems. Key idea: Official definitions focus on couples and parent–child units; Murdock's universal nuclear family is challenged by diversity; Morgan's family practices focus on what people do.

What is a blended family?

A family including a partner's children from a previous relationship. From “Blended families” →

In plain English: Blended families, or step-families, form when partners bring children from previous relationships. Key idea: Blended families form through re-partnering; they face role ambiguity and loyalty issues but also offer support; outcome differences are small and linked to transitions and conflict.

When did no-fault divorce come into force in England and Wales?

April 2022. From “Divorce” →

In plain English: Divorce is the legal ending of a marriage. Key idea: Divorce peaked in the 1990s and has fallen to 105,002 in 2025; law changes, attitudes and women's independence explain trends; views differ on its meaning.

What is lagged adaptation?

Men slowly adjusting their share of domestic work as women's paid work increases. From “Domestic labour” →

In plain English: Domestic labour is the unpaid work of running a home and caring for its members. Key idea: Domestic labour includes housework and care; women did about 60% more unpaid work than men in 2015; cultural and material explanations both contribute.

In which direction does support flow between generations?

Both downwards (to children and grandchildren) and upwards (to ageing parents). From “Intergenerational relationships” →

In plain English: Intergenerational relationships link grandparents, parents, children and grandchildren. Key idea: Support flows up and down generations; longer lives and changing families reshape ties; debates about intergenerational fairness should consider inequality within generations.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Family and Relationships

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who argued the nuclear family is universal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    George Murdock. George Murdock studied 250 societies and claimed the nuclear family was universal in 1949.

  2. Which family type made up the largest share of UK families in 2025? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Married couples. Married couples made up 65.3% of UK families in 2025, according to ONS.

  3. A household of grandparents, parents and children is a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Vertically extended family. It spans three generations under one roof.

  4. Three friends rent a flat together and share a kitchen. In ONS terms they are… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A household but not a family. Sharers form a household without being a family.

  5. Rising rents mean more young adults live with parents. Which explanation does this support? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Economic pressures. High housing costs delay leaving home, an economic explanation of family change.

  6. Laslett found most pre-industrial English households were nuclear. This challenges… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Parsons's functional fit thesis. Nuclear families existed before industrialisation.

  7. Why do definitions of the family matter for policy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They decide eligibility. Who counts as family affects access to support.

  8. Why should sociologists report uncertainty in survey estimates? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small changes may be due to chance. Sampling variation can mimic real change.

  9. Why are beanpole families becoming more common? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    People live longer and have fewer children. Longer lives and lower fertility create thin, tall families.

Key takeaways

  • Official definitions focus on couples and parent–child units; Murdock's universal nuclear family is challenged by diversity; Morgan's family practices focus on what people do.
  • UK families are mostly married couples, with growing cohabiting and lone-parent families; in 2025 there were 19.9 million families; housing, attitudes and law drive change.
  • The nuclear family is parents and children; Parsons linked it to industrial society, but historical and current evidence shows extended kin remain important in modified forms.
  • Lone-parent families (3.2 million in 2025) are mostly headed by mothers; they grew with divorce and changing norms; poverty, not structure, explains many disadvantages.
  • Blended families form through re-partnering; they face role ambiguity and loyalty issues but also offer support; outcome differences are small and linked to transitions and conflict.
  • Same-sex families gained legal recognition from 2005 to 2020; research finds children's outcomes depend on family processes; same-sex couples often share roles more equally.

Common mistakes in Family and Relationships

  • Treating "household" and "family" as the same. Many households, such as people living alone or sharing flats, aren't families.
  • Treating every year-to-year change in survey data as a trend. Some changes aren't statistically significant.
  • Assuming the extended family has disappeared. Support across generations remains strong, often at a distance.
  • Assuming lone parenthood itself harms children. Poverty, conflict and instability explain much of the difference.
  • Assuming step-families are inherently problematic. Most function well; transitions and conflict explain much of the difference.
  • Assuming children need parents of both sexes for healthy development. Research finds processes, not parents' sex or orientation, matter.

Family and Relationships mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. What is a family?
    Defining the family is harder than it seems.
  2. Family structures
    Family structure refers to the composition of families: who lives together and how they are related.
  3. Nuclear and extended families
    The nuclear family is parents and their children; the extended family includes wider kin.
  4. Lone-parent families
    Lone-parent families are headed by one parent living with at least one child.
  5. Blended families
    Blended families, or step-families, form when partners bring children from previous relationships.
  6. Same-sex families
    Same-sex families are headed by same-sex couples, with or without children.
  7. Marriage
    Marriage is a legally and socially recognised union.
  8. Cohabitation
    Cohabitation is an unmarried couple living together.
  9. Divorce
    Divorce is the legal ending of a marriage.
  10. Fertility and family size
    Fertility refers to the number of children born.
  11. Parenting
    Parenting is shaped by class, culture, gender and policy.
  12. Childhood as a social construction
    Sociologists argue childhood is socially constructed: what it means to be a child varies across time and cultures.
  13. Domestic labour
    Domestic labour is the unpaid work of running a home and caring for its members.
  14. Power in the family
    Power in families concerns who makes decisions, controls resources and shapes others' lives.
  15. Domestic violence
    Domestic violence and abuse is harmful behaviour between people who are or have been intimate partners or family members.
  16. Family diversity
    Families in the UK are increasingly diverse.
  17. Intergenerational relationships
    Intergenerational relationships link grandparents, parents, children and grandchildren.
  18. Ageing and care
    An ageing population increases the need for care, much of it provided unpaid by families.
  19. Family policy
    Family policies are laws and measures that affect families, from benefits and parental leave to childcare and child protection.
  20. Global families
    Families around the world differ in structure, marriage practices and roles, and globalisation is changing family life.
  21. Family change
    Family life has changed greatly since the mid-20th century.
