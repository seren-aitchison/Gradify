Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three lenses of design thinking?

Desirability (do people want it?), feasibility (can it be made?) and viability (can it be sustained financially?). From “What is design?” →

In plain English: Design is the deliberate shaping of products, places, services and systems to meet human needs. Key idea: Good design sits where the three lenses overlap. IDEO and Brown (2009) argue that design thinking starts with desirability, then tests feasibility and viability, rather than starting from a technology looking for a use.

Define design thinking.

A human-centred, iterative approach to problem solving that uses designers' methods: empathy, framing, ideation, prototyping and testing. From “Design thinking defined” →

In plain English: Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems that borrows the methods designers use: understanding people, framing problems, generating ideas and learning through prototypes. Key idea: Design thinking treats problem and solution as moving together: making a rough prototype often reveals what the real problem is.

What is the purpose of the first diamond?

To understand the issue and define the right problem before generating solutions. From “The Double Diamond” →

In plain English: The Double Diamond is the Design Council's model of the design process: four phases, Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver, shaped as two diamonds of divergent and convergent thinking. Key idea: The first diamond stops a team from solving the wrong problem well. Skipping it is the most common way to waste a good second diamond.

What are the three parts of the D&T iterative cycle?

Explore, create and evaluate, repeated as understanding grows. From “Iterative design in Design and Technology” →

In plain English: GCSE and A level Design and Technology require an iterative design process: explore, create and evaluate in repeated cycles. Key idea: Iteration is evidenced by decisions: each cycle should show what was tested, what was learned and what changed as a result.

Which standard sets out principles of human-centred design for interactive systems?

ISO 9241-210. From “Human-centred design” →

In plain English: Human-centred design puts the people who will use a product or service at the centre of every decision, from research to testing. Key idea: "You are not the user." Designers know too much about their own product to see it as a newcomer does, which is why HCD insists on involving real users.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Design Thinking

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes design? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Planning something new to change a situation into a preferred one. Design is purposeful problem solving (Simon, 1969); decoration and self-expression are narrower activities.

  2. Which school, founded in 1919, united art, craft and industrial production? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Bauhaus. The Bauhaus (1919–1933) taught design for machine production; Memphis was 1980s postmodernism.

  3. Which is a core characteristic of design thinking? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It is iterative, learning through repeated prototypes. Iteration (make, test, learn, repeat) is central; avoiding users or fixing a plan contradicts the approach.

  4. A bank's new app is easy to use and customers love it, but each account costs more to run than it earns. Which lens does it fail? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Viability. It works and people want it, so desirability and feasibility are met; it fails viability because it cannot be sustained financially.

  5. Why did industrial mass production create the role of the professional designer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Planning a product was separated from making the many copies of it. Once machines or workers made thousands of copies, someone had to plan the product in advance: design became a separate job.

  6. A council team spends a week interviewing residents before deciding what the parking problem is. Which characteristic does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Human-centred understanding before solving. Starting from residents' needs and context is the human-centred core of design thinking.

  7. A critic says "design is just making things pretty". Which reply is strongest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Appearance is one requirement, but a design is judged on function, use, cost and impact too. The strongest reply accepts appearance matters and shows it is one of several requirements; the other options are exaggerations.

  8. Which is the best evaluation of "form follows function"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A useful discipline against needless ornament, but function alone cannot settle appearance or meaning. The phrase usefully focuses on purpose, but many forms can serve the same function, and emotion and meaning are part of what a design does.

  9. Which is the strongest criticism of design thinking as practised in some organisations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can shrink to workshop rituals without real research, craft or follow-through. The common critique is shallow, ritualised use; the other statements misdescribe the method and its history.

Key takeaways

  • Good design sits where the three lenses overlap. IDEO and Brown (2009) argue that design thinking starts with desirability, then tests feasibility and viability, rather than starting from a technology looking for a use.
  • Each movement responded to the problems of the last: Arts and Crafts to shoddy machine goods, modernism to ornament, postmodernism to austerity, and user-centred design to products that looked good but confused people.
  • Design thinking treats problem and solution as moving together: making a rough prototype often reveals what the real problem is.
  • In design, making is a way of thinking. Each sketch or prototype is a question put to the problem, and the answer changes the problem.
  • The first diamond stops a team from solving the wrong problem well. Skipping it is the most common way to waste a good second diamond.
  • Separate the modes in time. Judging ideas while generating them makes people self-censor and stops unusual ideas from appearing at all.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Design Thinking

  • Equating design with appearance. A beautiful chair that collapses, or an app that looks polished but confuses users, is badly designed. Appearance matters because it affects use and emotion, but it is one requirement among many.
  • Treating movements as tidy, sequential periods. They overlapped, many designers worked across them, and dates are approximate markers.
  • Treating design thinking as a fixed five-step recipe. The models are scaffolds; experienced designers move back and forth between activities as they learn.
  • Assuming a process that loops back has failed. Iteration is the normal, efficient route to a good design: it is cheaper to learn from a sketch than from a finished product.
  • Treating the diamonds as a one-way sequence. The Design Council stresses loops: findings in Develop or Deliver often show the problem was defined wrongly.
  • Converging on the first workable idea. Early ideas are usually the most obvious; the strongest often appear after the obvious ones are exhausted.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is design?
    Design is the deliberate shaping of products, places, services and systems to meet human needs.
  2. A short history of design
    Design became a separate profession when industry separated planning from making.
  3. Design thinking defined
    Design thinking is a human-centred approach to solving problems that borrows the methods designers use: understanding people, framing problems, generating ideas and learning through prototypes.
  4. Design as a process, not a product
    Designers do not move in a straight line from problem to answer.
  5. The Double Diamond
    The Double Diamond is the Design Council's model of the design process: four phases, Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver, shaped as two diamonds of divergent and convergent thinking.
  6. Divergent and convergent thinking
    Design alternates between opening up possibilities and narrowing down to the best.
  7. The d.school and IDEO models
    Stanford's d.school teaches design thinking as five modes: empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test.
  8. Iterative design in Design and Technology
    GCSE and A level Design and Technology require an iterative design process: explore, create and evaluate in repeated cycles.
  9. Designers and design disciplines
    Design is a family of disciplines, from product and graphic design to UX, service design and architecture.
  10. Human-centred design
    Human-centred design puts the people who will use a product or service at the centre of every decision, from research to testing.
  11. Wicked problems
    Some problems have no clear definition, no right answer and no point at which they are solved.
  12. The value of design
    Design creates value for users, organisations and society, but measuring it is hard.

Foundations of Design Thinking: questions students ask

Who founded the Bauhaus, and when?

Walter Gropius, in Weimar, in 1919.

What is abductive reasoning?

Reasoning towards what might work, proposing a possible solution to be tested, rather than deducing what must be true.

What did Schön mean by reflection-in-action?

Thinking while doing: the designer makes a move, the situation "talks back" with unexpected results, and the designer reframes and acts again.

What is divergent thinking?

Producing many different possible answers to an open question, deferring judgement.
