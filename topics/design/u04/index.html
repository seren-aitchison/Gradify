Key terms and formulas at a glance

User research
The systematic study of the people a design is for: their goals, behaviour, contexts and needs
Research question
A statement of what the team needs to learn to make a design decision
Qualitative research
Research gathering rich, non-numerical data to understand reasons, contexts and experiences
Informed consent
Voluntary agreement to take part, given with an understanding of what the research involves
Screener
A short questionnaire used to check that potential participants meet recruitment criteria
Discussion guide
A flexible list of topics and questions used to structure an interview
Contextual inquiry
A research method combining observation and interviewing where an activity actually happens
Likert scale
A rating scale measuring agreement or satisfaction across ordered, balanced options
Diary study
A research method in which participants record their experiences over time, near the moment they happen
Ethnography
Research that studies people's everyday lives and cultures through sustained immersion
Desk research
Gathering and analysing existing information rather than collecting new data
Empathy map
A four-quadrant summary of what a user says, thinks, does and feels

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why can't designers rely on their own assumptions about users?

They know the product too well, differ in skills and background from users, and are not the users. From “Why research users” →

In plain English: Designers research users because their own assumptions are unreliable guides to what other people need and do. Key idea: Research what people do, not just what they say. Behaviour in context is the most reliable evidence of need.

What makes consent "informed"?

The participant understands the purpose, what is involved, how data will be used and that taking part is voluntary. From “Research ethics and consent” →

In plain English: Research with people must protect them from harm, respect their choices and handle their data lawfully. Key idea: Consent is a process, not a signature: check understanding, remind people they can stop, and ask again before any new use of their data.

What is the master-apprentice model?

In contextual inquiry, the participant is the expert and the researcher learns by watching and asking as an apprentice would. From “Contextual inquiry and observation” →

In plain English: Contextual inquiry combines observation and interviewing in the place where people actually do the work or activity. Key idea: Workarounds are design gold: each one marks a need the current system fails to meet.

What are cultural probes?

Kits of materials such as cameras and postcards given to participants to record their lives for designers. From “Ethnography in design” →

In plain English: Ethnography is the study of people's everyday lives and cultures through immersion. Key idea: Ethnography reveals the meanings and tacit practices behind behaviour, which help designers create things that fit people's lives rather than disrupt them.

What should be at the core of a persona?

Goals and behaviours from research, rather than demographics. From “Personas” →

In plain English: A persona is a fictional but research-based profile representing a group of users with similar goals and behaviours. Key idea: Personas turn research into a shared, memorable reference that keeps decisions focused on real goals and behaviours.

Quick quiz: test yourself on User Research and Empathy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a main purpose of user research? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Discovering needs and checking assumptions before building. Research discovers needs and reduces risk; it should test, not confirm, assumptions.

  2. Which is the best research question for a design project? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Where do new users get stuck when setting up an account, and why?. It is focused, open and tied to a design decision; the others are vague or closed.

  3. Which is an example of quantitative data? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The percentage of users who complete a task. Percentages are numerical measures; quotes, photos and descriptions are qualitative.

  4. Shoppers say they always read nutrition labels, but in-store observation shows most glance for under two seconds. What explains the gap? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Social desirability bias and the gap between saying and doing. People report what sounds responsible; observation reveals actual behaviour.

  5. A team wants to know how many customers abandon checkout at each step. Which method fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Analytics of checkout behaviour. "How many" about behaviour is a quantitative behavioural question, suited to analytics.

  6. Analytics show 60% of users leave a form at the address step. What should the team do next to learn why? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Observe a few users completing the form. Numbers show where; qualitative observation explains why (an explanatory sequence).

  7. A start-up says user research is too slow and costly. Which is the strongest reply? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Finding a wrong assumption early costs far less than discovering it after launch. Early research reduces the much larger cost of building the wrong thing, though it guarantees nothing.

  8. A plan lists "survey, interviews, focus group" but no research questions. What is the main weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Methods are chosen without knowing what decision they must inform. Without questions tied to decisions, methods may collect data nobody can use.

  9. A team interviews five users; three mention slow loading. A manager reports "60% of our customers find the site slow". What is wrong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A small qualitative sample shows an issue exists but cannot estimate a percentage. Qualitative samples are small and unrepresentative; a survey or analytics would be needed to estimate prevalence.

Key takeaways

  • Research what people do, not just what they say. Behaviour in context is the most reliable evidence of need.
  • Start from the decision the research must inform, write questions that would change that decision, then choose methods that can answer them.
  • Qualitative research tells you why; quantitative tells you how many. Design decisions usually need both.
  • Consent is a process, not a signature: check understanding, remind people they can stop, and ask again before any new use of their data.
  • Recruit for the variety of the people affected, not for convenience: the participants you leave out are the problems you won't find.
  • Ask about specific past events in the participant's own words. Hypothetical questions about future behaviour ("would you buy...?") are unreliable predictors.

Common mistakes in User Research and Empathy

  • Treating research as a box to tick at the start. Research is most valuable when it shapes decisions throughout, from framing to testing.
  • Choosing a method first ("let's do a survey") and then deciding what to ask. Method should follow question.
  • Treating qualitative findings as statistics ("4 of 6 users said..., so 67% of customers...") or treating numbers as explanations. Small samples reveal issues; they don't estimate percentages.
  • Assuming public behaviour can be recorded freely. Filming identifiable people for research still raises consent and data protection issues.
  • Recruiting only enthusiastic existing customers. They are easy to find but over-represent people for whom the product already works.
  • Pitching your idea in the interview. Once people know what you hope to hear, politeness and social desirability shape their answers.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why research users
    Designers research users because their own assumptions are unreliable guides to what other people need and do.
  2. Research questions and plans
    Good research starts with clear questions and a plan that matches methods to them.
  3. Qualitative and quantitative research
    Qualitative research explores why and how; quantitative research measures how many and how much.
  4. Research ethics and consent
    Research with people must protect them from harm, respect their choices and handle their data lawfully.
  5. Recruiting participants
    Research is only as good as the people in it.
  6. User interviews
    Interviews are the most common user research method.
  7. Contextual inquiry and observation
    Contextual inquiry combines observation and interviewing in the place where people actually do the work or activity.
  8. Surveys
    Surveys collect standardised answers from many people quickly.
  9. Diary studies
    Diary studies ask participants to record their experiences over days or weeks, capturing behaviour that unfolds over time or in private settings.
  10. Ethnography in design
    Ethnography is the study of people's everyday lives and cultures through immersion.
  11. Desk research and competitor analysis
    Before talking to users, designers learn what is already known and what already exists.
  12. Empathy maps
    Empathy maps summarise what a team has learned about a user in four quadrants: what they say, think, do and feel.
  13. Personas
    A persona is a fictional but research-based profile representing a group of users with similar goals and behaviours.
  14. Customer journey maps
    A journey map visualises the steps a person goes through to achieve a goal, with their actions, thoughts and emotions at each step.
  15. Jobs to be done
    Jobs to be done is a way of understanding why people "hire" a product: to make progress in a particular situation.
  16. Synthesis and insight
    Research only helps if it is turned into insight: a new understanding that changes what the team will do.

User Research and Empathy: questions students ask

How does a research question differ from an interview question?

A research question states what the team needs to learn; interview questions are what participants are asked to help answer it.

What kind of question does qualitative research answer best?

Why and how: reasons, contexts and experiences.

What is a screener?

A short questionnaire that checks potential participants meet the recruitment criteria without revealing the answers wanted.

Why ask about past behaviour rather than future intentions?

People are poor at predicting their own behaviour; specific past events give more reliable evidence.
