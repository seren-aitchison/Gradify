Key terms and formulas at a glance

Prototype
A representation of a design idea made to explore, test or communicate it before it is finished
Fidelity
How closely a prototype resembles the finished product in appearance, interaction and content
Paper prototype
A hand-drawn, paper version of an interface manipulated by a person to simulate interaction
Wireframe
A simplified, usually greyscale layout of a screen showing structure, content and functions
Sketch model
A quick, rough physical model used to explore form and scale
Wizard of Oz prototype
A prototype in which a hidden person simulates the responses of a system that has not been built
Clickable prototype
Linked digital screens with hotspots that simulate navigation
Usability
The extent to which specified users can achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a context of use
Think-aloud protocol
A method in which participants verbalise their thoughts continuously while performing tasks
Problem discovery rate (L)
The average probability that a single test user reveals a given usability problem
Heuristic evaluation
An expert review of an interface against a set of usability principles
A/B test
A randomised experiment comparing two versions of a design on a predefined metric

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are Houde and Hill's three dimensions of a prototype?

Role, look and feel, and implementation. From “Why prototype” →

In plain English: A prototype is any representation of a design that lets people explore, test or communicate an idea before it is finished. Key idea: Prototype to answer a specific question as cheaply as possible, and make several in parallel early, before committing to one.

What does a wireframe leave out?

Colour, imagery, detailed typography and branding. From “Wireframes” →

In plain English: A wireframe is a simplified visual guide to the structure of a screen or page, showing layout, content and functions without visual styling. Key idea: Wireframes let teams agree structure and priorities before visual design, so discussions focus on what goes where and why, not on colours.

What is a clickable prototype?

Linked digital screens with hotspots that simulate navigation through an interface. From “Digital prototyping tools” →

In plain English: Designers use digital tools to create interactive prototypes, from linked screens to coded components. Key idea: Choose the tool for the question, not the question for the tool. Skills in structuring and testing outlast any particular software.

Why test in several small rounds rather than one big one?

Each round's fixes let the next round find new problems, and returns diminish within a round. From “How many users to test” →

In plain English: How many participants does a usability test need? Key idea: Small, repeated rounds of testing with fixes in between usually find more problems than one large test.

Why report "5 of 8" as well as 62.5%?

With small samples, percentages look more precise than they are; counts show the real basis. From “Analysing test results” →

In plain English: Testing produces notes, recordings, numbers and quotes. Key idea: Report issues with evidence, severity and a recommendation, prioritised so the team fixes the most serious problems first.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Prototyping, Testing and Iteration

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a main purpose of prototyping? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Finding problems while changes are still cheap. Prototypes help learn and reduce risk early, before costly commitments.

  2. Which is a low-fidelity prototype? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Paper sketches of app screens. Paper screens are quick, rough and cheap: low fidelity.

  3. Which role in a paper prototype test gives the user tasks? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The facilitator. The facilitator sets tasks and prompts thinking aloud; the computer changes screens.

  4. A team wants to know if a new wearable fits comfortably on the wrist. Which prototype dimension is it testing? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Look and feel. Comfort and fit concern what it is like to wear and use: look and feel.

  5. A team wants to test whether users trust a new brand look on a checkout page. Which prototype fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A visually polished mock-up of the page. Trust in appearance needs high visual fidelity; interaction can stay low.

  6. After two paper prototype sessions, users can't find the search button. What should the team do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Redraw the screen and test the change in the next session. Paper's speed allows changes between sessions, so fix and retest.

  7. A team has two weeks and is unsure which of three concepts to pursue. What does the research suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Make rough prototypes of all three, get feedback, then choose. Parallel prototyping improves outcomes and avoids early lock-in (Dow et al., 2010).

  8. Which is the best interpretation of Walker, Takayama and Landay's finding? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Low fidelity is efficient for many usability questions, but not for those about look and feel. Similar problem counts suggest low fidelity is efficient; aesthetic and feel questions still need higher fidelity.

  9. For which question is a paper prototype least suitable? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Whether an animation feels smooth enough. Animation and timing need digital prototypes; paper suits concepts, labels and flow.

Key takeaways

  • Prototype to answer a specific question as cheaply as possible, and make several in parallel early, before committing to one.
  • Match fidelity to the question: as low as possible, but high enough in the dimension you're testing.
  • Paper prototypes are so quick to change that designs can be fixed between test sessions, and users feel free to criticise them because they're obviously unfinished.
  • Wireframes let teams agree structure and priorities before visual design, so discussions focus on what goes where and why, not on colours.
  • Each model should answer a specific question; a cheap, rough model that answers it is better than an expensive one made too soon.
  • Wizard of Oz and experience prototypes test what an experience is like before investing in technology or buildings.

Common mistakes in Prototyping, Testing and Iteration

  • Building a polished prototype before knowing what question it should answer. Effort spent on detail early is often wasted when the concept changes.
  • Showing a polished prototype too early. People comment on colours and fonts and hesitate to criticise something that looks finished, so concept problems go unnoticed.
  • Making paper prototypes too neat. Rough sketches are faster and signal that feedback is welcome.
  • Treating a wireframe as a finished design to hand over. Wireframes need annotation, testing and visual design before build.
  • Relying only on screen models for things people hold. Weight, balance, texture and grip are hard to judge on a screen.
  • Leaving participants deceived. If users believe a system is automated, debrief them afterwards and get consent for using their data; avoid simulating something users could rely on for safety.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why prototype
    A prototype is any representation of a design that lets people explore, test or communicate an idea before it is finished.
  2. Prototype fidelity
    Fidelity is how closely a prototype resembles the finished product.
  3. Paper prototyping
    Paper prototyping uses hand-drawn screens and pieces of paper to simulate an interface, with a person playing the computer.
  4. Wireframes
    A wireframe is a simplified visual guide to the structure of a screen or page, showing layout, content and functions without visual styling.
  5. Physical models and mock-ups
    Product designers use physical models to explore form, scale, ergonomics and function.
  6. Wizard of Oz and experience prototypes
    Some ideas are too complex or expensive to build just to test.
  7. Digital prototyping tools
    Designers use digital tools to create interactive prototypes, from linked screens to coded components.
  8. Usability testing
    Usability testing watches real people try to complete realistic tasks with a design, to find where it causes difficulty.
  9. Thinking aloud
    In a think-aloud test, participants say what they are thinking as they use a design.
  10. How many users to test
    How many participants does a usability test need?
  11. Heuristic evaluation
    Heuristic evaluation is an expert review in which evaluators check an interface against a set of usability principles.
  12. A/B testing
    A/B testing compares two versions of a design with real users at random to see which performs better on a chosen measure.
  13. Analysing test results
    Testing produces notes, recordings, numbers and quotes.
  14. Iterating on feedback
    Iteration means using what testing reveals to improve the design, then testing again.
  15. Minimum viable product
    A minimum viable product is the smallest version of a product that lets a team learn whether its idea works with real customers.
  16. Design sprints
    A design sprint is a five-day process for answering a critical business question through design, prototyping and testing with users.
