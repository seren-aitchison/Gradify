Key terms and formulas at a glance

Accessibility
The degree to which a design can be used by people with disabilities
Social model of disability
The view that people are disabled by barriers in society rather than by their impairments
Inclusive design
A design methodology that considers the full range of human diversity and involves excluded people
Universal design
Design of products and environments usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without adaptation
WCAG
The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the W3C's international standard for digital accessibility
Conformance level
One of WCAG's three levels, A, AA or AAA, each requiring all criteria at that level and below
Contrast ratio
A measure of the difference in luminance between two colours, from 1:1 to 21:1
Keyboard trap
A situation where keyboard focus enters a component and cannot leave it
Alt text
A short text alternative for an image, read by screen readers and shown if the image fails to load
Label
Visible text identifying a form field, programmatically associated with it
Screen reader
Software that reads screen content aloud or to a braille display
Automated accessibility testing
Using software to scan code for detectable accessibility failures

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a situational disability?

A temporary limitation caused by context, such as glare on a screen or holding something in one hand. From “Why accessibility matters” →

In plain English: Accessibility means people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate and use a design. Key idea: Accessible design is good design for everyone. Microsoft's inclusive design guidance captures this as "solve for one, extend to many": a design for someone with one arm also helps a parent holding a child.

How does inclusive design differ from accessibility compliance?

Inclusive design is a process involving diverse people throughout; compliance checks a minimum standard. From “Inclusive design principles” →

In plain English: Inclusive design is a way of designing that considers the full range of human diversity from the start. Key idea: Inclusive design is a process that involves excluded people throughout; accessibility standards set a minimum outcome.

Why must a complete process conform?

If any step fails, people can't complete the activity, so the whole sequence must meet the level. From “Conformance levels” →

In plain English: WCAG success criteria are grouped into three conformance levels: A, AA and AAA. Key idea: AA is the level referenced by most laws and policies, including UK public sector requirements; it covers the most significant barriers while remaining achievable for most content.

What alt text should a decorative image have?

Empty alt (alt=""), so screen readers skip it. From “Text alternatives” →

In plain English: People who can't see images or hear audio need equivalent information in another form. Key idea: Alt text should say what the image means in its context, as briefly as possible; the same picture may need different alt text in different places.

Why can't automated tools fully test accessibility?

They can detect some code failures but can't judge meaning, quality or logic, such as whether alt text is accurate or focus order makes sense. From “Testing for accessibility” →

In plain English: No single method finds all accessibility barriers. Key idea: Automated testing is a fast first filter; manual, assistive technology and user testing find most of what matters.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Accessibility and Inclusive Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. About what proportion of the world's population does the WHO estimate experience significant disability? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 16%. The WHO (2022) estimated around 1.3 billion people, roughly 16%.

  2. Which model sees disability mainly as a problem in a person's body to be treated? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The medical model. The medical model focuses on diagnosis and treatment of the individual.

  3. Which is one of Microsoft's inclusive design principles? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Recognise exclusion. Recognising who is excluded is the first principle.

  4. A designer adds large touch targets for users with tremors. Who else is most likely to benefit? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    People using a phone on a bumpy bus. Larger targets help anyone with reduced precision, including situational limits.

  5. A college website uses images of text with no alternatives, so a blind student can't read the timetable. How would the social model describe this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The website's design disables the student. The barrier is in the design, which the college can remove.

  6. A team adds a "listen to this page" feature first designed for blind users, and commuters use it on the move. Which principle does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Solve for one, extend to many. A solution for one group benefits many others.

  7. Which is the strongest argument for designing accessibility in from the start? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Retrofitting is costly, and early inclusion improves the design for everyone. Designing in early avoids expensive rework and brings wider usability benefits.

  8. Which is the strongest criticism of the strong social model? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can underplay effects of impairment, such as pain, that design cannot remove. Critics like Shakespeare argue impairment effects matter alongside barriers.

  9. A website passes automated WCAG checks but disabled users still struggle to complete tasks. What is the best conclusion? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Compliance is a minimum; inclusive design needs testing with disabled people. Passing checks doesn't guarantee a usable experience; involving people reveals real barriers.

Key takeaways

  • Accessible design is good design for everyone. Microsoft's inclusive design guidance captures this as "solve for one, extend to many": a design for someone with one arm also helps a parent holding a child.
  • Under the social model, the designer's job is to find and remove the barriers a design creates.
  • Inclusive design is a process that involves excluded people throughout; accessibility standards set a minimum outcome.
  • Universal design builds access into the mainstream design, so nobody needs a separate, stigmatising "special" version.
  • POUR gives a quick way to think about any barrier: can people perceive it, operate it, understand it, and does it work robustly with their technology?
  • AA is the level referenced by most laws and policies, including UK public sector requirements; it covers the most significant barriers while remaining achievable for most content.

Common mistakes in Accessibility and Inclusive Design

  • Treating accessibility as a final check before launch. Retrofitting is expensive; accessibility is cheapest when designed in from the start.
  • Assuming language preferences are universal. Many UK disabled people prefer "disabled people", while elsewhere "people with disabilities" is common; some prefer identity-first terms such as "autistic person". Ask, and follow the person's preference.
  • Equating inclusive design with meeting WCAG. Standards matter, but a design can pass checks and still exclude people in practice.
  • Assuming one design can suit absolutely everyone. Universal design aims for "the greatest extent possible"; some needs still require options or assistive technology.
  • Thinking WCAG covers every accessibility need. It is a baseline; W3C notes it does not address every need, especially for cognitive and learning disabilities.
  • Claiming a site is "WCAG compliant" without saying which version and level, or after testing only some pages.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why accessibility matters
    Accessibility means people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate and use a design.
  2. Models of disability
    How disability is understood shapes how designers respond to it.
  3. Inclusive design principles
    Inclusive design is a way of designing that considers the full range of human diversity from the start.
  4. Universal design
    Universal design aims to create products and environments usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without need for adaptation.
  5. WCAG 2.2 and the POUR principles
    The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the international standard for digital accessibility.
  6. Conformance levels
    WCAG success criteria are grouped into three conformance levels: A, AA and AAA.
  7. Colour contrast
    Text and interface elements must stand out enough from their background for people with low vision or colour vision deficiency to see them.
  8. Keyboard access and focus
    Many people use a keyboard, or devices that act like one, instead of a mouse.
  9. Text alternatives
    People who can't see images or hear audio need equivalent information in another form.
  10. Accessible forms
    Forms are where many people get stuck: booking, applying, paying.
  11. Assistive technologies
    Assistive technologies help disabled people use computers, phones and other products.
  12. Testing for accessibility
    No single method finds all accessibility barriers.
  13. Cognitive accessibility
    Cognitive and learning disabilities affect memory, attention, language, reasoning and processing.
  14. Accessibility and UK law
    In the UK, accessibility is required by equality law and, for public bodies, by specific regulations for websites and apps.
