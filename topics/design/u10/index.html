Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What do the letters IHIP stand for in describing services?

Intangible, heterogeneous, inseparable and perishable. From “What is service design?” →

In plain English: Service design is the design of whole services, from what customers see to the people, processes and systems behind them. Key idea: Service design plans the people, processes, infrastructure and communication of a whole service; services are intangible, variable, produced and consumed together and perishable, and designers must work on both frontstage and backstage, following principles such as user-centred, co-creative, sequencing, evidencing and holistic.

What is a touchpoint?

Any point at which a user interacts with a service. From “Touchpoints and channels” →

In plain English: Users experience a service through many touchpoints across several channels. Key idea: Touchpoints are the points where users meet a service and channels are the media that carry them; omnichannel services share information across channels, and focusing on key moments, pain points and the ending can transform how a service is experienced.

What are the four phases of UK government service delivery?

Discovery, alpha, beta and live. From “Designing public services” →

In plain English: Public services must work for everyone, including people in difficult circumstances. Key idea: UK public services are guided by the Government Design Principles, starting with user needs, and the 14-point Service Standard; delivery moves through discovery, alpha, beta and live, and good services are easy to find, named for what users want to do, need no prior knowledge and hide organisational structure.

What is a value exchange?

Something of value, such as money, information, goods or trust, that flows between actors. From “Ecosystem maps” →

In plain English: Services depend on networks of people and organisations. Key idea: Ecosystem maps show all the actors in a service and the value exchanges between them, often in concentric circles around the user; mapping money, information, goods and trust reveals gaps, duplication and opportunities that a journey map might miss.

What is sludge?

Friction that makes beneficial actions harder, such as complicated cancellation. From “Nudges and choice architecture” →

In plain English: The way choices are presented changes what people choose. Key idea: Choice architecture is how options are presented, and nudges such as defaults, social norms, framing and reminders steer choices without removing options; some, like pension auto-enrolment, have large effects, but evidence for many nudges is mixed and they raise ethical questions of transparency and manipulation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Service and Systems Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which characteristic means an unsold cinema seat cannot be stored and sold tomorrow? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Perishability. Services are perishable: unused capacity is lost.

  2. Which lane of a blueprint shows the receipt or confirmation email a customer receives? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Physical evidence. Physical evidence is the tangible proof of the service at each step.

  3. Which is an example of a channel rather than a touchpoint? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The telephone. A channel is the medium, such as phone, web or post, through which touchpoints are delivered.

  4. A bank's app is easy to use but transfers take days because two internal systems do not talk to each other. Where is the problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Backstage. The failure lies in the hidden systems that support the visible service.

  5. How does a service blueprint differ from a customer journey map? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It also shows backstage and support activities. Blueprints extend journey maps to show how the organisation delivers each step.

  6. A customer must repeat her details each time she moves from chat to phone. What kind of service is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Multichannel but not omnichannel. Multichannel services offer several channels that do not share information.

  7. A hospital asks a designer to improve its appointment letter, but patients miss appointments because the booking phone line is always busy. What should the designer do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Look at the whole booking service, not just the letter. Service design is holistic, so the designer should address the real cause across channels.

  8. A blueprint shows three teams each re-entering the same customer details. What does this suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A backstage inefficiency and a likely failure point. Repeated handoffs and re-entry waste time and create errors.

  9. A council moves all applications online. What should it also provide? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Assisted digital support for those who cannot use online channels. Digital by default must not exclude people who cannot use digital channels.

Key takeaways

  • Service design plans the people, processes, infrastructure and communication of a whole service; services are intangible, variable, produced and consumed together and perishable, and designers must work on both frontstage and backstage, following principles such as user-centred, co-creative, sequencing, evidencing and holistic.
  • A service blueprint, introduced by Shostack in 1984, maps customer actions with the frontstage, backstage and support processes that deliver them, separated by lines of interaction, visibility and internal interaction, and helps teams find failure points, delays and handoffs.
  • Touchpoints are the points where users meet a service and channels are the media that carry them; omnichannel services share information across channels, and focusing on key moments, pain points and the ending can transform how a service is experienced.
  • Participatory design began in 1970s Scandinavia with workers shaping new technology, and co-design treats users as experts of their experience; Arnstein's ladder shows that participation can range from tokenism to real power, and good co-design is paid, accessible, trusting and closes the loop.
  • UK public services are guided by the Government Design Principles, starting with user needs, and the 14-point Service Standard; delivery moves through discovery, alpha, beta and live, and good services are easy to find, named for what users want to do, need no prior knowledge and hide organisational structure.
  • A system is a set of connected elements serving a purpose; stocks and flows explain how quantities such as waiting lists change, delays and emergence make systems complex, and the iceberg model helps designers look from events to patterns, structures and mental models.

Common mistakes in Service and Systems Design

  • Treating service design as designing a website or app. Digital touchpoints are part of a service, but the policies, staff and systems behind them often matter more.
  • Drawing a blueprint from what the process manual says. Blueprints should be based on research into how the service really runs, including workarounds.
  • Designing each channel separately. Users move between channels, so information, language and status must carry across them.
  • Calling a feedback survey on a finished design co-design. Co-design involves people in decisions throughout the process, not just commenting at the end.
  • Jumping straight to building a digital product. Discovery should first understand users and the problem, and may show a different solution is needed.
  • Treating every symptom as a separate problem. Many symptoms share a deeper structural cause that a systems view can reveal.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is service design?
    Service design is the design of whole services, from what customers see to the people, processes and systems behind them.
  2. Service blueprints
    A service blueprint maps how a service is delivered, linking what users do to what staff and systems do behind the scenes.
  3. Touchpoints and channels
    Users experience a service through many touchpoints across several channels.
  4. Co-design and participatory design
    Co-design involves the people affected by a service in designing it.
  5. Designing public services
    Public services must work for everyone, including people in difficult circumstances.
  6. Systems thinking for designers
    Many design challenges sit inside complex systems where changing one part affects others.
  7. Feedback loops and leverage points
    Feedback loops drive how systems grow, stabilise or collapse.
  8. Ecosystem maps
    Services depend on networks of people and organisations.
  9. Designing for behaviour change
    Many services aim to help people change behaviour, such as saving, recycling or attending appointments.
  10. Nudges and choice architecture
    The way choices are presented changes what people choose.
  11. Platforms and networks
    Many modern services are platforms that connect groups of users, such as buyers and sellers.
  12. Measuring service outcomes
    Good services are measured by whether they achieve their purpose, not just by activity.

Service and Systems Design: questions students ask

Who introduced the service blueprint and when?

G. Lynn Shostack, in 1984.

Where and when did participatory design begin?

In Scandinavia in the 1970s, working with trade unions.

What is the first UK Government Design Principle?

Start with user needs.

What three things make up a system according to Meadows?

Elements, interconnections and a function or purpose.
