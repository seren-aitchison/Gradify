Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the units of stress?

Pascals (N/m2); in engineering usually MPa, which equals N/mm2. From “Normal stress and strain” →

In plain English: Stress is internal force per unit area, and strain is deformation per unit length. Key idea: Normal stress is axial force per unit area (MPa = N/mm2); normal strain is change in length over original length; both are found for each section of a member.

What is the formula for axial deformation?

δ = PL/AE. From “Axially loaded members” →

In plain English: Bars, rods and columns under axial load stretch or shorten. Key idea: Axial deformation is δ = PL/AE, summed over segments; indeterminate problems need compatibility as well as equilibrium; factors of safety keep working stresses well below failure.

Where is bending stress zero in a beam?

At the neutral axis, through the centroid of the section. From “Bending stress in beams” →

In plain English: Bending creates tension on one side of a beam and compression on the other. Key idea: Bending stress σ = My/I varies linearly from zero at the neutral axis to a maximum M/Z at the outer fibres; deep sections with large Z resist bending efficiently.

What is the middle-third rule?

For a rectangular section, a load within the middle third (e ≤ b/6) causes no tension. From “Combined loading” →

In plain English: Many members carry more than one type of load at once. Key idea: Combined stresses are found by superposition, σ = P/A ± My/I; eccentric loads add a moment Pe; keeping loads within the middle third avoids tension in rectangular sections.

What is the effective length factor for a column fixed at one end and free at the other?

K = 2.0. From “Buckling of columns” →

In plain English: Slender columns can fail by buckling sideways at stresses well below their crushing strength. Key idea: Slender columns buckle at the Euler load π2EI/(KL)2, which falls with the square of effective length; slenderness ratio decides whether buckling or crushing governs.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Mechanics of Materials

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A 2,000 N force acts on an area of 40 mm2. What is the stress? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    50 MPa. Stress = F/A = 2,000 ÷ 40 = 50 N/mm2, which is 50 MPa.

  2. In which direction does shear stress act relative to the area? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Parallel to it. Shear stress acts in the plane of the area; normal stress acts perpendicular to it.

  3. What does Young's modulus measure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Stiffness: resistance to elastic deformation. E is the slope of the elastic part of the stress–strain curve.

  4. A 2 m rod extends by 1.2 mm under load. What is the strain? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.0006. Strain = δ/L = 1.2 ÷ 2,000 = 0.0006, or 600 microstrain.

  5. A pin of area 100 mm2 in double shear carries 12 kN. What is the shear stress? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    60 MPa. In double shear each plane carries 6 kN: τ = 6,000 ÷ 100 = 60 MPa.

  6. A material has E = 70 GPa. What strain does a stress of 140 MPa produce? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.002. Applying the standard result: ε = σ/E = 140 ÷ 70,000 = 0.002.

  7. A tensile test specimen necks before fracture. Why does engineering stress fall while true stress keeps rising? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Engineering stress uses the original area, but the actual area shrinks rapidly at the neck. Engineering stress divides load by the original area; true stress uses the smaller, actual area.

  8. A bolt in a lap joint has acceptable shear stress, but the joint fails by the hole elongating. Which check was missed? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Bearing stress between the bolt and the plate. Excessive bearing stress crushes or elongates the hole even when the bolt is strong enough.

  9. Two rods of the same size, one steel and one aluminium, carry the same load. Which statement is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The aluminium rod stretches about three times as much, though both carry the same stress. Same load and area give the same stress, but aluminium's lower E gives larger strain.

Key takeaways

  • Normal stress is axial force per unit area (MPa = N/mm2); normal strain is change in length over original length; both are found for each section of a member.
  • Shear stress is force parallel to an area divided by that area; double shear halves the stress in a bolt; bearing stress checks the plate around the hole.
  • In the elastic range, σ = Eε and τ = Gγ; the stress–strain curve shows yield, hardening, ultimate strength and fracture; Poisson's ratio links lateral and axial strain.
  • Axial deformation is δ = PL/AE, summed over segments; indeterminate problems need compatibility as well as equilibrium; factors of safety keep working stresses well below failure.
  • Free thermal strain is αΔT; if expansion is prevented, the stress is EαΔT, which can be large; joints, gaps and compatible materials accommodate thermal movement.
  • Torsional shear stress τ = Tr/J is largest at the surface; twist θ = TL/GJ; shafts are sized from P = Tω, and hollow shafts save material.

Common mistakes in Mechanics of Materials

  • Mixing units. Using N with mm2 gives N/mm2, which equals MPa; using N with m2 gives Pa. Keep units consistent throughout a calculation.
  • Forgetting double shear. If a bolt passes through three plates with the load shared across two planes, using the single-shear area doubles the calculated stress.
  • Confusing stiffness with strength. Young's modulus measures stiffness (resistance to elastic deformation); yield and ultimate strength measure resistance to permanent deformation and fracture.
  • Using the total load for every segment of a stepped bar. Each segment's internal force must be found by equilibrium at a section through that segment.
  • Assuming thermal stress depends on the member's length. For a fully restrained member, σ = EαΔT doesn't depend on length; free expansion does.
  • Using the second moment of area I for torsion. Circular shafts in torsion use the polar second moment J, which for a circle is twice I.

Lessons in this school

  1. Normal stress and strain
    Stress is internal force per unit area, and strain is deformation per unit length.
  2. Shear stress and strain
    Shear stress acts parallel to a surface.
  3. Hooke's law and elastic constants
    In the elastic range, stress is proportional to strain.
  4. Axially loaded members
    Bars, rods and columns under axial load stretch or shorten.
  5. Thermal stress
    Temperature changes make materials expand or contract.
  6. Torsion of circular shafts
    Shafts transmit power by twisting.
  7. Bending stress in beams
    Bending creates tension on one side of a beam and compression on the other.
  8. Shear stress in beams
    Beams carry shear force as well as bending moment.
  9. Beam deflection
    Beams must be stiff enough not to deflect too much in service.
  10. Combined loading
    Many members carry more than one type of load at once.
  11. Stress transformation and Mohr's circle
    Stresses at a point depend on the orientation of the plane considered.
  12. Failure theories
    Failure theories predict when a material will yield or fracture under combined stresses.
  13. Buckling of columns
    Slender columns can fail by buckling sideways at stresses well below their crushing strength.
  14. Thin-walled pressure vessels
    Pressure vessels such as tanks, pipes and boilers hold fluids under pressure.
  15. Strain energy
    When a material deforms elastically, it stores energy.

Mechanics of Materials: questions students ask

What is double shear?

Loading in which a pin or bolt is sheared across two planes, each carrying half the force.

What extra condition is needed to solve statically indeterminate axial problems?

A compatibility condition relating the deformations.

What is the coefficient of thermal expansion of steel, approximately?

About 12 × 10-6 per °C.

Where is the shear stress greatest in a shaft under torsion?

At the outer surface.
