Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is greenwashing?

Making misleading claims about the environmental benefits of a product or organisation. From “Design and the environment” →

In plain English: Design decisions shape how much energy, material and waste a product creates over its whole life. Key idea: Design decisions about materials, energy, lifespan and end of life fix most of a product's environmental impact; impacts include climate change, resource depletion, pollution, waste and biodiversity loss, and environmental claims must be truthful, clear, complete and substantiated to avoid greenwashing.

What does nesting mean in manufacturing?

Arranging parts closely on a sheet to minimise offcuts. From “Designing out waste” →

In plain English: The best way to deal with waste is to stop it being created. Key idea: Waste arises in manufacture, packaging, use and disposal, and designers prevent it through material efficiency, nesting, zero-waste cutting, standard parts, digital prototyping, durability and minimal, recyclable or reusable packaging, supported by UK measures such as the Plastic Packaging Tax.

What is the central principle of cradle to cradle?

Waste equals food. From “Cradle to cradle” →

In plain English: Cradle to cradle is a design philosophy in which materials are designed to cycle safely forever. Key idea: Cradle to cradle, from McDonough and Braungart (2002), seeks eco-effectiveness rather than eco-efficiency: waste equals food, materials are designed as biological or technical nutrients that cycle safely, renewable energy is used and diversity celebrated, though perfect loops are hard to achieve.

What is a roach motel pattern?

A design that is easy to get into, such as a subscription, but hard to get out of. From “Dark patterns” →

In plain English: Dark patterns, also called deceptive patterns, are design tricks that push people into choices they did not intend. Key idea: Dark or deceptive patterns trick or pressure users through techniques such as hard cancellation, confirmshaming, sneaking items into baskets, hidden costs, fake urgency and preselection; they are common, harm trust and are increasingly regulated by UK consumer and data protection law and by EU and US regulators.

What did the Gender Shades study find?

Commercial gender classifiers had much higher error rates for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. From “Bias in design and data” →

In plain English: Designs and data sets can systematically disadvantage some groups. Key idea: Bias arises from teams, research samples, default users and data, including sampling, historical and measurement bias; examples in crash testing, facial analysis, pulse oximeters and hiring show real harm, and designers can reduce it with diverse teams and participants, disaggregated data, audits and questioning defaults.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Sustainable and Ethical Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which UK body published the Green Claims Code? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Competition and Markets Authority. The CMA published the Green Claims Code in 2021 to help businesses avoid misleading claims.

  2. Which standard sets out the principles and framework for life cycle assessment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    ISO 14040. ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 cover LCA; ISO 9001 is quality management.

  3. Which phrase describes the linear economy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Take, make, waste. The linear economy extracts resources, makes products and discards them.

  4. A product is labelled "eco-friendly" with no evidence or explanation. Which Green Claims Code principle does this most clearly fail? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Claims must be clear and substantiated. Vague claims without evidence can mislead consumers.

  5. Why do inner loops such as reuse keep more value than recycling? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They keep the product's form and function intact. Recycling breaks products down to materials, losing the value of their form and labour.

  6. Which strategy prevents fabric waste in garment making? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Zero-waste pattern cutting. Zero-waste cutting fits pieces together so no fabric is left over.

  7. A designer can reduce a kettle's environmental impact most at which point? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    When choosing its materials, energy use and lifespan. Early design choices determine most of a product's footprint.

  8. Why is a functional unit needed in an LCA? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    So options delivering the same function are compared fairly. Comparing per functional unit makes reusable and single-use options comparable.

  9. A washing machine maker wants an incentive to build longer-lasting machines. Which model fits best? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lease machines as a service and keep ownership. When the maker owns the machine, durability lowers its costs, aligning incentives.

Key takeaways

  • Design decisions about materials, energy, lifespan and end of life fix most of a product's environmental impact; impacts include climate change, resource depletion, pollution, waste and biodiversity loss, and environmental claims must be truthful, clear, complete and substantiated to avoid greenwashing.
  • Life cycle assessment, standardised in ISO 14040 and 14044, quantifies environmental impacts through goal and scope, inventory, impact assessment and interpretation; functional units and system boundaries make comparisons fair, and LCA helps designers find hotspots and compare options.
  • The circular economy replaces take, make, waste with systems that eliminate waste, circulate products and materials at their highest value and regenerate nature; the butterfly diagram's technical and biological cycles show that inner loops like reuse keep more value than recycling, and business models such as product as a service support this.
  • Waste arises in manufacture, packaging, use and disposal, and designers prevent it through material efficiency, nesting, zero-waste cutting, standard parts, digital prototyping, durability and minimal, recyclable or reusable packaging, supported by UK measures such as the Plastic Packaging Tax.
  • Design for disassembly uses reversible, standard and few fasteners, modular parts and material marking so products can be repaired, upgraded and recycled; planned obsolescence shortens lives, and right to repair rules in the UK, EU and France push makers to provide parts, information and repairable designs.
  • Embodied energy and carbon measure the impact of producing materials, recycling can cut them sharply, and terms such as recycled, recyclable, bio-based, biodegradable and compostable mean different things; sustainable material choice balances function, durability, end of life and hazards across the whole life cycle.

Common mistakes in Sustainable and Ethical Design

  • Thinking environmental impact is mainly a manufacturing or recycling problem. Designers set the material, energy and lifespan choices that determine impact across the whole life cycle.
  • Comparing products per item rather than per functional unit. A reusable product must be compared with the number of disposable items it replaces.
  • Equating the circular economy with recycling. Recycling is the outermost loop; keeping products in use through sharing, repair and reuse retains more value.
  • Thinking recyclable packaging solves the waste problem. Avoiding or reusing packaging prevents waste, which is better than recycling it.
  • Assuming glue and sealed cases are needed for good design. Many products achieve slimness and robustness with screws, clips and modular parts that allow repair.
  • Assuming bio-based means biodegradable or always better. Some bio-based plastics do not biodegrade, and a bio-based material can have higher impact than a recycled one.

Sustainable and Ethical Design: questions students ask

What are the four phases of LCA in ISO 14040?

Goal and scope definition, inventory analysis, impact assessment and interpretation.

What are the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's three circular economy principles?

Eliminate waste and pollution, circulate products and materials at their highest value, and regenerate nature.

What was the Phoebus cartel?

A 1924 agreement among light bulb makers to limit bulb life to about 1,000 hours.

What is embodied carbon?

The greenhouse gas emitted in extracting, processing and making a material.
