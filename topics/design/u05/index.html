Lessons in this school

  1. Problem framing and reframing
    How a problem is framed decides which solutions anyone will consider.
  2. Point-of-view statements
    A point-of-view statement captures a user, a need and an insight in one sentence, giving the team a clear, human-centred problem to solve.
  3. Design briefs
    A design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom and why.
  4. Design specifications
    A design specification lists the measurable requirements a design must meet.
  5. Requirements and constraints
    Requirements say what a design must do; constraints limit how it can do it.
  6. Stakeholder mapping
    Every design affects more people than its users.
  7. Root cause analysis and the five whys
    Designers often meet symptoms rather than causes.
  8. Prioritisation methods
    Design teams always have more problems and ideas than time.
  9. Assumptions and hypotheses
    Every design rests on assumptions about users, problems and solutions.
  10. Success criteria and metrics
    To know whether a design works, teams need success criteria agreed in advance and metrics that reflect real user value.
  11. Scoping the problem space
    A problem can be too big to solve or too small to matter.
  12. The ethics of problem choice
    Designers choose, or accept, which problems to work on, and that choice is itself ethical.

Key terms

Frame
A way of seeing a situation that defines what the problem is and what kind of solution counts
Point-of-view statement
A problem statement combining a user, a need and an insight
Design brief
A short statement of the problem, users, context, aims, constraints and deliverables for a design
Design specification
A list of measurable requirements that any design solution must meet
Functional requirement
A statement of what a design must do
Stakeholder
Anyone who affects or is affected by a project
Five whys
Asking "why?" repeatedly to trace a problem from symptom to root cause
Impact-effort matrix
A two-by-two grid prioritising items by value delivered and work required
Assumption
Something believed to be true about users, the problem or the solution, without sufficient evidence
Success criterion
A statement of what a design must achieve to be judged successful
Problem space
The full situation surrounding a problem: users, needs, causes and context
Design justice
An approach that asks who participates in, benefits from and is harmed by design, and centres affected communities
