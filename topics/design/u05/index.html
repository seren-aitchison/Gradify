Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a problem frame?

A way of seeing a situation that defines what the problem is, what matters and what solutions count. From “Problem framing and reframing” →

In plain English: How a problem is framed decides which solutions anyone will consider. Key idea: Spending time on framing is cheap compared with building the wrong solution well. The first framing of a problem is rarely the best one.

What should a design brief include?

The context and problem, client and users, aims, constraints and deliverables. From “Design briefs” →

In plain English: A design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom and why. Key idea: A good brief defines the problem and the people clearly, sets constraints and deliverables, and leaves room for the solution.

Give an example of a non-functional requirement.

The page must load in under two seconds (or must meet WCAG 2.2 AA, or work for five years). From “Requirements and constraints” →

In plain English: Requirements say what a design must do; constraints limit how it can do it. Key idea: Requirements conflict. Make trade-offs explicit and agree priorities with stakeholders, rather than leaving them to chance late in the project.

What is a "must-be" feature in the Kano model?

An expected basic whose absence causes dissatisfaction, but whose presence doesn't delight. From “Prioritisation methods” →

In plain English: Design teams always have more problems and ideas than time. Key idea: Prioritise by evidence of user impact, not by who shouts loudest or what is most fun to build.

State Goodhart's law.

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. From “Success criteria and metrics” →

In plain English: To know whether a design works, teams need success criteria agreed in advance and metrics that reflect real user value. Key idea: Measure outcomes that matter to users, not just activity. More clicks or time on a page can mean confusion, not engagement.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Defining Problems

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What did Schön call the activity of defining what a problem is before solving it? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Problem setting. Schön distinguished problem setting (framing) from problem solving.

  2. Which element of a POV explains why the need exists? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The insight. The insight gives a surprising reason drawn from research.

  3. Which belongs in a design brief? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The users and the problem to be addressed. A brief sets out the problem and people; detailed requirements belong in the specification.

  4. A school says "we need a bigger car park" because the road is jammed at drop-off. Which reframe opens the most options? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How might we make it easy for families to get pupils to school without jams?. Reframing to the goal (getting pupils to school) opens walking buses, staggered times and other options.

  5. Which is the strongest POV? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A new nurse needs a way to feel confident asking for help, because she fears looking incompetent in her first weeks. It names a specific user, a verb-based need and a research insight.

  6. Which is the most open brief? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Help elderly residents feel less isolated in winter. It states the goal without choosing a product type.

  7. When is reframing least useful? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    After a frame has been tested and works, when the team needs to deliver. Reframing is valuable early or when solutions fail; once a tested frame works, delivery matters.

  8. A team's POV reads "Shoppers need to save money because things are expensive". What is the main weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The insight is obvious, suggesting the research hasn't gone deep enough. A POV's insight should surprise; an obvious reason gives little direction for design.

  9. A client insists the brief says "design a mobile app", but research shows many users have no smartphone. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Raise the evidence and negotiate a brief based on the need. Briefs can be refined; designers should show evidence and agree changes with the client.

Key takeaways

  • Spending time on framing is cheap compared with building the wrong solution well. The first framing of a problem is rarely the best one.
  • A POV states a need, not a solution, and its insight should surprise: if it's obvious, the research hasn't gone deep enough.
  • A good brief defines the problem and the people clearly, sets constraints and deliverables, and leaves room for the solution.
  • A good specification point can be tested: anyone could check whether the design meets it and agree on the answer.
  • Requirements conflict. Make trade-offs explicit and agree priorities with stakeholders, rather than leaving them to chance late in the project.
  • In human-centred design, low-power, high-interest stakeholders are often the people the design most affects; they need deliberate involvement because they are easy to ignore.

Common mistakes in Defining Problems

  • Reframing endlessly. Reframing is valuable early, but a team must eventually commit to a frame, test it and act.
  • Writing the need as a noun or a feature ("needs an app", "needs a better website"). That decides the solution before ideation.
  • Writing the solution into the brief ("design a phone app that..."). Unless the solution type is a genuine constraint, it narrows creativity too early.
  • Writing specification points that describe a solution ("must be made of wood with a hinge") rather than requirements, unless the material is truly required.
  • Treating every requirement as a must. If everything is essential, nothing guides decisions when time or money runs short.
  • Treating the client as the only stakeholder. Designs that please the client but fail staff or users who must live with them often fail in use.

Lessons in this school

  1. Problem framing and reframing
    How a problem is framed decides which solutions anyone will consider.
  2. Point-of-view statements
    A point-of-view statement captures a user, a need and an insight in one sentence, giving the team a clear, human-centred problem to solve.
  3. Design briefs
    A design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom and why.
  4. Design specifications
    A design specification lists the measurable requirements a design must meet.
  5. Requirements and constraints
    Requirements say what a design must do; constraints limit how it can do it.
  6. Stakeholder mapping
    Every design affects more people than its users.
  7. Root cause analysis and the five whys
    Designers often meet symptoms rather than causes.
  8. Prioritisation methods
    Design teams always have more problems and ideas than time.
  9. Assumptions and hypotheses
    Every design rests on assumptions about users, problems and solutions.
  10. Success criteria and metrics
    To know whether a design works, teams need success criteria agreed in advance and metrics that reflect real user value.
  11. Scoping the problem space
    A problem can be too big to solve or too small to matter.
  12. The ethics of problem choice
    Designers choose, or accept, which problems to work on, and that choice is itself ethical.
