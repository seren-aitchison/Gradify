Key terms and formulas at a glance

Formative assessment
Using evidence of pupils' learning to adapt teaching and learning to meet their needs
Exit ticket
A short task at the end of a lesson that shows the teacher what pupils have understood
Validity
The degree to which the inferences drawn from an assessment's results are justified for a given purpose
Assessment for learning
Seeking and interpreting evidence to decide where learners are, where they need to go and how to get there
Command word
A word in a question, such as describe, explain or evaluate, that signals the kind of answer required
Stem
The question or statement part of a multiple-choice item
Feedback
Information given to a learner about their performance that is intended to help them improve
Live marking
Giving feedback, written or spoken, while pupils are working
Deep marking
Detailed written marking with extensive comments and often a required pupil response
Peer assessment
Pupils judging and giving feedback on each other's work against shared criteria
Whole-class feedback
Feedback given to the class based on common strengths and errors identified from reading a set of work
Data drop
A scheduled point at which teachers enter assessment data into a school tracking system

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name four purposes of assessment.

For example: formative, summative, diagnostic, certification, accountability. From “The purposes of assessment” →

In plain English: Assessment is any process that gathers evidence of what pupils know and can do. Key idea: Start with the purpose. Ask what decision the assessment will inform, then design it to give the evidence that decision needs.

How did the Assessment Reform Group define assessment for learning?

Seeking and interpreting evidence for learners and teachers to decide where learners are, where they need to go and how best to get there. From “Assessment for learning” →

In plain English: Assessment for learning (AfL) uses evidence of learning, minute by minute and day by day, to help pupils improve. Key idea: AfL is not a set of gimmicks. It is a cycle: know where pupils are going, find out where they are, and help them get there, with pupils increasingly taking part themselves.

What three questions does effective feedback answer, according to Hattie and Timperley?

Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next? From “Effective feedback: the EEF guidance” →

In plain English: Feedback is information that helps pupils close the gap between where they are and where they need to be. Key idea: Feedback works when pupils use it to improve their work or thinking. Focus it on the task, the process and next steps, and make sure pupils have time and reason to act on it.

Why might pupils' self-assessment be inaccurate?

Novices may lack the knowledge to judge quality, so those who know least may overestimate their performance. From “Peer and self-assessment” →

In plain English: When pupils assess their own and each other's work, they can learn what quality looks like and how to improve. Key idea: Peer and self-assessment work when pupils know what good looks like and know what to do next. Teach them how to assess, with examples and criteria, before expecting it to help.

What does a key stage 2 scaled score of 100 represent?

The expected standard. From “Standardised tests and scaled scores” →

In plain English: Standardised tests compare a pupil's performance with a large reference group. Key idea: Standardised scores compare pupils with a reference group: 100 is average, and most pupils score between 85 and 115. Small differences are within measurement error.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Assessment and Feedback

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the main purpose of summative assessment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    To sum up what pupils have learned at a particular point. Summative assessment sums up learning, often for reporting; formative and diagnostic uses guide next steps.

  2. Which is an example of summative assessment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An end-of-unit test used to report attainment. The end-of-unit test sums up learning for reporting; the others guide immediate teaching decisions.

  3. Which best defines reliability in assessment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The consistency of results across occasions, tasks and markers. Reliability is about consistency; curriculum coverage relates to validity.

  4. A teacher uses a Friday quiz to decide which topics to reteach on Monday. How is the quiz being used? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Formatively, because the results change what is taught next. Use, not timing or format, makes assessment formative.

  5. How can a summative test be used formatively? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    By analysing which questions pupils got wrong and planning to revisit those topics. Question-level analysis turns summative results into information for teaching.

  6. A maths test uses long, complex word problems, and weak readers score poorly regardless of maths ability. What threat is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Construct-irrelevant variance. Reading ability, which is not the target, is affecting scores.

  7. A school uses one half-termly GCSE-style grade both to plan teaching and to report progress. What is the strongest criticism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The grade is too broad to guide teaching and too unreliable on a few topics to report progress. Christodoulou argues that formative and summative needs differ; one broad grade serves neither well.

  8. A department sets a full summative test every three weeks. What is the most likely effect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Teaching time is lost and workload rises without much better information. Frequent summative tests take time from teaching; low-stakes formative checks give more useful information more cheaply.

  9. A teacher concludes that a pupil "can write well" from one spelling test. What is the main problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The inference is not valid, because a spelling test under-represents writing. Validity concerns whether the conclusion is justified; spelling is a small part of writing.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the purpose. Ask what decision the assessment will inform, then design it to give the evidence that decision needs.
  • Formative assessment is for deciding what to do next; summative assessment is for saying what has been learned. Both are needed, but they need different designs.
  • Ask "What do I want to conclude from this result, and is that conclusion justified?" A reliable assessment that measures the wrong thing still leads to wrong decisions.
  • AfL is not a set of gimmicks. It is a cycle: know where pupils are going, find out where they are, and help them get there, with pupils increasingly taking part themselves.
  • Decide exactly what you want to find out, then write the simplest question that will show it. Write the mark scheme at the same time.
  • A good multiple-choice question is hard to get right for the wrong reason and easy to diagnose when it is wrong. Every distractor should be plausible and should reveal a specific misconception.

Common mistakes in Assessment and Feedback

  • Assuming that more testing always gives more useful information. If an assessment isn't designed for the decision you need to make, its results can mislead.
  • Using end-of-unit tests only to record a score. Analyse which questions pupils got wrong and plan to revisit them.
  • Treating a single score as a precise measure. Small differences between pupils, or between one test and the next, are often within measurement error.
  • Writing the learning objective on the board and assuming it has been "shared". Pupils understand quality by seeing and discussing examples, not by copying a sentence.
  • Writing a mark scheme after marking a few scripts. This lets the first answers shape the standard and makes marking inconsistent.
  • Writing one correct answer and then quickly adding obviously wrong options. Implausible distractors make the question too easy and reveal nothing.

Lessons in this school

  1. The purposes of assessment
    Assessment is any process that gathers evidence of what pupils know and can do.
  2. Formative and summative assessment
    Formative and summative assessment play different roles in teaching.
  3. Validity and reliability
    Validity and reliability are the two key qualities of any assessment.
  4. Assessment for learning
    Assessment for learning (AfL) uses evidence of learning, minute by minute and day by day, to help pupils improve.
  5. Designing assessment questions
    Good assessment depends on good questions.
  6. Multiple-choice questions and diagnostic distractors
    Well-written multiple-choice questions can assess understanding quickly and precisely, especially when the wrong options are based on real misconceptions.
  7. Effective feedback: the EEF guidance
    Feedback is information that helps pupils close the gap between where they are and where they need to be.
  8. Written and verbal feedback
    Feedback can be written or spoken, and each has strengths and costs.
  9. Marking, purpose and workload
    Marking takes a large share of teachers' time, yet much of it has little effect on learning.
  10. Peer and self-assessment
    When pupils assess their own and each other's work, they can learn what quality looks like and how to improve.
  11. Whole-class feedback
    Whole-class feedback means reading pupils' work, identifying the common strengths and errors, and addressing them with the whole class.
  12. Using assessment data
    Schools collect a great deal of assessment data.
  13. Standardised tests and scaled scores
    Standardised tests compare a pupil's performance with a large reference group.
  14. Statutory assessment in England
    Pupils in England take a series of statutory assessments from Reception to the end of key stage 2, followed by public qualifications.
  15. GCSEs, grading and examinations
    GCSEs are the main qualifications taken at 16 in England.
  16. Reporting to parents and carers
    Schools must report pupils' progress to parents, and good communication with families supports learning.

Assessment and Feedback: questions students ask

According to Wiliam, what makes assessment formative?

How the information is used: it is formative when it changes teaching or learning.

What makes a summative assessment fair across pupils?

Sampling the whole domain, standardised conditions and consistent marking.

What is construct under-representation?

When an assessment misses important parts of what it claims to measure.

Why write the mark scheme before marking?

So the standard is set in advance and marking is consistent, rather than shaped by the first scripts.
