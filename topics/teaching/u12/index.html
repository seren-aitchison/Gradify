Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How did Durkheim think education creates social solidarity?

By passing on shared culture and values that make children feel part of society. From “Functionalist perspectives on education” →

In plain English: Functionalists see education as serving the needs of society: building shared values, teaching skills and sorting people into roles. Key idea: Functionalists see education building solidarity, teaching skills and universalistic standards, and allocating roles meritocratically; critics dispute meritocracy and shared values.

What is the hidden curriculum?

Norms and values learned from school organisation rather than formal lessons. From “The hidden curriculum” →

In plain English: The hidden curriculum is what pupils learn from the way schools are organised, not from formal lessons. Key idea: The hidden curriculum teaches norms through school organisation; functionalists, Marxists, feminists and interactionists interpret it differently.

What are Bourdieu's three forms of capital?

Economic, cultural and social. From “Cultural capital and education” →

In plain English: Cultural capital is the knowledge, tastes and confidence that bring educational advantage. Key idea: Bourdieu's capitals and habitus explain how middle-class advantage appears as merit; research shows capitals at work in school choice and university entry, alongside material factors.

What is polarisation?

Pupils moving towards pro-school or anti-school responses. From “The self-fulfilling prophecy” →

In plain English: A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true because it was made. Key idea: Labels can become self-fulfilling through treatment and internalisation; pupils may form subcultures, but can also resist, making prophecies self-negating.

What are contextual admissions?

Admissions that take applicants' backgrounds into account. From “Higher education” →

In plain English: Higher education has expanded greatly, but access and outcomes remain unequal. Key idea: Higher education has become a mass system with about half of young people entering by 25; access remains unequal by class and to elite universities; fees and widening participation are debated.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Education

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who described school as a bridge between family and society? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Talcott Parsons. Parsons saw school bridging particularistic and universalistic standards.

  2. Who described education as an ideological state apparatus? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Louis Althusser. Louis Althusser described education as an ideological state apparatus in 1971.

  3. Who argued boys take up more classroom space and teacher attention? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Dale Spender. Spender's Invisible Women made this argument.

  4. A country expands university places to boost economic growth. Which theory supports this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Human capital theory. Education is treated as investment in productivity.

  5. Pupils learn to obey bells and teachers, as workers obey managers. This illustrates… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The correspondence principle. Obeying bells and teachers mirrors workplace hierarchy: the correspondence principle.

  6. A school introduces female engineering role models to increase girls' uptake of physics. This reflects… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Liberal feminism. Reform and role models are liberal feminist strategies.

  7. Which is the main criticism of functionalist meritocracy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Background strongly shapes outcomes. Class, ethnicity and gender influence achievement.

  8. Why does Willis's study challenge simple Marxist accounts? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Resistance still reproduces class. Pupils' agency and class reproduction coexist in Willis's account.

  9. Why do Black feminists add to feminist analysis of education? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Experiences differ by ethnicity as well as gender. Racial and gender stereotyping intersect.

Key takeaways

  • Functionalists see education building solidarity, teaching skills and universalistic standards, and allocating roles meritocratically; critics dispute meritocracy and shared values.
  • Marxists see education reproducing and legitimating class inequality through ideology and correspondence with work; Willis shows resistance that still reproduces class.
  • Liberal feminists stress progress and equal opportunities; radical feminists stress continuing patriarchy in schools; Black feminists highlight intersecting inequalities.
  • Schools socialise pupils into norms and values and control behaviour formally and informally; exclusions and surveillance raise questions of fairness.
  • The hidden curriculum teaches norms through school organisation; functionalists, Marxists, feminists and interactionists interpret it differently.
  • The disadvantage gap in England narrowed to 3.82 in 2025/26 but remains large; material deprivation, cultural explanations and school processes all contribute.

Common mistakes in Education

  • Assuming education is fully meritocratic. Background strongly influences outcomes.
  • Assuming pupils passively absorb school values. Many resist, as Willis showed.
  • Assuming girls' higher average achievement means gender inequality in education is over. Harassment, subject segregation and later pay gaps persist.
  • Treating school discipline as neutral. Rules and sanctions affect some groups more than others.
  • Assuming the hidden curriculum is deliberately planned. Much of it is unintended.
  • Blaming working-class families' attitudes. Material constraints and school bias are also important.

Lessons in this school

  1. Functionalist perspectives on education
    Functionalists see education as serving the needs of society: building shared values, teaching skills and sorting people into roles.
  2. Marxist perspectives on education
    Marxists see education as reproducing class inequality and legitimating it.
  3. Feminist perspectives on education
    Feminist sociologists examine how education reproduces or challenges gender inequality.
  4. Education, socialisation and social control
    Schools socialise young people and regulate their behaviour.
  5. The hidden curriculum
    The hidden curriculum is what pupils learn from the way schools are organised, not from formal lessons.
  6. Educational achievement and social class
    Social class is one of the strongest predictors of educational achievement in England.
  7. Educational achievement and gender
    Girls have outperformed boys on average in English schools for decades.
  8. Educational achievement and ethnicity
    Educational outcomes vary widely by ethnic group.
  9. Cultural capital and education
    Cultural capital is the knowledge, tastes and confidence that bring educational advantage.
  10. Linguistic codes
    Bernstein argued that differences in how families use language affect school success.
  11. Labelling in education
    Labelling theory examines how teachers' judgements about pupils can shape pupils' identities and outcomes.
  12. Teacher expectations
    Teacher expectations are beliefs teachers hold about pupils' future achievement.
  13. The self-fulfilling prophecy
    A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true because it was made.
  14. Selection
    Selection refers to how schools and systems sort pupils by ability or other criteria.
  15. Educational inequality
    Educational inequality is unequal access to, experience of and outcomes from education.
  16. Private education
    Private (independent) schools educate a small share of pupils but a large share of elites.
  17. Higher education
    Higher education has expanded greatly, but access and outcomes remain unequal.
  18. Education policy
    Education policy shapes how schools are organised, funded and held accountable.
  19. Global education
    Access to and quality of education vary greatly around the world.
  20. Digital learning
    Digital technology is changing how people learn, from online courses to AI tools.
  21. Educational reform
    Educational reform aims to improve standards and reduce inequality.

Education: questions students ask

What is an ideological state apparatus?

An institution, such as education, that spreads ideas maintaining ruling-class power.

What do liberal feminists welcome in education?

Equal opportunities progress such as anti-discrimination law, the National Curriculum and girls' rising achievement.

What is normalising judgement?

Foucault's term for comparing people against norms through grading, ranking and timetables.

What did Keddie argue about cultural deprivation?

It is a myth that blames working-class culture rather than schools' bias.
