Key terms and formulas at a glance

Principles of Instruction
Rosenshine's ten research-based principles for effective teaching, published in 2012
Explicit instruction
Systematic teaching in which the teacher explains, models, checks and guides practice before pupils work independently
Daily review
A short retrieval activity at the start of a lesson on recent and prerequisite learning
Small steps
Manageable portions of new material taught and practised one at a time
Modelling
Demonstrating how to complete a task so pupils can see and learn from expert performance
Faded guidance
Gradually removing steps from worked examples so learners complete more of each problem
Checking for understanding
Gathering evidence from all pupils about whether they have understood, in order to adjust teaching
Cold calling
Naming the pupil to answer after posing a question, regardless of who has hands up
Hinge question
A quick diagnostic question at a key point in a lesson whose answers determine the next teaching step
Wait time
The pause after a question (wait time 1) or after a pupil's answer (wait time 2) before the next speaker
Probing question
A follow-up question that pushes a pupil to clarify, justify or extend an answer
Scaffolding
Temporary, contingent support that enables a learner to do a task they cannot yet do alone, withdrawn as competence grows

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the three research traditions behind Rosenshine's principles.

Cognitive science, process-product research on master teachers, and research on cognitive supports such as scaffolding. From “Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction” →

In plain English: In 2012 Barak Rosenshine summarised decades of research into ten principles of effective instruction. Key idea: Rosenshine's principles describe a pattern: review, small steps, lots of questions, models, guided practice, checks, a high success rate, scaffolds, independent practice and regular review. Together they move pupils from new material to fluent, independent use.

How can a teacher tell if a step was too big?

Many pupils fail the check question after it. From “Presenting new material in small steps” →

In plain English: Because working memory is limited, new material is best presented in small steps, with practice after each one. Key idea: Teach one manageable step at a time, practise it, check it, then add the next. Step size should fit what pupils already know.

In cold calling, what is the right order?

Ask the question, pause for thinking time, then name the pupil. From “Cold calling and no opt out” →

In plain English: Cold calling means choosing who answers rather than waiting for hands up; "no opt out" means a pupil who can't answer at first still ends up giving the right answer. Key idea: With hands-up, a few pupils do the thinking. With cold calling, everyone has to think, because anyone may be asked.

What is Lemov's "Stretch It"?

Rewarding a correct answer with a harder follow-up question. From “Probing questions and extending answers” →

In plain English: A pupil's first answer is often incomplete. Key idea: Don't stop at the first answer. Follow up so pupils explain, justify and improve their answers, and hold out for an answer that is fully right.

Give two risks of not removing scaffolds.

Pupils become dependent on them and can't perform without them, and redundant support can add cognitive load as expertise grows. From “Removing scaffolds” →

In plain English: Scaffolds help only if they are eventually taken away. Key idea: Plan from the start how each scaffold will be removed. Fade in steps, check that pupils can cope, and be ready to bring support back for those who need it.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Classroom Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is one of Rosenshine's ten principles? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Present new material in small steps, with pupil practice after each step. Small steps with practice is principle 2. The others are not among Rosenshine's principles, and learning styles lack evidence.

  2. Which is the best description of explicit instruction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Stepwise explanation and modelling with frequent questions and guided practice. Explicit instruction is interactive and structured. A lecture lacks checks and practice; discovery and choice are minimally guided.

  3. Which two of Rosenshine's principles concern review? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Begin with a short review, and engage pupils in weekly and monthly review. Principles 1 and 10 are about daily, weekly and monthly review.

  4. Rosenshine drew on studies observing the teachers whose classes made the most progress. What were these called? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Process-product studies. Process-product research related observed teaching behaviours to pupils' progress; it was one of his three sources.

  5. Why do Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argue minimal guidance is less effective for novices? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Searching for solutions without guidance overloads working memory and builds little knowledge. Their argument rests on cognitive architecture: unguided search uses the working memory needed to learn.

  6. A trainee's starter asks "Who can remember what we did last week?" and takes one answer. What is the best improvement? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Set every pupil three short retrieval questions on the prerequisites for today. Every pupil should retrieve, and the questions should target what today's lesson needs.

  7. A school observes trainees against all ten principles in every 50-minute lesson. What is the strongest criticism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The principles describe teaching over time, so one lesson need not show all ten. Weekly and monthly review, for example, cannot happen in every lesson, and a lesson may focus on some stages. The principles are research-based and not limited to primary.

  8. Which statement about productive failure is most accurate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It involves problem-solving before instruction but still ends with explicit teaching of the solution. Kapur's design pairs an initial attempt with later explicit instruction; it is about sequence, not abandoning teaching.

  9. A review shows that most pupils have forgotten the prerequisite for today's new topic. What should the teacher do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reteach the prerequisite briefly before introducing the new material. New learning depends on prior knowledge; a brief reteach now prevents overload and misconceptions later.

Key takeaways

  • Rosenshine's principles describe a pattern: review, small steps, lots of questions, models, guided practice, checks, a high success rate, scaffolds, independent practice and regular review. Together they move pupils from new material to fluent, independent use.
  • For novices learning new material, explicit teaching with modelling and guided practice is more effective than leaving them to discover it. Guidance can be reduced as expertise grows.
  • Begin lessons by having every pupil retrieve what they need for today, and return to important material regularly over weeks and months.
  • Teach one manageable step at a time, practise it, check it, then add the next. Step size should fit what pupils already know.
  • Show pupils how to do it and say what you are thinking as you do it. The model should reveal the decisions, not just the finished product.
  • Fade worked examples gradually, one step at a time, so that pupils take on more of the problem as their knowledge grows.

Common mistakes in Classroom Practice

  • Treating the principles as a lesson-observation checklist where every principle must appear in every lesson. They describe teaching over time; a single lesson may emphasise only some.
  • Confusing explicit instruction with "chalk and talk". Explicit teaching is short steps with frequent questions and practice, not a long monologue.
  • Running a review as "What did we do last lesson?" and taking an answer from one volunteer. It checks one pupil's memory and gives everyone else nothing to retrieve.
  • Breaking a skill into steps but never recombining them. Pupils also need practice of the whole task, or the steps remain fragments.
  • Showing a finished exemplar and expecting pupils to work out how it was made. Novices see the product but not the process; model the process.
  • Moving from one worked example straight to a page of independent problems. The abrupt jump leaves many pupils to solve by trial and error.

Lessons in this school

  1. Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction
    In 2012 Barak Rosenshine summarised decades of research into ten principles of effective instruction.
  2. Explicit instruction and its critics
    Explicit instruction means the teacher clearly explains, models and guides practice, rather than leaving pupils to discover new material for themselves.
  3. Daily, weekly and monthly review
    Rosenshine's first and tenth principles concern review: begin lessons by reviewing previous learning, and return to material weekly and monthly.
  4. Presenting new material in small steps
    Because working memory is limited, new material is best presented in small steps, with practice after each one.
  5. Modelling and thinking aloud
    Modelling means showing pupils how an expert does a task, and thinking aloud makes the expert's hidden decisions visible.
  6. Worked examples and faded guidance
    Worked examples are most effective when support is gradually removed, so that pupils move from studying full solutions to completing problems on their own.
  7. Questioning to check understanding
    Questions are teachers' main tool for finding out what pupils understand.
  8. Cold calling and no opt out
    Cold calling means choosing who answers rather than waiting for hands up; "no opt out" means a pupil who can't answer at first still ends up giving the right answer.
  9. Hinge questions and mini-whiteboards
    A hinge question is a quick, carefully designed check at the point in a lesson where the teacher must decide whether to move on.
  10. Wait time and think-pair-share
    Giving pupils time to think before they answer changes who answers and how well.
  11. Probing questions and extending answers
    A pupil's first answer is often incomplete.
  12. Scaffolding
    Scaffolding is temporary support that lets pupils do a task they could not yet do alone, removed as they become able to do it themselves.
  13. Guided practice
    Guided practice is the stage between the teacher's model and pupils' independent work, when pupils practise with the teacher close by to prompt, check and correct.
  14. Independent practice and a high success rate
    Pupils need extensive independent practice to become fluent, and they learn best when they succeed most of the time.
  15. Removing scaffolds
    Scaffolds help only if they are eventually taken away.
  16. Teacher explanations and analogies
    A clear explanation is one of the most powerful things a teacher does.
  17. Classroom discussion and dialogic teaching
    Well-structured talk helps pupils reason, explain and learn from each other.
  18. Collaborative learning and groupwork
    Pupils working together can learn more than they would alone, but only when groupwork is carefully structured.

Classroom Practice: questions students ask

What are Sherrington's four strands of the principles?

Sequencing concepts and modelling; questioning; reviewing material; stages of practice.

What is explicit instruction?

Systematic, interactive teaching in which the teacher explains and models new content in small steps, checks understanding and guides practice before independent work.

What did Rosenshine find about how effective teachers began lessons?

With a short review of previous learning, around five to eight minutes.

Why should new material be presented in small steps?

Working memory is limited, so each new step must be processed and practised before another is added.
