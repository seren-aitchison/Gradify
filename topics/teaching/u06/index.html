Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the enacted curriculum?

The curriculum as actually taught in lessons, which may differ from the plan. From “What a curriculum is” →

In plain English: The curriculum is what pupils are meant to learn, in what order and why. Key idea: Curriculum is what pupils should learn and in what order; pedagogy is how it is taught; assessment is how we find out whether it has been learned. All three must line up.

What did Young mean by powerful knowledge?

Specialised, systematic disciplinary knowledge that lets people think beyond their everyday experience. From “Powerful knowledge and knowledge-rich curricula” →

In plain English: Many schools in England describe their curriculum as knowledge-rich, drawing on ideas such as Michael Young's powerful knowledge and E. Key idea: A knowledge-rich curriculum specifies the knowledge pupils should learn, sequences it carefully and makes sure it is remembered, on the grounds that knowledge underpins thinking, reading and opportunity.

Which two factors did Coe et al. (2014) find had the strongest evidence of impact?

Teachers' pedagogical content knowledge and the quality of instruction. From “Subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge” →

In plain English: Teachers need deep knowledge of their subject and also knowledge of how to teach it: which examples, explanations and representations work, and what pupils find hard. Key idea: Knowing a subject is necessary but not enough. Good teachers also know how pupils learn that subject: where they go wrong, and which explanations and examples help.

What did Bourdieu mean by cultural capital?

Valued knowledge, tastes, skills and credentials, largely acquired through family, that advantage some groups in schools and other institutions. From “Cultural capital and a diverse curriculum” →

In plain English: Debates about curriculum often turn on whose knowledge is taught and who benefits. Key idea: A good curriculum gives every pupil access to powerful knowledge and rich experiences, while thinking carefully about whose knowledge, voices and histories are included.

Name three criteria for judging a curriculum resource.

For example: accuracy, coherence, clear explanations and examples, sufficient practice, alignment, clear layout, inclusive representation. From “Textbooks and curriculum resources” →

In plain English: High-quality textbooks and curriculum resources can support coherent teaching and reduce workload. Key idea: Choose resources that are accurate, coherent and well sequenced. A good resource supports a curriculum; a collection of unconnected worksheets does not.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Curriculum and Subject Knowledge

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which best distinguishes curriculum from pedagogy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Curriculum is what pupils learn; pedagogy is how it is taught. Curriculum concerns content and sequence; pedagogy concerns teaching methods.

  2. In the 2019 framework, what did "impact" refer to? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    What pupils have learned, as shown in what they know, remember and can do. Impact concerned pupils' learning and outcomes.

  3. Which years make up key stage 3? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Years 7 to 9. Key stage 3 covers Years 7 to 9; key stage 2 is Years 3 to 6 and key stage 4 Years 10 and 11.

  4. A planned unit includes historians' debates, but a teacher skips them for time. What does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A gap between the planned and enacted curriculum. The taught curriculum differed from the intended one.

  5. A school has a detailed intent statement, but teaching hasn't changed. Which link in the chain is weak? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Implementation. Implementation turns intent into teaching; without it intent has no effect.

  6. Which statement about academies is correct? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They need not follow the National Curriculum but must offer a broad and balanced curriculum. Funding agreements require a broad and balanced curriculum including core subjects and RE.

  7. Which is the strongest evidence of the quality of a school's curriculum? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    What pupils know, remember and can do across subjects over time. The experienced curriculum, what pupils actually learn, is the real test.

  8. Which criticism of the 2019 framework's influence is most often made? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Some schools produced curriculum paperwork without changing teaching. Critics noted elaborate intent documents that did not change classroom practice.

  9. A school says its curriculum "is the National Curriculum". What is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The National Curriculum is a minimum within a wider school curriculum the school must design. The framework says it forms one part of the school curriculum; schools must build around it.

Key takeaways

  • Curriculum is what pupils should learn and in what order; pedagogy is how it is taught; assessment is how we find out whether it has been learned. All three must line up.
  • Intent, implementation and impact describe one chain: decide what pupils should learn, teach it well over time, and check what they have learned. Each link depends on the others.
  • The National Curriculum is a statutory minimum for maintained schools, not the whole school curriculum. Schools build a wider curriculum around it.
  • A knowledge-rich curriculum specifies the knowledge pupils should learn, sequences it carefully and makes sure it is remembered, on the grounds that knowledge underpins thinking, reading and opportunity.
  • Teach both what a subject knows and how it knows it. Disciplinary knowledge is not a generic skill: it is specific to each subject and itself needs to be taught explicitly.
  • If the curriculum is well sequenced, progress means knowing more and remembering more of it. The curriculum itself sets out what progress looks like.

Common mistakes in Curriculum and Subject Knowledge

  • Treating the curriculum as the list of topics on a plan. The real test is what pupils know and can do as a result.
  • Writing a long intent statement and stopping there. Intent matters only if it changes what is taught and what pupils learn.
  • Assuming every state school must follow the National Curriculum. Academies and free schools need not, though most use it as a basis.
  • Equating a knowledge-rich curriculum with memorising lists of facts. Powerful knowledge is connected and conceptual, and must be understood and used, not just recalled.
  • Teaching "source analysis" or "enquiry skills" as generic skills separate from content. Disciplinary thinking depends on substantive knowledge and must be taught within the subject.
  • Sequencing by what resources are available or what topics are popular rather than by what pupils need to know first.

Lessons in this school

  1. What a curriculum is
    The curriculum is what pupils are meant to learn, in what order and why.
  2. Intent, implementation and impact
    Ofsted's 2019 framework described the curriculum through three words: intent, implementation and impact.
  3. The National Curriculum in England
    The National Curriculum sets out the subjects and content that maintained schools in England must teach.
  4. Powerful knowledge and knowledge-rich curricula
    Many schools in England describe their curriculum as knowledge-rich, drawing on ideas such as Michael Young's powerful knowledge and E.
  5. Substantive and disciplinary knowledge
    Every subject has two kinds of knowledge: substantive knowledge, the content it has established, and disciplinary knowledge, how that knowledge is created, tested and debated.
  6. Sequencing and the curriculum as progression model
    The order in which content is taught matters, because later learning depends on earlier learning.
  7. Subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge
    Teachers need deep knowledge of their subject and also knowledge of how to teach it: which examples, explanations and representations work, and what pupils find hard.
  8. Threshold concepts
    Some ideas in a subject act like doorways: once understood, they transform how a learner sees the subject.
  9. Anticipating misconceptions in your subject
    Every subject has predictable misconceptions.
  10. Core knowledge and hinterland
    Not everything taught in a topic needs to be memorised.
  11. Cultural capital and a diverse curriculum
    Debates about curriculum often turn on whose knowledge is taught and who benefits.
  12. Curriculum mapping and coherence
    A coherent curriculum fits together: within a subject from year to year, across units in a year, and with assessment.
  13. Schemes of work
    A scheme of work turns the curriculum into a sequence of lessons for a unit.
  14. Textbooks and curriculum resources
    High-quality textbooks and curriculum resources can support coherent teaching and reduce workload.
  15. Spiral and mastery curricula
    Two influential ideas about curriculum structure are the spiral curriculum, which revisits topics at increasing depth, and mastery, which secures each step before moving on.
  16. Cross-curricular links
    Making links between subjects can help pupils see connections and apply knowledge.
  17. Reviewing a curriculum
    Curricula need regular review to check that they are working.
