Lessons in this school

  1. What a curriculum is
    The curriculum is what pupils are meant to learn, in what order and why.
  2. Intent, implementation and impact
    Ofsted's 2019 framework described the curriculum through three words: intent, implementation and impact.
  3. The National Curriculum in England
    The National Curriculum sets out the subjects and content that maintained schools in England must teach.
  4. Powerful knowledge and knowledge-rich curricula
    Many schools in England describe their curriculum as knowledge-rich, drawing on ideas such as Michael Young's powerful knowledge and E.
  5. Substantive and disciplinary knowledge
    Every subject has two kinds of knowledge: substantive knowledge, the content it has established, and disciplinary knowledge, how that knowledge is created, tested and debated.
  6. Sequencing and the curriculum as progression model
    The order in which content is taught matters, because later learning depends on earlier learning.
  7. Subject knowledge and pedagogical content knowledge
    Teachers need deep knowledge of their subject and also knowledge of how to teach it: which examples, explanations and representations work, and what pupils find hard.
  8. Threshold concepts
    Some ideas in a subject act like doorways: once understood, they transform how a learner sees the subject.
  9. Anticipating misconceptions in your subject
    Every subject has predictable misconceptions.
  10. Core knowledge and hinterland
    Not everything taught in a topic needs to be memorised.
  11. Cultural capital and a diverse curriculum
    Debates about curriculum often turn on whose knowledge is taught and who benefits.
  12. Curriculum mapping and coherence
    A coherent curriculum fits together: within a subject from year to year, across units in a year, and with assessment.
  13. Schemes of work
    A scheme of work turns the curriculum into a sequence of lessons for a unit.
  14. Textbooks and curriculum resources
    High-quality textbooks and curriculum resources can support coherent teaching and reduce workload.
  15. Spiral and mastery curricula
    Two influential ideas about curriculum structure are the spiral curriculum, which revisits topics at increasing depth, and mastery, which secures each step before moving on.
  16. Cross-curricular links
    Making links between subjects can help pupils see connections and apply knowledge.
  17. Reviewing a curriculum
    Curricula need regular review to check that they are working.

Key terms

Curriculum
What pupils are intended to learn, structured over time, and the rationale for it
Intent
The aims of a curriculum: what pupils should learn at each stage and why
Programme of study
The statutory content for a subject at a key stage in the National Curriculum
Powerful knowledge
Young's term for specialised, systematic disciplinary knowledge that allows people to think beyond everyday experience
Substantive knowledge
The content a subject has established as knowledge, such as facts, concepts and models
Vertical coherence
The way each year or stage of a curriculum builds on what came before
Pedagogical content knowledge
Knowledge of how to make a particular subject understandable, including representations and pupils' difficulties
Threshold concept
A transformative, often troublesome concept that opens up a new way of thinking about a subject
Diagnostic question bank
A collection of questions with distractors based on known misconceptions
Core knowledge
The knowledge in a unit that pupils must secure and remember
Cultural capital
Bourdieu's term for valued knowledge, tastes and credentials, largely acquired through family, that advantage some groups in institutions
Horizontal coherence
The way units taught at the same time support each other
Scheme of work
A medium-term plan setting out the sequence of lessons and knowledge for a unit
Curriculum resource
Material such as a textbook, scheme or slides used to teach the curriculum
