Five sample flashcards

Which Teachers' Standard includes promoting high standards of literacy?

Standard 3, demonstrate good subject and curriculum knowledge. From “Literacy in every subject” →

In plain English: Reading, writing and talk are the main ways pupils learn in every subject, so every teacher is a teacher of literacy. Key idea: Because pupils learn through reading, writing and talk in every subject, every teacher is responsible for literacy, and weak literacy widens gaps over time unless it is taught.

Why does fluency matter for comprehension?

Automatic word reading frees working memory to focus on meaning. From “Reading fluency” →

In plain English: Fluent readers read accurately, at a good pace and with expression, which frees attention for understanding. Key idea: Fluency combines accuracy, automaticity and prosody, frees working memory for comprehension and can be built through modelled, echo, choral and repeated reading.

What does the simple view of reading say?

Reading comprehension is the product of word reading and language comprehension. From “Systematic synthetic phonics and early reading” →

In plain English: In England, early reading is taught through systematic synthetic phonics, which teaches children the links between sounds and letters and how to blend them. Key idea: Systematic synthetic phonics teaches GPCs in a planned order, with blending and segmenting, decodable books and frequent assessment, while language comprehension is built through talk, stories and vocabulary.

What did Myhill and colleagues find about grammar teaching?

Linking grammar to its effect in writing improved writing quality. From “Spelling, punctuation and grammar” →

In plain English: Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar help writers communicate clearly, and they are assessed in national tests and examinations. Key idea: Spelling, punctuation and grammar are set out in the national curriculum and assessed nationally, and they are best taught through patterns, morphology and links to their effect in real writing.

What does structuring talk involve planning?

Who talks, about what, for how long and how the talk is shared. From “Structuring classroom talk” →

In plain English: Productive classroom talk rarely happens by accident; it is planned through routines, roles and clear expectations. Key idea: Classroom talk is productive when it is structured, with a worthwhile question, enough knowledge, clear roles or routines, a time limit and a planned way of sharing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Literacy and Oracy Across the Curriculum

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who is responsible for promoting literacy in a secondary school, according to the Teachers' Standards? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Every teacher. Standard 3 applies to all teachers, whatever their subject.

  2. Who argued that literacy becomes more specialised by subject as pupils progress? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Shanahan and Shanahan. Shanahan and Shanahan (2008) proposed a model of increasing specialisation.

  3. What does prosody mean in reading? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reading with expression and phrasing. Prosody is the expressive, well-phrased part of fluency.

  4. A geography lesson asks pupils to read a dense report on climate change. Which is a literacy demand of this lesson? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reading complex text with subject vocabulary. Reading the report and its vocabulary is a literacy demand built into the subject.

  5. Which is an example of disciplinary reading in science? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Checking a claim against the data in a table. Scientists read for precise claims and evidence, such as data.

  6. Which activity involves pupils reading back each sentence after the teacher? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Echo reading. In echo reading, pupils repeat the teacher's modelled sentence.

  7. A school asks every teacher to correct the same ten spellings in all subjects. What is the main limitation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It ignores each subject's own literacy demands. The EEF recommends subject-specific approaches over generic ones applied identically everywhere.

  8. A school asks all subjects to use the same paragraph structure for every written answer. What is the main risk? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It ignores how writing differs between subjects. Generic structures can suit some subjects poorly, so disciplinary approaches are recommended.

  9. A Year 6 pupil reads accurately but very slowly and without expression. What is the best support? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Repeated reading of short passages. An accurate but slow reader needs fluency practice, such as repeated reading.

Key takeaways

  • Because pupils learn through reading, writing and talk in every subject, every teacher is responsible for literacy, and weak literacy widens gaps over time unless it is taught.
  • Disciplinary literacy is the subject-specific way experts read, write and talk, and teachers should make these ways explicit rather than relying on generic literacy skills alone.
  • Fluency combines accuracy, automaticity and prosody, frees working memory for comprehension and can be built through modelled, echo, choral and repeated reading.
  • Comprehension strategies such as predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarising help pupils read actively, and work best when modelled explicitly and combined with knowledge and vocabulary.
  • Vocabulary comes in three tiers, and teachers should explicitly teach key Tier 2 and Tier 3 words with definitions, examples, morphology and repeated use.
  • Systematic synthetic phonics teaches GPCs in a planned order, with blending and segmenting, decodable books and frequent assessment, while language comprehension is built through talk, stories and vocabulary.

Common mistakes in Literacy and Oracy Across the Curriculum

  • Assuming literacy is the English department's job. Every subject has its own reading, writing and vocabulary demands that its teachers are best placed to teach.
  • Thinking literacy is the same in every subject. The way a historian reads a source is different from the way a scientist reads data, and both must be taught.
  • Treating fast reading as fluent reading. Fluency also needs accuracy and expression; racing through a text without phrasing does not support understanding.
  • Teaching strategies as stand-alone exercises with short, easy texts. Model them with real subject texts and remember that knowledge of the topic matters too.
  • Only teaching technical Tier 3 terms. Tier 2 academic words such as evaluate or significant are just as important and often assumed to be known.
  • Giving early readers books full of words they cannot yet decode and encouraging them to guess from pictures. Give decodable books matched to the GPCs they know.

Lessons in this school

  1. Literacy in every subject
    Reading, writing and talk are the main ways pupils learn in every subject, so every teacher is a teacher of literacy.
  2. Disciplinary literacy
    Each subject reads, writes and talks in its own way, and pupils need to be taught those ways.
  3. Reading fluency
    Fluent readers read accurately, at a good pace and with expression, which frees attention for understanding.
  4. Reading comprehension strategies
    Comprehension strategies, such as predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarising, help pupils actively make sense of texts.
  5. Vocabulary: tiers and explicit teaching
    Vocabulary is closely linked to reading comprehension and to success in every subject.
  6. Systematic synthetic phonics and early reading
    In England, early reading is taught through systematic synthetic phonics, which teaches children the links between sounds and letters and how to blend them.
  7. Teaching writing in subjects
    Writing is hard because it asks pupils to manage ideas, language and structure at the same time.
  8. Modelling academic writing
    Pupils learn to write in a subject by seeing how an expert writes and hearing the decisions behind it.
  9. Spelling, punctuation and grammar
    Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar help writers communicate clearly, and they are assessed in national tests and examinations.
  10. What oracy is
    Oracy is the ability to speak well and to use talk to think and learn.
  11. Learning to talk and learning through talk
    Oracy has two sides: teaching pupils to talk well, and using talk as a way to learn content.
  12. Structuring classroom talk
    Productive classroom talk rarely happens by accident; it is planned through routines, roles and clear expectations.
  13. Sentence stems and speaking frames
    Sentence stems and speaking frames give pupils the language to start and structure what they want to say.
  14. Assessing oracy
    Spoken language can be assessed, but it is harder to capture than writing because talk is fleeting and depends on context.
