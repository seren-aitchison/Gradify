Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

For safeguarding purposes, who counts as a child?

Anyone under the age of 18. From “What safeguarding means” →

In plain English: Safeguarding is the action taken to promote the welfare of children and protect them from harm, and it is everyone's responsibility. Key idea: Safeguarding means protecting children from maltreatment, preventing impairment of their health or development, ensuring safe and effective care and enabling the best outcomes; child protection is part of it, and every member of staff must act on concerns.

What is a section 47 enquiry?

An enquiry made when there is reasonable cause to suspect a child is suffering or likely to suffer significant harm. From “Working Together to Safeguard Children” →

In plain English: Working Together to Safeguard Children is the statutory guidance on how organisations and agencies should work together to protect children. Key idea: Working Together sets out how the local authority, health and police lead local safeguarding, with schools as key partners in early help, children in need and child protection work.

Does UK GDPR prevent sharing information to keep a child safe?

No; data protection law does not prevent sharing for safeguarding. From “Recording and reporting concerns” →

In plain English: Clear, accurate records help the DSL and other agencies protect children, and they may later be used in court. Key idea: Safeguarding records should be factual, timely, signed and dated, using the child's words and separating fact from opinion, and data protection law does not prevent sharing information to keep a child safe.

Which law created the Prevent duty for schools?

Section 26 of the Counter-Terrorism and Security Act 2015. From “Radicalisation and the Prevent duty” →

In plain English: Schools have a legal duty to have due regard to the need to prevent people from being drawn into terrorism. Key idea: The Prevent duty requires schools to have due regard to preventing people being drawn into terrorism; staff should notice signs, challenge extremist views, keep open discussion and report concerns to the DSL.

Who should an allegation about the headteacher be reported to?

The chair of governors, or the proprietor or equivalent. From “Allegations against staff and low-level concerns” →

In plain English: Schools must deal promptly and fairly with concerns about adults who work with children. Key idea: Allegations that meet the harm threshold go to the headteacher, or chair of governors if about the headteacher, who consults the LADO; low-level concerns are shared under the school's policy to catch patterns early and protect everyone.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What should a trainee do first if they are worried about a pupil's welfare? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Speak to the DSL straight away. KCSIE expects staff to act on concerns immediately by speaking to the DSL.

  2. What type of document is Keeping Children Safe in Education? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Statutory guidance. KCSIE is statutory guidance that schools must have regard to.

  3. Who should normally be the designated safeguarding lead? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A member of the senior leadership team. KCSIE says the DSL should be a member of the senior leadership team.

  4. Which is an example of safeguarding rather than child protection specifically? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Teaching pupils about online safety. Teaching online safety promotes welfare for all; child protection concerns specific children at risk of significant harm.

  5. A parent asks a teacher to keep a worrying conversation about their child secret. What does KCSIE expect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Share the concern with the DSL. Fears about sharing information must not stand in the way of protecting a child.

  6. A teacher is unhappy that a concern she raised seems to have led to no action. What should she do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Escalate the concern and, if needed, refer it herself. KCSIE says staff should press for reconsideration and can refer to children's social care if a child's situation does not improve.

  7. A teaching assistant says a concern is probably nothing, so she will not mention it. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Share it with the DSL, who can see the wider picture. Small concerns can form part of a bigger picture that only the DSL can see.

  8. A school says abuse does not happen among its pupils, so it does not need regular training. What principle does this break? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It could happen here. KCSIE expects staff to adopt the attitude that it could happen in their school.

  9. A teacher starts asking a pupil detailed questions to find out exactly what happened at home. What is the main problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Staff should not investigate; that is for the DSL and agencies. Investigating can distress the child and harm later enquiries, so staff pass concerns on.

Key takeaways

  • Safeguarding means protecting children from maltreatment, preventing impairment of their health or development, ensuring safe and effective care and enabling the best outcomes; child protection is part of it, and every member of staff must act on concerns.
  • KCSIE is statutory guidance that schools must have regard to; all staff must read Part one, know the DSL and the procedures and act in the best interests of the child.
  • The DSL, a senior leader, takes lead responsibility for safeguarding; staff should pass concerns to the DSL or a deputy promptly, never investigate themselves, and escalate or refer directly if needed.
  • Working Together sets out how the local authority, health and police lead local safeguarding, with schools as key partners in early help, children in need and child protection work.
  • The four categories of abuse are physical, emotional, sexual abuse and neglect; they often overlap, and staff should report concerns without needing to decide the category.
  • Children often do not tell, so staff must notice physical, behavioural and attendance indicators and report them to the DSL, without assuming that any single sign proves or disproves abuse.

Common mistakes in Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare

  • Thinking safeguarding is only the DSL's job. Every adult in school must notice, act and pass on concerns; the DSL then decides what happens next.
  • Relying on last year's version of KCSIE. It is updated regularly, so always check the current version and your school's policy.
  • Waiting until the next day because the DSL has gone home. Speak to a deputy DSL or senior leader, and call 999 if a child is in immediate danger.
  • Thinking schools only matter once abuse is confirmed. Schools are vital in spotting early signs and supporting early help before problems escalate.
  • Thinking you must be sure what type of abuse it is before reporting. Report any concern to the DSL; working out the category is not your job.
  • Dismissing a change in behaviour as a phase or as part of a pupil's disability. Record and report it; the DSL can see whether it fits a wider pattern.

Lessons in this school

  1. What safeguarding means
    Safeguarding is the action taken to promote the welfare of children and protect them from harm, and it is everyone's responsibility.
  2. Keeping Children Safe in Education
    Keeping Children Safe in Education is the statutory guidance that sets out what schools and colleges in England must do to safeguard children.
  3. The designated safeguarding lead
    Every school must have a designated safeguarding lead who takes lead responsibility for safeguarding and child protection.
  4. Working Together to Safeguard Children
    Working Together to Safeguard Children is the statutory guidance on how organisations and agencies should work together to protect children.
  5. The four categories of abuse
    Statutory guidance describes four categories of abuse: physical, emotional, sexual abuse and neglect.
  6. Signs of abuse and neglect
    Children rarely tell an adult directly that they are being abused, so staff need to notice signs and changes.
  7. Responding to a disclosure
    When a child tells an adult about abuse, how the adult responds matters greatly.
  8. Recording and reporting concerns
    Clear, accurate records help the DSL and other agencies protect children, and they may later be used in court.
  9. Child-on-child abuse
    Children can abuse other children, and schools must take this seriously rather than dismiss it as banter or growing up.
  10. Online safety
    Online life brings benefits and risks for children, and online safety is part of safeguarding.
  11. Child criminal and sexual exploitation
    Children can be exploited by individuals or groups who take advantage of an imbalance of power to coerce them into criminal or sexual activity.
  12. Radicalisation and the Prevent duty
    Schools have a legal duty to have due regard to the need to prevent people from being drawn into terrorism.
  13. Female genital mutilation: the mandatory reporting duty
    Female genital mutilation is illegal in the UK and a form of child abuse with serious lifelong consequences.
  14. Children missing education
    A child who is not on a school roll or not receiving suitable education may be at risk of harm and of falling behind.
  15. Pupil mental health
    Many pupils experience mental health difficulties, and teachers are often the first to notice.
  16. Allegations against staff and low-level concerns
    Schools must deal promptly and fairly with concerns about adults who work with children.
  17. Safer working practice
    Safer working practice protects children and staff by setting clear professional boundaries.
  18. Safeguarding children with SEND
    Children with special educational needs and disabilities are at greater risk of abuse, and face extra barriers to being recognised and protected.
  19. Attendance as a safeguarding issue
    Regular attendance is vital for learning and is also one of the ways schools keep children safe.

Safeguarding and Pupil Welfare: questions students ask

What does it mean that schools must have regard to KCSIE?

They should follow it unless there is a good reason not to.

How often should a DSL's training be updated?

At least every two years, with knowledge refreshed at least annually.

Who are the three safeguarding partners?

The local authority, the integrated care board and the chief officer of police.

What are the four categories of abuse?

Physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse and neglect.
