Key terms and formulas at a glance

Learning
A lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding, held in long-term memory
Working memory
The limited, temporary system that holds and processes the information a person is currently thinking about
Long-term memory
The durable, high-capacity store of a person's knowledge and skills
Prior knowledge
What a pupil already knows and can do that bears on a new topic
Misconception
A belief that conflicts with accepted subject understanding but makes sense to the learner
Cognitive load theory
A theory of instructional design based on the limits of working memory and the role of schemas in long-term memory
Intrinsic load
Working-memory demand that comes from the complexity of the material relative to the learner's knowledge
Worked example
A step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem or complete a task
Expertise reversal effect
The finding that techniques effective for novices can become ineffective or harmful for more knowledgeable learners
Split-attention effect

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How does the ITTECF describe learning?

As a lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding. From “Learning as a change in long-term memory” →

In plain English: The Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework starts from one idea: learning is a lasting change in what pupils know, understand or can do. Key idea: Learning is a lasting change in long-term memory. A lesson succeeds when pupils can still use what was taught days and weeks later, not only when they can do it at the end of the lesson.

What did Recht and Leslie (1988) show?

Weak readers who knew a lot about baseball understood a baseball passage better than strong readers who knew little about it. From “Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning” →

In plain English: What pupils already know is the strongest single influence on what they learn next. Key idea: New learning builds on old. Before teaching something new, check that the knowledge it depends on is secure; if it isn't, teach or revisit that first.

Why do worked examples help novices, according to cognitive load theory?

They remove the costly search for a solution, freeing working memory to understand and store the steps. From “The worked example effect” →

In plain English: Studying a worked example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem, is one of the most reliable ways for novices to learn a procedure. Key idea: For novices, studying how an expert solves a problem is more efficient than struggling to find the solution alone. Pair each example with a similar problem for pupils to try.

What is the testing effect?

The finding that retrieving information strengthens long-term memory more than restudying it for the same time. From “Retrieval practice” →

In plain English: Retrieval practice means bringing information to mind from memory, for example through low-stakes quizzes, rather than re-reading it. Key idea: Getting information out of memory makes it easier to retrieve next time. Quizzing is a learning tool, not just an assessment tool.

What shape is the forgetting curve?

Rapid forgetting at first, then a slower decline. From “Forgetting and desirable difficulties” →

In plain English: Forgetting is normal and, managed well, useful. Key idea: Pupils will forget much of what they are taught unless it is revisited. Plan for forgetting by building in retrieval and spacing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on How Pupils Learn

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best captures learning as the ITTECF describes it? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A lasting change in pupils' capabilities or understanding. The framework defines learning by what lasts. Completion, repetition and engagement can happen without any lasting change in long-term memory.

  2. Which description of working memory is most accurate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A small, temporary workspace for information being actively processed. Working memory is limited in capacity and duration and holds what is being processed now; long-term memory is the permanent store.

  3. Which best describes a schema? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An organised network of related knowledge in long-term memory. A schema is a connected structure in long-term memory. A temporary store is working memory; preferences are not schemas.

  4. A trainee notes that every pupil answered the plenary questions correctly. What is the most accurate conclusion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pupils can perform the skill now; retention needs checking later. Correct answers immediately after teaching show current performance. Whether it has become learning is only visible after a delay, so the next step is planned retrieval.

  5. A trainee's science slide has a dense paragraph, a diagram, a video and spoken explanation all at once for a new concept. What is the best change? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Present the concept in steps, with the diagram explained aloud and the paragraph removed. Several new elements competing at once overload working memory. Stepping the content and removing redundant text reduces load; copying and speeding up add to it.

  6. A pupil who believes all metals are magnetic learns that copper is not. What must happen to her schema? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It must be restructured to separate metals from magnetic materials. Contradictory information requires accommodation: the schema is reorganised. Schemas are revised rather than wiped.

  7. Which lesson feature is the strongest evidence that pupils have learned last month's content? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Accurate answers to unprompted questions on it at the start of today's lesson. Unprompted retrieval after a delay is direct evidence of a change in long-term memory. Quiet work, completion and self-report are proxies that can occur without retention.

  8. Two pupils are given the same multi-step algebra problem. One knows the rules of signs fluently; the other is unsure. Why is the second pupil more likely to fail? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Uncertain rules must be worked out in working memory, leaving less capacity for the steps. Fluent knowledge is retrieved as chunks and costs little working memory. Working out the rules each time consumes capacity needed for the problem. Nothing suggests a difference in capacity or motivation.

  9. A department wants pupils to analyse unseen texts better. Based on schema theory, which plan is likely to help most? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Teach rich, connected knowledge of contexts and genres alongside analysis practice. Analysis depends on knowledge organised into schemas. Generic drills without knowledge transfer poorly; motivation helps but cannot supply missing knowledge.

Key takeaways

  • Learning is a lasting change in long-term memory. A lesson succeeds when pupils can still use what was taught days and weeks later, not only when they can do it at the end of the lesson.
  • Working memory can hold only a few new items at once, for a few seconds. Any task that asks novices to juggle many new elements at the same time risks overload, and overloaded pupils learn little.
  • The richer and better connected pupils' schemas, the easier it is to learn more. Teaching should build connections deliberately, not just add isolated facts.
  • New learning builds on old. Before teaching something new, check that the knowledge it depends on is secure; if it isn't, teach or revisit that first.
  • Misconceptions are logical to the learner and are part of connected knowledge. They must be surfaced and confronted, not just overwritten with the correct statement.
  • Cognitive load theory says novices learn best from instruction that reduces unnecessary working-memory load and directs their limited capacity towards building schemas.

Common mistakes in How Pupils Learn

  • Judging a lesson by how well it went on the day. A lesson where pupils struggled productively may have produced more learning than one where everyone finished easily.
  • Thinking pupils with a poor working memory "just need to concentrate". Overload is a design problem: reduce the number of new elements, give steps one at a time and provide external memory aids.
  • Teaching topics as unconnected chunks and assuming pupils will join them up. Make the links explicit: refer back, compare, and show how the new idea fits the big picture.
  • Assuming that because a topic was "covered" last year, pupils know it. Coverage is not retention: always check.
  • Telling pupils "that is wrong" and moving on. Without addressing why the idea seemed sensible, many pupils will revert to it when the lesson is over.
  • Treating cognitive load theory as "make everything easy". The theory aims to remove unnecessary load, not all challenge; difficulty that requires thinking about the material is what produces learning.

Lessons in this school

  1. Learning as a change in long-term memory
    The Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework starts from one idea: learning is a lasting change in what pupils know, understand or can do.
  2. Working memory and its limits
    Working memory is where pupils hold and manipulate information while they think, and it is small.
  3. Long-term memory and schemas
    Long-term memory is not a filing cabinet of separate facts but a web of connected knowledge called schemas.
  4. Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning
    What pupils already know is the strongest single influence on what they learn next.
  5. Misconceptions and conceptual change
    Pupils do not arrive as blank slates: they bring intuitive ideas that are often wrong and hard to shift.
  6. Cognitive load theory: Sweller 1988
    Cognitive load theory, first set out by John Sweller in 1988, asks how instruction should be designed given the limits of working memory.
  7. Intrinsic and extraneous load
    Cognitive load theory distinguishes the load that comes from the material itself from the load that comes from how it is taught.
  8. The worked example effect
    Studying a worked example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem, is one of the most reliable ways for novices to learn a procedure.
  9. The expertise reversal effect
    Guidance that helps novices can become a hindrance as pupils gain expertise.
  10. Split attention and redundancy
    Two effects from cognitive load theory explain why some slides and worksheets make learning harder: split attention and redundancy.
  11. Dual coding
    Dual coding means combining words with visuals such as diagrams, timelines and graphic organisers, so that pupils can process information through two connected channels.
  12. Retrieval practice
    Retrieval practice means bringing information to mind from memory, for example through low-stakes quizzes, rather than re-reading it.
  13. Spaced practice
    Spreading practice of the same material over time leads to better long-term retention than cramming it into one session.
  14. Interleaving
    Interleaving means mixing different types of problem or topic in practice, rather than practising one type in a block.
  15. Elaboration and generative learning
    Learning is deeper when pupils actively make sense of material: explaining why, connecting it to what they know, and transforming it into a new form.
  16. Forgetting and desirable difficulties
    Forgetting is normal and, managed well, useful.
  17. Motivation and self-efficacy
    Pupils are more likely to persist and succeed when they believe they can, value the work and feel some control and belonging.
  18. Metacognition and self-regulated learning
    Metacognition is thinking about thinking: how pupils plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning.
  19. Learning styles and other neuromyths
    Some popular ideas about the brain and learning have little or no evidence behind them.

How Pupils Learn: questions students ask

Why does knowledge reduce the load on working memory?

Knowledge organised in long-term memory can be retrieved as a single chunk, so it takes up less working-memory space than many separate new elements.

What is a schema?

An organised structure of connected knowledge in long-term memory, which can be handled in working memory as a single unit.

What did Ausubel (1968) call the most important single factor in learning?

What the learner already knows.

Why are misconceptions hard to change?

They are part of a coherent schema that makes sense to the pupil, often supported by everyday experience.
