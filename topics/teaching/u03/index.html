Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does Teachers' Standard 5 require?

That teachers adapt teaching to respond to the strengths and needs of all pupils. From “What adaptive teaching is” →

In plain English: Adaptive teaching means keeping high expectations for every pupil while adjusting how you teach, in response to what pupils know and need, so that all of them can succeed. Key idea: Adaptive teaching keeps the same ambitious goal for all and changes the support, explanation, time or practice pupils get. It is responsive, not a set of separate worksheets planned in advance.

Who attracts Pupil Premium funding?

Pupils eligible for free school meals in the last six years, looked-after and previously looked-after children, and (separately) service children. From “Disadvantaged pupils and the Pupil Premium” →

In plain English: Pupils from disadvantaged backgrounds in England do less well at school, on average, than their peers. Key idea: Disadvantaged pupils benefit most from high-quality teaching every day. The best use of Pupil Premium funding usually starts there, not with one-off extras.

Name the four broad areas of need.

Communication and interaction; cognition and learning; social, emotional and mental health; sensory and/or physical needs. From “The four broad areas of need” →

In plain English: The SEND Code of Practice groups special educational needs into four broad areas. Key idea: The four areas describe kinds of need, to help schools plan support. Many pupils have needs in more than one area, and the aim is to understand the individual, not to fit them into a category.

What did the DISS project find?

Pupils who received the most TA support made less progress than similar pupils with less support, because of how TAs were deployed. From “Working with teaching assistants” →

In plain English: Teaching assistants (TAs) make up a large part of the school workforce, but research has shown that how they are deployed matters greatly. Key idea: TAs should add to the teacher's teaching, not replace it. The pupils who need most help should not get less of the teacher.

What are the two main areas of difference in the diagnostic criteria for autism?

Social communication and interaction; and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities (including sensory differences). From “Autism in the classroom” →

In plain English: Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication and interaction, often with focused interests, a preference for routine and sensory differences. Key idea: For many autistic pupils the barriers are unpredictability, ambiguous language, social demands and sensory overload. Predictable routines, clear explicit language and sensory awareness help, and benefit many other pupils too.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Adaptive Teaching and SEND

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the best description of adaptive teaching? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Keeping the same high goal for all pupils while adjusting support in response to their needs. The ITTECF describes responsive adjustment of support with high expectations; separate tasks risk lowering expectations.

  2. What is differentiation by outcome? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    All pupils do the same task and produce different levels of response. By outcome means a common task with varied responses; by task means different tasks.

  3. Which is the best source of evidence of understanding during a lesson? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Every pupil's answer to a short check question. All-pupil responses give direct evidence; hands, silence and self-report are unreliable.

  4. Which practice is most clearly adaptive teaching? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Re-explaining a step with a new example for pupils who got a hinge question wrong. Adaptive teaching responds to evidence of understanding with targeted support towards the same goal.

  5. Which adaptation removes a barrier without lowering the goal? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pre-teaching key vocabulary and providing a shorter text for a pupil with weak reading. The pupil still learns and explains the same content; only the reading barrier is reduced.

  6. A check shows a quarter of the class has a specific misconception. What is an efficient response? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Work with that group briefly while others continue independently. A short targeted group addresses the misconception without holding back the rest of the class.

  7. A trainee says her lesson is adaptive because it has "all, most, some" objectives, with "some" reserved for the top table. What is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It risks capping expectations for pupils not on the top table, which the ITTECF warns against. Pre-deciding that only some pupils will reach the full goal can lower expectations; adaptive teaching aims for all to succeed with varied support.

  8. Why did the ITTECF move away from encouraging distinct tasks for different groups? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Pre-planned tiered tasks can set lower expectations and fix pupils into levels. The framework warns that artificial distinct tasks and lower expectations reduce the value of adaptation.

  9. Midway through a lesson, most pupils cannot do the step the next activity depends on. What should the teacher do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Change the plan: re-teach the step and move the next activity to a later lesson. The next activity depends on the step; pressing on means pupils practise without understanding.

Key takeaways

  • Adaptive teaching keeps the same ambitious goal for all and changes the support, explanation, time or practice pupils get. It is responsive, not a set of separate worksheets planned in advance.
  • Adapt the support, not the ambition. Ask of any adaptation: does it help this pupil reach the same goal, or does it quietly lower the goal?
  • Check understanding at key points and change what you do in response. A plan is a starting point, not a script.
  • Grouping alone does not raise attainment; what matters is what is taught to each group. Fixed low groups risk lowering expectations; flexible, purposeful groups that change often are safer.
  • Disadvantaged pupils benefit most from high-quality teaching every day. The best use of Pupil Premium funding usually starts there, not with one-off extras.
  • EAL pupils need to learn English and subject content at the same time. Make content accessible and teach the academic language explicitly; don't lower the cognitive challenge.

Common mistakes in Adaptive Teaching and SEND

  • Planning three different worksheets for "low, middle and high" pupils. This can cap expectations and fix pupils' attainment, and it is time-consuming; the ITTECF warns against artificially distinct tasks.
  • Reducing the content of a task because a pupil finds reading or writing hard. Remove the barrier to access, not the learning.
  • Noticing that pupils are confused and carrying on because "I need to get through the plan". Covering content that pupils haven't understood wastes the rest of the lesson.
  • Assuming that setting helps everyone because it narrows the range in each class. On average it helps little and can harm lower-attaining pupils.
  • Treating the Pupil Premium flag as a measure of ability or a reason for easier work. Disadvantaged pupils include high attainers who also need challenge.
  • Placing new-to-English pupils with the lowest-attaining group by default. Their difficulty is language, not prior learning or ability.

Lessons in this school

  1. What adaptive teaching is
    Adaptive teaching means keeping high expectations for every pupil while adjusting how you teach, in response to what pupils know and need, so that all of them can succeed.
  2. Adaptive teaching versus differentiation by task
    For many years "differentiation" in English schools often meant planning different tasks for different groups.
  3. Responding to pupils within the lesson
    Much of adaptive teaching happens in the moment: noticing that pupils are stuck and changing what you do.
  4. Attainment grouping and its evidence
    Schools often group pupils by prior attainment, between classes (setting and streaming) or within a class.
  5. Disadvantaged pupils and the Pupil Premium
    Pupils from disadvantaged backgrounds in England do less well at school, on average, than their peers.
  6. Pupils with English as an additional language
    About one in five pupils in England speaks English as an additional language (EAL).
  7. Challenging high-attaining pupils
    Pupils who already do well also need to be challenged, or they may coast and fall behind their potential.
  8. The SEND Code of Practice 2015
    The Special Educational Needs and Disability Code of Practice: 0 to 25 years (2015) is the statutory guidance that sets out how schools must identify and support pupils with SEND in England.
  9. The four broad areas of need
    The SEND Code of Practice groups special educational needs into four broad areas.
  10. The graduated approach: assess, plan, do, review
    When a pupil needs SEN support, the Code of Practice expects schools to follow a graduated approach: a cycle of assess, plan, do and review.
  11. The role of the SENCO
    Every mainstream school in England must have a special educational needs co-ordinator (SENCO).
  12. Education, health and care plans
    An education, health and care (EHC) plan is a legal document for a child or young person aged 0 to 25 whose needs cannot be met through SEN support alone.
  13. Working with teaching assistants
    Teaching assistants (TAs) make up a large part of the school workforce, but research has shown that how they are deployed matters greatly.
  14. The Equality Act 2010 and reasonable adjustments
    The Equality Act 2010 protects pupils from discrimination, including disabled pupils, and requires schools to make reasonable adjustments.
  15. Communication and interaction needs
    Speech, language and communication needs are among the most common special educational needs in primary schools, and they are often missed.
  16. Dyslexia and specific learning difficulties
    Specific learning difficulties affect particular aspects of learning, such as reading, spelling, number or coordination, rather than learning as a whole.
  17. Autism in the classroom
    Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference affecting social communication and interaction, often with focused interests, a preference for routine and sensory differences.
  18. ADHD in the classroom
    Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) affects attention, activity and impulse control, and can have a large effect on learning and relationships at school.
  19. Social, emotional and mental health needs
    Social, emotional and mental health (SEMH) needs range from anxiety and low mood to attachment difficulties and behaviour that challenges.
  20. Sensory and physical needs
    Some pupils have a vision impairment, hearing impairment, multi-sensory impairment, physical disability or medical condition that affects access to learning.

Adaptive Teaching and SEND: questions students ask

What question should a teacher ask of any adaptation?

Does it help the pupil reach the same goal, or does it lower the goal?

What is responsive teaching?

Checking understanding during teaching and adjusting what you do in response.

What is the difference between setting and streaming?

Setting groups pupils by attainment for a particular subject; streaming groups them for most subjects.

What is the difference between BICS and CALP?

BICS is everyday conversational language; CALP is the academic language of subjects, which takes longer to develop.
