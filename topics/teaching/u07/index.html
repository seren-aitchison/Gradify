Key terms and formulas at a glance

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three stages of backward design?

Identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, then plan learning experiences. From “Planning backwards from outcomes” →

In plain English: Good planning starts from what pupils should know and be able to do at the end, not from an activity. Key idea: Backward design fixes the learning outcome and the evidence of learning first, then chooses teaching that leads straight to them, which protects against planning around activities or coverage.

What did Ausubel say was the most important single factor influencing learning?

What the learner already knows. From “Activating prior knowledge” →

In plain English: New learning is built on what pupils already know, so lessons often begin by bringing relevant knowledge to mind. Key idea: Activating relevant prior knowledge at the start of a lesson strengthens it through retrieval and shows the teacher whether pupils are ready, so the lesson can adapt.

Why should a planned explanation include non-examples?

They show the boundaries of a concept, helping pupils see what it is not. From “Planning explanations and models” →

In plain English: Clear explanations rarely happen by chance, so expert teachers plan the words, examples and models they will use. Key idea: Expert teachers plan explanations in advance, choosing the order of points, precise words, examples, non-examples, a worked model and check questions.

Name three of the five approaches recommended in the EEF SEND guidance.

Any three of explicit instruction, cognitive and metacognitive strategies, scaffolding, flexible grouping and using technology. From “Planning for adaptive teaching” →

In plain English: Adaptive teaching starts at the planning stage, by anticipating where pupils may struggle and planning support and challenge. Key idea: Adaptive planning means one ambitious lesson for all, with anticipated barriers, prepared scaffolds and challenge, and checks that show who needs support, rather than separate tasks for fixed groups.

What is an action step?

A specific, practical change a teacher agrees to make after feedback. From “Lesson observation and feedback” →

In plain English: Trainees and early-career teachers are regularly observed, and the feedback can speed up their development. Key idea: Lesson observation is most useful as a developmental process with a clear focus, specific action steps and follow-up, because single-lesson judgements are unreliable.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Planning Lessons and Sequences

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In backward design, what is planned first? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The intended learning outcomes. Wiggins and McTighe begin with desired results, before evidence or activities.

  2. What does a medium-term plan usually cover? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A sequence of lessons across a topic. Medium-term plans turn the scheme of work into lessons across a topic, often a half-term.

  3. Which of these is a learning intention rather than a task? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    To explain why volcanoes form at plate boundaries. The intention names what pupils learn; the others describe what they do.

  4. A trainee plans a lesson around a card sort but cannot say what pupils will learn from it. Which fault is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Activity-focused planning. Building the lesson around an engaging task with no link to an outcome is the activity sin.

  5. A sequence's fourth lesson asks pupils to use a formula that was never taught. Which problem is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A gap in the sequence. A later lesson assumes knowledge not yet taught, which is a gap.

  6. Which success criterion is a process criterion for a science investigation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Change only one variable at a time. Process criteria describe the steps to follow, such as controlling variables.

  7. A head of department says every lesson must have pupils producing lots of written work, as proof of learning. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Volume of work is a poor proxy; plan the evidence that shows the outcome. Coe and colleagues (2014) note that lots of work produced is a poor proxy, so evidence should be chosen to match the outcome.

  8. A checkpoint quiz shows most pupils are not secure on lesson 3's content. What should the teacher do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Adapt the sequence to reteach before moving on. Checkpoints exist so that the sequence can adapt when pupils are not ready.

  9. Pupils tick every item on a long success-criteria checklist, but their writing is still dull. What is the best next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Show and discuss strong and weak examples. Sadler's point is that quality is often best shown through examples, not longer lists.

Key takeaways

  • Backward design fixes the learning outcome and the evidence of learning first, then chooses teaching that leads straight to them, which protects against planning around activities or coverage.
  • Lessons should be planned as a sequence that breaks the end goal into components, orders them so each builds on the last, and builds in review and checkpoints.
  • Learning intentions describe what pupils will learn rather than what they will do, and success criteria, best shown through examples, describe what successful learning looks like.
  • Activating relevant prior knowledge at the start of a lesson strengthens it through retrieval and shows the teacher whether pupils are ready, so the lesson can adapt.
  • A common lesson shape moves from review through explanation and modelling to guided then independent practice, with checks throughout, but structure should serve learning rather than a fixed template.
  • Pupils remember what they think about, so the best tasks make them think hard about the intended content and avoid busy work and hidden demands.

Common mistakes in Planning Lessons and Sequences

  • Starting with a fun activity and then looking for an objective to attach to it. Decide what pupils should learn and how you will know first, then choose the activity.
  • Planning each lesson as a stand-alone performance. Plan the sequence first so that each lesson builds on the previous one and prepares for the next.
  • Writing the task as the learning intention, such as to complete the worksheet. State the learning the task is meant to produce.
  • Using a starter that is fun but unrelated to the lesson. Choose a starter that brings to mind the knowledge the new content will build on.
  • Following a template so rigidly that each section gets fixed minutes. Move between stages according to what checks show about pupils' understanding.
  • Choosing a task because pupils enjoy it. Choose it because of what it makes pupils think about, and check that enjoyment comes from the content.

Lessons in this school

  1. Planning backwards from outcomes
    Good planning starts from what pupils should know and be able to do at the end, not from an activity.
  2. Planning a sequence of lessons
    Learning rarely fits into a single lesson, so teachers plan sequences that build knowledge over time.
  3. Learning intentions and success criteria
    Learning intentions say what pupils will learn, and success criteria describe what successful learning looks like.
  4. Activating prior knowledge
    New learning is built on what pupils already know, so lessons often begin by bringing relevant knowledge to mind.
  5. The structure of a lesson
    Most lessons follow a recognisable shape, from review to new content, practice and checking.
  6. Choosing tasks and activities
    The tasks pupils do determine what they think about, and so what they learn.
  7. Planning explanations and models
    Clear explanations rarely happen by chance, so expert teachers plan the words, examples and models they will use.
  8. Planning questions in advance
    Good questions are often prepared before the lesson rather than invented on the spot.
  9. Homework and independent study
    Homework can extend learning beyond the lesson, but its value depends on its purpose and design.
  10. Planning for adaptive teaching
    Adaptive teaching starts at the planning stage, by anticipating where pupils may struggle and planning support and challenge.
  11. Planning practical work and trips
    Practical work and educational visits can make learning memorable, but only if they are planned with a clear purpose and managed safely.
  12. Evaluating a lesson
    Teachers improve by reflecting on what happened in a lesson and why, then changing what they do next.
  13. Lesson observation and feedback
    Trainees and early-career teachers are regularly observed, and the feedback can speed up their development.
  14. Planning and workload
    Planning is essential, but it can also become one of the biggest drivers of teacher workload.
  15. Digital technology in the classroom
    Digital technology can support explanations, practice and feedback, but it does not improve learning by itself.
  16. Artificial intelligence and teaching
    Generative artificial intelligence tools can help teachers plan and prepare, but they also bring risks to accuracy, data protection and assessment.

Planning Lessons and Sequences: questions students ask

What is the difference between a component and a composite?

A component is a smaller piece of knowledge or skill; a composite is the complex goal it builds towards.

What is the difference between a learning intention and a task?

The learning intention says what pupils will learn; the task is what they will do to learn it.

What does I do, we do, you do describe?

The gradual release of responsibility from teacher modelling to guided and then independent practice.

What did Willingham mean by memory is the residue of thought?

Pupils remember what they think about, so tasks should make them think about the intended content.
