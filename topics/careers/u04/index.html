Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between strong and weak ties?

Strong ties are close friends and family, with high trust but overlapping information; weak ties are acquaintances who connect you to new information and circles. From “What networking is and why it works” →

In plain English: Networking means building and maintaining professional relationships that share information, advice and opportunities. Key idea: Networking is building reciprocal professional relationships; weak ties (acquaintances) often bring new information and opportunities, which is why networks shape careers and why unequal networks create unequal access.

Name two good questions to ask at a careers fair stand.

Any two such as: What do graduates work on in their first year? What distinguishes the strongest applicants? How did you get your role? From “Careers fairs and networking events” →

In plain English: Careers fairs, employer presentations and networking events put you in the same room as recruiters and professionals. Key idea: Prepare for events with a priority list, research, an introduction and specific questions; engage, note names and details, and follow up promptly, mentioning your conversation in later applications.

What are Kram's two kinds of mentoring function?

Career functions (coaching, exposure, challenging assignments, sponsorship) and psychosocial functions (role modelling, acceptance, counselling, friendship). From “Mentors and sponsors” →

In plain English: A mentor gives you advice and support; a sponsor uses their influence to open doors for you. Key idea: Mentors give advice and support, coaches help you find your own answers and sponsors use their influence to advocate for you; find mentors through formal schemes and relationships, and earn sponsorship through visible, reliable work.

What is career path research on LinkedIn?

Studying the profiles of people in a target role to see the degrees, first jobs and moves that led there. From “Using LinkedIn to research and connect” →

In plain English: LinkedIn is as useful for research as for being found. Key idea: Use LinkedIn to research roles (people search, career paths, company pages) and connect with short, personalised notes that say how you know someone or why you're contacting them; ask for advice separately, not jobs in a first message.

Name four protected characteristics under the Equality Act 2010.

Any four of: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation. From “Managing your digital footprint” →

In plain English: Your digital footprint is everything about you that can be found online. Key idea: Your digital footprint includes what you share and what others publish about you; audit it by searching yourself, review privacy settings, remove harmful content and build positive content, knowing employers mustn't discriminate on protected characteristics they find.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Networking and Your Professional Presence

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who wrote "The strength of weak ties" (1973)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Mark Granovetter. Granovetter's sociological study found job information often came through acquaintances; the others are career theorists.

  2. Which is the best first step in building a network? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Map the contacts you already have. Mapping existing contacts shows where you already have links and where the gaps are.

  3. Which question should you avoid at a careers fair stand? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "What does your company do?". You should research what the company does beforehand; the other questions show interest and gather useful insight.

  4. Which contact is most likely to tell a student about an opportunity she hasn't heard of? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An alumna she met once at a careers event. Weak ties connect to different circles and information; close contacts tend to know what she already knows.

  5. A student interested in HR has no contacts in the field. Which step is most likely to build relevant contacts? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Joining the CIPD as a student and attending its local events. The professional body for HR offers events and contacts in the field; mass CVs and general posts rarely work.

  6. After a good conversation at a fair, what is the best next step? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Send a short message within a day or two mentioning what you discussed. A prompt, specific follow-up keeps the contact and makes you memorable without being pushy.

  7. Which is the strongest reason networks contribute to unequal access to professional careers? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Opportunities shared informally reach those already connected to professionals. Informal sharing advantages those whose strong and weak ties already include professionals, which access schemes try to counter.

  8. Which approach best supports a shy, first-generation student in networking? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small, structured steps such as one alumni conversation a fortnight with prepared questions. Small, prepared steps build confidence and contacts gradually; large unstructured events can overwhelm, and avoidance loses opportunities.

  9. Which is the strongest evaluation of virtual careers fairs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They widen access, but rapport and informal conversation are often weaker. Virtual fairs remove travel costs and barriers, but many people find relationships harder to build online.

Key takeaways

  • Networking is building reciprocal professional relationships; weak ties (acquaintances) often bring new information and opportunities, which is why networks shape careers and why unequal networks create unequal access.
  • Map the network you already have, identify routes towards your target field, and grow it through academics, professional bodies, societies, work and events with small regular actions and a simple record.
  • Prepare for events with a priority list, research, an introduction and specific questions; engage, note names and details, and follow up promptly, mentioning your conversation in later applications.
  • Effective cold messages are short, specific and personal, with one small request; send a single polite follow-up after about a week, thank people promptly and close the loop by updating them later.
  • Mentors give advice and support, coaches help you find your own answers and sponsors use their influence to advocate for you; find mentors through formal schemes and relationships, and earn sponsorship through visible, reliable work.
  • Alumni networks, through alumni offices, platforms, events and LinkedIn's alumni tool, connect you to graduates who are often willing to help because of a shared identity; approach them for advice and give back later.

Common mistakes in Networking and Your Professional Presence

  • Thinking networking means asking strangers for jobs. It is about relationships, information and mutual help over time; job leads follow from trust.
  • Believing you have no network. Most people have contacts who know people in their field of interest; mapping helps you see them.
  • Collecting freebies and leaflets without talking to anyone. The value of an event is the conversations and contacts, which need preparation and follow-up.
  • Writing long messages asking for a job or a referral from a stranger. A short request for advice is easier to say yes to and starts a relationship.
  • Expecting a mentor to find you a job. Mentors help you think and learn; job opportunities are more likely to come from your own efforts and, later, from sponsors who know your work.
  • Contacting only very senior alumni. Recent graduates a few years ahead often give the most relevant advice on applications and are more likely to reply.

Lessons in this school

  1. What networking is and why it works
    Networking means building and maintaining professional relationships that share information, advice and opportunities.
  2. Building a network from where you are
    Everyone already has a network, even if it doesn't feel professional.
  3. Careers fairs and networking events
    Careers fairs, employer presentations and networking events put you in the same room as recruiters and professionals.
  4. Speculative approaches and follow-up
    Reaching out to someone you don't know, whether for advice, an introduction or an opportunity, is a core networking skill.
  5. Mentors and sponsors
    A mentor gives you advice and support; a sponsor uses their influence to open doors for you.
  6. Alumni networks
    Your university's alumni, its former students, are one of the largest and most willing networks you can join.
  7. Building a LinkedIn profile
    LinkedIn is the most widely used professional networking platform, and recruiters use it to find and check candidates.
  8. Using LinkedIn to research and connect
    LinkedIn is as useful for research as for being found.
  9. Content, engagement and visibility
    Posting and commenting on LinkedIn and similar platforms can make you visible to people in your field.
  10. Managing your digital footprint
    Your digital footprint is everything about you that can be found online.
  11. Professional email communication
    Emails to employers, recruiters and contacts form part of how you are judged.
  12. Job scams and staying safe online
    Fake job adverts and recruitment scams target students and graduates, aiming to steal money or personal data or to recruit "money mules".

What is a network map?

A list of your existing contacts, what they do and who they might know, used to plan networking.

What should a follow-up message after an event include?

A thank-you and a specific reference to what you discussed, sent within a day or two.

What four elements should a cold message include?

Who you are, why you're contacting them specifically, one clear small request, and thanks with flexibility.
