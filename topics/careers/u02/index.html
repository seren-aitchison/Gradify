Key terms and formulas at a glance

Labour market information
Data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills, demand and entry routes used to inform career decisions
Economically inactive
People who are neither employed nor unemployed, such as students not seeking work, carers, long-term sick or retired people
Job profile
A summary of a role's duties, pay, entry requirements, skills, conditions and prospects
Skill-shortage vacancy
A vacancy that is hard to fill because applicants lack the required skills, qualifications or experience
Industry
The kind of activity an organisation carries out, classified in the UK by SIC
Public sector
Organisations owned or funded by the state to provide public services
SME
Small or medium-sized enterprise: in the UK a business with fewer than 250 employees
Graduate Outcomes
HESA's survey of UK graduates about 15 months after they finish their course
Job family
A group of roles with similar work, skills and progression, such as finance or marketing
Commercial awareness
Understanding how an organisation and its sector work, including customers, competitors, pressures and the economy
Informational interview
A short conversation with someone in a role or sector, held to learn about it rather than to ask for a job

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between quantitative and qualitative LMI?

Quantitative LMI is statistical (numbers of jobs, pay, projections); qualitative LMI is descriptive (what a job is like, culture, routes in). From “What labour market information is” →

In plain English: Labour market information (LMI) is data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills and trends. Key idea: LMI combines statistics about jobs, pay and trends with first-hand accounts of work; used critically, it turns guesses about careers into informed decisions.

What does a Prospects "What can I do with my degree?" page show?

Jobs directly related to the subject and jobs where the degree would be useful, with skills and typical employers. From “Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service” →

In plain English: Job profiles summarise what a role involves, what it pays, how to get in and where it can lead. Key idea: Job profiles from Prospects and the National Careers Service describe duties, pay, entry routes and prospects; read them actively, separating essential from typical requirements and checking against current vacancies and people in the job.

Which SOC 2020 major groups are often used as a proxy for graduate-level work?

Major groups 1 to 3: managers, professionals, and associate professional and technical occupations. From “How sectors and industries are organised” →

In plain English: Jobs sit inside industries and occupations, which are classified in official systems. Key idea: Industry is what an organisation does and occupation is what a person does; classifications such as SIC and SOC, and mapping organisations and functions in a sector, reveal many more options than obvious job titles.

When does the Graduate Outcomes survey contact graduates?

About 15 months after they finish their course. From “Graduate destinations: what degrees lead to” →

In plain English: What do graduates actually do after their degrees? Key idea: Graduate Outcomes (15 months), LEO (up to ten years) and DfE statistics show graduates' destinations and earnings; vocational degrees lead mainly into a profession, while non-vocational degrees open many any-discipline routes.

Which employers must publish gender pay gap reports?

Employers with 250 or more employees in Great Britain. From “Researching an employer” →

In plain English: Employers expect candidates to know who they are, what they do and why the candidate wants to join. Key idea: Research what an employer does, how it is performing, its culture, the role and recent news, using its own reports plus independent sources such as Companies House, news and staff, to show genuine motivation and commercial awareness.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Exploring Sectors and Roles

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an example of qualitative labour market information? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A nurse describing a typical shift. A first-hand description of work is qualitative; rates, salaries and projections are statistical, so quantitative.

  2. What is the denominator of the unemployment rate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The economically active population. The unemployment rate is the unemployed as a share of employed plus unemployed people; inactive people are excluded.

  3. Which organisation runs the Prospects careers website? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Jisc. Prospects is run by Jisc, the UK digital and data body for further and higher education; the others publish statistics, set careers standards or collect tax.

  4. A job profile gives a national average salary of £38,000 for an occupation. What should a student in the North East also check? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Regional pay and entry-level salaries for the role. National averages hide differences by region and experience; entry salaries locally are what matter for a new graduate.

  5. A full-time student who is not looking for work is classed by the ONS as what? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Economically inactive. Someone neither working nor seeking work is inactive; to be unemployed they must have looked for work and be available to start.

  6. A profile says "most entrants to this role have a master's degree." What should a graduate with a bachelor's degree do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Check current vacancies to see whether a master's is actually required. "Most entrants" describes the typical route, not a rule; real vacancies show what employers currently require.

  7. Which combination gives the most reliable picture of a career option? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Official data on pay and demand plus first-hand accounts from people in the job. Combining independent statistics with several first-hand accounts balances breadth and depth; the others are single, partial or promotional views.

  8. The unemployment rate falls by 0.1 percentage points in one month. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It may be within sampling error, so look at the trend over several quarters. Small changes in survey estimates can be noise; trends over time, alongside other sources, are more reliable.

  9. Which is the strongest limitation of relying on job profiles alone? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They describe typical entrants and averages, not current vacancies or real day-to-day variety. Profiles are generic and may lag behind the market; current vacancies and people in the role add accuracy and detail.

Key takeaways

  • LMI combines statistics about jobs, pay and trends with first-hand accounts of work; used critically, it turns guesses about careers into informed decisions.
  • The ONS measures employment, ILO unemployment and inactivity mainly through the Labour Force Survey; the unemployment rate is a share of the economically active, and survey figures need careful, trend-based reading.
  • Job profiles from Prospects and the National Careers Service describe duties, pay, entry routes and prospects; read them actively, separating essential from typical requirements and checking against current vacancies and people in the job.
  • Skills shortages are measured through employer surveys, migration advice and pay and vacancy trends; growth and replacement demand both create jobs, but a shortage at experienced level doesn't guarantee easy entry.
  • Industry is what an organisation does and occupation is what a person does; classifications such as SIC and SOC, and mapping organisations and functions in a sector, reveal many more options than obvious job titles.
  • The public sector provides state-funded services, the private sector seeks profit and the third sector pursues social purposes; most occupations exist in all three, so compare pay, recruitment, culture and mission for the specific employer.

Common mistakes in Exploring Sectors and Roles

  • Treating one figure, such as a national average salary or a growth projection, as a promise. LMI describes averages and trends, not what will happen to you.
  • Calculating the employment rate as 100% minus the unemployment rate. The two use different denominators because economically inactive people are in neither group.
  • Ruling yourself out because a profile says "most entrants have" a qualification or experience you lack. Typical isn't always essential; check employers' actual requirements.
  • Assuming "shortage" means "easy to get into". Shortages are often for experienced or specialist staff, or result from pay and conditions rather than a lack of applicants.
  • Thinking you can only work in a sector by doing its most visible job. Every sector employs people in finance, HR, marketing, data, legal and operations roles.
  • Assuming every public sector job is secure and slow, every private one is well paid and every charity job is low paid. Conditions vary more within sectors than between them.

Lessons in this school

  1. What labour market information is
    Labour market information (LMI) is data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills and trends.
  2. Using ONS and official labour market data
    The Office for National Statistics publishes the UK's headline figures on employment, unemployment, vacancies and pay.
  3. Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service
    Job profiles summarise what a role involves, what it pays, how to get in and where it can lead.
  4. Skills shortages and growth areas
    Some occupations can't find enough skilled people while others shrink.
  5. How sectors and industries are organised
    Jobs sit inside industries and occupations, which are classified in official systems.
  6. Public, private and third sector employers
    Employers fall into three broad sectors with different purposes, funding and cultures.
  7. Large employers, SMEs and start-ups
    Most UK businesses are small, yet graduate recruitment advertising is dominated by large employers.
  8. Graduate destinations: what degrees lead to
    What do graduates actually do after their degrees?
  9. Job titles, job families and role descriptions
    Job titles are inconsistent: the same title can mean very different jobs and different titles can mean the same one.
  10. Researching an employer
    Employers expect candidates to know who they are, what they do and why the candidate wants to join.
  11. Informational interviews
    An informational interview is a short conversation with someone in a job or sector you're curious about, to learn rather than to ask for a job.
  12. Weighing options and making a career decision
    Career decisions involve uncertainty, trade-offs and emotion.

Exploring Sectors and Roles: questions students ask

What is the ILO definition of unemployment?

Without a job, has looked for work in the past four weeks and is available to start within the next two weeks.

What is the difference between a skill-shortage vacancy and a hard-to-fill vacancy?

A skill-shortage vacancy is hard to fill because applicants lack skills or experience; a hard-to-fill vacancy may be hard to fill for any reason, such as low pay.

What is the difference between an industry and an occupation?

An industry is what an organisation does; an occupation is what a person does.

What distinguishes the third sector?

Organisations such as charities and social enterprises that pursue social or environmental purposes and reinvest any surplus rather than distributing profit.
