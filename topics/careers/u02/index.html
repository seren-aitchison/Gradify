Five sample flashcards

What is the difference between quantitative and qualitative LMI?

Quantitative LMI is statistical (numbers of jobs, pay, projections); qualitative LMI is descriptive (what a job is like, culture, routes in). From “What labour market information is” →

What does a Prospects "What can I do with my degree?" page show?

Jobs directly related to the subject and jobs where the degree would be useful, with skills and typical employers. From “Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service” →

Which SOC 2020 major groups are often used as a proxy for graduate-level work?

Major groups 1 to 3: managers, professionals, and associate professional and technical occupations. From “How sectors and industries are organised” →

When does the Graduate Outcomes survey contact graduates?

About 15 months after they finish their course. From “Graduate destinations: what degrees lead to” →

Which employers must publish gender pay gap reports?

Employers with 250 or more employees in Great Britain. From “Researching an employer” →

Lessons in this school

  1. What labour market information is
    Labour market information (LMI) is data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills and trends.
  2. Using ONS and official labour market data
    The Office for National Statistics publishes the UK's headline figures on employment, unemployment, vacancies and pay.
  3. Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service
    Job profiles summarise what a role involves, what it pays, how to get in and where it can lead.
  4. Skills shortages and growth areas
    Some occupations can't find enough skilled people while others shrink.
  5. How sectors and industries are organised
    Jobs sit inside industries and occupations, which are classified in official systems.
  6. Public, private and third sector employers
    Employers fall into three broad sectors with different purposes, funding and cultures.
  7. Large employers, SMEs and start-ups
    Most UK businesses are small, yet graduate recruitment advertising is dominated by large employers.
  8. Graduate destinations: what degrees lead to
    What do graduates actually do after their degrees?
  9. Job titles, job families and role descriptions
    Job titles are inconsistent: the same title can mean very different jobs and different titles can mean the same one.
  10. Researching an employer
    Employers expect candidates to know who they are, what they do and why the candidate wants to join.
  11. Informational interviews
    An informational interview is a short conversation with someone in a job or sector you're curious about, to learn rather than to ask for a job.
  12. Weighing options and making a career decision
    Career decisions involve uncertainty, trade-offs and emotion.

Key terms

Labour market information
Data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills, demand and entry routes used to inform career decisions
Economically inactive
People who are neither employed nor unemployed, such as students not seeking work, carers, long-term sick or retired people
Job profile
A summary of a role's duties, pay, entry requirements, skills, conditions and prospects
Skill-shortage vacancy
A vacancy that is hard to fill because applicants lack the required skills, qualifications or experience
Industry
The kind of activity an organisation carries out, classified in the UK by SIC
Public sector
Organisations owned or funded by the state to provide public services
SME
Small or medium-sized enterprise: in the UK a business with fewer than 250 employees
Graduate Outcomes
HESA's survey of UK graduates about 15 months after they finish their course
Job family
A group of roles with similar work, skills and progression, such as finance or marketing
Commercial awareness
Understanding how an organisation and its sector work, including customers, competitors, pressures and the economy
Informational interview
A short conversation with someone in a role or sector, held to learn about it rather than to ask for a job
Weighted decision matrix
A table scoring options against criteria multiplied by each criterion's importance
