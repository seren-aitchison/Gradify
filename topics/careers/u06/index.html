Key terms and formulas at a glance

Application form
A standardised set of questions every candidate answers, allowing consistent comparison and scoring
Competency
An observable behaviour or skill linked to effective performance in a role
STAR
A structure for describing an example: Situation, Task, Action, Result
Strengths-based question
A question exploring what a candidate enjoys, does naturally and is energised by
Motivational question
A question assessing why a candidate wants the role, sector and organisation
Supporting statement
A section of an application explaining, with evidence, how the candidate meets each criterion of the person specification
Success Profiles
The UK Civil Service recruitment framework assessing behaviours, strengths, ability, experience and technical skills
Norm group
The comparison group against which an individual's test score is interpreted
Percentage change
The change between two values divided by the original value, times 100
Cannot Say
The answer when a passage doesn't give enough information to decide whether a statement is true or false
Inductive reasoning
Inferring general rules from specific examples, such as a sequence of shapes
Situational judgement test
An assessment presenting realistic work scenarios and asking candidates to judge the effectiveness of responses

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why do employers use application forms instead of CVs?

Every candidate answers the same questions, making comparison and scoring fairer and easier. From “How application forms work” →

In plain English: Most large graduate employers, the public sector and the NHS use online application forms rather than CVs. Key idea: Online application forms standardise information so answers can be scored fairly; expect screening questions, history, competency and motivation questions and tests, apply early when recruitment is rolling, and manage applications with drafts and a tracker.

Give two examples of strengths-based questions.

Any two such as: What do you enjoy doing most? When are you at your best? What do friends come to you for? Do you prefer starting or finishing tasks? From “Strengths-based questions” →

In plain English: Strengths-based questions explore what you enjoy and do naturally, rather than what you've done. Key idea: Strengths-based questions explore what energises you and what you do naturally; answer honestly, with energy and a brief example, having researched the strengths the role needs.

What are the five elements of Success Profiles?

Behaviours, Strengths, Ability, Experience and Technical. From “Civil Service Success Profiles” →

In plain English: The UK Civil Service assesses candidates using Success Profiles, a framework of five elements including nine behaviours. Key idea: Success Profiles assess up to five elements (behaviours, strengths, ability, experience, technical); nine behaviours are described by grade; write 250-word STAR examples showing the behaviour as described for your grade.

When should you answer Cannot Say?

When the passage doesn't give enough information to decide whether the statement is true or false. From “Verbal reasoning tests” →

In plain English: Verbal reasoning tests check how accurately you understand and draw conclusions from written passages. Key idea: Verbal reasoning tests ask whether statements are True, False or Cannot Say based only on a passage; read precisely, ignore outside knowledge and watch qualifiers, causation and terms like sales versus profit.

Why shouldn't personality questionnaires be used alone to reject candidates?

They are self-report, predict performance only modestly and are best used alongside other evidence, as professional guidance advises. From “Personality questionnaires” →

In plain English: Many employers use personality questionnaires to understand candidates' preferred ways of working. Key idea: Personality questionnaires describe preferred ways of working, usually related to the Big Five, and are used to judge role fit and shape interviews; answer honestly and consistently, since faking is detectable and can lead to a poor fit.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Applications and Psychometric Tests

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What usually happens to equal opportunities monitoring information on an application form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It is kept separate from assessment and used for monitoring. Monitoring data is separated from selection and used to check that processes are fair.

  2. How do competency-based questions typically begin? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "Tell me about a time when...". Competency questions ask for real past examples; "What would you do if" is a situational question.

  3. In STAR, what does the T stand for? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Task. T is Task: your responsibility or the challenge you faced in the situation.

  4. An employer recruits on a rolling basis with a deadline of 30 November. When should a student apply? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    As early as possible once she has prepared a strong application. With rolling recruitment, places fill as applications arrive, so a strong early application is best.

  5. Which answer opening fits a competency question about meeting a tight deadline? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    In my final year, I had two reports due on the same day after a group member dropped out.... Competency answers describe a specific past situation; the others are claims, hypotheticals or general statements.

  6. A 250-word STAR answer spends 150 words describing the situation. What is the best improvement? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cut the situation to about 30 words and expand the actions and reasoning. Assessors score actions, so situation should be brief and actions detailed.

  7. Which is the strongest reason employers prefer forms for large graduate schemes? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Consistent questions allow fair, criteria-based scoring across many applicants. Standardised answers can be scored consistently against criteria, which improves fairness and efficiency.

  8. Which is the strongest criticism of competency-based selection? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can favour candidates with richer experience or coaching, not just ability. Competency questions predict performance reasonably well, but candidates with more opportunities and coaching can produce stronger examples.

  9. Which result statement is strongest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Sales rose 15% over the next term and the approach was adopted by two other stores. A measurable, specific result with wider impact is most convincing; the others are vague or uncertain.

Key takeaways

  • Online application forms standardise information so answers can be scored fairly; expect screening questions, history, competency and motivation questions and tests, apply early when recruitment is rolling, and manage applications with drafts and a tracker.
  • Competencies are observable behaviours linked to performance; competency questions ask for real past examples, which assessors score against positive and negative behavioural indicators.
  • STAR structures an answer as Situation, Task, Action and Result, with most words on your own actions and reasoning and a clear, preferably measurable result; adding Reflection shows learning.
  • Strengths-based questions explore what energises you and what you do naturally; answer honestly, with energy and a brief example, having researched the strengths the role needs.
  • Motivational answers should explain why the sector, why the role and why the organisation, with specific, first-hand evidence and a link to what you'd contribute.
  • Supporting statements are scored against the person specification, so address each essential criterion explicitly with specific evidence, in the same order or as headings; personal statements for study focus on academic interest and suitability.

Common mistakes in Applications and Psychometric Tests

  • Leaving applications until the deadline. With rolling recruitment, places may already be filled; and rushed answers are easy to spot.
  • Answering with what you would do or usually do. Competency questions ask for one specific past situation; hypothetical answers score poorly.
  • Writing a long story about the situation and squeezing the actions into one line. Assessors mark the actions, so give them most of your words.
  • Trying to guess and fake the "right" strengths. Assessors watch for energy and consistency, and a role that doesn't fit your strengths is unlikely to suit you anyway.
  • Writing an answer that could be sent to any employer. If it doesn't mention specific features of this organisation and role, it won't convince.
  • Writing a general essay about yourself instead of addressing the criteria. Shortlisters can only award marks for criteria you evidence.

Lessons in this school

  1. How application forms work
    Most large graduate employers, the public sector and the NHS use online application forms rather than CVs.
  2. Competency-based questions
    Competency-based questions ask for a specific example of when you showed a skill or behaviour.
  3. The STAR method
    STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard way to structure answers to competency questions.
  4. Strengths-based questions
    Strengths-based questions explore what you enjoy and do naturally, rather than what you've done.
  5. Motivational questions
    "Why do you want this job?" and "Why us?" appear on almost every application.
  6. Supporting and personal statements
    Many public sector, NHS, education and charity applications ask for a supporting statement that shows how you meet the person specification.
  7. Civil Service Success Profiles
    The UK Civil Service assesses candidates using Success Profiles, a framework of five elements including nine behaviours.
  8. What psychometric tests measure
    Psychometric tests are standardised assessments of ability, aptitude or personality used in selection.
  9. Numerical reasoning tests
    Numerical reasoning tests check how well you interpret data in tables and charts and calculate under time pressure.
  10. Verbal reasoning tests
    Verbal reasoning tests check how accurately you understand and draw conclusions from written passages.
  11. Logical and inductive reasoning tests
    Logical, inductive and diagrammatic reasoning tests assess how you spot patterns and rules in abstract shapes or sequences.
  12. Situational judgement tests
    Situational judgement tests (SJTs) present realistic work scenarios and ask how you would or should respond.
  13. Personality questionnaires
    Many employers use personality questionnaires to understand candidates' preferred ways of working.
  14. Game-based assessments
    Some employers now replace traditional tests with short online games that measure cognitive abilities and behavioural traits.
  15. Reasonable adjustments in assessment
    Disabled candidates have a legal right to reasonable adjustments in recruitment, including tests and interviews.

Applications and Psychometric Tests: questions students ask

What principle underlies competency-based questions?

Past behaviour predicts future behaviour.

Which part of a STAR answer should usually be longest?

Action, around half or more of the answer.

Why do employers use strengths-based questions?

To find people who'll thrive in the role, reduce rehearsed answers and the advantage of coaching or experience, and widen access.

What three levels should a motivational answer cover?

Why the sector, why the role, and why the organisation.
