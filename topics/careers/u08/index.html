Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

When does the Graduate Outcomes survey contact graduates?

About 15 months after they finish their course. From “The graduate recruitment market” →

In plain English: The graduate recruitment market is the set of employers, jobs and routes open to people finishing degrees. Key idea: Most UK graduates work outside large graduate schemes, and sources such as Graduate Outcomes, DfE statistics, LEO and the ISE survey describe the market, but you must check who each covers before comparing figures.

When are direct-entry graduate jobs usually advertised?

Year-round, as vacancies arise. From “Graduate jobs outside schemes” →

In plain English: Most graduates start in direct-entry jobs rather than formal schemes. Key idea: Most graduates start in direct-entry jobs advertised year-round by SMEs, charities, the public sector and large employers, so use job boards, agencies, employer sites and networking, and ask about training and progression.

When must an employer provide a written statement of employment particulars?

On or before the first day of work. From “Offers, negotiation and accepting a job” →

In plain English: Receiving an offer is the start of a new set of decisions. Key idea: Check whether an offer is conditional, review all its terms, negotiate politely with evidence where flexibility exists, accept in writing only when sure, and decline promptly rather than reneging.

What is a conversion course?

A postgraduate course that lets you move into a field unrelated to your first degree. From “Postgraduate study as a career step” →

In plain English: Postgraduate study can open doors, but it is a significant investment of time and money. Key idea: Postgraduate study includes taught master's, conversion courses, professional qualifications and research degrees, funded by loans, studentships and scholarships, and it pays off most when your target career requires or clearly values it.

What is voluntourism?

Short-term paid volunteering trips abroad that may benefit visitors more than local communities. From “Gap years and career breaks” →

In plain English: A planned gap year or career break can build skills, confidence and clarity. Key idea: Gap years and career breaks are valued when they are planned with goals, a budget and responsible activities, and explained afterwards as deliberate choices with skills and outcomes linked to the role.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which source surveys graduates 15 months after they finish their course? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Graduate Outcomes. HESA's Graduate Outcomes survey asks graduates about their activities around 15 months after finishing.

  2. What is a rotation on a graduate scheme? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A set period in one team before moving to another. Rotations give graduates experience of several areas within the same employer.

  3. What is a direct-entry job? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A specific vacancy filled when the employer needs someone. Direct-entry roles fill specific needs as they arise, rather than as part of an annual cohort.

  4. Why might a salary figure from a survey of large employers be higher than one from Graduate Outcomes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It covers only large recruiters rather than all graduate jobs. Large employer surveys cover a well-paid segment, while Graduate Outcomes covers graduates in all jobs.

  5. Which is a common drawback of graduate schemes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Less control over location and placements. Schemes often place graduates where the business needs them, which can limit choice of location.

  6. Which is a common advantage of direct-entry roles over graduate schemes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    You can often start sooner and choose the location. Direct-entry jobs often start within weeks and are tied to a particular place.

  7. A news story says "graduates earn more, so a degree always pays off." What is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Averages hide large variation, and causation is debated. Average gains are real, but they vary widely and may partly reflect prior characteristics.

  8. A student wants flexibility to stay in her home city and start work immediately after graduating. What should she consider? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Direct-entry jobs at local employers as well as schemes. Direct-entry roles are advertised year-round and are often local, suiting her priorities.

  9. A direct-entry role sounds interesting but the advert says little about training. What should the candidate do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ask at interview how new starters are trained and supported. Asking shows interest and helps you judge how you will develop, since training is often less formal.

Key takeaways

  • Most UK graduates work outside large graduate schemes, and sources such as Graduate Outcomes, DfE statistics, LEO and the ISE survey describe the market, but you must check who each covers before comparing figures.
  • Graduate schemes are structured one to three year programmes with a cohort, rotations, training and sometimes professional qualifications, offering strong support but strong competition, less flexibility and early deadlines.
  • Most graduates start in direct-entry jobs advertised year-round by SMEs, charities, the public sector and large employers, so use job boards, agencies, employer sites and networking, and ask about training and progression.
  • Large UK employers recruit about a year ahead, with insight weeks, internships, placements and graduate schemes mostly opening in the autumn, so plan in the summer, prepare early and apply early because of rolling recruitment.
  • Check whether an offer is conditional, review all its terms, negotiate politely with evidence where flexibility exists, accept in writing only when sure, and decline promptly rather than reneging.
  • Higher (levels 4 to 5) and degree (levels 6 to 7) apprenticeships are paid jobs with funded study and no tuition fees, offering experience and qualifications together, but they are competitive and demand good time management.

Common mistakes in Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes

  • Assuming graduate scheme salaries are typical. Large employer surveys cover a small, well-paid part of the market.
  • Assuming a scheme is the only good start. Many successful careers begin with direct-entry jobs at smaller employers.
  • Waiting for schemes to reopen. Direct-entry roles are advertised all year, so you can apply whenever you are ready.
  • Waiting until final-year spring to look for graduate schemes. Many open in September and fill well before their deadlines.
  • Accepting several offers to keep options open. Accepting usually forms a contract; ask for more time instead, and decline promptly when you decide.
  • Thinking apprenticeships are only for school leavers or manual trades. Degree apprenticeships cover professions such as law, nursing, engineering and software.

Lessons in this school

  1. The graduate recruitment market
    The graduate recruitment market is the set of employers, jobs and routes open to people finishing degrees.
  2. Graduate schemes
    Graduate schemes are structured training programmes for new graduates, mostly at large employers.
  3. Graduate jobs outside schemes
    Most graduates start in direct-entry jobs rather than formal schemes.
  4. The recruitment calendar
    Many UK opportunities follow an annual cycle, with deadlines months before the start date.
  5. Offers, negotiation and accepting a job
    Receiving an offer is the start of a new set of decisions.
  6. Degree and higher apprenticeships
    Degree and higher apprenticeships combine paid work with study for a higher-level qualification.
  7. School leaver programmes
    School leaver programmes offer paid training with large employers straight after A levels or equivalent.
  8. Postgraduate study as a career step
    Postgraduate study can open doors, but it is a significant investment of time and money.
  9. Working abroad and visas
    Working abroad can build skills and broaden your career, but it depends on having the right permission to work.
  10. Gap years and career breaks
    A planned gap year or career break can build skills, confidence and clarity.
  11. Creative and portfolio careers
    Many people in the creative industries and beyond build careers from freelance work, projects and several part-time roles.
  12. Public service and fast-track routes
    The public and third sectors offer structured fast-track programmes for graduates who want to make a difference.

Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes: questions students ask

When do most large employers' graduate schemes open?

September to November of the final year.

Do degree apprentices pay tuition fees?

No; training is paid by the employer and government.

At what stage do most school leaver programmes recruit?

In the autumn of the final year of school or college, for example Year 13.

Where should you check visa rules for working in another country?

The destination country's official government immigration website.
