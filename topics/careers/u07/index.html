Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a semi-structured interview?

An interview with set core questions plus follow-up probes chosen by the interviewer. From “Interview formats” →

In plain English: Interviews range from short phone screens to panel interviews and multiple mini interviews. Key idea: UK employers use screening, one-to-one, panel, video and specialist interviews, and universities may use MMIs; structured interviews, with the same questions and scoring for everyone, are more predictive and fairer than unstructured ones.

Where should you look during a video interview to create eye contact?

At the camera. From “Telephone and live video interviews” →

In plain English: Many first-round and some final interviews now happen by phone or video call. Key idea: Remote interviews are as formal as face-to-face ones, so test your technology, set up a quiet and well-lit space, look at the camera, speak clearly with pauses and recover calmly from technical problems.

What should you do first when given a technical problem?

Repeat it and ask clarifying questions. From “Technical interviews” →

In plain English: Technical interviews test the specialist knowledge and problem-solving a role needs, from coding to engineering principles and finance. Key idea: Technical interviews test role-specific knowledge and problem-solving, so revise core concepts, know your CV projects in depth and think aloud by clarifying, explaining your approach, starting simple and testing your answer.

What is a wash-up meeting?

The meeting where assessors combine scores and agree decisions. From “What happens at an assessment centre” →

In plain English: An assessment centre brings several selection exercises together over half a day or more, in person or online. Key idea: Assessment centres combine exercises such as group tasks, presentations, interviews, in-trays and role-plays, each scored by trained assessors against set competencies, so you are measured against criteria rather than other candidates.

What should you do before acting on any item in an e-tray exercise?

Skim all items to spot links and clashes. From “In-tray and e-tray exercises” →

In plain English: In-tray and e-tray exercises simulate a busy inbox of emails, documents and requests. Key idea: In-tray and e-tray exercises test prioritising and decision making, so skim everything first, rank items by urgency and importance, delegate appropriately, treat safety and legal issues as top priority and justify each decision briefly.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Interviews and Assessment Centres

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which interview format asks every candidate the same questions and scores them against set criteria? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Structured interview. Structured interviews use standard questions and scoring, which improves fairness and prediction.

  2. What is the most useful starting point for predicting interview questions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The person specification for the role. Each criterion in the person specification is likely to be assessed with a question.

  3. What is a probe in an interview? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A follow-up question asking for more detail. Probes ask for clarification or extra detail, often about your personal role.

  4. Why do employers often start with a phone or video screening interview? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It filters large numbers of applicants quickly and cheaply. Screening formats are efficient early filters before more costly, in-depth stages.

  5. Why should you practise answers aloud rather than only in your head? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It shows up rambling and gaps that silent rehearsal hides. Speaking answers reveals timing, clarity and structure problems that you can then fix.

  6. An interviewer asks, "What exactly did you do?" What are they testing? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Your personal contribution separate from the team's. This probe separates your own actions from collective ones so that they can be credited.

  7. A firm's interviews are free-flowing chats where each manager asks whatever they like. What is the main weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Candidates are compared unfairly, with weaker prediction of performance. Unstructured interviews are less consistent, more open to bias and predict performance less well.

  8. A candidate has one excellent example and plans to use it for every question. What is the risk? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Interviewers see little range and later answers seem weak. Using varied examples shows breadth; repeating one story suggests limited experience.

  9. In a fast strengths interview, a candidate gives a three-minute STAR answer to every question. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Give shorter, natural answers that show genuine energy. Strengths interviews reward brief, authentic answers; long rehearsed ones can lose energy.

Key takeaways

  • UK employers use screening, one-to-one, panel, video and specialist interviews, and universities may use MMIs; structured interviews, with the same questions and scoring for everyone, are more predictive and fairer than unstructured ones.
  • Prepare by analysing the person specification and your own application, researching the employer and sector, preparing STAR examples, practising aloud and sorting out logistics in advance.
  • In interviews, signpost a concise STAR structure aloud, spend most time on your actions and reasoning, welcome probes as chances to add evidence, and answer strengths questions briefly and authentically.
  • Remote interviews are as formal as face-to-face ones, so test your technology, set up a quiet and well-lit space, look at the camera, speak clearly with pauses and recover calmly from technical problems.
  • Pre-recorded interviews give short reading times and fixed answer times, so practise with the test question, look at the camera, plan with key words and finish firmly, while being aware of debates about automated scoring.
  • Case interviews test structured thinking on business problems, so clarify the question, build a logical structure, analyse with stated assumptions and clear arithmetic, and finish with a recommendation.

Common mistakes in Interviews and Assessment Centres

  • Assuming every interview is an informal chat. Most graduate interviews are structured and scored, so prepare evidence for each criterion.
  • Preparing answers but never saying them aloud. Practising out loud reveals rambling and gaps that silent rehearsal misses.
  • Treating a follow-up question as a sign of failure. Probes are standard in structured interviews and help you add evidence.
  • Treating a phone or video interview as less important. Remote interviews are usually scored just like in-person ones.
  • Looking at your own face on screen. Look at the camera lens so you appear to make eye contact.
  • Jumping straight to solutions. Clarify the objective and set out a structure first, so your analysis stays focused.

Lessons in this school

  1. Interview formats
    Interviews range from short phone screens to panel interviews and multiple mini interviews.
  2. Preparing for an interview
    Good interview performance rests on preparation.
  3. Answering competency and strengths questions
    In interviews, competency and strengths questions are spoken, probed and followed up, which changes how you answer them.
  4. Telephone and live video interviews
    Many first-round and some final interviews now happen by phone or video call.
  5. Pre-recorded video interviews
    Pre-recorded or asynchronous video interviews ask you to record answers to on-screen questions with no live interviewer.
  6. Case interviews
    Case interviews ask you to work through a business problem out loud with the interviewer.
  7. Technical interviews
    Technical interviews test the specialist knowledge and problem-solving a role needs, from coding to engineering principles and finance.
  8. Questions to ask the interviewer
    Most interviews end with "Do you have any questions for us?" This lesson explains why the question matters, what makes a good question and which questions to avoid.
  9. Handling nerves and difficult questions
    Some nervousness before interviews is normal and can even help.
  10. What happens at an assessment centre
    An assessment centre brings several selection exercises together over half a day or more, in person or online.
  11. Group exercises
    Group exercises ask several candidates to discuss a problem or complete a task together while assessors watch.
  12. Presentations
    Many assessment centres and interviews include a short presentation, either prepared in advance or on the day.
  13. In-tray and e-tray exercises
    In-tray and e-tray exercises simulate a busy inbox of emails, documents and requests.
  14. Role-plays
    Role-play exercises ask you to handle a realistic work conversation with an actor or assessor, such as a customer complaint or a difficult colleague.
  15. Feedback and learning from rejection
    Rejection is a normal part of applying for jobs and courses, even for strong candidates.

Interviews and Assessment Centres: questions students ask

Which document best predicts likely interview questions?

The person specification, as each criterion is likely to be tested.

What is signposting in an interview answer?

Briefly signalling the structure, such as "My role was..." and "The result was...".

Why pause slightly at the end of answers on video?

To allow for delay and avoid talking over the interviewer.

What formula underpins profitability cases?

Profit equals revenue minus costs.
