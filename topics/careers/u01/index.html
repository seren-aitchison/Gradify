What is employability?

The achievements, skills, understandings and attributes that make a person more likely to gain work and succeed in it; it is not the same as being employed. From “What career development is” →

In plain English: Career development is the lifelong process of managing learning, work and life. Key idea: A career is the lifelong pattern of learning, work and life roles, and career development is the skill of managing it; employability makes getting and keeping work more likely but doesn't guarantee it.

Which Gatsby Benchmark covers one-to-one guidance?

Benchmark 8, personal guidance from a careers adviser trained to an appropriate level. From “The Gatsby Benchmarks and careers support” →

In plain English: The Gatsby Benchmarks define good careers guidance in English schools and colleges, and universities and the National Careers Service extend that support into adulthood. Key idea: The eight Gatsby Benchmarks define good careers guidance in English schools and colleges, and the same ingredients (information, encounters, experience and personal guidance) make up good support at university and beyond.

Name Tomlinson's five graduate capitals.

Human, social, cultural, identity and psychological capital. From “Transferable skills and graduate attributes” →

In plain English: Universities describe the qualities they aim to develop in every student as graduate attributes, and employers publish the skills they look for. Key idea: Universities and employers list overlapping skills; models like CareerEDGE and graduate capitals show that employability also depends on experience, networks, identity, reflection and confidence.

Which Big Five trait most consistently predicts job performance?

Conscientiousness. From “Interests and personality tools: uses and limits” →

In plain English: Career quizzes, interest inventories and personality questionnaires are everywhere. Key idea: Interest inventories and personality questionnaires can generate options to explore, but none can tell you which career to choose; prefer validated measures such as Big Five tools, and test suggestions against experience.

In Herzberg's theory, what is the difference between hygiene factors and motivators?

Hygiene factors (pay, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself) create satisfaction. From “Motivations and work-life priorities” →

In plain English: Why you want to work shapes which jobs will satisfy you. Key idea: Lasting job satisfaction comes mostly from intrinsic motivators such as autonomy, competence and relatedness; pay and conditions mainly prevent dissatisfaction, and life outside work belongs in every career decision.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Knowing Yourself

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the best description of a career as careers professionals use the word? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The lifelong pattern of a person's learning, work and life roles. Careers professionals use "career" broadly: education, work, caring and volunteering all form part of it, not just paid graduate roles or promotions.

  2. Who set out the original three-step matching model of vocational guidance in 1909? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Frank Parsons. Parsons's Choosing a Vocation (1909) proposed knowing yourself, knowing the world of work and reasoning between them; Super, Krumboltz and Savickas came later.

  3. How many Gatsby Benchmarks of good career guidance are there? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Eight. Holman's report set out eight benchmarks, from a stable careers programme to personal guidance, and the 2024 review kept all eight.

  4. Sam has a strong degree, internships and good references but can't find a job during a recession. What does this show about employability? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Being employable makes work more likely but does not guarantee it. Employability describes a person's assets; the labour market also matters, so an employable person can be unemployed when demand for workers is weak.

  5. Aisha, 45, leaves accountancy to retrain as a teacher and spends a year trying classroom work. Which idea best describes this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Recycling through exploration in Super's life-span theory. Super argued people can re-enter exploration at any age when they change direction; Aisha is testing a new self-concept.

  6. A school arranges for every Year 10 pupil to spend a week in a workplace. Which benchmark does this mainly meet? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Benchmark 6, experiences of workplaces. A placement in a workplace is an experience of a workplace; Benchmark 5 covers shorter encounters such as talks and mentoring.

  7. Which is the strongest reason the CDI framework treats career development as lifelong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    People change roles and sectors and return to learning, so they must keep managing transitions. Non-linear careers with repeated transitions are the norm, so the ability to manage change is needed throughout life, not just at one decision point.

  8. Which is the strongest criticism of relying only on matching theories? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They assume stable people and jobs, but interests develop and jobs change. Matching does have some evidence and does use interests; its weakness is a static view of people and work in a labour market where both change.

  9. Which most directly addresses the risk that students without family contacts in professional work miss out? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Benchmark 5's employer encounters for every pupil. Guaranteed encounters with employers give every pupil contacts and role models that some get at home, which narrows a gap that information alone does not.

Key takeaways

  • A career is the lifelong pattern of learning, work and life roles, and career development is the skill of managing it; employability makes getting and keeping work more likely but doesn't guarantee it.
  • Matching theories focus on fit between person and job, developmental theories on change across the lifespan, and chance and construction theories on adapting to unplanned events and authoring your own story.
  • The eight Gatsby Benchmarks define good careers guidance in English schools and colleges, and the same ingredients (information, encounters, experience and personal guidance) make up good support at university and beyond.
  • Skills come from every part of life; identify them by analysing what you actually did, then evidence them with actions, numbers and outcomes.
  • Universities and employers list overlapping skills; models like CareerEDGE and graduate capitals show that employability also depends on experience, networks, identity, reflection and confidence.
  • Values are what you need from work; identifying and ranking them lets you compare options and explains why a job that fits your skills may still feel wrong.

Common mistakes in Knowing Yourself

  • Thinking career development means choosing the "right" job once. Most people's careers involve many decisions and transitions, so the skills of managing a career matter more than any single choice.
  • Treating a matching quiz result as the answer. Matching theories assume stable people and stable jobs; real careers also involve development, chance and changing labour markets.
  • Assuming careers guidance just means one meeting with an adviser. Personal guidance is only one benchmark; the others are about programmes, information, encounters and experiences over time.
  • Listing skills as bare adjectives ("excellent communicator, team player"). Without evidence a recruiter can't tell claim from reality; every skill needs an example.
  • Treating employability as a checklist of skills you either have or lack. Models show it also depends on networks, confidence, reflection and the labour market.
  • Assuming values are fixed for life. Priorities often shift with experience and circumstances, such as caring responsibilities or debt, so revisit them at each decision.

Knowing Yourself: questions students ask

What are Holland's six RIASEC types?

Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising and Conventional.

What is the difference between a skill and an attribute?

A skill is something you can do and develop with practice, such as negotiating; an attribute is a personal quality, such as resilience or integrity.

What are the five elements of CareerEDGE?

Career development learning, Experience, Degree subject knowledge and skills, Generic skills, and Emotional intelligence.

How do values differ from interests?

Interests are activities you enjoy; values are what you need the work situation to provide, such as security or autonomy.
