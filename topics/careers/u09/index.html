Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name two rights that both workers and employees have.

The National Minimum Wage and paid holiday; also rest breaks and protection from discrimination. From “Employment status: employee, worker and self-employed” →

In plain English: Your employment rights in the UK depend on your employment status. Key idea: UK law recognises employees, workers and the self-employed, each with different rights, and status depends on how the work really operates, such as personal service, control and mutual obligation, not just the contract's label.

Which organisation enforces the National Minimum Wage?

HMRC. From “Pay, the minimum wage and payslips” →

In plain English: Knowing how your pay is set and recorded helps you spot mistakes and protect your rights. Key idea: Workers must be paid at least the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage for their age, and payslips show gross pay, deductions such as tax, National Insurance, pension and student loan, and net pay, so check them every period.

How long a rest break are adult workers entitled to if they work more than six hours?

20 minutes. From “Working hours and holiday” →

In plain English: The Working Time Regulations 1998 set limits on working hours and give workers rights to rest breaks and paid holiday. Key idea: Adult workers are limited to an average 48-hour week unless they opt out, have rights to daily, weekly and in-work rest breaks, and are entitled to 5.6 weeks of paid holiday, which is 28 days for a five-day week and pro rata for part-time work.

What must you usually do before making an employment tribunal claim?

Contact Acas for early conciliation. From “Equality and discrimination at work” →

In plain English: The Equality Act 2010 protects workers from discrimination, harassment and victimisation. Key idea: The Equality Act 2010 protects nine characteristics against direct and indirect discrimination, harassment and victimisation at work, so keep records, raise concerns through informal or formal routes and seek advice promptly from HR, a union or Acas.

Who can accompany an employee to a grievance meeting?

A colleague or a trade union representative. From “Raising a grievance” →

In plain English: A grievance is a concern or complaint raised with your employer. Key idea: Try to resolve concerns informally first, then raise a written, factual formal grievance, which under the Acas Code should lead to a meeting, a right to be accompanied, a written decision and a right of appeal.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Starting Work: Contracts and Rights

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which status gives the widest set of employment rights? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Employee. Employees have extra rights such as unfair dismissal protection and redundancy pay on top of worker rights.

  2. Which law requires the written statement of employment particulars? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Employment Rights Act 1996. Section 1 of the Employment Rights Act 1996, as amended, sets out the written statement requirement.

  3. What is net pay? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pay received after all deductions. Net or take-home pay is what remains after tax, National Insurance and other deductions.

  4. Which right do workers have but self-employed people do not? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The National Minimum Wage. Workers are entitled to the minimum wage and paid holiday, unlike the genuinely self-employed.

  5. Which item must appear in a written statement of particulars? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pay and how often it is paid. Pay, hours, holiday, notice and other key terms must be stated in writing.

  6. What does a tax code on a payslip tell your employer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How much tax-free pay to allow you. The tax code sets your tax-free allowance, so a wrong code can mean paying the wrong tax.

  7. A contract calls Asha "self-employed", but she must work set shifts, cannot send anyone else and uses the firm's equipment. What is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She may legally be a worker or employee despite the label. Status depends on the reality, and personal service, control and the firm's equipment point away from self-employment.

  8. A new starter's statement omits a bonus promised at interview. What should she do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ask politely for written confirmation of how the bonus works. Asking for clarification in writing protects her, since verbal terms can form part of a contract.

  9. A worker notices his payslip shows fewer hours than he worked. What should he do first? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Raise it promptly with his manager or payroll, keeping a record. Raising errors promptly and keeping records is the first step; Acas or HMRC can help if unresolved.

Key takeaways

  • UK law recognises employees, workers and the self-employed, each with different rights, and status depends on how the work really operates, such as personal service, control and mutual obligation, not just the contract's label.
  • Employers must give employees and workers a written statement of the main terms, such as pay, hours, holiday, notice and probation, on or before day one, so check it carefully against what you were promised and ask about anything unclear.
  • Workers must be paid at least the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage for their age, and payslips show gross pay, deductions such as tax, National Insurance, pension and student loan, and net pay, so check them every period.
  • Income Tax and National Insurance are deducted through PAYE above the £12,570 allowance, at 20% and 8% for basic rate earnings outside Scotland, and student loans take 9% of earnings above the plan threshold, such as £25,000 for Plan 5.
  • Employers must auto-enrol eligible workers aged 22 or over earning over £10,000 into a workplace pension with at least 8% total contributions, including at least 3% from the employer, and opting out means losing employer contributions and years of growth.
  • Adult workers are limited to an average 48-hour week unless they opt out, have rights to daily, weekly and in-work rest breaks, and are entitled to 5.6 weeks of paid holiday, which is 28 days for a five-day week and pro rata for part-time work.

Common mistakes in Starting Work: Contracts and Rights

  • Assuming the contract's label settles your status. Tribunals look at the reality of the working relationship.
  • Thinking the written statement is the whole contract. Contracts can also include verbal, handbook and implied terms.
  • Not checking your payslip. Errors in hours, rates or tax codes are common, so check every time and raise problems early.
  • Thinking student loan repayments depend on how much you owe. They depend on what you earn above the threshold.
  • Opting out to boost take-home pay without considering what you lose. You give up employer contributions, tax relief and long-term growth.
  • Thinking part-time workers get less holiday per week worked. They receive the same 5.6 weeks, calculated pro rata to the days they work.

Lessons in this school

  1. Employment status: employee, worker and self-employed
    Your employment rights in the UK depend on your employment status.
  2. The written statement of employment particulars
    Employees and workers in the UK are entitled to a written statement of their main terms of employment from day one.
  3. Pay, the minimum wage and payslips
    Knowing how your pay is set and recorded helps you spot mistakes and protect your rights.
  4. Tax, National Insurance and student loan repayments
    Your first full-time job usually brings Income Tax, National Insurance and, for graduates, student loan repayments.
  5. Pensions and auto-enrolment
    Most employees are automatically enrolled into a workplace pension.
  6. Working hours and holiday
    The Working Time Regulations 1998 set limits on working hours and give workers rights to rest breaks and paid holiday.
  7. Probation periods
    Many jobs begin with a probation period during which your employer assesses whether the role is working out.
  8. Equality and discrimination at work
    The Equality Act 2010 protects workers from discrimination, harassment and victimisation.
  9. Disability, disclosure and reasonable adjustments
    Disabled employees have a right to reasonable adjustments at work, and choosing whether and how to disclose a condition is a personal decision.
  10. Flexible working
    Employees in the UK can request changes to when, where and how much they work.
  11. Raising a grievance
    A grievance is a concern or complaint raised with your employer.
  12. Your first 90 days
    The first three months in a new job shape how colleagues see you and how quickly you become effective.
  13. Workplace wellbeing
    Looking after your wellbeing helps you perform well and enjoy work.

Starting Work: Contracts and Rights: questions students ask

What did the Supreme Court decide about Uber drivers in 2021?

They were workers, entitled to the minimum wage and holiday pay.

When must a written statement of particulars be provided?

On or before the first day of work.

What is the difference between gross and net pay?

Gross is pay before deductions; net is what you receive after deductions.

What are the age and earnings conditions for auto-enrolment?

Aged 22 to State Pension age and earning over £10,000 a year.
