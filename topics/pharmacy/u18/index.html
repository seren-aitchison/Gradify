Key terms and formulas at a glance

Independent prescriber
A practitioner responsible for assessing patients and making prescribing decisions, including for undiagnosed conditions, within their competence
Competency framework
A set of competencies describing the knowledge, skills and behaviours expected for a role
Scope of practice
The areas in which a prescriber has the knowledge, skills and experience to prescribe safely
Calgary-Cambridge model
A consultation framework: initiating, gathering information, physical examination, explanation and planning, and closing, with structure and relationship throughout
Presenting complaint
The main problem the person describes, in their own words
Inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation
The four classic stages of physical examination
Vital signs
Measurements of essential body functions: temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and consciousness
Urea and electrolytes (U&Es)
Tests of kidney function and electrolytes: sodium, potassium, urea and creatinine
Differential diagnosis
A list of possible conditions that could explain the person's presentation
Prescription-only medicine (POM)
A medicine that can only be supplied against a prescription from an appropriate practitioner
Prescribing error
An unintentional mistake in the prescribing decision or prescription writing process
Delayed (back-up) prescribing
A prescription to use only if symptoms don't improve or worsen

When did pharmacist independent prescribing begin?

2006. From “The independent prescriber” →

In plain English: Pharmacist independent prescribers can prescribe any medicine for any condition within their competence, including most controlled drugs. Key idea: Pharmacist independent prescribers can prescribe within their competence, including most controlled drugs; from 2026, new pharmacists register as prescribers.

What does ICE stand for?

Ideas, Concerns and Expectations. From “Consultation models” →

In plain English: Consultation models give structure to clinical conversations, helping prescribers gather information, build rapport and reach shared decisions. Key idea: Use consultation models (Calgary-Cambridge, Neighbour, ICE) to structure care; listen actively, share decisions and always safety net.

Which vital sign is often the earliest sign of deterioration?

Respiratory rate. From “Vital signs and red flags” →

In plain English: Vital signs give an objective picture of how unwell someone is. Key idea: Measure vital signs and use NEWS2 to judge deterioration; respiratory rate is an early warning sign; recognise red flags and escalate promptly.

When should a medicine be prescribed by brand?

When brands differ, such as modified-release, narrow-margin and biological medicines. From “Writing a prescription” →

In plain English: A prescription is a legal document and an instruction to others. Key idea: Prescriptions must meet legal requirements; write generically, avoid abbreviations and trailing zeros, and include dose, route, frequency, duration and indication.

Which medicines need extra safeguards online?

For example opioids, gabapentinoids and weight-loss medicines. From “Remote prescribing” →

In plain English: Prescribing by phone, video or online questionnaire is now common, but it brings risks: you can't examine, identity is harder to confirm, and some medicines are open to misuse. Key idea: Remote prescribing needs identity checks, information sharing, extra safeguards for high-risk medicines, and face-to-face referral when needed.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Prescribing and Clinical Assessment

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What must happen before a pharmacist can practise as an independent prescriber? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Annotation on the GPhC register. The register shows prescribing annotation.

  2. Who publishes the competency framework for all prescribers? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Royal Pharmaceutical Society. RPS publishes it for all prescribing professions.

  3. Who is accountable for a prescribing decision? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The prescriber. Prescribers are personally accountable for their prescribing.

  4. What limits what a pharmacist independent prescriber should prescribe? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their competence and scope of practice. The law is broad, but professional standards require competence.

  5. How does a supplementary prescriber differ from an independent prescriber? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They prescribe under a clinical management plan with an independent prescriber. Supplementary prescribing works within an agreed plan.

  6. Which competency falls under the consultation domain? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Present options and reach a shared decision. Shared decision-making is part of the consultation domain.

  7. Why is prescribing for close family discouraged? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Objectivity, proper assessment and records may be compromised. GPhC guidance advises against it except in emergencies.

  8. Why ask about herbal and recreational drugs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They cause interactions and adverse effects. Unrecorded products cause important interactions.

  9. Why is respiratory rate so important? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It's often the earliest sign of deterioration. A rising respiratory rate often precedes changes in other vital signs.

Key takeaways

  • Pharmacist independent prescribers can prescribe within their competence, including most controlled drugs; from 2026, new pharmacists register as prescribers.
  • The RPS framework has two domains and ten competencies covering the consultation and prescribing governance; use it to reflect and develop.
  • Define and extend scope with training and evidence; work within governance, indemnity and audit; separate prescribing and supply where possible.
  • Use consultation models (Calgary-Cambridge, Neighbour, ICE) to structure care; listen actively, share decisions and always safety net.
  • Take a structured history (presenting complaint, medicines, allergies, family, social, systems review), use SOCRATES for pain, and recognise red flags.
  • Examine with consent and dignity, systematically, within your competence; document findings and refer when unsure.

Common mistakes in Prescribing and Clinical Assessment

  • Prescribing outside your area of competence because the law technically allows it.
  • Treating the framework as a one-off checklist rather than a tool for ongoing development.
  • Prescribing for a friend at a social event without a proper assessment.
  • Ending a consultation without telling the person what to do if symptoms worsen.
  • Recording "allergic to penicillin" without describing the reaction.
  • Carrying out an intimate examination without offering a chaperone.

Lessons in this school

  1. The independent prescriber
    Pharmacist independent prescribers can prescribe any medicine for any condition within their competence, including most controlled drugs.
  2. The RPS competency framework for all prescribers
    The Royal Pharmaceutical Society's competency framework sets out what good prescribing looks like for every prescriber, whatever their profession.
  3. Prescribing governance and scope
    Safe prescribing needs clear governance: defined scope, supervision, audit and accountability.
  4. Consultation models
    Consultation models give structure to clinical conversations, helping prescribers gather information, build rapport and reach shared decisions.
  5. History taking
    A thorough history leads to the diagnosis in most cases.
  6. Clinical examination skills
    Clinical examination confirms or refutes hypotheses from the history.
  7. Vital signs and red flags
    Vital signs give an objective picture of how unwell someone is.
  8. Interpreting blood tests
    Prescribers order and interpret blood tests to diagnose, choose medicines and monitor safety.
  9. Diagnostic reasoning
    Diagnostic reasoning is how clinicians move from symptoms to a diagnosis.
  10. Writing a prescription
    A prescription is a legal document and an instruction to others.
  11. Prescribing safety and common errors
    Prescribing errors are common and can be serious.
  12. Antimicrobial prescribing
    Prescribers are central to antimicrobial stewardship.
  13. Remote prescribing
    Prescribing by phone, video or online questionnaire is now common, but it brings risks: you can't examine, identity is harder to confirm, and some medicines are open to misuse.
  14. Influences on prescribing
    Prescribing decisions are shaped by evidence, but also by patients, colleagues, industry, cost and our own habits.
  15. Monitoring and follow-up
    Prescribing doesn't end when the prescription is signed.
  16. Prescribing in practice: case reviews
    Case reviews bring the prescribing process together: assessment, decision, prescription, monitoring and reflection.
