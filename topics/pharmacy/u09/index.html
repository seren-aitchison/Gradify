Key terms and formulas at a glance

Basal-bolus regimen
A long-acting (basal) insulin once or twice daily plus rapid-acting (bolus) insulin with meals
Analogue insulin
A modified insulin with altered absorption, such as rapid-acting aspart or long-acting glargine
HbA1c
Glycated haemoglobin: a measure of average blood glucose over about 2 to 3 months
GLP-1 receptor agonist
A drug mimicking glucagon-like peptide 1, increasing insulin release and satiety, such as semaglutide
Hypoglycaemia
Blood glucose below 4 mmol/L in a person with diabetes
Steroid-induced hyperglycaemia
Raised glucose caused by corticosteroids, typically in the afternoon and evening after a morning dose
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
An emergency with high ketones, acidosis and usually high glucose, caused by insulin deficiency
TSH
Thyroid-stimulating hormone: the main blood test for monitoring thyroid replacement
Adrenal crisis
A life-threatening emergency from cortisol deficiency, with vomiting, low blood pressure and collapse
Fragility fracture
A fracture from a fall from standing height or less
UKMEC
UK Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use: categories 1 to 4 for each method and condition
Combined HRT
Oestrogen with a progestogen, needed to protect the womb lining in people with a uterus

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a basal-bolus regimen?

Long-acting insulin for background needs plus rapid-acting insulin with meals. From “Type 1 diabetes” →

In plain English: Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing beta cells, so insulin is lifelong and essential. Key idea: Type 1 diabetes needs lifelong insulin (basal-bolus or pump), education, CGM and sick day rules: never stop basal insulin.

When should an SGLT2 inhibitor be added?

When the person has heart failure, cardiovascular disease or high cardiovascular risk. From “Type 2 diabetes” →

In plain English: Type 2 diabetes results from insulin resistance and reduced insulin secretion. Key idea: Metformin first, add an SGLT2 inhibitor for cardiovascular or heart failure risk, and individualise HbA1c targets and later choices.

When does steroid-induced hyperglycaemia typically peak after a morning dose?

In the afternoon and evening. From “Diabetes in special situations” →

In plain English: Illness, steroids, pregnancy, older age, fasting and surgery all change glucose control and medicine needs. Key idea: Anticipate how illness, steroids, pregnancy, fasting and surgery change glucose, and plan medicine changes with the person and their team.

Drug card: Levothyroxine. Key adverse effects, cautions and monitoring?

Adverse effects and cautions: Symptoms of over-replacement (palpitations, tremor, weight loss, insomnia); angina in people with heart disease Monitoring and counselling: TSH 6 to 8 weeks after dose changes; take on an empty stomach; separate from calcium, iron and antacids (check the BNF) From “Thyroid disorders” →

In plain English: Underactive and overactive thyroid disease are common and treatable. Key idea: Levothyroxine on an empty stomach with TSH checks; carbimazole needs immediate reporting of sore throat or fever for agranulocytosis.

Which is the most effective emergency contraception?

The copper intrauterine device. From “Contraception” →

In plain English: Pharmacists supply, advise on and increasingly provide contraception, including emergency contraception. Key idea: Use UKMEC to check suitability; the copper IUD is the most effective emergency method; enzyme inducers need a contraceptive method they don't affect.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Why is insulin essential in type 1 diabetes? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The pancreas makes little or no insulin. Autoimmune destruction of beta cells removes insulin production.

  2. Which is a rapid-acting insulin analogue? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Insulin aspart. Rapid-acting analogues are given with meals.

  3. What does HbA1c measure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Average blood glucose over 2 to 3 months. Glycated haemoglobin reflects glucose over the red cell lifespan.

  4. Someone with type 1 diabetes has gastroenteritis and isn't eating. What should they do with their basal insulin? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Continue it and check glucose and ketones more often. Stopping basal insulin risks diabetic ketoacidosis.

  5. What does NICE recommend offering adults with type 1 diabetes for monitoring? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Continuous glucose monitoring. NG17 recommends offering CGM to adults with type 1 diabetes.

  6. Why should high-strength insulin never be drawn up with a syringe? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It risks a large overdose. Syringes are calibrated for 100 units/mL, so the dose would be multiplied.

  7. What HbA1c target suits someone on a sulfonylurea? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    53 mmol/mol. NICE suggests 53 when drugs can cause hypoglycaemia.

  8. Someone on insulin has a glucose of 4.6 mmol/L before driving. What's the advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Have a snack and recheck before driving. The DVLA advises not driving below 5 mmol/L without treating it first.

  9. Someone with type 1 diabetes has vomiting and ketones of 3.5 mmol/L. What's right? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Urgent hospital assessment. High ketones with vomiting suggest DKA, which needs urgent hospital treatment.

Key takeaways

  • Type 1 diabetes needs lifelong insulin (basal-bolus or pump), education, CGM and sick day rules: never stop basal insulin.
  • Insulin is high risk: write "units" in full, prescribe by brand, device and strength, never draw high-strength insulin into a syringe, and counsel on technique and hypos.
  • Metformin first, add an SGLT2 inhibitor for cardiovascular or heart failure risk, and individualise HbA1c targets and later choices.
  • Know each class's risks: hypos (insulin, sulfonylureas), DKA (SGLT2 inhibitors), fluid retention (pioglitazone), GI effects (metformin, GLP-1 agonists), and apply sick day guidance.
  • Under 4 mmol/L: 15 to 20 g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 10 to 15 minutes, then a longer-acting snack; unconscious means 999 and nothing by mouth.
  • Anticipate how illness, steroids, pregnancy, fasting and surgery change glucose, and plan medicine changes with the person and their team.

Common mistakes in Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics

  • Advising someone with type 1 diabetes who isn't eating because of a stomach bug to stop their insulin.
  • Writing "10U" on a prescription, which can be read as 100 units.
  • Chasing a tight HbA1c target in a frail older person, causing hypoglycaemia and falls.
  • Reassuring someone on an SGLT2 inhibitor with vomiting and abdominal pain because their glucose is only modestly raised: euglycaemic DKA is possible.
  • Giving a sugary drink to someone who is unconscious from hypoglycaemia, risking choking.
  • Starting a course of prednisolone in someone with diabetes without advising extra glucose monitoring.

Lessons in this school

  1. Type 1 diabetes
    Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing beta cells, so insulin is lifelong and essential.
  2. Insulins
    Insulin is a high-risk medicine: errors with type, strength, device or dose can cause severe hypoglycaemia or ketoacidosis.
  3. Type 2 diabetes
    Type 2 diabetes results from insulin resistance and reduced insulin secretion.
  4. Glucose-lowering drugs
    Each class of glucose-lowering drug has its own benefits, risks and monitoring.
  5. Hypoglycaemia
    Hypoglycaemia is the commonest acute complication of diabetes treatment and can be dangerous.
  6. Diabetes in special situations
    Illness, steroids, pregnancy, older age, fasting and surgery all change glucose control and medicine needs.
  7. Diabetic emergencies
    Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) are life-threatening emergencies.
  8. Thyroid disorders
    Underactive and overactive thyroid disease are common and treatable.
  9. Adrenal insufficiency and corticosteroids
    Adrenal insufficiency, whether from Addison's disease or long-term steroid use, can lead to a life-threatening adrenal crisis.
  10. Osteoporosis
    Osteoporosis makes bones fragile, so fractures happen after minor falls.
  11. Contraception
    Pharmacists supply, advise on and increasingly provide contraception, including emergency contraception.
  12. Menopause and hormone replacement therapy
    Menopause symptoms affect many people's health and work.
  13. Obesity and weight management
    Obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, some cancers and joint problems.
