Key terms and formulas at a glance

Less severe depression
Depression with fewer or milder symptoms (formerly mild and subthreshold), as categorised by NICE
Serotonin syndrome
A potentially serious reaction to excess serotonergic activity: agitation, tremor, clonus, sweating, fever
Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)
Excessive, persistent worry about everyday matters, for at least several months
Mood stabiliser
A medicine reducing the frequency and severity of mood episodes, such as lithium
Treatment-resistant schizophrenia
Schizophrenia not responding to adequate trials of at least two antipsychotics
Extrapyramidal side effects (EPSEs)
Movement effects of dopamine blockade: dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia and tardive dyskinesia
Stimulant
An ADHD medicine increasing dopamine and noradrenaline, such as methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor
A drug increasing acetylcholine, such as donepezil, used in mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease
CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)
A structured psychological treatment for chronic insomnia, the first-line treatment
Opioid substitution therapy (OST)
Treatment of opioid dependence with methadone or buprenorphine
Focal seizure
A seizure starting in one area of the brain
Enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicine
A drug such as carbamazepine or phenytoin that induces liver enzymes and lowers levels of other drugs

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How long should an antidepressant usually continue after remission?

At least 6 months. From “Depression” →

In plain English: Depression is common, disabling and treatable. Key idea: Step care by severity and choice; review antidepressants early, continue at least 6 months after remission, and ask about suicide risk.

When is clozapine used?

After at least two antipsychotics haven't worked. From “Schizophrenia and psychosis” →

In plain English: Psychosis involves losing touch with reality through hallucinations, delusions or disordered thinking. Key idea: Antipsychotics with psychological and physical health support; clozapine for treatment resistance with mandatory blood monitoring and vigilance for constipation and myocarditis.

What is first-line treatment for chronic insomnia?

CBT for insomnia (CBT-I). From “Insomnia” →

In plain English: Insomnia is common and often linked to stress, health problems or habits. Key idea: CBT-I first; hypnotics only short term; avoid sedating antihistamines in older people.

Why are Parkinson's medicines time-critical?

Late or missed doses cause rapid loss of mobility and other complications. From “Parkinson's disease” →

In plain English: Parkinson's disease causes slowness, stiffness and tremor from dopamine loss. Key idea: Levodopa and dopamine agonists treat Parkinson's; doses are time-critical; avoid metoclopramide and antipsychotics.

What does NICE advise about starting analgesics for chronic primary pain?

Don't start paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids, gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines. From “Analgesic ladder and simple analgesics” →

In plain English: Paracetamol and NSAIDs are the foundation of treating acute pain, used at the right dose and duration. Key idea: Paracetamol and NSAIDs for acute pain, used safely; for chronic primary pain, NICE favours exercise, psychological therapy and antidepressants over analgesics.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How soon do antidepressants usually start to work? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Within 2 to 4 weeks. Benefit builds over weeks, so early review supports adherence.

  2. Which antidepressant is most dangerous in overdose? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Amitriptyline. Tricyclics cause arrhythmias and seizures in overdose.

  3. Why is blood monitoring mandatory with clozapine? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Risk of neutropenia and agranulocytosis. Clozapine can cause severe falls in white cells.

  4. A 22-year-old starts sertraline. When should they be reviewed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Within about 1 week. Young people are reviewed early because suicide risk can rise at the start.

  5. Which first-line option suits less severe depression for most people? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Guided self-help or behavioural activation. NICE NG222 favours psychological interventions first for less severe depression.

  6. An 80-year-old on sertraline becomes confused and drowsy. What should be checked? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Serum sodium. SSRIs can cause hyponatraemia, especially in older people.

  7. How should an SSRI usually be stopped after long-term use? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Gradually tapered over weeks or longer. NICE recommends gradual tapering to reduce discontinuation symptoms.

  8. What are the MHRA restrictions on starting valproate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Not in new patients under 55 unless two specialists agree. The 2024 MHRA measures restrict new starts in people under 55.

  9. Someone on methadone has missed four days of doses. What should happen? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Contact the prescriber before supplying. Tolerance may have fallen, so the usual dose could be dangerous.

Key takeaways

  • Step care by severity and choice; review antidepressants early, continue at least 6 months after remission, and ask about suicide risk.
  • SSRIs first for most; know each class's harms; watch for serotonin syndrome and hyponatraemia; taper gradually when stopping.
  • Psychological therapy first; SSRIs as the main medicines; benzodiazepines only very short term, and pregabalin with care.
  • Lithium: levels 12 hours post-dose, regular renal and thyroid tests, avoid interacting drugs and dehydration, and prescribe by brand.
  • Antipsychotics with psychological and physical health support; clozapine for treatment resistance with mandatory blood monitoring and vigilance for constipation and myocarditis.
  • Antipsychotics differ in EPSEs and metabolic effects; monitor weight, glucose, lipids, BP and ECG, and avoid in dementia unless essential.

Common mistakes in Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics

  • Stopping an antidepressant as soon as the person feels better, increasing the risk of relapse.
  • Supplying tramadol to someone on an SSRI without considering serotonin syndrome and seizure risk.
  • Repeating benzodiazepine prescriptions for months for generalised anxiety without a plan to reduce.
  • Recommending ibuprofen for back pain to someone on lithium.
  • Ignoring constipation in someone on clozapine: it can progress to fatal bowel obstruction.
  • Starting an antipsychotic for agitation in dementia without a review date and attempts at non-drug approaches.

Lessons in this school

  1. Depression
    Depression is common, disabling and treatable.
  2. Antidepressants
    Antidepressant classes differ in side effects, interactions and safety in overdose.
  3. Anxiety disorders
    Generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder and related conditions are common.
  4. Bipolar disorder and lithium
    Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania and depression.
  5. Schizophrenia and psychosis
    Psychosis involves losing touch with reality through hallucinations, delusions or disordered thinking.
  6. Antipsychotics
    Antipsychotics block dopamine D2 receptors and other receptors.
  7. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
    ADHD affects attention, impulsivity and activity from childhood, often into adulthood.
  8. Dementia
    Dementia causes progressive decline in memory and thinking.
  9. Insomnia
    Insomnia is common and often linked to stress, health problems or habits.
  10. Substance misuse and harm reduction
    Pharmacies are central to treatment and harm reduction for people who use drugs or alcohol: supervised consumption of opioid substitution therapy, needle and syringe programmes, naloxone and alcohol support.
  11. Epilepsy
    Epilepsy is a tendency to recurrent seizures.
  12. Antiepileptic drugs
    Antiseizure medicines have narrow margins, many interactions and specific serious adverse effects.
  13. Parkinson's disease
    Parkinson's disease causes slowness, stiffness and tremor from dopamine loss.
  14. Migraine
    Migraine causes disabling headaches, often with nausea and sensitivity to light.
  15. Multiple sclerosis
    Multiple sclerosis is an immune-mediated condition damaging the central nervous system.
  16. Pain physiology and assessment
    Pain is a personal experience shaped by biology, psychology and circumstances.
  17. Analgesic ladder and simple analgesics
    Paracetamol and NSAIDs are the foundation of treating acute pain, used at the right dose and duration.
  18. Opioids
    Opioids relieve severe acute and cancer pain but have limited benefit and real harms in long-term non-cancer pain.
  19. Neuropathic pain
    Neuropathic pain responds poorly to standard analgesics.
  20. Palliative care symptom control
    In palliative and end-of-life care, the aim is comfort and dignity.

Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics: questions students ask

What is less severe depression in NICE terms?

Depression with fewer or milder symptoms, covering what was formerly called mild and subthreshold depression.

Drug card: Mirtazapine. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressant (alpha-2 antagonist) Uses: Depression, especially with poor sleep or appetite

What is a first-choice medicine for GAD?

An SSRI such as sertraline.

Drug card: Lithium carbonate. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Mood stabiliser (mechanism not fully understood) Uses: Prophylaxis and treatment of bipolar disorder; augmentation in depression
