Key terms and formulas at a glance

Functional group
A specific arrangement of atoms within a molecule that gives characteristic chemical properties
pKa
The pH at which a weak acid or base is 50% ionised
Henderson-Hasselbalch equation
An equation relating pH, pKa and the ratio of ionised to un-ionised forms
Partition coefficient (P)
The ratio of a compound's concentration in octanol to its concentration in water at equilibrium, for the un-ionised form
Chiral centre
A carbon atom bonded to four different groups, giving non-superimposable mirror images
Structure-activity relationship (SAR)
The relationship between a molecule's chemical structure and its biological activity
Pharmacophore
The spatial arrangement of features, such as hydrogen bond donors, acceptors, charges and hydrophobic regions, needed for activity
Prodrug
A compound that is converted in the body into an active drug
Bioisostere
A group that can replace another with broadly similar biological properties
Drug-likeness
How closely a compound's properties match those of known oral drugs
Beta-lactam ring
A strained four-membered cyclic amide that is the active core of penicillins and related antibiotics
Steroid nucleus
Four fused rings, three six-membered (A, B, C) and one five-membered (D)

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why are most amine drugs positively charged in plasma?

They are bases that accept a proton at pH 7.4. From “Functional groups in drugs” →

In plain English: A drug's functional groups decide how it binds its target, how it dissolves and how the body handles it. Key idea: Functional groups such as hydroxyls, acids, amines, esters, amides, aromatic rings and halogens determine binding, solubility, metabolism and stability.

What is a racemate?

A 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers. From “Stereochemistry and chirality” →

In plain English: Many drugs exist as mirror-image forms that the body can tell apart. Key idea: Chiral drugs have R and S enantiomers that chiral targets distinguish; racemates contain both, and single-enantiomer drugs aim to give the more active form.

What is a bioisostere?

A group that can replace another with broadly similar biological properties. From “Bioisosteres” →

In plain English: Bioisosteres are groups that can replace each other while keeping similar biological effects. Key idea: Bioisosteres swap groups with similar properties, such as tetrazole for carboxylic acid or F for H, to improve stability, absorption or selectivity while keeping activity.

What structural change turns morphine into codeine?

Methylation of the 3-phenol group. From “Opioid chemistry” →

In plain English: Morphine's structure is the template for many opioids. Key idea: Morphine's 3-phenol, 6-hydroxyl and tertiary amine are key; methylation gives codeine, acetylation gives diamorphine, and N-allyl substitution gives antagonists such as naloxone.

What is the difference between accuracy and precision?

Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is closeness of repeated results. From “Pharmaceutical analysis” →

In plain English: Pharmaceutical analysis checks that medicines contain the right drug, in the right amount, at the right purity. Key idea: Pharmaceutical analysis tests identity, assay, purity and performance; validated methods show accuracy, precision, specificity, linearity, range, detection limits and robustness.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which functional group is most likely to form an ionic bond with an aspartate residue in a receptor? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A protonated amine. A positively charged amine pairs with the negatively charged carboxylate of aspartate.

  2. A weak acid has a pKa of 4. At what pH is it 50% ionised? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    pH 4. At pH equal to pKa, ionised and un-ionised forms are present in equal amounts.

  3. Which form of a drug crosses lipid membranes most readily by passive diffusion? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The un-ionised form. Un-ionised molecules are more lipid soluble and diffuse through membranes.

  4. Why does adding a chlorine to an aromatic ring often prolong a drug's action? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It can block metabolism at that position. Halogens resist oxidation, so they can block a site of metabolism.

  5. Ibuprofen is a weak acid with a pKa of about 4.4. In which form is it mainly found in plasma at pH 7.4? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ionised, as the pH is above its pKa. Acids are mostly ionised when the pH is above their pKa.

  6. A weakly basic drug crosses into a more acidic compartment. What happens? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It becomes more ionised and is trapped there. Bases ionise in acid, and ionised drug cannot easily diffuse back.

  7. A drug contains an ester and a phenol. Which storage risks should you expect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Hydrolysis and oxidation. Esters hydrolyse in moisture and phenols oxidise in air and light.

  8. Which basic drug is the strongest base? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    One with a pKa of 10. For bases, a higher pKa of the conjugate acid means a stronger base.

  9. Why is sodium bicarbonate used in salicylate poisoning? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It ionises salicylate in urine, increasing excretion. Alkaline urine traps the weak acid salicylate, so less is reabsorbed.

Key takeaways

  • Functional groups such as hydroxyls, acids, amines, esters, amides, aromatic rings and halogens determine binding, solubility, metabolism and stability.
  • pKa is the pH of 50% ionisation; acids ionise above their pKa and bases ionise below theirs, with a tenfold change per pH unit.
  • For acids, ratio ionised to un-ionised equals 10^(pH − pKa); for bases, 10^(pKa − pH); drugs become trapped where they are more ionised.
  • Log P measures lipophilicity of the un-ionised form and log D includes ionisation at a given pH; higher lipophilicity increases permeability but reduces solubility.
  • Chiral drugs have R and S enantiomers that chiral targets distinguish; racemates contain both, and single-enantiomer drugs aim to give the more active form.
  • SAR studies vary structure one feature at a time to find essential groups and tune activity and selectivity; QSAR models this quantitatively.

Common mistakes in Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry

  • Treating a drug as one fixed property. Each functional group contributes separately to binding, ionisation and breakdown.
  • Reading a base's pKa as if it were an acid's. A high pKa means a strong base, which is mostly ionised at plasma pH.
  • Using the acid form of the equation for a base. Write out which form is ionised before calculating.
  • Assuming more lipophilic always means better absorption. Very lipophilic drugs may not dissolve well enough to be absorbed.
  • Assuming a single-enantiomer product is always safer. Some enantiomers interconvert in the body, as thalidomide does.
  • Changing several features at once. It becomes impossible to tell which change caused the effect.

Lessons in this school

  1. Functional groups in drugs
    A drug's functional groups decide how it binds its target, how it dissolves and how the body handles it.
  2. Acids, bases and pKa
    Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases.
  3. Ionisation and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation
    The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation turns pKa and pH into a ratio of ionised to un-ionised drug.
  4. Lipophilicity and partition coefficients
    Lipophilicity describes how much a drug prefers fats to water.
  5. Stereochemistry and chirality
    Many drugs exist as mirror-image forms that the body can tell apart.
  6. Structure-activity relationships
    Structure-activity relationships link changes in a molecule's structure to changes in its biological effect.
  7. Pharmacophores and lead optimisation
    A pharmacophore is the arrangement of features a molecule needs to act at its target.
  8. Prodrugs
    A prodrug is inactive or less active until the body converts it into the active drug.
  9. Bioisosteres
    Bioisosteres are groups that can replace each other while keeping similar biological effects.
  10. Drug-likeness and Lipinski's rule of five
    Drug-likeness describes properties that make a compound likely to be absorbed orally.
  11. Beta-lactam chemistry
    Beta-lactam antibiotics share a strained four-membered ring that attacks bacterial cell wall enzymes.
  12. Steroid chemistry
    Steroids share a rigid four-ring skeleton.
  13. Opioid chemistry
    Morphine's structure is the template for many opioids.
  14. Benzodiazepine chemistry
    Benzodiazepines share a fused ring system that binds the GABA-A receptor.
  15. Chemical degradation pathways
    Medicines break down by predictable chemical reactions.
  16. Drug stability and shelf life
    Shelf life is how long a product stays within its specification under stated storage conditions.
  17. Pharmaceutical analysis
    Pharmaceutical analysis checks that medicines contain the right drug, in the right amount, at the right purity.
  18. Spectroscopy in pharmaceutical analysis
    Spectroscopy measures how molecules interact with light and other radiation.
  19. Chromatography
    Chromatography separates mixtures so their components can be identified and measured.
  20. Quality control and pharmacopoeias
    Quality control makes sure every batch of a medicine meets its specification before release.

Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry: questions students ask

What is true when the pH equals a drug's pKa?

The drug is 50% ionised.

Why does alkalinising urine increase salicylate excretion?

Salicylate is a weak acid, so it becomes more ionised and is trapped in the urine.

What does a log P of 2 mean?

The drug is 100 times more concentrated in octanol than in water.

What is the aim of a structure-activity relationship study?

To find which parts of a molecule are needed for activity and how changes affect it.
