Key terms and formulas at a glance

Gram stain
A staining method dividing bacteria into Gram-positive (thick peptidoglycan wall) and Gram-negative (thin wall with an outer membrane)
Beta-lactam
An antibiotic with a beta-lactam ring that blocks cell wall synthesis: penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)
The ability of microorganisms to survive medicines that would normally kill them or stop their growth
Start Smart then Focus
UKHSA's antimicrobial stewardship toolkit for hospitals
Empirical treatment
Treatment started before the organism is known, based on the likely cause and local resistance
Lower UTI
Infection of the bladder (cystitis), causing frequency, urgency and dysuria
FeverPAIN score
A score (fever, purulence, attend rapidly, inflamed tonsils, no cough) predicting streptococcal sore throat
Cellulitis
A bacterial infection of the deeper skin and tissues, usually streptococcal or staphylococcal
Sepsis
Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection
Clostridioides difficile
A spore-forming anaerobic bacterium causing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and colitis
Active TB
Tuberculosis causing symptoms and disease, needing treatment
Dermatophyte
A fungus infecting skin, hair or nails, such as the causes of athlete's foot and ringworm

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why don't beta-lactams treat Mycoplasma?

It lacks a typical cell wall, which beta-lactams target. From “Microbiology for pharmacists” →

In plain English: Choosing an antimicrobial well starts with understanding the organisms that cause infection. Key idea: Gram stain, culture and sensitivities guide treatment; take samples before antibiotics where possible and know the major resistant organisms.

What decisions are made at the 48 to 72 hour review?

Stop, switch IV to oral, change, continue, or outpatient parenteral therapy. From “Antimicrobial stewardship” →

In plain English: Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated programme to use antimicrobials well: the right drug, dose, route and duration, reviewed regularly. Key idea: Start smart (indication, guidelines, cultures), then focus (review at 48 to 72 hours); in primary care, use guidelines, back-up prescriptions and clear advice.

What is first-line treatment for low-severity community-acquired pneumonia?

Amoxicillin, usually for 5 days. From “Respiratory tract infections” →

In plain English: Most respiratory tract infections are viral and self-limiting, but pneumonia and some bacterial infections need antibiotics. Key idea: Most respiratory infections need no antibiotic; use FeverPAIN and CRB-65 to identify those who do, and follow NICE durations.

How long is standard treatment for drug-sensitive pulmonary TB?

6 months. From “Tuberculosis” →

In plain English: Tuberculosis needs months of combination treatment to cure it and prevent resistance. Key idea: Drug-sensitive TB: 6 months (four drugs for 2 months, then two for 4); rifampicin is a major enzyme inducer; support adherence.

How soon must PEP be started?

As soon as possible, within 72 hours of exposure. From “HIV” →

In plain English: HIV is now a manageable long-term condition: effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) gives a near-normal life expectancy and prevents transmission. Key idea: Lifelong ART suppresses HIV and prevents transmission (U=U); check every new medicine for interactions and support adherence.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which organism is Gram-positive? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Staphylococcus aureus. Staphylococci have a thick peptidoglycan wall.

  2. Which antibiotic is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Clarithromycin. Clarithromycin interacts with simvastatin, some DOACs and many others.

  3. What drives antimicrobial resistance most? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Antibiotic use, especially unnecessary use. Use creates selection pressure for resistant organisms.

  4. Why take a sample before starting antibiotics? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    So culture results can guide treatment. Antibiotics can stop organisms growing in the sample.

  5. Which drug targets anaerobic bacteria? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Metronidazole. Metronidazole is active against anaerobes.

  6. Which antibiotic should be taken with water, upright, and not with milk? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Doxycycline. Doxycycline causes oesophageal irritation and is chelated by calcium.

  7. Which question is most useful when someone reports a penicillin allergy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    What exactly happened and how severe was it?. The reaction's nature and severity decide whether it's a true allergy.

  8. Why does a wrong penicillin allergy label matter? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It leads to broader-spectrum, less effective antibiotics. Alternatives are often broader and more likely to cause C. difficile and resistance.

  9. A woman with UTI symptoms has fever and loin pain. What's right? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Refer: these suggest pyelonephritis. Upper UTI needs different treatment and assessment.

Key takeaways

  • Gram stain, culture and sensitivities guide treatment; take samples before antibiotics where possible and know the major resistant organisms.
  • Match each class to its spectrum, toxicities and interactions; quinolones are restricted because of disabling adverse effects.
  • AMR is driven by antibiotic use and spread; prescribe only when needed, narrow and short, and prevent infection.
  • Start smart (indication, guidelines, cultures), then focus (review at 48 to 72 hours); in primary care, use guidelines, back-up prescriptions and clear advice.
  • Choose by infection, local guidelines, patient factors and drug factors; take a careful allergy history, because most penicillin allergy labels are wrong.
  • Uncomplicated UTI in women: 3 days of nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim; men and pregnancy need 7 days and cultures; refer upper UTI and red flags.

Common mistakes in Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship

  • Starting antibiotics before sending a sample, when the result would have guided better treatment.
  • Choosing a quinolone for an uncomplicated infection when another antibiotic would work.
  • Supplying leftover antibiotics or encouraging people to keep antibiotics "just in case".
  • Continuing IV antibiotics past 72 hours without review, when the person could switch to oral treatment.
  • Accepting "allergic to penicillin" without asking what happened, when it was nausea or diarrhoea.
  • Treating a man's UTI with a 3-day course without sending a sample.

Lessons in this school

  1. Microbiology for pharmacists
    Choosing an antimicrobial well starts with understanding the organisms that cause infection.
  2. Antibiotic classes and mechanisms
    Antibiotics act on bacterial cell walls, protein synthesis, nucleic acids or metabolism.
  3. Antimicrobial resistance
    Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) makes infections harder to treat and threatens modern medicine.
  4. Antimicrobial stewardship
    Antimicrobial stewardship is a coordinated programme to use antimicrobials well: the right drug, dose, route and duration, reviewed regularly.
  5. Choosing an antibiotic
    Choosing an antibiotic means matching the likely organism and site of infection with the person's factors: allergy, kidney function, pregnancy, interactions and route.
  6. Urinary tract infections
    Urinary tract infections are among the commonest infections managed in primary care and pharmacies.
  7. Respiratory tract infections
    Most respiratory tract infections are viral and self-limiting, but pneumonia and some bacterial infections need antibiotics.
  8. Skin and soft tissue infections
    Skin infections range from impetigo and minor wound infections to cellulitis and bites.
  9. Sepsis
    Sepsis is a life-threatening response to infection that damages the body's own organs.
  10. Clostridioides difficile infection
    C. difficile infection causes diarrhoea and colitis, usually after antibiotics disrupt the gut flora.
  11. Tuberculosis
    Tuberculosis needs months of combination treatment to cure it and prevent resistance.
  12. Fungal infections
    Fungal infections range from common skin, nail and vaginal infections to serious systemic infections in immunocompromised people.
  13. Antiviral drugs
    Antivirals treat a growing range of infections, including herpes viruses, influenza and COVID-19.
  14. HIV
    HIV is now a manageable long-term condition: effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) gives a near-normal life expectancy and prevents transmission.
  15. Viral hepatitis
    Hepatitis B and C cause chronic liver disease, but hepatitis C can now be cured with short courses of tablets and hepatitis B is preventable by vaccination.
  16. Vaccines and immunisation
    Vaccines prevent serious diseases and pharmacies give millions of doses every year.
  17. Travel health
    Travellers may need advice on malaria, vaccines, food and water safety and existing medicines.

Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship: questions students ask

Why are quinolones restricted?

Rare but disabling, long-lasting tendon, nerve and psychiatric adverse effects, and aortic aneurysm risk.

What is selection pressure?

The advantage antimicrobial use gives to resistant organisms.

What does empirical treatment mean?

Treatment based on the likely organism before culture results are available.

What is a lower UTI?

Bladder infection (cystitis), causing frequency, urgency and dysuria.
