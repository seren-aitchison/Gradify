Key terms and formulas at a glance

Microcytic anaemia
Anaemia with small red cells, most often caused by iron deficiency
Patient blood management
An approach reducing unnecessary transfusion by optimising the patient's own blood
Systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT)
Medicines treating cancer throughout the body, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy and hormone therapy
Body surface area (BSA)
A calculation from height and weight used to dose many cytotoxic drugs
Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)
An oral targeted drug blocking signalling enzymes in cancer cells, such as imatinib
Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV)
Nausea and vomiting caused by chemotherapy, classified by emetic risk
Hazardous medicine
A medicine with carcinogenic, mutagenic or reproductive toxicity, requiring special handling
Calcineurin inhibitor
An immunosuppressant (tacrolimus, ciclosporin) that blocks T-cell activation
Rejection
The immune system attacking a transplanted organ
Biologic
A medicine made by living cells, such as a monoclonal antibody, targeting the immune system

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the most common cause of microcytic anaemia?

Iron deficiency. From “Anaemias” →

In plain English: Anaemia is common and has many causes, from iron deficiency to B12 and folate deficiency and chronic disease. Key idea: Classify anaemia by cell size; replace iron, B12 or folate correctly; investigate unexplained iron deficiency; counsel on iron interactions.

What is adjuvant therapy?

Treatment given after the main treatment (such as surgery) to reduce recurrence. From “Principles of cancer treatment” →

In plain English: Cancer treatment combines surgery, radiotherapy and systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT). Key idea: Cancer treatment may be curative or palliative; pharmacists verify SACT against protocols, check interactions and support oral treatment.

Name two immune-related adverse effects of checkpoint inhibitors.

For example colitis and pneumonitis. From “Targeted therapies and immunotherapy” →

In plain English: Targeted therapies and immunotherapies have transformed cancer treatment. Key idea: Targeted therapies need biomarker selection and interaction checks; checkpoint inhibitors cause immune-related effects in any organ, which need urgent oncology assessment.

What colour is cytotoxic waste containers' lid?

Purple. From “Safe handling of cytotoxic medicines” →

In plain English: Cytotoxic medicines can harm staff and others through exposure. Key idea: Cytotoxics are hazardous: prepare in aseptic units with PPE, avoid crushing tablets, use purple waste streams and spill kits, and advise carers.

Why is adherence crucial after transplant?

Missed doses can trigger rejection. From “Transplantation” →

In plain English: Organ transplantation saves lives but needs lifelong immunosuppression and careful medicines management. Key idea: Transplant patients need lifelong immunosuppression and infection prophylaxis; support adherence, brand consistency and interaction checks.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Oncology, Haematology and Immunology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What type of anaemia is typical of iron deficiency? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Microcytic. Iron deficiency produces small red cells.

  2. Which product reverses warfarin in major bleeding? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Prothrombin complex concentrate. PCC rapidly replaces vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

  3. What does neoadjuvant therapy mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Treatment given before the main treatment. It shrinks tumours before surgery or radiotherapy.

  4. Which medicine can cause B12 deficiency with long-term use? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Metformin. Metformin reduces B12 absorption over time.

  5. What should happen if a person develops fever and breathlessness during a transfusion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stop the transfusion and get urgent assessment. These may be signs of a serious transfusion reaction.

  6. What does filgrastim do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stimulates white cell production to shorten neutropenia. Filgrastim is G-CSF, which stimulates neutrophil production after chemotherapy.

  7. Why does iron deficiency in a 65-year-old man need investigation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It may come from gastrointestinal blood loss, including cancer. Unexplained iron deficiency can signal bowel cancer.

  8. What is the main risk of treating oral SACT as routine? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Errors and toxicity are as serious as with IV chemotherapy. Oral SACT needs the same checks as IV treatment.

  9. Why does mycophenolate need a pregnancy prevention programme? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It's teratogenic. Mycophenolate causes miscarriage and birth defects.

Key takeaways

  • Classify anaemia by cell size; replace iron, B12 or folate correctly; investigate unexplained iron deficiency; counsel on iron interactions.
  • Use blood products only when needed, identify patients carefully, and know reversal agents: vitamin K and PCC for warfarin, idarucizumab for dabigatran, protamine for heparin.
  • Cancer treatment may be curative or palliative; pharmacists verify SACT against protocols, check interactions and support oral treatment.
  • Cytotoxics cause myelosuppression and class-specific toxicities; fever during chemotherapy is an emergency; test for DPD deficiency before fluoropyrimidines.
  • Targeted therapies need biomarker selection and interaction checks; checkpoint inhibitors cause immune-related effects in any organ, which need urgent oncology assessment.
  • Use antiemetics by emetic risk, support mouth, bowel and skin care; recognise oncological emergencies such as spinal cord compression and neutropenic sepsis.

Common mistakes in Oncology, Haematology and Immunology

  • Giving folic acid alone to someone with undiagnosed B12 deficiency.
  • Delaying vitamin K and PCC for someone on warfarin with a major bleed.
  • Treating oral chemotherapy as low-risk because it's a tablet.
  • Advising someone on chemotherapy with a fever to take paracetamol and wait.
  • Treating diarrhoea in someone on pembrolizumab with loperamide without contacting their oncology team.
  • Ignoring new back pain and leg weakness in someone with cancer.

Lessons in this school

  1. Anaemias
    Anaemia is common and has many causes, from iron deficiency to B12 and folate deficiency and chronic disease.
  2. Blood products and haematological support
    Blood transfusion and haematological support are complex areas with serious risks.
  3. Principles of cancer treatment
    Cancer treatment combines surgery, radiotherapy and systemic anti-cancer therapy (SACT).
  4. Cytotoxic chemotherapy
    Cytotoxic drugs kill rapidly dividing cells, including healthy ones, causing predictable toxicities.
  5. Targeted therapies and immunotherapy
    Targeted therapies and immunotherapies have transformed cancer treatment.
  6. Supportive care in cancer
    Supportive care manages the symptoms and side effects of cancer and its treatment.
  7. Safe handling of cytotoxic medicines
    Cytotoxic medicines can harm staff and others through exposure.
  8. Immunosuppressants
    Immunosuppressants prevent transplant rejection and treat autoimmune disease, but carry risks of infection, cancer and toxicity.
  9. Transplantation
    Organ transplantation saves lives but needs lifelong immunosuppression and careful medicines management.
  10. Biologics in inflammatory disease
    Biological medicines such as TNF inhibitors have changed the treatment of inflammatory diseases.

Oncology, Haematology and Immunology: questions students ask

Why must B12 deficiency be excluded before folic acid?

Folic acid alone can worsen B12-related nerve damage.

Why test for DPD deficiency before capecitabine?

Deficiency causes severe, potentially fatal toxicity.

Why do many TKIs have interactions?

Many are metabolised by CYP3A4.

Which antiemetic class is aprepitant?

NK1 receptor antagonist.
