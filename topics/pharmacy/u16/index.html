Key terms and formulas at a glance

Human Medicines Regulations 2012
The main UK law on licensing, manufacture, wholesale, classification, sale, supply, prescribing and labelling of medicines
Pharmacy medicine (P)
A medicine that can be sold only from a registered pharmacy by or under the supervision of a pharmacist
Appropriate date
The date on the prescription, or a later "start date" given by the prescriber, from which validity is counted
Independent prescriber
A practitioner who is responsible for assessing patients and deciding on the clinical management, including prescribing, within their competence
Emergency supply
A supply of a POM without a prescription by a pharmacist, under conditions in the Human Medicines Regulations 2012
Patient group direction (PGD)
A written instruction for the supply or administration of a named medicine to groups of patients who may not be individually identified before presenting
Wholesale dealing
Selling or supplying medicines to someone who will sell, supply or administer them in the course of a business
Dispensing label
The label on a dispensed medicine with the particulars required by the Human Medicines Regulations 2012
Controlled drug (CD)
A drug controlled under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 because of its potential for misuse
CD prescription requirements
The additional particulars required by the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001 for Schedule 2 and 3 CDs
CD register
The legally required record of receipts and supplies of Schedule 2 controlled drugs
Safe custody
Storage of specified CDs in a locked receptacle meeting the Misuse of Drugs (Safe Custody) Regulations 1973

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which law governs the classification and supply of medicines?

The Human Medicines Regulations 2012. From “The legal framework for medicines” →

In plain English: Medicines in the UK are governed by a small number of key laws. Key idea: Human Medicines Regulations 2012 for medicines generally; Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001 for controlled drugs; the GPhC (Great Britain) and PSNI (Northern Ireland) for professionals and premises.

How soon must a prescriber provide the prescription after requesting an emergency supply?

Within 72 hours. From “Emergency supply” →

In plain English: The law allows a pharmacist to supply a prescription-only medicine without a prescription in an emergency, at the request of a prescriber or of the patient, if strict conditions are met. Key idea: Patient-requested emergency supply needs an interview, immediate need, previous treatment and an appropriate dose; maximum 30 days, and no Schedule 1 to 3 CDs except phenobarbital for epilepsy (5 days).

Which schedule are gabapentin and pregabalin in?

Schedule 3. From “The Misuse of Drugs Regulations: schedules” →

In plain English: Controlled drugs are divided into five schedules under the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. Key idea: Schedule 2 has the strictest healthcare controls (register, safe custody, witnessed destruction); Schedule 3 needs CD prescription requirements; Schedules 4 and 5 have fewer.

How long can the RP be absent during business hours?

Up to 2 hours, remaining contactable. From “The responsible pharmacist” →

In plain English: A registered pharmacy can only operate with a responsible pharmacist (RP) in charge. Key idea: The RP must sign in, display a notice, keep the RP record and ensure procedures; absence is limited to 2 hours and no P or POM supply happens while they're away.

Why record near misses?

They reveal system weaknesses before harm occurs. From “Clinical governance” →

In plain English: Clinical governance is the system that keeps pharmacy care safe and continually improving: procedures, audit, incident learning, staff training and patient feedback. Key idea: Clinical governance combines SOPs, incident learning, audit, training, feedback and information governance.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which body licenses medicines in the UK? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The MHRA. The MHRA grants marketing authorisations and regulates medicines' safety.

  2. Which class of medicine can be sold from a supermarket? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    General sale list. GSL medicines can be sold from non-pharmacy lockable premises.

  3. How long is a prescription for a Schedule 4 controlled drug valid for? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    28 days. Prescriptions for Schedule 2, 3 and 4 CDs are valid for 28 days.

  4. Which legislation sets out the schedules of controlled drugs for healthcare use? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. The 2001 Regulations, made under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, set the five schedules.

  5. A pharmacist plans to work in Belfast. Which regulator do they register with? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The PSNI. Pharmacy in Northern Ireland is regulated by the Pharmaceutical Society of Northern Ireland.

  6. What is the largest pharmacy pack of paracetamol tablets normally sold? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    32. Pharmacy packs go up to 32; larger quantities only in exceptional, justified circumstances.

  7. A patient asks for an emergency supply of their morphine modified-release tablets. What applies? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The law doesn't allow it: morphine is Schedule 2. Schedule 1 to 3 CDs can't be supplied in an emergency at a patient's request, except phenobarbital for epilepsy.

  8. Which requirement applies to Schedule 2 but not Schedule 3 CDs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Entry in the CD register. CD registers are required for Schedule 2; the prescription requirements apply to both.

  9. For how long is a prescription for oxycodone valid? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    28 days. Schedule 2, 3 and 4 CD prescriptions are valid for 28 days from the appropriate date.

Key takeaways

  • Human Medicines Regulations 2012 for medicines generally; Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001 for controlled drugs; the GPhC (Great Britain) and PSNI (Northern Ireland) for professionals and premises.
  • POM needs a prescription (or another legal authority); P needs a pharmacy and pharmacist supervision; GSL can be sold more widely, with limits.
  • Check the signature, prescriber details, date, patient name and address (and age under 12), and that the prescription is within its validity period.
  • Independent prescribers decide on treatment within their competence; supplementary prescribers work within a clinical management plan.
  • Patient-requested emergency supply needs an interview, immediate need, previous treatment and an appropriate dose; maximum 30 days, and no Schedule 1 to 3 CDs except phenobarbital for epilepsy (5 days).
  • A PGD covers groups of unidentified patients under strict criteria, for named competent registered professionals; a PSD is for a named patient.

Common mistakes in Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance

  • Assuming the GPhC regulates pharmacy in Northern Ireland: that's the PSNI.
  • Assuming a product is P because the medicine is P: the same drug can be POM at a higher strength or larger pack.
  • Supplying a Schedule 4 controlled drug on a prescription dated over 28 days ago, treating it like an ordinary 6-month POM prescription.
  • Assuming any independent prescriber can prescribe any controlled drug for addiction: diamorphine, dipipanone and cocaine for addiction need a Home Office licence.
  • Making an emergency supply of a Schedule 2 controlled drug such as morphine at a patient's request: the law doesn't allow it.
  • Supplying under a PGD to someone who meets an exclusion criterion, or working under a PGD you haven't been authorised to use.

Lessons in this school

  1. The legal framework for medicines
    Medicines in the UK are governed by a small number of key laws.
  2. Classes of medicines: POM, P and GSL
    Every licensed medicine is classified as prescription-only, pharmacy or general sale.
  3. Prescriptions and prescription requirements
    A prescription is a legal authority to supply a prescription-only medicine.
  4. Who can prescribe
    Doctors and dentists aren't the only prescribers.
  5. Emergency supply
    The law allows a pharmacist to supply a prescription-only medicine without a prescription in an emergency, at the request of a prescriber or of the patient, if strict conditions are met.
  6. Patient group directions and patient specific directions
    Patient group directions (PGDs) let named, registered health professionals supply or administer specified medicines to groups of patients without a prescription.
  7. Wholesale dealing and supply
    Supplying medicines to other businesses or professionals for them to sell or use is wholesale dealing, which is regulated separately from supply to patients.
  8. Labelling requirements
    Dispensed medicines must carry a label with legally required information, plus advice that helps people take them safely.
  9. The Misuse of Drugs Regulations: schedules
    Controlled drugs are divided into five schedules under the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001.
  10. Controlled drug prescriptions
    Prescriptions for Schedule 2 and 3 controlled drugs have extra legal requirements designed to prevent misuse and forgery.
  11. Controlled drug registers and records
    Schedule 2 controlled drugs must be recorded in a register every time they are received or supplied.
  12. Safe custody and destruction
    Many controlled drugs must be kept in a locked cabinet that meets legal standards, and their destruction is tightly controlled.
  13. The responsible pharmacist
    A registered pharmacy can only operate with a responsible pharmacist (RP) in charge.
  14. Records and record keeping
    Pharmacies must keep various records for set periods, some by law and some as good practice.
  15. Fitness to practise
    The GPhC can act when a pharmacy professional's fitness to practise is impaired, to protect the public and maintain confidence in the profession.
  16. Safeguarding in pharmacy
    Pharmacy teams see people often and informally, so they may notice signs that a child or adult is at risk of abuse or neglect.
  17. Clinical governance
    Clinical governance is the system that keeps pharmacy care safe and continually improving: procedures, audit, incident learning, staff training and patient feedback.
  18. Pharmacy across the four nations
    Medicines law is largely UK-wide, but health services, funding, regulation and some pharmacy services differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  19. Ethics in pharmacy practice
    Many pharmacy decisions are ethical as well as legal: balancing a patient's wishes against their safety, respecting confidentiality, and handling conflicts with personal beliefs.
  20. Veterinary medicines in pharmacy
    Pharmacists can supply medicines for animals, which are governed by separate veterinary legislation with its own categories.

Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance: questions students ask

Who must supervise the sale of a P medicine?

A pharmacist, in a registered pharmacy.

How long is a POM prescription valid for?

6 months from the appropriate date.

What does a supplementary prescriber need?

A clinical management plan agreed with an independent prescriber (doctor or dentist) and the patient.

Who must sign a PGD?

A doctor or dentist and a pharmacist, with authorisation by the organisation.
