Key terms and formulas at a glance

Microgram
One thousandth of a milligram; written in full on prescriptions and labels, never abbreviated
Significant figures
The digits in a number that carry meaning about its precision, starting from the first non-zero digit
Proportion
Two equal ratios, such as 250 mg : 5 mL = 400 mg : x mL
% w/v
Grams of solute in 100 mL of the final solution
Ratio strength
A concentration written as 1 part in a number of parts, such as 1 in 1,000 w/v (1 g in 1,000 mL)
Millimole (mmol)
One thousandth of a mole; mmol = mass in mg ÷ relative molecular mass
C1V1 = C2V2
Concentration × volume before dilution equals concentration × volume after, because the amount of drug doesn't change
Alligation
A method for finding the parts of a stronger and a weaker preparation needed to make an intermediate strength
Total daily dose
The dose per administration multiplied by the number of times a day
Ideal body weight (IBW)
An estimate of lean weight from height and sex, used for some drugs in people with obesity
Body surface area (BSA)
The calculated surface area of the body in square metres (m²)
BNFC
The BNF for Children, the UK reference for children's doses by age, weight or BSA

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many micrograms are in 1 mg?

1,000 From “Units and conversions” →

In plain English: Almost every pharmaceutical calculation starts with units. Key idea: Check every answer's unit as well as its number. A thousandfold error usually comes from moving between milligrams and micrograms.

What is 1 in 1,000 w/v in mg/mL?

1 mg/mL From “Ratio strengths and parts per million” →

In plain English: Some products describe very dilute concentrations as ratios (1 in 1,000) or parts per million. Key idea: For w/v ratios, 1 in 1,000 is 1 mg/mL. Each extra zero in the ratio divides the concentration by 10.

Total daily dose of 250 mg four times a day?

1 g (1,000 mg) From “Dose calculations” →

In plain English: A dose calculation turns a prescription into what's measured and given, and what's supplied. Key idea: Separate the dose per administration, the frequency and the daily total, and check the result against the BNF.

How long does a 200-dose inhaler last at 2 puffs twice a day?

50 days From “Quantities to supply” →

In plain English: Working out how much to supply is everyday pharmacy arithmetic: tablets for a course, millilitres of a liquid, doses in an inhaler, or grams of a cream. Key idea: Quantity = dose × frequency × duration; for inhalers, divide the doses in the device by the doses used each day.

State the Cockcroft-Gault equation.

CrCl = (140 − age) × weight × constant (1.23 men, 1.04 women) ÷ serum creatinine (micromol/L). From “Estimating renal function” →

In plain English: Many drugs are cleared by the kidneys, so doses depend on renal function. Key idea: Use Cockcroft-Gault CrCl for DOACs and for narrow-margin renally cleared drugs, especially in older people and at extremes of weight.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pharmaceutical Calculations

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How many micrograms are in 2.5 mg? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    2,500 micrograms. 1 mg is 1,000 micrograms, so 2.5 mg is 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 micrograms.

  2. How many milligrams are in 0.04 g? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    40 mg. 1 g is 1,000 mg, so 0.04 g is 0.04 × 1,000 = 40 mg.

  3. What is 12.348 rounded to one decimal place? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    12.3. The second decimal place is 4, which is less than 5, so the first decimal place stays 3.

  4. Which way of writing a dose is safest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.5 mg. A leading zero prevents ".5" being read as 5; trailing zeros and abbreviations such as mcg cause misreading.

  5. In a two-step calculation, when is it best to round? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    At the end only. Rounding intermediate values carries errors into later steps; round the final answer.

  6. What is 0.006285 to two significant figures? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.0063. Count from the first non-zero digit (6): the next digit is 2, then 8 rounds it up to 3, giving 0.0063.

  7. How many mEq of calcium are in 2 mmol of calcium ions? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    4 mEq. Calcium is divalent: mEq = mmol × 2 = 4 mEq.

  8. A 1 in 50 stock is diluted 1 in 10. What is the final strength? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    1 in 500. Serial dilutions multiply: 50 × 10 = 500.

  9. Using the Devine formula, what is the ideal body weight of a man who is 5 ft 10 in? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    73 kg. He is 10 inches over 5 feet, so IBW = 50 + (2.3 × 10) = 73 kg.

Key takeaways

  • Check every answer's unit as well as its number. A thousandfold error usually comes from moving between milligrams and micrograms.
  • Carry full precision through the working; round once, at the end, to what's asked and to what can be measured.
  • Estimate before calculating. If the dose you want is more than the stock strength, the volume must be more than the stock volume, and vice versa.
  • For w/v, percentage × 10 = mg/mL. Always check whether a percentage is w/v, w/w or v/v before using it.
  • For w/v ratios, 1 in 1,000 is 1 mg/mL. Each extra zero in the ratio divides the concentration by 10.
  • mmol = mg ÷ Mr. Know which ions each formula unit releases, and multiply by the valency for mEq.

Common mistakes in Pharmaceutical Calculations

  • Abbreviating micrograms as "mcg" or "µg" on a label or record: the convention in UK practice is to write it in full.
  • Rounding an intermediate step (for example a concentration) and then using the rounded figure in the next step.
  • Inverting the ratio (dividing the stock by the dose), which gives an answer that is wrong by a large factor.
  • Treating a w/w cream strength as if it were w/v, or forgetting that the percentage refers to the final product, not the solvent.
  • Confusing 1 in 1,000 with 1 in 10,000 adrenaline: a tenfold error. Read the mg/mL strength on the label.
  • Forgetting that one unit of calcium chloride releases two chloride ions, or treating mEq and mmol as the same for divalent ions.

Lessons in this school

  1. Units and conversions
    Almost every pharmaceutical calculation starts with units.
  2. Significant figures and rounding
    Rounding decides whether a calculated dose can actually be measured and given.
  3. Ratio and proportion
    Most dose and volume calculations are proportions: if a known amount of drug is in a known volume, how much volume holds the dose you need?
  4. Percentage concentrations
    Percentage strengths describe how much drug is in a product.
  5. Ratio strengths and parts per million
    Some products describe very dilute concentrations as ratios (1 in 1,000) or parts per million.
  6. Molarity, millimoles and milliequivalents
    Electrolytes and many infusions are prescribed in millimoles, not milligrams.
  7. Dilutions
    Diluting a stock solution is routine in pharmacy, from making up preparations to preparing injections.
  8. Concentrating and mixing
    Sometimes you need a strength between two that you have, or you mix products of different strengths.
  9. Dose calculations
    A dose calculation turns a prescription into what's measured and given, and what's supplied.
  10. Weight-based dosing
    Many medicines are dosed per kilogram of body weight, especially in children, in critical care and for drugs with narrow therapeutic ranges.
  11. Body surface area dosing
    Cytotoxic chemotherapy and a few other medicines are dosed by body surface area (BSA), which tracks metabolic rate more closely than weight.
  12. Doses in children
    Children's doses come from the BNF for Children (BNFC), by age band, weight or BSA.
  13. Quantities to supply
    Working out how much to supply is everyday pharmacy arithmetic: tablets for a course, millilitres of a liquid, doses in an inhaler, or grams of a cream.
  14. Infusion rates
    Intravenous fluids and many medicines are given by infusion.
  15. Drug infusions in mg per hour
    Medicine infusions are prescribed as a dose rate (mg per hour, or micrograms per kg per minute).
  16. Displacement values
    When a powder is dissolved or a drug is incorporated into a suppository base, the drug itself takes up space.
  17. Estimating renal function
    Many drugs are cleared by the kidneys, so doses depend on renal function.
  18. Dose adjustment in renal impairment
    When kidney function falls, drugs cleared by the kidneys accumulate.
  19. Pharmacokinetic calculations
    A few simple pharmacokinetic equations explain loading doses, steady state and dosing intervals, and they appear in exams and in therapeutic drug monitoring.
  20. Calculations in the registration assessment
    The GPhC registration assessment has a calculations paper, and calculation errors are a common reason candidates struggle.
