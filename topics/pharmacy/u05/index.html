Key terms and formulas at a glance

Dosage form
The physical form in which a drug is given, such as a tablet, solution or cream
Solubility
The maximum amount of a substance that dissolves in a solvent at a given temperature
Micronisation
Reduction of particles to a few micrometres, often by jet milling
Viscosity
A fluid's resistance to flow, measured in pascal seconds (Pa·s)
Surfactant
A molecule with a water-loving head and a fat-loving tail that adsorbs at interfaces
Cosolvent
A water-miscible solvent, such as ethanol or propylene glycol, added to improve drug solubility
Stokes' law
v = 2r²(ρs − ρl)g ÷ 9η, giving the sedimentation velocity of a particle
Emulsion
A dispersion of droplets of one liquid in another immiscible liquid, stabilised by an emulsifying agent
Ointment
A greasy, usually anhydrous semisolid that is occlusive and emollient
Granulation
Enlarging powder particles into granules to improve flow and compression
Hard capsule
A two-piece shell, usually filled with powder or pellets
Delayed release
Release that starts after a delay, such as enteric-coated products that release in the intestine

Five sample flashcards

What is preformulation?

Study of a drug's physical and chemical properties before designing a dosage form. From “Introduction to pharmaceutics” →

In plain English: Pharmaceutics is the science of turning a drug into a medicine that patients can take safely and effectively. Key idea: Pharmaceutics turns drugs into medicines; dosage forms aim to deliver the right dose, stay stable, be acceptable and be manufacturable, and the form strongly affects clinical behaviour.

Which HLB range favours oil-in-water emulsions?

High HLB, about 8 to 18. From “Surfactants and interfacial phenomena” →

In plain English: Surfactants gather at surfaces and interfaces and lower surface tension. Key idea: Surfactants are amphiphilic and may be anionic, cationic, non-ionic or amphoteric; above the CMC they form micelles that solubilise drugs, and the HLB scale guides emulsifier choice.

What is the role of a disintegrant?

To help the tablet break apart in fluid. From “Tablets” →

In plain English: Tablets are the most widely used dosage form. Key idea: Tablets are made by wet or dry granulation or direct compression, with diluents, binders, disintegrants, lubricants and glidants; tests check mass, content, disintegration, dissolution, friability and strength.

Why must intrathecal products be preservative free?

Preservatives can be toxic to nerve tissue. From “Parenteral products” →

In plain English: Parenteral products are given by injection or infusion, bypassing the gut. Key idea: Parenteral products bypass the gut by IV, IM, SC and other routes; they must be sterile, pyrogen-free, particle-free and ideally isotonic, and wrong-route and compatibility errors must be prevented.

How do fatty suppository bases release drug?

They melt at body temperature. From “Rectal and vaginal dosage forms” →

In plain English: Rectal and vaginal dosage forms deliver drugs locally or systemically when oral use is unsuitable. Key idea: Suppositories use fatty or water-soluble bases, enemas and pessaries treat local and systemic conditions, the lower rectum partly avoids first-pass metabolism, and patients need clear, private counselling.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which branch of pharmaceutics studies how dosage forms affect absorption? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Biopharmaceutics. Biopharmaceutics links the dosage form to the rate and extent of absorption.

  2. What does "practically insoluble" mean in pharmacopoeial terms? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    More than 10,000 parts of solvent are needed per part of solute. Pharmacopoeial terms give the parts of solvent needed per part of solute.

  3. Which method is commonly used to measure particle size distribution? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Laser diffraction. Laser diffraction measures how particles scatter light to give a size distribution.

  4. Which is an aim of dosage form design? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Keeping the drug stable until it is used. Stability is a key aim alongside dose accuracy and acceptability.

  5. According to the Noyes-Whitney equation, which change increases dissolution rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Increasing the surface area of the particles. Rate is proportional to surface area and to (Cs − C).

  6. What does segregation in a powder blend cause? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Poor content uniformity between doses. Separated particles give uneven drug content between units.

  7. Why may a modified-release and an immediate-release tablet of the same drug not be swapped dose for dose? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their release rates and peak levels differ. Release profile changes peak concentration and duration of action.

  8. Which approach is used to improve dissolution of a poorly soluble weak acid? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Forming a sodium salt. Salts of weak acids dissolve faster than the free acid.

  9. A powder has a Hausner ratio of 1.45. What does this suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Poor flow that may need granulation or a glidant. Ratios well above 1.25 indicate poor, cohesive flow.

Key takeaways

  • Pharmaceutics turns drugs into medicines; dosage forms aim to deliver the right dose, stay stable, be acceptable and be manufacturable, and the form strongly affects clinical behaviour.
  • Solubility is the maximum amount that dissolves; dissolution rate rises with surface area and solubility as the Noyes-Whitney equation shows; salts, polymorphs, micronisation, surfactants and solid dispersions help poorly soluble drugs.
  • Particle size is reduced by milling and measured by sieving, microscopy or laser diffraction; flow is assessed by angle of repose, Carr's index and Hausner ratio, and poor flow causes variable doses.
  • Newtonian fluids have constant viscosity; pseudoplastic fluids thin with shear, plastic systems need a yield value, dilatant systems thicken, and thixotropic systems thin with shaking and recover at rest.
  • Surfactants are amphiphilic and may be anionic, cationic, non-ionic or amphoteric; above the CMC they form micelles that solubilise drugs, and the HLB scale guides emulsifier choice.
  • Solutions contain dissolved drug in a vehicle with buffers, cosolvents, sweeteners and preservatives; they absorb quickly and dose flexibly but are bulky, less stable and prone to taste and microbial problems.

Common mistakes in Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms

  • Treating different formulations of the same drug as interchangeable. Release rate, bioavailability and dose can differ.
  • Confusing solubility with dissolution rate. Solubility is how much can dissolve; dissolution rate is how fast it does.
  • Assuming smaller particles are always better. Very fine powders dissolve faster but often flow and handle poorly.
  • Assuming viscosity is fixed. Many pharmaceutical products change viscosity with shaking, rubbing or temperature.
  • Assuming surfactants are inert. They can irritate tissues, cause hypersensitivity and inactivate preservatives.
  • Measuring liquid doses with household spoons. These vary widely in volume and cause dosing errors.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to pharmaceutics
    Pharmaceutics is the science of turning a drug into a medicine that patients can take safely and effectively.
  2. Solubility and dissolution
    A drug must dissolve before it can be absorbed.
  3. Particle size and powders
    Most medicines start as powders.
  4. Rheology
    Rheology is the study of how materials flow and deform.
  5. Surfactants and interfacial phenomena
    Surfactants gather at surfaces and interfaces and lower surface tension.
  6. Solutions
    Solutions contain a drug fully dissolved in a liquid.
  7. Suspensions
    A suspension contains undissolved drug particles dispersed in a liquid.
  8. Emulsions
    An emulsion is a mixture of two immiscible liquids, with one dispersed as droplets in the other.
  9. Creams, ointments and gels
    Semisolid preparations deliver drugs to the skin and mucous membranes.
  10. Tablets
    Tablets are the most widely used dosage form.
  11. Capsules
    Capsules enclose a drug in a shell, usually of gelatin.
  12. Modified-release dosage forms
    Modified-release products change when or how fast a drug is released.
  13. Excipients
    Excipients are the ingredients other than the active drug.
  14. Parenteral products
    Parenteral products are given by injection or infusion, bypassing the gut.
  15. Sterile products and aseptic manufacture
    Sterile products must be free from viable microorganisms.
  16. Ophthalmic preparations
    Eye preparations must be sterile and comfortable, and they must overcome the eye's rapid tear turnover.
  17. Inhaled dosage forms
    Inhaled medicines deliver drugs straight to the lungs.
  18. Transdermal and topical delivery
    Transdermal patches deliver drugs through the skin into the bloodstream over hours or days.
  19. Rectal and vaginal dosage forms
    Rectal and vaginal dosage forms deliver drugs locally or systemically when oral use is unsuitable.
  20. Extemporaneous preparation
    Extemporaneous preparation means making a medicine in the pharmacy for an individual patient.
  21. Packaging and storage
    Packaging protects medicines from light, moisture, air, contamination and damage, and helps patients use them correctly.
  22. Pharmaceutical microbiology and preservation
    Microorganisms can spoil medicines and harm patients.
  23. Manufacturing and quality assurance
    Quality assurance is the whole system that makes sure every medicine is fit for its purpose.

Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms: questions students ask

How does reducing particle size increase dissolution rate?

It increases the surface area in contact with the fluid.

What is micronisation?

Reducing particles to a few micrometres, often by jet milling.

What is a Newtonian fluid?

A fluid whose viscosity stays constant at all shear rates.

What happens above the critical micelle concentration?

Surfactant molecules form micelles.
