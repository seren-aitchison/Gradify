Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define pharmacodynamics.

The study of what a drug does to the body: its targets, mechanisms and effects. From “What pharmacology is” →

In plain English: Pharmacology is the study of how drugs affect the body and how the body handles drugs. Key idea: Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug. Both decide what a dose achieves.

Name three narrow therapeutic index drugs.

For example digoxin, lithium and warfarin (also phenytoin, theophylline, aminoglycosides). From “Dose-response relationships” →

In plain English: As the dose of a drug increases, the response increases until it reaches a maximum. Key idea: The dose-response curve flattens near the maximum, so higher doses add more harm than benefit. Narrow therapeutic index drugs need monitoring.

Drug card: Omeprazole. Key adverse effects, cautions and monitoring?

Adverse effects and cautions: Headache, diarrhoea; with long-term use, Clostridioides difficile infection risk, low magnesium and fracture risk Monitoring and counselling: Review long-term need and step down where possible; taken before food; interacts with clopidogrel through CYP2C19 (check the BNF) From “Enzymes as drug targets” →

In plain English: Many medicines work by inhibiting an enzyme, stopping the production of a harmful product or the breakdown of a useful one. Key idea: Irreversible inhibitors (aspirin, PPIs) act long after the drug has gone; reversible ones act while the drug is present.

Drug card: Salbutamol. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Short-acting β2-adrenoceptor agonist Uses: Relief of bronchospasm in asthma and COPD From “Adrenergic pharmacology” →

In plain English: Adrenoceptors (α1, α2, β1, β2, β3) respond to noradrenaline and adrenaline. Key idea: β1 is the heart, β2 the airways, α1 vessels and prostate. Non-selective β-blockers risk bronchospasm; all can mask hypoglycaemia.

Name three enzyme inducers.

For example rifampicin, carbamazepine and St John's wort (also phenytoin). From “Mechanisms of drug interactions” →

In plain English: Drug interactions happen when one drug changes the effect or level of another. Key idea: Pharmacokinetic interactions change levels (absorption, metabolism, excretion, transport); pharmacodynamic ones add or oppose effects.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pharmacology: How Drugs Act

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does pharmacokinetics describe? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    What the body does to a drug. Pharmacokinetics covers absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion.

  2. Which drug inhibits an enzyme? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Omeprazole. Omeprazole inhibits the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (proton pump), an enzyme.

  3. Which drug has a narrow therapeutic index? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Lithium. Lithium's toxic levels are close to its therapeutic range, so levels are monitored.

  4. Why are non-selective β-blockers avoided in asthma? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Blocking β2 receptors can cause bronchospasm. β2 receptors mediate bronchodilation, so blocking them can trigger bronchospasm.

  5. Which drug acts by a physical or chemical means, not a protein target? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An antacid. Antacids neutralise acid chemically; the others act on receptors, enzymes or transporters.

  6. Sertraline acts mainly on which kind of target? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A transporter. It blocks the serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT).

  7. Why does prednisolone take hours to reduce inflammation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It acts on nuclear receptors to change gene transcription. Nuclear receptor effects depend on new gene transcription and protein synthesis.

  8. Aspirin's antiplatelet effect lasts for days after a single dose. Why? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It irreversibly inhibits platelet cyclo-oxygenase. Platelets have no nucleus to make new COX, so the effect lasts their lifespan (about 7 to 10 days).

  9. Why do loop diuretics produce more diuresis than thiazides at any dose? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Loop diuretics have greater efficacy. Their maximum effect (Emax) is higher, which reflects efficacy, not potency.

Key takeaways

  • Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug. Both decide what a dose achieves.
  • Group drugs by target: receptor, enzyme, ion channel or transporter. Side effects often come from the same target elsewhere, or other targets at higher doses.
  • Ion channel receptors act in milliseconds, GPCRs in seconds, kinase-linked receptors in minutes to hours, and nuclear receptors in hours.
  • Agonists activate; antagonists block. Competitive antagonism can be overcome by more agonist; irreversible antagonism can't.
  • The dose-response curve flattens near the maximum, so higher doses add more harm than benefit. Narrow therapeutic index drugs need monitoring.
  • Potency is about dose size; efficacy is about the maximum effect. A more potent drug isn't automatically more effective.

Common mistakes in Pharmacology: How Drugs Act

  • Learning drugs as lists of names and doses without their mechanisms: mechanisms are what let you predict effects, interactions and harms.
  • Assuming a "selective" drug has no effect on other targets: selectivity is a matter of degree.
  • Expecting an oral corticosteroid to work as quickly as an inhaled bronchodilator: nuclear receptor effects take hours.
  • Assuming one dose of naloxone is enough after a long-acting opioid overdose: its effect can wear off first.
  • Increasing a dose that's already near the top of its dose-response curve and expecting more benefit.
  • Assuming "stronger" (more potent) means "works better": a less potent drug at an adequate dose can work just as well.

Lessons in this school

  1. What pharmacology is
    Pharmacology is the study of how drugs affect the body and how the body handles drugs.
  2. Drug targets
    Most drugs work by binding to a protein in the body and changing what it does.
  3. Receptors and receptor families
    Receptors are the body's sensors for its own signals, such as hormones and neurotransmitters.
  4. Agonists and antagonists
    A drug that binds a receptor either activates it (an agonist) or prevents activation (an antagonist).
  5. Dose-response relationships
    As the dose of a drug increases, the response increases until it reaches a maximum.
  6. Affinity, efficacy and potency
    Three terms describe how a drug interacts with its receptor: affinity (how well it binds), efficacy (how well it activates) and potency (how much is needed).
  7. Tolerance, desensitisation and dependence
    Repeated exposure to a drug can reduce its effect (tolerance) and lead the body to adapt so that stopping causes withdrawal (dependence).
  8. G protein-coupled receptors and second messengers
    G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest family of drug targets.
  9. Ion channels as drug targets
    Ion channels control the electrical activity of nerves, muscle and the heart.
  10. Enzymes as drug targets
    Many medicines work by inhibiting an enzyme, stopping the production of a harmful product or the breakdown of a useful one.
  11. Transporters as drug targets
    Transporters carry ions and molecules across membranes, against gradients if they use energy.
  12. Nuclear receptors
    Nuclear receptors sit inside the cell and act as transcription factors when a hormone or drug binds.
  13. The autonomic nervous system
    The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions: heart rate, blood pressure, airways, gut, bladder and glands.
  14. Cholinergic pharmacology
    Drugs that mimic, block or prolong acetylcholine treat conditions from overactive bladder to myasthenia gravis and dementia.
  15. Adrenergic pharmacology
    Adrenoceptors (α1, α2, β1, β2, β3) respond to noradrenaline and adrenaline.
  16. Histamine and its antagonists
    Histamine is released from mast cells in allergy and from stomach cells to drive acid secretion.
  17. Eicosanoids and inflammation
    Prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes are lipid mediators made from arachidonic acid.
  18. Neurotransmitters in the brain
    Psychiatric and neurological medicines act on a handful of brain transmitter systems: dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA, glutamate and acetylcholine.
  19. Adverse drug reactions
    Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are a major cause of hospital admission.
  20. Mechanisms of drug interactions
    Drug interactions happen when one drug changes the effect or level of another.
  21. Drug toxicity and overdose
    Toxicity can follow overdose, accumulation or interactions.
  22. Pharmacogenomics
    Genetic differences change how people respond to some medicines, by altering drug-metabolising enzymes or targets, or by increasing the risk of serious reactions.
  23. Drug development and clinical trials
    A new medicine takes over a decade to move from discovery to licence, through preclinical studies and phased clinical trials, then regulatory review and post-marketing surveillance.
  24. Biological medicines and biosimilars
    Biological medicines are large, complex molecules made in living cells, such as monoclonal antibodies, insulins and vaccines.

Pharmacology: How Drugs Act: questions students ask

What three questions does pharmacokinetics ask?

How much drug reaches its target, for how long, and how it's removed.

Which family of target does amlodipine act on?

Ion channels (L-type calcium channels).

Where do benzodiazepines act?

The GABA-A receptor, a ligand-gated ion channel.

Drug card: Naloxone. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Competitive opioid receptor antagonist, mainly at μ receptors Uses: Reversal of opioid overdose and opioid-induced respiratory depression
