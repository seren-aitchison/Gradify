Key terms and formulas at a glance

Clinical check
A pharmacist's assessment that a prescribed medicine is appropriate for the individual: indication, dose, interactions, contraindications and monitoring
Medicines reconciliation
Obtaining an accurate list of a person's current medicines and comparing it with prescribed medicines at a transfer of care
Clinically significant interaction
An interaction likely to cause harm or loss of effect for this person, given the drugs, doses and their circumstances
Causality assessment
Judging how likely a drug caused a reaction, using timing, known effects, dechallenge and rechallenge
Yellow Card scheme
The MHRA's system for reporting suspected adverse drug reactions and other problems with medicines and medical devices
Clinical intervention
An action by a pharmacist to resolve or prevent a medicines-related problem
Medicines optimisation
A person-centred approach to safe and effective medicines use to get the best outcomes
Adherence
The extent to which a person's medicine-taking matches the agreed recommendations
Structured medication review (SMR)
A comprehensive, shared decision-making review of all a person's medicines, their benefits, harms and the person's goals
Decision aid
A tool presenting options, benefits and harms in an accessible way to support a decision
Counselling
Giving information and advice to help a person use a medicine safely and effectively
WWHAM

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name five elements of a clinical check.

For example indication, dose, interactions, contraindications and monitoring. From “The clinical check” →

In plain English: Every prescription gets a clinical check before supply: is this medicine, at this dose, right for this person now? Key idea: Check indication, dose, interactions, contraindications and monitoring for this person, using their records and conversation; intervene before supply if needed.

What is a prescribing cascade?

When a side effect of one drug is treated as a new condition with another drug. From “Identifying and managing adverse drug reactions” →

In plain English: Many symptoms people report are adverse drug reactions (ADRs). Key idea: Ask about timing, known effects, other causes and dechallenge; record allergies accurately, avoid prescribing cascades, and report through Yellow Card.

What are the STOPP/START criteria?

Tools to identify potentially inappropriate and omitted medicines in older people. From “Medicines optimisation” →

In plain English: Medicines optimisation looks at the whole of a person's medicines and their outcomes, not single prescriptions. Key idea: Look at the whole list and the person's goals: keep what helps, stop what harms or burdens, and monitor outcomes.

Name three high-risk medicines that need specific counselling.

For example warfarin, methotrexate and insulin (also opioids and lithium). From “Counselling on medicines” →

In plain English: Good counselling helps people take medicines safely and effectively, and know what to do if something goes wrong. Key idea: Cover purpose, use, side effects, red flags, interactions, monitoring and missed doses; check understanding with teach-back.

Which scoring tool is used for sore throat?

FeverPAIN. From “Pharmacy First and common clinical conditions” →

In plain English: In England, the Pharmacy First service lets community pharmacists assess and treat seven common conditions, including supplying some prescription-only medicines under patient group directions. Key idea: Pharmacy First covers seven conditions with age limits, national pathways and PGDs; follow gateway criteria, refer when indicated, and record.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which question belongs to the clinical rather than the legal check? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Is the dose right for this person's kidneys?. Renal dosing is part of the clinical check; the others are legal requirements.

  2. How soon does NICE recommend reconciliation after hospital admission? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Within 24 hours. NICE NG5 recommends reconciliation within 24 hours of admission.

  3. Who can submit a Yellow Card report? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Anyone, including patients. Patients and carers can report as well as professionals.

  4. A repeat prescription arrives for lithium; the last level was 9 months ago. What's the best action? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Contact the prescriber about overdue monitoring. Lithium needs regular monitoring; overdue tests should be raised with the prescriber.

  5. What information often comes only from talking to the person? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Medicines bought elsewhere and how they take them. Records don't show OTC use or actual use.

  6. Which omission on admission is most time-critical? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Levodopa for Parkinson's disease. Missed Parkinson's doses can cause rapid deterioration.

  7. A medicine on the GP list isn't in the person's own supply. What should you do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Find out whether it was stopped, and why, before continuing it. It may have been stopped deliberately; confirm with the person and the GP.

  8. Which combination carries a risk of serotonin syndrome? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Tramadol with sertraline. Both have serotonergic effects, which add together.

  9. A rash appears two days after starting a new drug and settles when it's stopped. What does this suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A probable ADR, supported by timing and dechallenge. Temporal association and positive dechallenge support causality.

Key takeaways

  • Check indication, dose, interactions, contraindications and monitoring for this person, using their records and conversation; intervene before supply if needed.
  • Use at least two sources, find discrepancies, resolve them with the prescriber and document; omissions of critical medicines are the biggest risk.
  • Judge each interaction for this person; then avoid, adjust, monitor or advise, and record what you did.
  • Ask about timing, known effects, other causes and dechallenge; record allergies accurately, avoid prescribing cascades, and report through Yellow Card.
  • Report all suspected reactions to black triangle drugs and in children, and serious reactions to any drug, through Yellow Card; suspicion is enough.
  • Identify the problem, recommend a specific solution to the prescriber, agree the outcome and record it fully.

Common mistakes in Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation

  • Checking only that the dose is within BNF limits, without asking whether the medicine is right for this person.
  • Restarting a medicine that was deliberately stopped before admission, because it's still on an old list.
  • Contacting the prescriber about every flagged interaction, including trivial ones, so that the important ones lose impact.
  • Missing a prescribing cascade, where a new drug is added to treat an unrecognised side effect of another.
  • Not reporting because you aren't sure the drug was responsible.
  • Recording "spoke to GP, all fine" without the problem, the recommendation or the outcome.

Lessons in this school

  1. The clinical check
    Every prescription gets a clinical check before supply: is this medicine, at this dose, right for this person now?
  2. Medicines reconciliation
    Medicines reconciliation makes sure the list of medicines a person is taking is accurate when they move between care settings.
  3. Drug interactions in practice
    Interactions are common, but most aren't clinically important.
  4. Identifying and managing adverse drug reactions
    Many symptoms people report are adverse drug reactions (ADRs).
  5. Reporting adverse reactions: the Yellow Card scheme
    The Yellow Card scheme is how the MHRA collects reports of suspected adverse reactions and problems with medicines and devices.
  6. Interventions and documentation
    When a pharmacist identifies a problem and acts on it, that's an intervention.
  7. Medicines optimisation
    Medicines optimisation looks at the whole of a person's medicines and their outcomes, not single prescriptions.
  8. Adherence and concordance
    Many people don't take medicines as prescribed, for reasons that are often understandable.
  9. Structured medication reviews
    A structured medication review (SMR) is a comprehensive, person-centred review of all of a person's medicines.
  10. Shared decision-making
    Shared decision-making means the person and the professional decide together, using the evidence about benefits and harms and the person's own values.
  11. Counselling on medicines
    Good counselling helps people take medicines safely and effectively, and know what to do if something goes wrong.
  12. Responding to symptoms
    People often ask pharmacy teams for help with symptoms.
  13. Minor illness and referral
    Many minor illnesses can be managed with self-care and over-the-counter treatment, freeing GP and emergency capacity.
  14. Pharmacy First and common clinical conditions
    In England, the Pharmacy First service lets community pharmacists assess and treat seven common conditions, including supplying some prescription-only medicines under patient group directions.
  15. New Medicine Service and discharge services
    Two of the most effective community pharmacy services in England support people at moments of high risk: starting a new medicine, and leaving hospital.
  16. Hospital clinical pharmacy
    Hospital pharmacists work on wards and in specialist teams, making sure inpatients' medicines are safe and effective from admission to discharge.
  17. Transfers of care
    Moving between hospital, home, care homes and community services is when medicines errors and confusion are most likely.

Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation: questions students ask

What should you do if a clinical problem is found?

Resolve it with the prescriber before supplying, and record the intervention.

Why is methotrexate with trimethoprim dangerous?

Both are antifolates, so together they can cause severe bone marrow suppression.

For which medicines should all suspected reactions be reported?

Black triangle (▼) medicines (and all medicines in children).

What does SOAP stand for?

Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan.
