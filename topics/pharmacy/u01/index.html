Key terms and formulas at a glance

Pharmacist
A registered health professional expert in medicines, responsible for their safe and effective use
Community pharmacy
Pharmacies open to the public, providing dispensing, advice and NHS clinical services
Pharmacy technician
A registered professional (with the GPhC in Great Britain) who supplies medicines, manages dispensing and can take on clinical roles such as accuracy checking
General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC)
The independent regulator for pharmacy professionals and premises in Great Britain, established by the Pharmacy Order 2010
Standards for pharmacy professionals
The GPhC's nine standards that every registered pharmacy professional must meet
Person-centred care
Care that respects and responds to a person's preferences, needs and values, and involves them in decisions
ICE
Exploring the person's ideas, concerns and expectations
Health literacy
The ability to find, understand and use information to make decisions about health
Valid consent
Consent that is voluntary, informed and given by someone with capacity
Confidentiality
The duty to keep information about people private unless there is consent, a legal requirement or a justified public interest
Protected characteristic
One of the nine characteristics protected from discrimination by the Equality Act 2010
Accountability
Being answerable for your decisions and actions, and able to justify them

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is medicines optimisation?

A person-centred approach to safe and effective medicines use, to get the best outcomes. From “Introduction to pharmacy” →

In plain English: Pharmacy is the health profession that makes sure people get the most from their medicines safely. Key idea: Pharmacists are the medicines experts: making supply safe, advising people, optimising treatment and, increasingly, prescribing.

How many Standards for pharmacy professionals are there?

Nine. From “The standards for pharmacy professionals” →

In plain English: The GPhC's nine Standards for pharmacy professionals describe how every pharmacist and pharmacy technician is expected to practise. Key idea: Nine standards, from person-centred care to leadership, apply to every pharmacy professional, everywhere and at all times.

What are the three elements of valid consent?

Voluntary, informed, and given by someone with capacity. From “Consent in pharmacy practice” →

In plain English: Before any examination, treatment or service, a person must give valid consent. Key idea: Consent must be voluntary, informed and given with capacity; capacity is decision-specific and presumed in adults.

What is the duty of candour?

Being open and honest with people when things go wrong. From “Raising concerns and speaking up” →

In plain English: Pharmacy professionals must speak up when they have concerns about safety, or when things go wrong. Key idea: Speak up early about safety concerns, use escalation routes if needed, and be open and honest with people when things go wrong.

What replaced clinical commissioning groups in England in 2022?

Integrated care boards. From “The NHS and pharmacy services across the UK” →

In plain English: The NHS is organised differently in each UK nation, and pharmacy services and contracts differ too. Key idea: Each nation organises and funds pharmacy services differently; know the services available where you work.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Pharmacy Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which best describes medicines optimisation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Person-centred safe and effective use of medicines. Medicines optimisation focuses on safe, effective use that gets the best outcomes for the person, not on cost or volume.

  2. Which activity is typical of hospital pharmacy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Medicines reconciliation on admission. Hospital pharmacists reconcile medicines when people are admitted.

  3. Which team member is a registered professional? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A pharmacy technician. Pharmacy technicians are registered with the GPhC.

  4. What did the GPhC's 2021 education standards change for new pharmacists? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They register as independent prescribers. The standards prepare pharmacists to prescribe from registration.

  5. Which is part of a pharmacist's clinical check? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Is the medicine appropriate for this person?. The clinical check asks whether the medicine and dose are appropriate.

  6. Why are discharge referrals to community pharmacy valuable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Changes to medicines are explained and followed up. Errors and confusion are common after discharge.

  7. A 15-year-old asks for emergency contraception. What applies? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Assess Gillick competence and follow the Fraser guidelines and safeguarding duties. Under-16s can consent if competent; safeguarding must also be considered.

  8. Which Caldicott principle says sharing can be as important as protecting? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The duty to share information can be as important as the duty to protect confidentiality. The 2013 review added this principle: failing to share can harm people, so appropriate sharing for care and protection is part of good practice.

  9. A person with sight loss can't read their labels. What should the pharmacy do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Make a reasonable adjustment, such as large print or audio. The Equality Act 2010 requires reasonable adjustments, such as large-print or audio labels, so the person can use their medicines safely.

Key takeaways

  • Pharmacists are the medicines experts: making supply safe, advising people, optimising treatment and, increasingly, prescribing.
  • Pharmacists work in many sectors under the same standards; transitions between them are where medicines errors most often happen.
  • Delegate to trained, competent people under clear procedures; you remain accountable for the decision to delegate.
  • The GPhC protects the public by setting standards, registering, inspecting and acting on concerns; the RPS leads and supports the profession.
  • Nine standards, from person-centred care to leadership, apply to every pharmacy professional, everywhere and at all times.
  • Find out what matters to the person and involve them in decisions; respect their choices, including a decision not to take a medicine.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Pharmacy Practice

  • Thinking of pharmacy as only dispensing: most of the profession's value is in clinical judgement and advice.
  • Assuming another sector "knows" about a change in medicines: communicate changes at every transition.
  • Delegating a task to someone who hasn't been trained for it because the pharmacy is busy.
  • Thinking the GPhC represents pharmacists' interests: it regulates in the public interest.
  • Treating the standards as applying only during working hours: behaviour outside work can affect trust in the profession.
  • Assuming non-adherence is carelessness without asking about side effects, beliefs or practical problems.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to pharmacy
    Pharmacy is the health profession that makes sure people get the most from their medicines safely.
  2. Pharmacy across sectors
    Pharmacists work in community pharmacy, hospitals, general practice, care homes, industry and beyond.
  3. The pharmacy team
    Pharmacists work with pharmacy technicians, dispensing assistants and medicines counter assistants, each with defined training and roles.
  4. The GPhC and professional regulation
    The General Pharmaceutical Council regulates pharmacists, pharmacy technicians and registered pharmacies in Great Britain.
  5. The standards for pharmacy professionals
    The GPhC's nine Standards for pharmacy professionals describe how every pharmacist and pharmacy technician is expected to practise.
  6. Person-centred care
    Person-centred care means treating each person as an individual with their own values, preferences and circumstances, and involving them in decisions.
  7. Communication and consultation skills
    Good consultations make care safer and more effective.
  8. Health literacy and adapting information
    Health literacy is a person's ability to find, understand and use health information.
  9. Consent in pharmacy practice
    Before any examination, treatment or service, a person must give valid consent.
  10. Confidentiality and information governance
    People share sensitive information with pharmacy teams on the understanding that it will be kept confidential.
  11. Equality, diversity and inclusion
    Health outcomes differ between groups, and pharmacy teams can reduce or widen those gaps.
  12. Professional judgement and accountability
    Pharmacy is full of decisions that rules alone can't settle: whether to supply, refer, query or intervene.
  13. Raising concerns and speaking up
    Pharmacy professionals must speak up when they have concerns about safety, or when things go wrong.
  14. Patient safety and learning from errors
    Medication errors are common and many are preventable.
  15. Evidence-based practice
    Evidence-based practice combines the best research evidence with clinical expertise and the person's values.
  16. Reflective practice and CPD
    Reflection turns experience into learning, and continuing professional development (CPD) keeps knowledge and skills current.
  17. The NHS and pharmacy services across the UK
    The NHS is organised differently in each UK nation, and pharmacy services and contracts differ too.
  18. Public health and health promotion
    Pharmacies are in the heart of communities and see healthy people as well as unwell ones.
  19. Working in multidisciplinary teams
    Safe care depends on professionals working together.
  20. Digital pharmacy and technology
    Technology is reshaping pharmacy: electronic prescriptions, shared records, automation, online pharmacy and artificial intelligence.

Foundations of Pharmacy Practice: questions students ask

Where do medicines errors cluster?

At transitions of care, such as admission and discharge.

Who is accountable when a task is delegated?

The delegator for the decision to delegate; the delegate for doing it properly.

Which law established the GPhC?

The Pharmacy Order 2010.

Which standard covers raising concerns?

Standard 8: speak up when they have concerns or when things go wrong.
