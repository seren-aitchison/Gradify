Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which readings confirm a diagnosis of hypertension?

ABPM or home blood pressure averages of 135/85 mmHg or higher. From “Hypertension” →

In plain English: High blood pressure is the commonest treatable risk factor for heart attack, stroke, kidney disease and dementia. Key idea: Confirm raised clinic readings with ABPM or HBPM; treat to targets under NICE NG136, alongside lifestyle change.

Who is offered an NHS Health Check in England?

Adults aged 40 to 74 without existing conditions. From “Cardiovascular risk assessment” →

In plain English: Risk tools estimate a person's chance of a heart attack or stroke over 10 years, helping decide who benefits from treatment. Key idea: Use QRISK3 for primary prevention (not for people with existing disease, type 1 diabetes or CKD), and explain risk in absolute numbers.

Drug card: Bisoprolol. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Cardioselective beta-1 adrenoceptor blocker Uses: Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, angina, hypertension (not first line), atrial fibrillation rate control From “Chronic heart failure” →

In plain English: In heart failure the heart can't pump enough blood for the body's needs. Key idea: HFrEF: ACE inhibitor (or ARB or sacubitril-valsartan), beta-blocker, MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor, with loop diuretics for congestion; monitor potassium and renal function.

Which anticoagulants are first line for most people with VTE?

Apixaban or rivaroxaban (DOACs). From “Venous thromboembolism” →

In plain English: Venous thromboembolism (VTE), meaning deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, is common and can be fatal. Key idea: Treat VTE with a DOAC first line for at least 3 months; assess every hospital admission for VTE risk and give prophylaxis when indicated.

Drug card: Clopidogrel. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: P2Y12 inhibitor (irreversible; prodrug activated partly by CYP2C19) Uses: Secondary prevention after stroke or TIA, peripheral arterial disease, with aspirin after ACS or stents From “Antiplatelet drugs” →

In plain English: Antiplatelet drugs reduce arterial clots, which cause heart attacks and strokes. Key idea: Aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors prevent arterial clots; protect the stomach in at-risk people and avoid omeprazole with clopidogrel.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cardiovascular Therapeutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What confirms a diagnosis of hypertension after a raised clinic reading? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ambulatory or home blood pressure monitoring. NICE NG136 recommends ABPM or HBPM to confirm.

  2. Which enzyme do statins inhibit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    HMG-CoA reductase. Statins reduce cholesterol synthesis in the liver.

  3. Which group shouldn't have QRISK3 used to decide on statins? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    People who have had a stroke. Established cardiovascular disease means secondary prevention.

  4. What is the NICE clinic blood pressure target for an 84-year-old? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Below 150/90 mmHg. NICE sets a less strict target at 80 and over, adjusted for frailty.

  5. Which change most directly lowers blood pressure for many people? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reducing salt intake. Salt reduction is a key lifestyle measure.

  6. What is NICE's first step for a 62-year-old woman with hypertension and no diabetes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A calcium channel blocker. A CCB is first line at 55 and over without type 2 diabetes.

  7. Someone on amlodipine develops ankle swelling. A diuretic is suggested. What's best? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Review the amlodipine first. CCB oedema doesn't respond well to diuretics; adding one risks a prescribing cascade.

  8. A person on clopidogrel needs a PPI. Which is the best choice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lansoprazole. Omeprazole and esomeprazole reduce clopidogrel activation via CYP2C19.

  9. Someone with heart failure gains 2.5 kg in three days. What should they do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Contact their heart failure team or GP promptly. Rapid weight gain suggests fluid retention needing review.

Key takeaways

  • Confirm raised clinic readings with ABPM or HBPM; treat to targets under NICE NG136, alongside lifestyle change.
  • Choose step 1 by age, ethnicity and diabetes, then add classes in order; monitor ACE inhibitors and ARBs with U&Es.
  • Atorvastatin for primary prevention when QRISK3 is 10% or more; high-intensity statins and LDL or non-HDL targets for secondary prevention, then ezetimibe.
  • Use QRISK3 for primary prevention (not for people with existing disease, type 1 diabetes or CKD), and explain risk in absolute numbers.
  • GTN for attacks (sit down, repeat after 5 minutes, call 999 if pain persists 5 minutes later); beta-blocker or CCB to prevent; secondary prevention for all.
  • Chest pain suggesting a heart attack is a 999 emergency; after ACS, DAPT for up to 12 months then lifelong aspirin, with secondary prevention.

Common mistakes in Cardiovascular Therapeutics

  • Starting long-term treatment on a single raised clinic reading without confirming it with ambulatory or home readings.
  • Starting an ACE inhibitor without checking kidney function and potassium before and after.
  • Stopping a statin at the first mention of aches without assessing the symptoms or trying another statin or dose.
  • Calculating QRISK3 for someone who has already had a heart attack: they need secondary prevention regardless.
  • Supplying sildenafil to someone using GTN or another nitrate.
  • Advising someone with crushing chest pain to see their GP tomorrow instead of calling 999.

Lessons in this school

  1. Hypertension
    High blood pressure is the commonest treatable risk factor for heart attack, stroke, kidney disease and dementia.
  2. Antihypertensive drugs
    NICE's step-wise approach chooses the first drug by age, ethnicity and diabetes, then adds drugs from the main classes.
  3. Lipid management
    Lowering cholesterol, especially LDL and non-HDL cholesterol, reduces heart attacks and strokes.
  4. Cardiovascular risk assessment
    Risk tools estimate a person's chance of a heart attack or stroke over 10 years, helping decide who benefits from treatment.
  5. Stable angina
    Stable angina is chest pain on exertion from narrowed coronary arteries.
  6. Acute coronary syndromes
    Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) are emergencies: unstable angina and heart attacks (NSTEMI and STEMI).
  7. Secondary prevention after a heart attack
    After a heart attack, a set of medicines and lifestyle changes greatly reduces the risk of another event.
  8. Chronic heart failure
    In heart failure the heart can't pump enough blood for the body's needs.
  9. Atrial fibrillation
    Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the commonest heart rhythm disorder and a major cause of stroke.
  10. Antiarrhythmic drugs
    Antiarrhythmic drugs restore or maintain a normal rhythm, but many can also cause arrhythmias and have serious adverse effects.
  11. Venous thromboembolism
    Venous thromboembolism (VTE), meaning deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, is common and can be fatal.
  12. Anticoagulants
    Anticoagulants prevent and treat clots by acting on the clotting cascade.
  13. Warfarin management
    Warfarin has a narrow therapeutic range, many interactions and is affected by diet and illness, so it needs regular INR monitoring and careful counselling.
  14. Direct oral anticoagulants
    DOACs have largely replaced warfarin for atrial fibrillation and VTE.
  15. Antiplatelet drugs
    Antiplatelet drugs reduce arterial clots, which cause heart attacks and strokes.
  16. Stroke and transient ischaemic attack
    Stroke is a medical emergency: rapid recognition and treatment limit brain damage.
  17. Peripheral arterial disease
    Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) narrows the arteries to the legs, causing pain on walking and, in severe cases, threatening the limb.
  18. Cardiovascular adverse drug reactions
    Many drugs affect the heart and circulation: slowing the heart, prolonging the QT interval, causing fluid retention or raising potassium.

Cardiovascular Therapeutics: questions students ask

Drug card: Ramipril. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: ACE inhibitor: blocks conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II Uses: Hypertension, heart failure, after a heart attack, diabetic kidney disease

Drug card: Atorvastatin. Class, mechanism and main uses?

Class and mechanism: Statin: inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, lowering LDL cholesterol Uses: Primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease

Who should not have a QRISK3 calculation?

People with existing cardiovascular disease, type 1 diabetes, CKD or familial hypercholesterolaemia.

When should someone with angina call 999?

If pain persists 5 minutes after the second dose of GTN.
