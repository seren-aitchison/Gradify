Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to pharmacokinetics
    Pharmacokinetics describes how drug concentrations change over time as the body absorbs, distributes, metabolises and excretes a drug.
  2. Routes of administration
    The route of administration decides how quickly and how completely a drug reaches the circulation, and sometimes where it acts.
  3. Absorption and bioavailability
    Before an oral drug can act, it must dissolve and cross the gut wall, then survive the liver.
  4. First-pass metabolism
    Drugs absorbed from the gut pass through the gut wall and liver before reaching the rest of the body, and some are extensively metabolised on the way.
  5. Distribution and volume of distribution
    Once in the blood, drugs distribute into tissues to different extents.
  6. Plasma protein binding
    Many drugs bind to plasma proteins, mainly albumin.
  7. Drug metabolism: phase I and II
    Most drugs are metabolised to more water-soluble compounds that the kidneys can excrete.
  8. Cytochrome P450 enzymes
    The cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes carry out most phase I metabolism.
  9. Renal excretion
    The kidneys excrete many drugs and metabolites by filtration, tubular secretion and reabsorption.
  10. Biliary excretion and enterohepatic recycling
    Some drugs and metabolites are excreted into bile and pass into the gut.
  11. Clearance
    Clearance measures the body's ability to eliminate a drug.
  12. Half-life and the elimination rate constant
    Half-life is the time for a drug's concentration to halve.
  13. Steady state and accumulation
    With regular dosing, a drug accumulates until the amount eliminated each interval equals the amount given.
  14. Loading and maintenance doses
    A loading dose gets a drug to its effective concentration quickly; maintenance doses keep it there.
  15. Linear and non-linear kinetics
    For most drugs, concentration rises in proportion to dose (linear, first-order kinetics).
  16. Compartment models
    Pharmacokinetic models describe the body as one or more compartments.
  17. Therapeutic drug monitoring
    For some drugs, measuring blood levels guides dosing because the effective and toxic levels are close or response is hard to judge.
  18. Pharmacokinetics in renal impairment
    Kidney disease reduces the elimination of renally cleared drugs and their active metabolites, and can change absorption, distribution and protein binding.
  19. Pharmacokinetics in hepatic impairment
    Liver disease can reduce drug metabolism, change protein binding and increase sensitivity to some drugs.
  20. Biopharmaceutics and dissolution
    Biopharmaceutics links a formulation's physical properties to how the drug is absorbed.
  21. Bioequivalence
    Generic medicines are licensed when they're shown to be bioequivalent to the reference product.

Key terms

ADME
Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion: the four processes that determine drug concentrations
Enteral route
Administration via the gastrointestinal tract (oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal, via feeding tube)
Bioavailability (F)
The fraction of an administered dose reaching the systemic circulation unchanged
First-pass metabolism
Metabolism of a drug in the gut wall and liver before it reaches the systemic circulation
Volume of distribution (Vd)
The apparent volume into which a drug distributes: amount in the body ÷ plasma concentration
Free fraction
The proportion of drug in plasma that isn't bound to proteins and can act
Phase I metabolism
Reactions (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) that introduce or expose a functional group, often by cytochrome P450 enzymes
CYP inhibitor
A drug that reduces the activity of a CYP enzyme, raising levels of its substrates
Glomerular filtration
Passive filtration of unbound drug from plasma into the tubule
Biliary excretion
Active transport of drugs or metabolites from the liver into bile
Clearance (CL)
The volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time, such as litres per hour
Half-life (t½)
The time taken for the plasma concentration to fall by half during elimination
Steady state
The condition in which drug input equals elimination, so average concentrations stay constant
Loading dose
A larger initial dose to reach the target concentration quickly: target × Vd ÷ F
