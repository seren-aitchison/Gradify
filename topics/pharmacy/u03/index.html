Key terms and formulas at a glance

ADME
Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion: the four processes that determine drug concentrations
Enteral route
Administration via the gastrointestinal tract (oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal, via feeding tube)
Bioavailability (F)
The fraction of an administered dose reaching the systemic circulation unchanged
First-pass metabolism
Metabolism of a drug in the gut wall and liver before it reaches the systemic circulation
Volume of distribution (Vd)
The apparent volume into which a drug distributes: amount in the body ÷ plasma concentration
Free fraction
The proportion of drug in plasma that isn't bound to proteins and can act
Phase I metabolism
Reactions (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) that introduce or expose a functional group, often by cytochrome P450 enzymes
CYP inhibitor
A drug that reduces the activity of a CYP enzyme, raising levels of its substrates
Glomerular filtration
Passive filtration of unbound drug from plasma into the tubule
Biliary excretion
Active transport of drugs or metabolites from the liver into bile
Clearance (CL)
The volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time, such as litres per hour
Half-life (t½)
The time taken for the plasma concentration to fall by half during elimination

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does ADME stand for?

Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. From “Introduction to pharmacokinetics” →

In plain English: Pharmacokinetics describes how drug concentrations change over time as the body absorbs, distributes, metabolises and excretes a drug. Key idea: ADME determines drug concentrations; four parameters (bioavailability, volume of distribution, clearance, half-life) turn that into dosing decisions.

What does a very large Vd indicate?

Extensive distribution into and binding in tissues. From “Distribution and volume of distribution” →

In plain English: Once in the blood, drugs distribute into tissues to different extents. Key idea: Vd = amount in the body ÷ plasma concentration; a large Vd means tissue binding and a large loading dose.

Which drug reduces renal secretion of penicillin?

Probenecid. From “Renal excretion” →

In plain English: The kidneys excrete many drugs and metabolites by filtration, tubular secretion and reabsorption. Key idea: Filtration, secretion and reabsorption determine renal excretion; renally cleared drugs need dose adjustment as kidney function falls.

What determines the average steady-state level?

The dose rate, bioavailability and clearance. From “Steady state and accumulation” →

In plain English: With regular dosing, a drug accumulates until the amount eliminated each interval equals the amount given. Key idea: Time to steady state depends on half-life; the level depends on dose rate and clearance. Recheck levels only once a new steady state is reached.

Why isn't TDM used for warfarin?

Its effect is measured directly with the INR. From “Therapeutic drug monitoring” →

In plain English: For some drugs, measuring blood levels guides dosing because the effective and toxic levels are close or response is hard to judge. Key idea: Monitor narrow-margin drugs, and interpret every level with its timing, steady state, adherence, organ function and symptoms.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which process removes a drug from the body unchanged? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Excretion. Excretion (mainly renal or biliary) removes drug from the body; metabolism changes it chemically.

  2. Which route gives 100% bioavailability? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intravenous. IV administration delivers the whole dose into the circulation.

  3. Why is glyceryl trinitrate given sublingually for angina? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    To avoid extensive first-pass metabolism. Swallowed GTN is largely metabolised by the liver before reaching the circulation.

  4. Why might two people on the same dose have different drug levels? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Differences in kidney function, age or interactions. Organ function, age, genetics and interactions all change pharmacokinetics.

  5. Which two processes together are called elimination? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Metabolism and excretion. Elimination is the irreversible loss of drug by metabolism and excretion.

  6. Why are inhaled corticosteroids preferred to oral ones for asthma control? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They act locally with less systemic exposure. Inhaled delivery targets the airways and limits systemic side effects.

  7. A slower-dissolving formulation has the same AUC but a lower Cmax. What changed? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The rate, not the extent, of absorption. Same AUC means the same extent; a lower, later peak reflects slower absorption.

  8. Why might an aminoglycoside's Vd rise in a critically ill patient? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Fluid shifts expand the extracellular volume. Water-soluble drugs distribute into expanded extracellular fluid.

  9. A patient with low albumin has a total phenytoin level within range but signs of toxicity. Why? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The free fraction is raised. Low albumin increases free phenytoin, so the total level underestimates exposure.

Key takeaways

  • ADME determines drug concentrations; four parameters (bioavailability, volume of distribution, clearance, half-life) turn that into dosing decisions.
  • Each route trades speed, completeness of absorption, convenience and risk; choose for the drug, the situation and the person.
  • Bioavailability is the fraction reaching the circulation unchanged; absorption depends on the drug, the formulation, the gut and interactions.
  • First-pass metabolism lowers oral bioavailability; bypassing the portal circulation (sublingual, transdermal, IV) avoids it.
  • Vd = amount in the body ÷ plasma concentration; a large Vd means tissue binding and a large loading dose.
  • Only free drug acts; when albumin is low, a "normal" total level of a highly bound drug like phenytoin may be toxic.

Common mistakes in Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics

  • Assuming everyone given the same dose reaches the same concentration: kidney function, age and interactions change it.
  • Assuming oral and IV doses are interchangeable: oral bioavailability is often lower, so the doses can differ.
  • Taking a tetracycline or quinolone with milk, antacids or iron, which chelate it and reduce absorption.
  • Converting between oral and IV morphine as if the doses were equivalent: oral doses are larger because of first-pass metabolism.
  • Expecting a high Vd to mean a high plasma level: it means the opposite, because the drug is in the tissues.
  • Interpreting a phenytoin level as within range without considering a low albumin.

Lessons in this school

  1. Introduction to pharmacokinetics
    Pharmacokinetics describes how drug concentrations change over time as the body absorbs, distributes, metabolises and excretes a drug.
  2. Routes of administration
    The route of administration decides how quickly and how completely a drug reaches the circulation, and sometimes where it acts.
  3. Absorption and bioavailability
    Before an oral drug can act, it must dissolve and cross the gut wall, then survive the liver.
  4. First-pass metabolism
    Drugs absorbed from the gut pass through the gut wall and liver before reaching the rest of the body, and some are extensively metabolised on the way.
  5. Distribution and volume of distribution
    Once in the blood, drugs distribute into tissues to different extents.
  6. Plasma protein binding
    Many drugs bind to plasma proteins, mainly albumin.
  7. Drug metabolism: phase I and II
    Most drugs are metabolised to more water-soluble compounds that the kidneys can excrete.
  8. Cytochrome P450 enzymes
    The cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes carry out most phase I metabolism.
  9. Renal excretion
    The kidneys excrete many drugs and metabolites by filtration, tubular secretion and reabsorption.
  10. Biliary excretion and enterohepatic recycling
    Some drugs and metabolites are excreted into bile and pass into the gut.
  11. Clearance
    Clearance measures the body's ability to eliminate a drug.
  12. Half-life and the elimination rate constant
    Half-life is the time for a drug's concentration to halve.
  13. Steady state and accumulation
    With regular dosing, a drug accumulates until the amount eliminated each interval equals the amount given.
  14. Loading and maintenance doses
    A loading dose gets a drug to its effective concentration quickly; maintenance doses keep it there.
  15. Linear and non-linear kinetics
    For most drugs, concentration rises in proportion to dose (linear, first-order kinetics).
  16. Compartment models
    Pharmacokinetic models describe the body as one or more compartments.
  17. Therapeutic drug monitoring
    For some drugs, measuring blood levels guides dosing because the effective and toxic levels are close or response is hard to judge.
  18. Pharmacokinetics in renal impairment
    Kidney disease reduces the elimination of renally cleared drugs and their active metabolites, and can change absorption, distribution and protein binding.
  19. Pharmacokinetics in hepatic impairment
    Liver disease can reduce drug metabolism, change protein binding and increase sensitivity to some drugs.
  20. Biopharmaceutics and dissolution
    Biopharmaceutics links a formulation's physical properties to how the drug is absorbed.
  21. Bioequivalence
    Generic medicines are licensed when they're shown to be bioequivalent to the reference product.

Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics: questions students ask

Why does sublingual GTN act quickly?

It's absorbed directly into the systemic circulation, avoiding first-pass metabolism.

How is bioavailability measured?

By comparing the AUC after oral and IV doses.

Which routes avoid first-pass metabolism?

For example sublingual, buccal, transdermal and intravenous.

Why does low albumin matter for phenytoin?

The free fraction rises, so a normal total level can still be toxic.
