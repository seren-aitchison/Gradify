Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is aspirin avoided in children under 16?

Because of the risk of Reye's syndrome. From “Children and young people” →

In plain English: Children aren't small adults: their bodies handle medicines differently and change rapidly as they grow. Key idea: Dose children by weight or age using the BNFC, check for tenfold errors, choose suitable formulations and avoid aspirin, codeine under 12 and some cough remedies.

When is the risk of structural birth defects highest?

In the first trimester, especially weeks 3 to 8. From “Pregnancy” →

In plain English: Medicines in pregnancy can affect the fetus, but untreated illness also causes harm. Key idea: The first trimester carries the highest risk of defects; avoid known teratogens, give folic acid, and use UKTIS to support decisions; don't stop essential medicines suddenly.

Name two anticholinergic medicines that cause confusion.

For example oxybutynin and amitriptyline. From “Older people” →

In plain English: Older people take the most medicines and are most vulnerable to their harms. Key idea: Ageing reduces clearance and increases sensitivity; watch sedatives, anticholinergics, NSAIDs and antihypertensives, and use STOPP/START.

What is deprescribing?

The planned, supervised reduction or stopping of medicines that may no longer help. From “Polypharmacy and deprescribing” →

In plain English: Taking many medicines increases the risk of harm, interactions and burden. Key idea: Review polypharmacy with structured approaches, deprescribe high-harm, low-benefit medicines with the person, and taper those causing withdrawal.

What is diagnostic overshadowing?

Attributing symptoms to a disability rather than illness. From “People with learning disabilities” →

In plain English: People with learning disabilities have poorer health outcomes and are often overmedicated with psychotropic medicines. Key idea: Make reasonable adjustments, avoid diagnostic overshadowing, and support STOMP reviews of psychotropic medicines.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Special Populations

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the UK reference for medicines in children? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    BNF for Children. The BNFC covers paediatric doses and licensing.

  2. How long is the neonatal period? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The first 28 days of life. A neonate is a baby in the first 28 days of life.

  3. Which vitamin should be taken before and in early pregnancy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Folic acid. Folic acid before conception and in early pregnancy reduces neural tube defects.

  4. Why are oral syringes supplied with children's liquids? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To measure small doses accurately. Household spoons vary in volume and cause errors.

  5. Which OTC medicine is avoided in children under 16? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Aspirin. Aspirin is linked to Reye's syndrome in children.

  6. Why do neonates need longer dosing intervals for gentamicin? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Immature kidneys clear it more slowly. Reduced clearance causes accumulation and toxicity.

  7. Why are tenfold errors more likely in neonatal care? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Very small doses and volumes make decimal errors easy. Small volumes and decimals make errors common, so double checks are used.

  8. Which combination raises the risk of falls in older people? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Zopiclone and an antihypertensive. Sedation plus low blood pressure increases falls.

  9. Which medicine is often a good candidate for deprescribing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A long-term PPI without a current indication. PPIs started for short-term reasons are often continued unnecessarily.

Key takeaways

  • Dose children by weight or age using the BNFC, check for tenfold errors, choose suitable formulations and avoid aspirin, codeine under 12 and some cough remedies.
  • Neonates clear drugs slowly and need doses by gestational and postnatal age; use standard concentrations and double checks to avoid tenfold errors.
  • The first trimester carries the highest risk of defects; avoid known teratogens, give folic acid, and use UKTIS to support decisions; don't stop essential medicines suddenly.
  • Most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding; avoid codeine; use specialist resources and monitor the infant.
  • Ageing reduces clearance and increases sensitivity; watch sedatives, anticholinergics, NSAIDs and antihypertensives, and use STOPP/START.
  • Estimate function properly (creatinine clearance for narrow-margin drugs), then reduce, extend or avoid renally cleared and nephrotoxic drugs using the BNF and SPC.

Common mistakes in Special Populations

  • Using a kitchen teaspoon to measure a child's antibiotic dose.
  • Confusing caffeine citrate with caffeine base when calculating a dose.
  • Advising a pregnant woman to stop her antiepileptic immediately without specialist advice.
  • Advising a mother to stop breastfeeding to take amoxicillin.
  • Recommending a sedating antihistamine for sleep to an 85-year-old with dementia.
  • Continuing morphine at the usual dose in someone with severe kidney impairment.

Lessons in this school

  1. Children and young people
    Children aren't small adults: their bodies handle medicines differently and change rapidly as they grow.
  2. Neonates
    Neonates, especially premature babies, are the most vulnerable patients.
  3. Pregnancy
    Medicines in pregnancy can affect the fetus, but untreated illness also causes harm.
  4. Breastfeeding
    Most medicines are compatible with breastfeeding, and stopping breastfeeding unnecessarily has harms.
  5. Older people
    Older people take the most medicines and are most vulnerable to their harms.
  6. Prescribing in renal impairment
    Kidney impairment causes many medicines to accumulate, and some worsen kidney function further.
  7. Prescribing in hepatic impairment
    Liver disease alters drug metabolism, protein binding and clotting, but there's no simple test to adjust doses.
  8. Polypharmacy and deprescribing
    Taking many medicines increases the risk of harm, interactions and burden.
  9. Frailty
    Frailty is a state of reduced reserve, making people vulnerable to sudden decline from minor stresses.
  10. People with learning disabilities
    People with learning disabilities have poorer health outcomes and are often overmedicated with psychotropic medicines.
  11. Swallowing difficulties and covert administration
    Many people struggle to swallow tablets, and some lack capacity to agree to medicines.
  12. Care homes
    Care home residents are frail, take many medicines and depend on others to give them.

Special Populations: questions students ask

Why is vitamin K given at birth?

To prevent haemorrhagic disease of the newborn.

Why is codeine avoided when breastfeeding?

Ultrarapid metabolisers can pass on enough morphine to harm the infant.

What does STOPP stand for?

Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions.

Why is morphine problematic in kidney impairment?

Its active metabolites accumulate, causing toxicity.
