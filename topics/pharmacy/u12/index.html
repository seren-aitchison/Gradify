Key terms and formulas at a glance

Dyspepsia
Upper abdominal pain or discomfort, often with bloating, early fullness or nausea
Helicobacter pylori
A bacterium living in the stomach lining that causes most peptic ulcers
Chemoreceptor trigger zone
An area of the brainstem sensing toxins and drugs in the blood, rich in dopamine and 5-HT3 receptors
Bulk-forming laxative
A laxative such as ispaghula husk that increases stool mass and needs good fluid intake
Oral rehydration salts (ORS)
A glucose and electrolyte solution replacing fluid and salts lost in diarrhoea
Ulcerative colitis
Inflammation of the colon lining, starting at the rectum
Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
A long-term functional bowel disorder with pain, bloating and change in bowel habit
Acute kidney injury (AKI)
A rapid fall in kidney function, shown by rising creatinine or falling urine output
eGFR
Estimated glomerular filtration rate, a measure of kidney function from creatinine
Hyperkalaemia
A high blood potassium level, which can cause dangerous heart rhythms
Maintenance fluids
Fluids replacing normal daily losses in people unable to drink enough
Hepatic encephalopathy
Confusion caused by toxins the failing liver can't clear, worsened by some medicines

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three alarm features in dyspepsia.

For example difficulty swallowing, weight loss and black stools. From “Dyspepsia and gastro-oesophageal reflux” →

In plain English: Dyspepsia and reflux are very common and often managed in pharmacies. Key idea: Lifestyle, medicine review, antacids, then a 4-week PPI or H. pylori testing; refer alarm features such as dysphagia, weight loss and bleeding.

Why is metoclopramide restricted to 5 days?

Because of the risk of extrapyramidal effects. From “Nausea and vomiting” →

In plain English: Nausea and vomiting have many causes, from infections and pregnancy to medicines and raised intracranial pressure. Key idea: Choose antiemetics by cause and receptor; limit metoclopramide to 5 days; refer dehydration, blood or severe pain.

What is checked before starting azathioprine?

TPMT activity. From “Inflammatory bowel disease” →

In plain English: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are long-term inflammatory conditions with flares and remissions. Key idea: Mesalazine by brand, steroids to induce not maintain, thiopurines with TPMT testing, biologics with screening; refer severe flares.

Which two tests classify CKD?

eGFR and ACR. From “Chronic kidney disease” →

In plain English: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects millions of people and raises cardiovascular risk. Key idea: Stage by eGFR and ACR; slow progression with blood pressure control, ACE inhibitors or ARBs and SGLT2 inhibitors; adjust renally cleared drugs.

Why are NSAIDs avoided in liver disease?

They increase bleeding, kidney injury and fluid retention. From “Liver disease and drug handling” →

In plain English: The liver metabolises many drugs, so liver disease changes how medicines work and how safe they are. Key idea: Liver disease raises drug levels, lowers albumin and impairs clotting; avoid sedatives, NSAIDs and constipating drugs, and check the BNF.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How long is an initial PPI course for uninvestigated dyspepsia? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4 weeks. NICE suggests a full-dose PPI for 4 weeks, then review.

  2. What is H. pylori eradication therapy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A PPI plus two antibiotics for 7 days. A PPI with two antibiotics for 7 days clears most H. pylori infections.

  3. Which antiemetic class does ondansetron belong to? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    5-HT3 antagonist. Ondansetron blocks 5-HT3 receptors in the gut and chemoreceptor trigger zone.

  4. Which feature in a person with indigestion needs urgent referral? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Difficulty swallowing. Dysphagia is an alarm feature for oesophageal cancer.

  5. Which medicine commonly causes dyspepsia? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ibuprofen. NSAIDs reduce protective prostaglandins in the stomach lining.

  6. How long should a PPI be stopped before an H. pylori breath test? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    2 weeks. PPIs suppress the bacteria and cause false negatives.

  7. Which combination most increases the risk of a bleeding ulcer? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    An NSAID with an SSRI and a corticosteroid. Each adds to gastrointestinal bleeding risk.

  8. Which feature with constipation needs referral? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Rectal bleeding and weight loss. Rectal bleeding with weight loss may indicate colorectal cancer and needs referral.

  9. A person with diarrhoea for 2 days takes metformin and ramipril. What advice fits? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Follow sick day rules, which may pause both. Dehydration plus these medicines increases kidney injury and lactic acidosis risk.

Key takeaways

  • Lifestyle, medicine review, antacids, then a 4-week PPI or H. pylori testing; refer alarm features such as dysphagia, weight loss and bleeding.
  • Test and treat H. pylori with a 7-day triple regimen; stop NSAIDs or protect with a PPI; refer bleeding urgently.
  • Choose antiemetics by cause and receptor; limit metoclopramide to 5 days; refer dehydration, blood or severe pain.
  • Lifestyle, bulk-forming, then osmotic and stimulant laxatives; opioid constipation needs osmotic and stimulant; refer change in bowel habit and bleeding.
  • Rehydrate first, use loperamide in uncomplicated adult diarrhoea, give sick day advice, and refer blood, fever, dehydration or recent antibiotics.
  • Mesalazine by brand, steroids to induce not maintain, thiopurines with TPMT testing, biologics with screening; refer severe flares.

Common mistakes in Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics

  • Selling antacids repeatedly to a 60-year-old with weight loss and difficulty swallowing.
  • Testing for H. pylori while the person is still taking a PPI.
  • Supplying metoclopramide long term to a young woman.
  • Recommending ispaghula for someone with opioid constipation.
  • Selling loperamide to someone with bloody diarrhoea and fever.
  • Switching between mesalazine brands without checking they are equivalent.

Lessons in this school

  1. Dyspepsia and gastro-oesophageal reflux
    Dyspepsia and reflux are very common and often managed in pharmacies.
  2. Peptic ulcer disease and Helicobacter pylori
    Peptic ulcers are mostly caused by H.
  3. Nausea and vomiting
    Nausea and vomiting have many causes, from infections and pregnancy to medicines and raised intracranial pressure.
  4. Constipation
    Constipation is common, especially in older people and those taking opioids.
  5. Diarrhoea
    Acute diarrhoea is usually viral and self-limiting, but dehydration is dangerous, especially in infants and older people.
  6. Inflammatory bowel disease
    Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are long-term inflammatory conditions with flares and remissions.
  7. Irritable bowel syndrome
    IBS causes abdominal pain with altered bowel habit and bloating, without structural disease.
  8. Acute kidney injury
    Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function, often caused by illness plus medicines.
  9. Chronic kidney disease
    Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects millions of people and raises cardiovascular risk.
  10. Electrolyte disorders
    Abnormal sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium levels can be life-threatening and are often caused or worsened by medicines.
  11. Fluid therapy
    IV fluids are medicines: the wrong type or amount causes harm.
  12. Liver disease and drug handling
    The liver metabolises many drugs, so liver disease changes how medicines work and how safe they are.
  13. Drug-induced liver injury
    Many medicines can damage the liver, from paracetamol overdose to idiosyncratic reactions.
  14. Alcohol-related liver disease
    Alcohol is the commonest cause of liver disease in the UK.

Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics: questions students ask

Why stop a PPI before H. pylori testing?

PPIs can cause false-negative results.

Which antiemetic is used for motion sickness?

Hyoscine hydrobromide, cyclizine or promethazine.

Which laxatives are used for opioid-induced constipation?

An osmotic laxative and a stimulant laxative.

What is the mainstay of treating acute diarrhoea?

Rehydration, with oral rehydration salts where needed.
