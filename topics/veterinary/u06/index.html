Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What colour do Gram-positive bacteria stain?

Purple. From “Bacteria of veterinary importance” →

In plain English: Bacteria cause many of the most important diseases of domestic animals. Key idea: Bacteria are classified by Gram stain, shape and oxygen needs, cause disease through adhesins, capsules, exotoxins, endotoxin, spores and intracellular survival, and important UK pathogens include Staphylococcus pseudintermedius, Streptococcus equi, E. coli, Salmonella, Clostridium, Mycobacterium bovis and Leptospira.

What is conjugation?

Transfer of DNA, often a plasmid, directly between bacteria. From “Antimicrobial resistance” →

In plain English: Antimicrobial resistance threatens both animal and human health. Key idea: Antimicrobial resistance arises by mutation or horizontal gene transfer and works through enzymes, altered targets, efflux pumps and reduced permeability; it spreads between animals, people and the environment, so vets should prescribe only when indicated, use culture and sensitivity, prefer narrow-spectrum or topical treatment and reserve HP-CIAs.

Which flea species is commonest on UK dogs?

The cat flea, Ctenocephalides felis. From “Ectoparasites: fleas, lice, mites and ticks” →

In plain English: Ectoparasites live on or in the skin and cause itching, skin disease and the spread of infections. Key idea: Fleas and lice are insects and mites and ticks are arachnids; fleas live mostly in the environment, lice stay on one host, mites cause mange and sheep scab, and ticks such as Ixodes ricinus transmit diseases including Lyme borreliosis, while diagnosis uses combing, scrapes, tape strips and blood tests.

Which cells link innate and adaptive immunity by carrying antigen to lymph nodes?

Dendritic cells. From “Innate immunity” →

In plain English: Innate immunity is the body's first, fast and non-specific defence against infection. Key idea: Innate immunity is fast and non-specific, relying on barriers, pattern recognition of PAMPs by receptors such as TLRs, cells including neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells and NK cells, and complement, which opsonises, attracts cells and lyses microbes.

What are the 3 Qs of colostrum management?

Quality, quantity and quickness. From “Maternally derived immunity and colostrum” →

In plain English: Newborn animals depend on antibodies from their mother for early protection. Key idea: Horses, pigs and ruminants have placentas that block antibody transfer, so their newborns rely on colostrum, while dogs and cats receive a small amount across the placenta; colostrum must be good quality, sufficient and given quickly before gut closure, FPT is detected by blood tests, and maternal antibody can block early vaccines.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which feature is found only in Gram-negative bacteria? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An outer membrane with LPS. The outer membrane carries endotoxin, the hallmark of Gram-negative bacteria.

  2. What is the protein coat of a virus called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Capsid. The capsid encloses and protects the viral genome.

  3. What causes ringworm in animals? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Dermatophyte fungi. Despite the name, ringworm is a fungal infection of keratin.

  4. How do spores help Clostridium species cause disease? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They survive for years in soil. Spores resist heat, drying and disinfectants, so they persist in the environment.

  5. Why does canine parvovirus persist for months in kennels? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is non-enveloped. Non-enveloped viruses resist drying and many detergents, so they persist in the environment.

  6. Why are prions hard to destroy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They resist normal disinfection and heat. Misfolded PrP is very stable and survives standard sterilisation and cooking.

  7. A colicky horse with a strangulating lesion becomes shocked with toxic mucous membranes. Which bacterial product is most likely involved? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Endotoxin from Gram-negative gut bacteria. Damaged gut lets LPS from Gram-negative bacteria enter the circulation, causing endotoxaemia.

  8. A cat with a history of feline herpesvirus develops conjunctivitis again after a stressful house move. What explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reactivation of latent virus. Herpesviruses establish latency and can reactivate during stress.

  9. A ewe shows itching, wool loss and incoordination, and the farmer suspects scrapie. What should happen next? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Report suspicion to APHA. Scrapie is a notifiable TSE, so suspicion must be reported to APHA.

Key takeaways

  • Bacteria are classified by Gram stain, shape and oxygen needs, cause disease through adhesins, capsules, exotoxins, endotoxin, spores and intracellular survival, and important UK pathogens include Staphylococcus pseudintermedius, Streptococcus equi, E. coli, Salmonella, Clostridium, Mycobacterium bovis and Leptospira.
  • Viruses have a DNA or RNA genome in a capsid, sometimes enveloped, replicate only inside host cells, are classified into families such as Parvoviridae, Herpesviridae and Retroviridae, and notifiable viral diseases such as foot-and-mouth disease, avian influenza, rabies and bluetongue must be reported to APHA.
  • Pathogenic fungi include dermatophytes that cause zoonotic ringworm, Malassezia yeasts in skin and ear disease, and opportunistic moulds such as Aspergillus, while prions are misfolded proteins that cause notifiable spongiform encephalopathies such as BSE and scrapie and resist normal disinfection.
  • Antimicrobial resistance arises by mutation or horizontal gene transfer and works through enzymes, altered targets, efflux pumps and reduced permeability; it spreads between animals, people and the environment, so vets should prescribe only when indicated, use culture and sensitivity, prefer narrow-spectrum or topical treatment and reserve HP-CIAs.
  • Cleaning removes organic matter, disinfection reduces microorganisms, antisepsis applies to living tissue and sterilisation destroys all life including spores; autoclaving is standard for instruments, and disinfectants must match the organism, with oxidising agents needed for resistant non-enveloped viruses such as parvovirus.
  • Nematodes are roundworms with egg and four larval stages, direct or indirect life cycles and sometimes arrested larvae; important species include Toxocara in pets, Angiostrongylus in dogs, Ostertagia and Dictyocaulus in cattle, Teladorsagia, Haemonchus and Nematodirus in sheep and cyathostomins in horses.

Common mistakes in Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology

  • Assuming Gram-negative means less dangerous. Gram-negative bacteria carry endotoxin, which can cause life-threatening shock.
  • Thinking all viruses are fragile. Non-enveloped viruses such as parvovirus survive for months and need disinfectants proven against them.
  • Relying on a negative Wood's lamp to rule out ringworm. Many dermatophyte strains do not fluoresce, so culture or PCR is needed.
  • Thinking resistance develops in the animal being treated. Bacteria become resistant; selection pressure favours resistant bacteria in every treated animal.
  • Spraying disinfectant onto dirty surfaces. Organic matter inactivates disinfectants, so surfaces must be cleaned first.
  • Assuming a negative faecal egg count means no worms. Arrested or immature larvae produce no eggs, so a count can be negative in serious disease.

Lessons in this school

  1. Bacteria of veterinary importance
    Bacteria cause many of the most important diseases of domestic animals.
  2. Viruses of veterinary importance
    Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that cause many notifiable and economically important animal diseases.
  3. Fungi and prions
    Fungi and prions are less common causes of disease than bacteria and viruses but include important zoonoses and fatal neurological diseases.
  4. Antimicrobial resistance
    Antimicrobial resistance threatens both animal and human health.
  5. Disinfection and sterilisation
    Killing or removing microorganisms from surfaces, instruments and skin prevents infection in practices and on farms.
  6. Nematodes of domestic animals
    Nematodes, or roundworms, are the most important parasites of grazing livestock and common in pets.
  7. Cestodes and trematodes
    Tapeworms and flukes are flatworms with complex life cycles that involve intermediate hosts.
  8. Ectoparasites: fleas, lice, mites and ticks
    Ectoparasites live on or in the skin and cause itching, skin disease and the spread of infections.
  9. Protozoa
    Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic parasites that cause diarrhoea, abortion and blood disease in animals.
  10. Parasite control and anthelmintic resistance
    Parasite control keeps animals healthy and productive, but overuse of wormers has bred resistant worms.
  11. Innate immunity
    Innate immunity is the body's first, fast and non-specific defence against infection.
  12. Adaptive immunity
    Adaptive immunity is specific, slower to start and creates memory.
  13. Antibodies and the humoral response
    Antibodies are the effector molecules of humoral immunity.
  14. Hypersensitivity and autoimmunity
    The immune system can cause disease when it overreacts or attacks the body's own tissues.
  15. Maternally derived immunity and colostrum
    Newborn animals depend on antibodies from their mother for early protection.
  16. Principles of vaccination
    Vaccination is one of the most effective tools in veterinary medicine.
  17. Vaccination of companion animals
    Vaccination protects dogs, cats and rabbits against serious infectious diseases.
  18. Vaccination of farm animals and horses
    Vaccination is central to herd health planning and to equine competition rules.

Microbiology, Parasitology and Immunology: questions students ask

Which stain identifies mycobacteria?

The Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) stain.

What do prions lack that all other infectious agents have?

Nucleic acid (DNA or RNA).

What is the standard method for sterilising surgical instruments?

Autoclaving with moist heat under pressure.

What test detects lungworm larvae in faeces?

The Baermann technique.
