Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are Fraser's three views of animal welfare?

Biological functioning, affective states and natural living. From “What animal welfare means” →

In plain English: Animal welfare is the state of an animal as it experiences it. Key idea: Animal welfare is the individual animal's state as it experiences it, assessed through biological functioning, affective states and natural living, which can conflict; it differs from animal ethics and rights, and the RCVS makes welfare vets' primary consideration.

What are the four principles of the Welfare Quality protocols?

Good feeding, good housing, good health and appropriate behaviour. From “Assessing welfare in practice” →

In plain English: Welfare must be assessed with evidence, not assumptions. Key idea: Welfare assessment combines resource-based measures of what animals are given with animal-based measures of their health, behaviour and emotional state; protocols such as Welfare Quality and farm assurance schemes increasingly rely on outcomes, which reveal problems that resource checks miss.

Why should horses have companions?

They are herd animals that rely on others for security. From “Husbandry of horses” →

In plain English: Horses evolved as grazing, herd-living animals on open plains. Key idea: Horses are trickle-feeding, herd-living prey animals needing friends, forage and freedom; good husbandry provides near-constant forage, turnout and company, suitable stabling, regular farriery and preventive care, while poor management leads to colic, laminitis, obesity, ulcers and stereotypies.

What proportion of a rabbit's diet should be hay or grass?

About 85%. From “Rabbits, small mammals and exotic pets” →

In plain English: Rabbits, guinea pigs and exotic pets are often kept in ways that do not meet their needs. Key idea: Rabbits need companions, space and a diet mainly of hay; guinea pigs need company and dietary vitamin C; small mammals and exotic pets such as reptiles have specific social, environmental and dietary needs, and poor husbandry causes dental, gut and metabolic bone problems.

Why are horses prone to gastric ulcers when forage is restricted?

Their stomach produces acid continuously, and without forage it is not buffered. From “Feeding horses” →

In plain English: The horse's digestive system is built for a near-continuous supply of fibre. Key idea: Horses are hindgut fermenters with small stomachs that produce acid continuously, so they need forage-based diets of about 1.5% of body weight in dry matter, fed little and often with gradual changes and constant water, which reduces colic, gastric ulcers and laminitis.

Key takeaways

  • Animal welfare is the individual animal's state as it experiences it, assessed through biological functioning, affective states and natural living, which can conflict; it differs from animal ethics and rights, and the RCVS makes welfare vets' primary consideration.
  • The Five Freedoms, from the Brambell Report and FAWC, set out freedoms from hunger, discomfort, pain, fear and the freedom to behave normally, while the Five Domains model links nutrition, environment, health and behavioural interactions to mental state, emphasising positive as well as negative experiences.
  • The Animal Welfare Act 2006 makes causing unnecessary suffering an offence and places a duty of care to meet five welfare needs, enabling action before suffering occurs; codes of practice guide owners, and recent Acts increased sentences and recognised animal sentience.
  • Welfare assessment combines resource-based measures of what animals are given with animal-based measures of their health, behaviour and emotional state; protocols such as Welfare Quality and farm assurance schemes increasingly rely on outcomes, which reveal problems that resource checks miss.
  • Animals are motivated to perform behaviours such as foraging and rooting; preventing them causes frustration and abnormal behaviours such as stereotypies, tail-biting and feather-pecking, while enrichment that meets real behavioural needs reduces these problems and promotes positive welfare.
  • Pain is often hidden, especially in prey species, so vets rely on species-specific behavioural signs and validated tools such as the Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale and grimace scales, scoring pain regularly to guide and check analgesia.

Common mistakes in Animal Husbandry, Nutrition and Welfare

  • Equating good productivity with good welfare. A productive animal can still experience pain, fear or frustration.
  • Treating the Five Freedoms as fully achievable. They are ideals; the aim is to minimise negative and promote positive experiences.
  • Thinking the law only acts once an animal is suffering. The duty of care allows intervention when welfare needs are not being met.
  • Assuming that meeting space and feed standards guarantees good welfare. Only animal-based measures show how animals actually fare.
  • Assuming a stereotypy always shows the animal is suffering now. It often reflects past or ongoing poor conditions, but should always prompt a review of the environment.
  • Assuming a quiet, still animal is comfortable. Many animals in pain become withdrawn and immobile.

Lessons in this school

  1. What animal welfare means
    Animal welfare is the state of an animal as it experiences it.
  2. The Five Freedoms and the Five Domains
    Two frameworks dominate practical welfare assessment.
  3. The Animal Welfare Act 2006
    The Animal Welfare Act 2006 is the main welfare law in England and Wales.
  4. Assessing welfare in practice
    Welfare must be assessed with evidence, not assumptions.
  5. Behaviour and its role in welfare
    Behaviour is both a window on an animal's welfare and part of its welfare.
  6. Pain recognition and scoring
    Animals cannot tell us they are in pain, so vets must recognise it from behaviour and physiology.
  7. Husbandry of dogs and cats
    Dogs and cats are the most common pets in the UK, and their welfare depends on owners understanding their needs.
  8. Husbandry of horses
    Horses evolved as grazing, herd-living animals on open plains.
  9. Dairy and beef cattle systems
    Cattle farming in the UK ranges from intensive dairy units to extensive upland suckler herds.
  10. Sheep and pig systems
    Sheep and pig farming present very different welfare challenges.
  11. Poultry systems
    Billions of chickens are farmed worldwide, so poultry welfare has enormous scale.
  12. Rabbits, small mammals and exotic pets
    Rabbits, guinea pigs and exotic pets are often kept in ways that do not meet their needs.
  13. Biosecurity on farm
    Biosecurity means keeping disease out of a farm and stopping it spreading within and beyond it.
  14. Principles of animal nutrition
    Every animal needs energy, protein, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals and water in the right balance.
  15. Feeding dogs and cats
    Dogs and cats have different nutritional needs.
  16. Feeding horses
    The horse's digestive system is built for a near-continuous supply of fibre.
  17. Ruminant nutrition and rationing
    Ruminants rely on rumen microbes to digest fibre.
  18. Body condition scoring
    Body condition scoring assesses an animal's fat reserves by sight and touch.
  19. Welfare at transport and slaughter
    Transport and slaughter are critical points for farm animal welfare.

Animal Husbandry, Nutrition and Welfare: questions students ask

Which report led to the Five Freedoms?

The Brambell Report of 1965.

What are the five welfare needs in section 9?

A suitable environment, a suitable diet, ability to exhibit normal behaviour, housing with or apart from other animals, and protection from pain, suffering, injury and disease.

What is a stereotypy?

A repetitive, unvarying behaviour with no obvious function, such as crib-biting.

Why do prey animals often hide pain?

Showing weakness could attract predators, so they tend to mask signs.
