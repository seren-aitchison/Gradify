Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is haematochezia?

Fresh blood in the faeces. From “Vomiting and diarrhoea in dogs and cats” →

In plain English: Vomiting and diarrhoea are among the commonest reasons pets are brought to a vet. Key idea: Vomiting is active and regurgitation passive; small bowel diarrhoea is high-volume with weight loss and large bowel diarrhoea is frequent with mucus, blood and straining; red flags such as repeated vomiting, blood, collapse or pain need urgent care, and chronic cases are investigated step by step, often including a diet trial.

Why is fructosamine useful in cats?

It helps tell diabetes from stress hyperglycaemia. From “Diabetes mellitus” →

In plain English: Diabetes mellitus is a common endocrine disease of dogs and cats. Key idea: Diabetes in dogs is like type 1 and needs lifelong insulin, while in cats it is like type 2 with insulin resistance and remission is possible; diagnosis needs persistent hyperglycaemia and glucosuria, with fructosamine in cats, monitoring uses glucose curves and sensors, and hypoglycaemia and ketoacidosis are emergencies.

What cells does canine parvovirus attack?

Rapidly dividing cells in the gut lining and bone marrow. From “Infectious diseases of dogs and cats” →

In plain English: Infectious diseases remain important in small animal practice despite vaccination. Key idea: In dogs, parvovirus causes bloody diarrhoea in the young and unvaccinated, leptospirosis is zoonotic and damages kidneys and liver, and lungworm causes bleeding; in cats, FeLV, FIV, FIP, cat flu and panleucopenia matter, with antiviral treatment now available for FIP; imported dogs can carry leishmaniosis and Brucella canis, which is reportable to APHA.

At what age does the Cat Group recommend neutering cats?

By four months. From “Neutering” →

In plain English: Neutering is one of the commonest operations in small animal practice. Key idea: Neutering removes reproductive organs by ovariohysterectomy, ovariectomy or castration, prevents pyometra and unwanted litters, and may reduce mammary tumour risk, but can cause weight gain, incontinence and joint disease in some large breeds; cats are usually neutered at four months, and timing in dogs should be decided individually from current evidence.

What is a medial buttress?

Firm thickening on the inner side of a stifle with chronic cruciate disease. From “Cruciate ligament disease” →

In plain English: Cranial cruciate ligament disease is the commonest cause of hind limb lameness in dogs. Key idea: The canine cranial cruciate ligament usually fails through gradual degeneration, with risk from breed, obesity and genetics and frequent disease in the other stifle; diagnosis uses the cranial drawer and tibial thrust tests with imaging, and treatment includes TPLO, TTA or extracapsular repair, meniscal management, weight control and rehabilitation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Small Animal Medicine and Surgery

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which sign suggests large bowel diarrhoea? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Mucus and straining. Large bowel disease causes frequent small amounts with mucus, blood and tenesmus.

  2. Which heart disease is most common in Cavalier King Charles Spaniels? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Myxomatous mitral valve disease. Cavaliers have a very high prevalence of MMVD.

  3. Which breathing pattern suggests pleural space disease? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Rapid, shallow breaths with quiet sounds. Fluid or air in the pleural space muffles lung sounds and restricts expansion.

  4. A dog brings up undigested, tube-shaped food with no effort. What is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Regurgitation. Passive return of food without abdominal effort is regurgitation.

  5. Why do cats with heart failure rarely cough? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their failure causes fast breathing, not coughing. Coughing is uncommon in feline heart failure; tachypnoea is the main sign.

  6. Which condition causes a honking cough in small breed dogs? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Tracheal collapse. Weak tracheal rings flatten, causing a goose-honk cough.

  7. A cat is vomiting and has lost weight despite a big appetite. Which extra-intestinal cause should be checked? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Hyperthyroidism. Hyperthyroidism commonly causes vomiting and weight loss with polyphagia in older cats.

  8. An owner says their cat is breathing with its mouth open after playing. What should the owner do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Take the cat to a vet immediately. Open-mouth breathing in cats is an emergency that may mean heart failure.

  9. A cat has a cough and pushes hard to breathe out, with wheezes. What is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lower airway disease such as asthma. Expiratory effort with wheezes localises disease to the lower airways.

Key takeaways

  • Vomiting is active and regurgitation passive; small bowel diarrhoea is high-volume with weight loss and large bowel diarrhoea is frequent with mucus, blood and straining; red flags such as repeated vomiting, blood, collapse or pain need urgent care, and chronic cases are investigated step by step, often including a diet trial.
  • Dogs most often develop MMVD in small breeds and DCM in large breeds such as Dobermanns, while cats most often develop HCM, sometimes without a murmur; heart failure causes fast breathing, coughing in dogs, effusions and weakness, cats may suffer aortic thromboembolism, and breathlessness or collapse needs emergency care.
  • Upper airway disease causes stertor or stridor on inspiration, lower airway disease causes expiratory effort and cough, parenchymal disease causes crackles and pleural disease causes rapid shallow breathing with quiet sounds; common conditions include BOAS, feline asthma, kennel cough, cat flu, tracheal collapse and laryngeal paralysis, and cyanosis or collapse is an emergency.
  • CKD is a long-term loss of kidney function, common in older cats, diagnosed by creatinine, SDMA and poorly concentrated urine; IRIS stages it from 1 to 4 and substages it by proteinuria and blood pressure, and management with a renal diet, hydration and control of hypertension, proteinuria and complications slows progression.
  • Diabetes in dogs is like type 1 and needs lifelong insulin, while in cats it is like type 2 with insulin resistance and remission is possible; diagnosis needs persistent hyperglycaemia and glucosuria, with fructosamine in cats, monitoring uses glucose curves and sensors, and hypoglycaemia and ketoacidosis are emergencies.
  • Feline hyperthyroidism is usually caused by benign thyroid hyperplasia, causing weight loss with good appetite and a raised TT4, and is treated with drugs, radioiodine, surgery or an iodine-restricted diet, with kidney values rechecked; canine hypothyroidism causes lethargy, weight gain and skin changes and is diagnosed by low TT4 with high TSH.

Common mistakes in Small Animal Medicine and Surgery

  • Treating every case of acute diarrhoea with antimicrobials. Most mild acute cases resolve with supportive care alone.
  • Thinking a cat without a murmur has a normal heart. Many cats with HCM have no murmur, so imaging is needed when heart disease is suspected.
  • Thinking snoring in flat-faced dogs is normal for the breed. It is a sign of airway obstruction that affects welfare and can lead to collapse.
  • Diagnosing CKD from a single high creatinine in a dehydrated animal. Dehydration raises creatinine, so tests should be repeated once hydrated, with urine concentration.
  • Assuming diabetic cats need insulin for life. Many cats reach remission with early treatment and a low-carbohydrate diet.
  • Diagnosing hypothyroidism in a sick dog from a low TT4 alone. Non-thyroidal illness and some drugs lower TT4, so TSH and the clinical picture matter.

Lessons in this school

  1. Vomiting and diarrhoea in dogs and cats
    Vomiting and diarrhoea are among the commonest reasons pets are brought to a vet.
  2. Cardiac disease in dogs and cats
    Heart disease is common in small animals and differs between dogs and cats.
  3. Respiratory disease in dogs and cats
    Respiratory disease ranges from mild infections to life-threatening airway obstruction.
  4. Chronic kidney disease
    Chronic kidney disease is common in older cats and dogs.
  5. Diabetes mellitus
    Diabetes mellitus is a common endocrine disease of dogs and cats.
  6. Hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism
    Thyroid disease is common in small animals: hyperthyroidism mostly affects older cats and hypothyroidism mostly affects dogs.
  7. Hyperadrenocorticism and hypoadrenocorticism
    The adrenal glands can produce too much or too little cortisol.
  8. Skin and ear disease
    Skin and ear problems are among the commonest reasons dogs see a vet.
  9. Infectious diseases of dogs and cats
    Infectious diseases remain important in small animal practice despite vaccination.
  10. Seizures and neurological disease
    Seizures are a common neurological emergency in dogs and cats.
  11. Common poisonings
    Pets often eat things that are toxic to them.
  12. Oncology in practice
    Cancer is a leading cause of death in older dogs and cats.
  13. Neutering
    Neutering is one of the commonest operations in small animal practice.
  14. Gastric dilatation and volvulus
    Gastric dilatation and volvulus is a life-threatening emergency in large, deep-chested dogs.
  15. Intestinal foreign bodies
    Dogs and cats often swallow objects that can block the gut.
  16. Fracture management principles
    Fractures are common in dogs and cats after road traffic accidents and falls.
  17. Cruciate ligament disease
    Cranial cruciate ligament disease is the commonest cause of hind limb lameness in dogs.
  18. Hip and elbow dysplasia
    Hip and elbow dysplasia are inherited developmental diseases that cause lameness and arthritis in many dog breeds.
  19. Dental disease
    Dental disease is the most common health problem in adult dogs and cats.
  20. Rabbit medicine
    Rabbits are the third most popular mammalian pet in the UK and have very different needs from dogs and cats.
  21. Reptiles and birds in practice
    Reptiles and birds are increasingly common pets, and most of their illnesses come from poor husbandry.
