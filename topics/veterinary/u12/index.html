Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is One Health?

An integrated approach that aims to balance and optimise the health of people, animals and ecosystems. From “One Health” →

In plain English: The health of people, animals and the environment are closely linked. Key idea: One Health links the health of people, animals and ecosystems; most emerging infections are zoonotic; vets contribute through surveillance, zoonosis control, food safety, welfare and antimicrobial stewardship.

Which body carries out animal disease investigation and control in Great Britain?

The Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA). From “Notifiable diseases and disease control” →

In plain English: Controlling serious animal diseases requires legal powers, planning and rapid action. Key idea: Animal disease control in Great Britain rests on the Animal Health Act 1981, with Defra policy and APHA action; measures include restrictions, zones, standstills, culling with compensation and vaccination; control involves trade-offs between protection, cost and welfare.

Under which Act are veterinary surgeons regulated in the UK?

The Veterinary Surgeons Act 1966. From “The RCVS and the regulation of the profession” →

In plain English: The Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons regulates vets and veterinary nurses in the UK. Key idea: The RCVS, established in 1844, regulates veterinary surgeons under the Veterinary Surgeons Act 1966 and veterinary nurses under its Charter; the Act restricts veterinary surgery to vets, with Schedule 3 allowing some tasks by VNs; serious concerns go to the Disciplinary Committee.

When can a vet disclose confidential client information without consent?

When justified by serious risk to animal welfare or public health, a legal requirement, or the public interest. From “Clinical records and confidentiality” →

In plain English: Good records and confidentiality protect animals, clients and vets. Key idea: Veterinary records must be clear, contemporaneous and complete; client confidentiality is a Code principle protected by data law; disclosure without consent is justified only in limited cases such as serious welfare risk, public health or legal duty.

What can a suitably qualified person do?

Prescribe and supply certain veterinary medicines, such as POM-VPS products. From “Working with veterinary nurses and the wider team” →

In plain English: Veterinary practice depends on effective teamwork. Key idea: Registered veterinary nurses are regulated by the RCVS and can perform Schedule 3 procedures under a vet's direction; SQPs supply certain medicines; appropriate delegation, clear communication and respect for team skills improve patient care.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which organisation is one of the One Health quadripartite partners? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The World Organisation for Animal Health. WOAH joins WHO, FAO and UNEP in the quadripartite.

  2. Which zoonosis is a fungal skin infection? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ringworm. Ringworm is caused by dermatophyte fungi, not a worm.

  3. Which agency is responsible for meat hygiene controls in England and Wales? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Food Standards Agency. The FSA oversees official controls in slaughterhouses.

  4. Why is antimicrobial resistance a One Health issue? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Resistant bacteria and genes move between animals, people and the environment. Resistance crosses species and environments.

  5. How is Q fever usually spread to people? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    By inhaling contaminated dust or aerosols from birth products. Coxiella burnetii spreads in dust and aerosols.

  6. What is the purpose of food chain information? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To share information on animal health and medicines to support inspection. It helps OVs assess risks before slaughter.

  7. Which factor most increases the risk of new diseases spilling over from animals to people? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Changes in land use and closer contact with wildlife. Habitat change and contact drive spillover.

  8. What is the most effective measure to prevent E. coli O157 at open farms? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Supervised handwashing with soap and water after animal contact. Handwashing removes bacteria more reliably than gel alone.

  9. Why is post-mortem inspection feedback useful to farmers? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It reveals disease, such as liver fluke or lameness, that the farm can control. Abattoir findings guide herd health improvements.

Key takeaways

  • One Health links the health of people, animals and ecosystems; most emerging infections are zoonotic; vets contribute through surveillance, zoonosis control, food safety, welfare and antimicrobial stewardship.
  • Important UK zoonoses include Campylobacter, Salmonella, E. coli O157, toxoplasmosis, ringworm, leptospirosis, Q fever and psittacosis; they spread by contact, faecal-oral, inhalation and vector routes; hygiene, cooking, rodent control and protecting vulnerable people prevent infection.
  • The FSA and official veterinarians protect food safety through ante-mortem and post-mortem inspection, food chain information and HACCP; welfare at slaughter is protected by law and CCTV; residue surveillance and withdrawal periods keep medicines out of food.
  • Animal disease control in Great Britain rests on the Animal Health Act 1981, with Defra policy and APHA action; measures include restrictions, zones, standstills, culling with compensation and vaccination; control involves trade-offs between protection, cost and welfare.
  • Surveillance can be passive, active or syndromic; APHA, an agency of Defra, runs scanning surveillance and responds to notifiable disease, with SRUC in Scotland; the VMD monitors antibiotic sales and resistance, which have fallen substantially since 2014.
  • Prevalence measures existing disease and incidence new cases; sensitivity and specificity describe test accuracy, while predictive values depend on prevalence; study designs include cross-sectional, cohort, case-control and randomised trials.

Common mistakes in Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice

  • Treating antimicrobial resistance as only a human medicine problem. Resistance moves between animals, people and the environment.
  • Thinking only exotic diseases are zoonotic. Many common infections, such as ringworm and Campylobacter, pass between animals and people.
  • Thinking meat inspection protects only human health. It also protects animal welfare and provides health feedback to farms.
  • Assuming vaccination always solves outbreaks. It can reduce culling, but may affect trade and must distinguish vaccinated from infected animals.
  • Thinking surveillance is only the government's job. Practising vets and farmers provide most scanning surveillance data.
  • Assuming a positive result on a good test means an animal is definitely infected. When prevalence is low, many positives may be false.

Veterinary Public Health and Professional Practice: questions students ask

Why should pregnant women avoid lambing ewes?

Infections such as chlamydial abortion and toxoplasmosis can harm the pregnancy.

What is ante-mortem inspection?

Inspection of live animals before slaughter for disease, injury and welfare problems.

What is active surveillance?

Planned testing of animals to detect disease, such as routine TB testing.

What is the difference between prevalence and incidence?

Prevalence is the proportion with disease at a point in time; incidence is the number of new cases over a period.
