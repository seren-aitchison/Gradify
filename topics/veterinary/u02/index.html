Key terms and formulas at a glance

Homeostasis
The maintenance of a stable internal environment by physiological control systems
Threshold
The membrane potential at which enough voltage-gated sodium channels open to trigger an action potential
Acetylcholinesterase
The enzyme in the synaptic cleft that hydrolyses acetylcholine, ending neuromuscular transmission
Muscarinic receptor
An acetylcholine receptor on parasympathetic target organs, blocked by atropine
Stroke volume
The volume of blood ejected by a ventricle in one beat
Mean arterial pressure
The average arterial pressure over a cardiac cycle, approximately diastolic plus one-third of the pulse pressure
Packed cell volume
The percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells, measured by centrifugation
Dead space
The part of each breath that does not take part in gas exchange (airways and unperfused alveoli)
Parabronchus
The tubular gas-exchange unit of the avian lung, surrounded by air and blood capillaries
Volatile fatty acids
Short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate) produced by microbial fermentation and absorbed as the ruminant's main energy source
Obligate carnivore
An animal that must eat animal tissue to meet nutrient needs it cannot synthesise, such as the cat's need for taurine and preformed vitamin A
Thermoneutral zone
The range of environmental temperature over which an animal maintains core temperature without extra energy for heat production or loss

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Roughly what proportion of an adult animal's body weight is water?

About 60%, two-thirds of it intracellular and one-third extracellular. From “Homeostasis and body fluids” →

In plain English: Physiology is the study of how an animal keeps its internal environment stable while the world around it changes. Key idea: Water follows osmolality between cells and ECF, and follows Starling forces between plasma and interstitium. Sodium sets ECF volume; albumin holds water in the vessels.

Why may atropine be ineffective in some rabbits?

Many rabbits produce atropinesterase, which rapidly breaks atropine down. From “The autonomic nervous system” →

In plain English: The autonomic nervous system controls the heart, blood vessels, glands and gut without conscious effort. Key idea: Sympathetic postganglionic fibres release noradrenaline onto α and β receptors; parasympathetic fibres release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors; all ganglia use nicotinic receptors.

What is the main chemical stimulus to breathing in a healthy animal?

Carbon dioxide, acting through pH on the central chemoreceptors in the medulla. From “Respiration and gas exchange” →

In plain English: Breathing brings oxygen to the blood and removes carbon dioxide, and the haemoglobin–oxygen curve explains how oxygen is loaded in the lung and unloaded in the tissues. Key idea: The sigmoid dissociation curve protects saturation at high PO₂ and releases oxygen in the tissues; it shifts right with CO₂, acid and heat. Most hypoxaemia in clinical animals comes from ventilation–perfusion mismatch or hypoventilation.

What are caecotrophs?

Soft faecal pellets from caecal fermentation, rich in microbial protein and B vitamins, which rabbits eat directly from the anus. From “Monogastric and hindgut digestion” →

In plain English: Dogs, cats and pigs digest mainly with their own enzymes, horses and rabbits rely on microbes in the hindgut, and the cat has metabolic needs that make it a true carnivore. Key idea: Cats need dietary taurine, arginine, preformed vitamin A, arachidonic acid and niacin, and a high protein intake; they are obligate carnivores, not small dogs.

Which hormones are released from the posterior pituitary?

Antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin) and oxytocin, made in hypothalamic neurones. From “The hypothalamus and pituitary” →

In plain English: The hypothalamus links the nervous system to the endocrine system through the pituitary gland. Key idea: The hypothalamus controls the anterior pituitary by hormones in portal blood and releases ADH and oxytocin directly from the posterior pituitary; target-gland hormones feed back to switch the axis off.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Veterinary Physiology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which ion is most concentrated in the intracellular fluid? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Potassium. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase keeps potassium high inside cells and sodium high outside; chloride and calcium are mainly extracellular.

  2. Which channels open first during the rising phase of an action potential? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Voltage-gated sodium channels. Opening of voltage-gated sodium channels causes the rapid depolarisation; potassium channels open later and repolarise the membrane.

  3. Which type of receptor does acetylcholine act on at the skeletal neuromuscular junction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nicotinic. The motor end-plate carries nicotinic receptors, ligand-gated cation channels. Muscarinic receptors are on parasympathetic target organs.

  4. A ewe with a heavy liver fluke burden develops a soft swelling under the jaw. What is the most likely mechanism? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Low plasma albumin reduces oncotic pressure, so fluid leaves the capillaries faster than lymph returns it. Fluke and Haemonchus cause protein loss, and hypoalbuminaemia lowers oncotic pressure, giving dependent oedema ("bottle jaw").

  5. Illustratively, a 10 kg dog has a total body water of about how many litres? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    6. Total body water is about 0.6 × body weight: 0.6 × 10 = 6 litres. About 2 litres is ECF and 0.5 litre plasma.

  6. A local anaesthetic is injected around a nerve. Which function is usually lost first? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pain sensation carried by small fibres. Thin Aδ and C fibres are blocked by lower concentrations than large myelinated fibres, so pain is lost before touch and motor function.

  7. A male cat with urethral obstruction has a plasma potassium far above normal and a slow heart rate. Which explanation is best? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Sustained depolarisation of cardiac cells inactivates sodium channels, reducing excitability and conduction. Raised extracellular potassium makes the resting potential less negative; the resulting inactivation of sodium channels slows conduction and can cause bradycardia, arrhythmias and arrest.

  8. A horse has a stiff, sawhorse stance, flared nostrils and prolapse of the third eyelid when startled, two weeks after a deep hoof wound. Which mechanism is responsible? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A toxin blocking release of inhibitory transmitters in the spinal cord, causing uncontrolled motor activity. These are signs of tetanus: Clostridium tetani toxin blocks glycine and GABA release from inhibitory interneurones, causing spastic paralysis. Botulism causes flaccid paralysis, and the tempting distractor reverses tetanus and botulism.

  9. A cow is sedated with the same dose per kilogram of xylazine that is used in horses and becomes deeply recumbent. What is the best explanation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ruminants are far more sensitive to xylazine than horses, so doses per kilogram are much lower. Species sensitivity to α2-agonists differs greatly; ruminant doses are a fraction of equine doses. This is why doses must be species-specific and never extrapolated.

Key takeaways

  • Water follows osmolality between cells and ECF, and follows Starling forces between plasma and interstitium. Sodium sets ECF volume; albumin holds water in the vessels.
  • The resting potential is set mainly by potassium; the action potential is the opening of voltage-gated sodium then potassium channels. Raising potassium reduces excitability; lowering calcium increases nerve excitability.
  • ACh released by calcium opens nicotinic receptors; acetylcholinesterase ends the signal. In the muscle fibre, calcium released through ryanodine receptors binds troponin, and ATP both powers the stroke and detaches myosin.
  • Sympathetic postganglionic fibres release noradrenaline onto α and β receptors; parasympathetic fibres release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors; all ganglia use nicotinic receptors.
  • S1 marks AV valve closure at the start of systole and S2 semilunar valve closure at its end; murmurs between S1 and S2 are systolic. Horses often have audible S3 and S4 sounds, and second-degree AV block at rest, without disease.
  • Arterial pressure is cardiac output times resistance. The baroreceptor reflex corrects it within seconds; the kidney, through renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and sodium balance, sets it in the long term.

Common mistakes in Veterinary Physiology

  • Thinking a dehydrated animal has lost only plasma. Most fluid losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, reduced intake) come from the whole ECF and, with time, the ICF; plasma volume is a small part of the deficit.
  • Saying hyperkalaemia makes cells more excitable because the resting potential is closer to threshold. Sustained depolarisation inactivates sodium channels, so excitability falls; this is why severe hyperkalaemia causes bradycardia and cardiac arrest.
  • Confusing tetanus with botulism. Tetanus blocks inhibition centrally, causing spastic rigidity; botulism blocks ACh release peripherally, causing flaccid weakness.
  • Assuming drugs act the same way in every species. Responses to autonomic drugs differ widely (α2-agonists in ruminants, atropine in rabbits), so species-specific data sheets and formularies are essential; nothing here is a dose.
  • Treating every irregular rhythm as disease. Sinus arrhythmia in dogs and second-degree AV block in resting horses are normal vagal effects; atrial fibrillation, by contrast, is the commonest pathological arrhythmia affecting performance in horses.
  • Relying on one blood pressure reading in a nervous cat. Stress can raise systolic pressure markedly; readings are taken after acclimatisation, repeated, and interpreted with the clinical findings.

Lessons in this school

  1. Homeostasis and body fluids
    Physiology is the study of how an animal keeps its internal environment stable while the world around it changes.
  2. Membrane potentials and nerve conduction
    Nerves and muscles signal with electrical changes across their membranes.
  3. The neuromuscular junction and muscle contraction
    Every voluntary movement depends on acetylcholine crossing the neuromuscular junction and calcium switching on actin and myosin.
  4. The autonomic nervous system
    The autonomic nervous system controls the heart, blood vessels, glands and gut without conscious effort.
  5. The cardiac cycle
    The heart is two pumps in series whose valves open and close as pressures change.
  6. Blood pressure and its control
    Blood pressure drives blood through the tissues and must be held within limits from moment to moment.
  7. Blood: cells, clotting and blood groups in animals
    Blood carries oxygen, fights infection and seals its own leaks, and its cells and blood groups differ between species.
  8. Respiration and gas exchange
    Breathing brings oxygen to the blood and removes carbon dioxide, and the haemoglobin–oxygen curve explains how oxygen is loaded in the lung and unloaded in the tissues.
  9. Avian respiration
    Birds breathe with rigid lungs ventilated by air sacs, a system more efficient than the mammalian lung.
  10. Rumen fermentation and volatile fatty acids
    A ruminant feeds its microbes, and its microbes feed it.
  11. Monogastric and hindgut digestion
    Dogs, cats and pigs digest mainly with their own enzymes, horses and rabbits rely on microbes in the hindgut, and the cat has metabolic needs that make it a true carnivore.
  12. Thermoregulation
    Mammals and birds hold their core temperature within narrow limits by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  13. Renal function and urine concentration
    The kidneys filter the plasma, reclaim what the body needs and excrete the rest, adjusting water and electrolyte balance minute by minute.
  14. Acid–base balance
    Enzymes work only within a narrow pH range, so the body buffers acid, breathes off carbon dioxide and excretes acid in urine.
  15. The hypothalamus and pituitary
    The hypothalamus links the nervous system to the endocrine system through the pituitary gland.
  16. Thyroid, adrenal and endocrine pancreas
    The thyroid sets metabolic rate, the adrenal glands manage stress, salt and water, and the endocrine pancreas controls blood glucose.
  17. Reproductive cycles in domestic species
    Each domestic species has its own breeding pattern, from the cow's year-round 21-day cycle to the bitch's single long cycle and the cat's induced ovulation.
  18. Pregnancy, parturition and lactation
    Pregnancy, birth and milk production are controlled by the placenta, the fetus and the mother's hormones, and they differ between species in ways that matter for every vet.

Veterinary Physiology: questions students ask

What does the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase do?

Pumps three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, using ATP.

What triggers release of acetylcholine at the motor nerve terminal?

Calcium entry through voltage-gated calcium channels opened by the action potential.

Which neurotransmitter do sympathetic postganglionic fibres usually release?

Noradrenaline (norepinephrine).

What causes the first heart sound?

Closure of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves at the start of ventricular systole.
