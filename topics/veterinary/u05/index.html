Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the most common cause of cell injury?

Hypoxia. From “Cell injury and cell death” →

In plain English: Disease begins when cells are injured. Key idea: Cell injury arises from hypoxia, toxins, infection, physical agents, immune reactions, nutrition and genetics; reversible injury includes cellular swelling and fatty change, irreversible injury leads to necrosis (coagulative, liquefactive, caseous or fat) with inflammation, and apoptosis is programmed death of individual cells without inflammation.

What is congestion?

Passive accumulation of blood in tissues due to impaired outflow. From “Disturbances of circulation” →

In plain English: Many diseases involve problems with blood flow and fluid balance. Key idea: Oedema results from raised hydrostatic pressure, low oncotic pressure, increased permeability or lymphatic obstruction; Virchow's triad promotes thrombosis, emboli can cause infarction, as in feline aortic thromboembolism, and shock, whether hypovolaemic, cardiogenic or distributive, is an emergency.

Which is the most common acquired heart disease in dogs?

Myxomatous mitral valve disease. From “Pathology of the cardiovascular system” →

In plain English: Heart disease is common in dogs and cats. Key idea: MMVD causes myxomatous thickening of the mitral valve and regurgitation, especially in small breeds, staged by ACVIM; DCM causes dilated, poorly contracting ventricles in large breeds, HCM causes thickened ventricles in cats, and heart failure produces pulmonary oedema (left) or ascites and hepatic congestion (right).

Which breed is commonly affected by exocrine pancreatic insufficiency?

German Shepherd Dog. From “Pathology of the liver and pancreas” →

In plain English: The liver and pancreas have vital metabolic and digestive roles. Key idea: Liver diseases include feline hepatic lipidosis after anorexia, copper-associated chronic hepatitis in dogs and fascioliasis in ruminants; portosystemic shunts let toxins bypass the liver, causing hepatic encephalopathy; pancreatic diseases include pancreatitis with fat necrosis, EPI and diabetes mellitus, which differs between dogs and cats.

What is autolysis?

Breakdown of tissue after death by the body's own enzymes. From “The post-mortem examination” →

In plain English: The post-mortem examination, or necropsy, helps establish the cause of death and supports disease surveillance. Key idea: Post-mortem examinations establish causes of death and support surveillance, legal cases, teaching and research; a systematic approach records history, external and internal findings and samples in formalin and fresh, distinguishing lesions from post-mortem changes, while safety and law require care with zoonoses and reporting notifiable diseases such as anthrax to APHA.

Key takeaways

  • Cell injury arises from hypoxia, toxins, infection, physical agents, immune reactions, nutrition and genetics; reversible injury includes cellular swelling and fatty change, irreversible injury leads to necrosis (coagulative, liquefactive, caseous or fat) with inflammation, and apoptosis is programmed death of individual cells without inflammation.
  • Acute inflammation involves vasodilation and increased permeability driven by mediators such as histamine and prostaglandins, with neutrophils migrating and phagocytosing microbes; the cardinal signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function, and exudates may be serous, fibrinous, suppurative or haemorrhagic.
  • Chronic inflammation involves macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells with tissue destruction and fibrosis; granulomatous inflammation forms focal collections of epithelioid macrophages and giant cells in response to mycobacteria, fungi and foreign bodies; repair occurs by regeneration or fibrosis, with granulation tissue in first- and second-intention healing.
  • Oedema results from raised hydrostatic pressure, low oncotic pressure, increased permeability or lymphatic obstruction; Virchow's triad promotes thrombosis, emboli can cause infarction, as in feline aortic thromboembolism, and shock, whether hypovolaemic, cardiogenic or distributive, is an emergency.
  • Hypertrophy increases cell size, hyperplasia increases cell number and atrophy reduces tissue size; metaplasia replaces one mature cell type with another, dysplasia is disordered growth that can precede neoplasia, and hypoplasia and aplasia are developmental failures, as in cerebellar hypoplasia in kittens.
  • Neoplasms grow uncontrollably; benign tumours are slow, differentiated and non-invasive, while malignant tumours invade and metastasise via lymphatics, blood or body cavities; causes include mutations, viruses such as FeLV, UV light and hormones, and cancers share hallmarks such as sustained growth signalling and angiogenesis.

Common mistakes in Veterinary Pathology

  • Treating necrosis and apoptosis as the same. Necrosis causes inflammation and affects groups of cells; apoptosis does not.
  • Thinking inflammation is always harmful. It is a protective response, although excessive inflammation can cause damage.
  • Expecting a chronic granuloma to resolve without removing its cause. Persistent agents such as foreign bodies must be removed.
  • Confusing hyperaemia with congestion. Hyperaemia is active inflow; congestion is passive build-up from impaired outflow.
  • Confusing canine hip dysplasia with cellular dysplasia. Hip dysplasia is a developmental joint condition.
  • Assuming all tumours are cancer. Benign tumours such as lipomas do not invade or spread.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cell injury and cell death
    Disease begins when cells are injured.
  2. Acute inflammation
    Acute inflammation is the body's rapid response to injury or infection.
  3. Chronic inflammation and repair
    When inflammation persists, its character changes, and tissues must repair.
  4. Disturbances of circulation
    Many diseases involve problems with blood flow and fluid balance.
  5. Disorders of growth
    Cells and tissues can adapt by changing their size, number or type.
  6. Neoplasia: principles
    Neoplasia is uncontrolled growth of cells.
  7. Tumour classification and grading
    Tumours are named and classified by their tissue of origin, and graded and staged to guide prognosis.
  8. Pathology of the cardiovascular system
    Heart disease is common in dogs and cats.
  9. Pathology of the respiratory system
    Respiratory diseases are common in all domestic species.
  10. Pathology of the alimentary system
    Diseases of the digestive tract range from dental disease to life-threatening obstructions.
  11. Pathology of the liver and pancreas
    The liver and pancreas have vital metabolic and digestive roles.
  12. Pathology of the urinary system
    Kidney and lower urinary tract disease are common, especially in cats.
  13. Pathology of the skin
    Skin disease is one of the most common reasons animals are taken to a veterinary surgeon.
  14. Pathology of the nervous system
    Diseases of the brain, spinal cord and nerves cause a wide range of signs.
  15. The post-mortem examination
    The post-mortem examination, or necropsy, helps establish the cause of death and supports disease surveillance.
  16. Cytology and histopathology
    Examining cells and tissues under the microscope is central to diagnosis.
  17. Clinical pathology: haematology
    The complete blood count provides essential information about health and disease.
  18. Clinical pathology: biochemistry
    Serum biochemistry helps assess organ function and metabolic disease.

Veterinary Pathology: questions students ask

What is a suppurative exudate?

An exudate containing pus with many neutrophils.

Which cells characterise chronic inflammation?

Macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.

What are the three parts of Virchow's triad?

Endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow and hypercoagulability.

What is hyperplasia?

An increase in cell number.
