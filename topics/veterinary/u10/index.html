Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Where in the large colon do impactions most often occur?

The pelvic flexure. From “Colic” →

In plain English: Colic means abdominal pain and is one of the most common equine emergencies. Key idea: Colic is abdominal pain from causes ranging from spasmodic and gas colic to impactions, displacements and strangulating lesions; assessment uses heart rate, membranes, gut sounds, rectal examination, nasogastric intubation, ultrasound and peritoneal fluid, and persistent pain, rising heart rate, poor membranes or reflux mean a critical case needing urgent referral.

Which bone moves within the hoof in severe laminitis?

The pedal bone (distal phalanx). From “Laminitis and equine metabolic syndrome” →

In plain English: Laminitis is a painful, often recurring disease of the hoof, and most cases are linked to hormonal disease. Key idea: Laminitis is failure of the laminae holding the pedal bone, most often endocrinopathic from insulin dysregulation in EMS or PPID, and also sepsis-associated or supporting limb; it causes a leaning-back stance and strong digital pulses, is an emergency, and long-term control relies on testing for insulin dysregulation, weight loss, exercise and restricted grazing.

What grade on the AAEP scale is a non-weight-bearing horse?

Grade 5. From “Lameness diagnosis in the horse” →

In plain English: Lameness is the most common cause of lost performance in horses. Key idea: Lameness examination combines history, observation, palpation, hoof testers, trotting in a straight line, lunging and flexion tests; in forelimb lameness the head drops when the sound leg lands, hind limb lameness shows as a hip hike, and the AAEP scale grades lameness from 0 to 5 to guide further investigation.

What is proud flesh?

Exuberant granulation tissue. From “Wounds and synovial sepsis” →

In plain English: Horses often injure themselves, and wounds near joints or tendon sheaths can be life-threatening. Key idea: Equine wounds are assessed for location, depth, contamination and involvement of synovial structures, bone and tendons; synovial sepsis causes rapidly worsening lameness and is diagnosed by synoviocentesis and communication testing, it needs urgent lavage, and any wound near a joint or tendon sheath should be seen by a vet promptly, with tetanus status checked.

By what age should a newborn foal be standing?

Within 1 hour. From “The neonatal foal” →

In plain English: The first days of a foal's life are critical, and problems can become emergencies within hours. Key idea: A healthy foal stands within 1 hour and sucks within 2 hours, the placenta is passed within 3 hours and meconium within a few hours; neonatal problems include meconium impaction, sepsis, maladjustment syndrome, ruptured bladder, limb deformities and neonatal isoerythrolysis, and sick foals deteriorate quickly, so the vet must be called at once.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Equine Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does colic mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Abdominal pain. Colic is a sign of abdominal pain with many causes.

  2. What is the main cause of equine asthma? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Inhaled dust and moulds. Hay and straw dust trigger airway inflammation.

  3. Which equine disease is notifiable in Great Britain? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Equine infectious anaemia. EIA must be reported to APHA as soon as it is suspected.

  4. Why are horses especially prone to colic? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They cannot vomit and have a large, mobile hindgut. Gut anatomy makes horses vulnerable to displacement, impaction and gas build-up.

  5. Which test samples lower airway cells for diagnosing asthma? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Bronchoalveolar lavage. BAL cytology shows the inflammatory cells in the lower airways.

  6. How is CEM controlled in breeding horses? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Swabbing before the breeding season. HBLB codes recommend swabbing mares and stallions to detect carriers.

  7. A horse with colic has nasogastric reflux and a rising heart rate despite pain relief. What is most appropriate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Urgent referral for possible surgery. Persistent pain, tachycardia and reflux suggest a critical surgical case.

  8. A yard has a new horse arriving from a sale. What is the best biosecurity step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Isolate and monitor it first. Isolation and monitoring prevent spread of infections such as strangles.

  9. During a strangles outbreak, which measure helps find new cases earliest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Twice-daily temperature checks. Fever appears before other signs, so temperature checks detect early cases.

Key takeaways

  • Colic is abdominal pain from causes ranging from spasmodic and gas colic to impactions, displacements and strangulating lesions; assessment uses heart rate, membranes, gut sounds, rectal examination, nasogastric intubation, ultrasound and peritoneal fluid, and persistent pain, rising heart rate, poor membranes or reflux mean a critical case needing urgent referral.
  • Equine asthma is lower airway inflammation from dust and moulds, mild to moderate in any age and severe in older horses, diagnosed by endoscopy and BAL and managed mainly by reducing dust; infectious diseases include equine influenza, EHV-1 and 4 and strangles, and upper airway disorders such as roaring and soft palate displacement affect performance.
  • Notifiable equine diseases in Great Britain include EIA, glanders, African horse sickness, dourine, EVA, CEM, West Nile virus, viral encephalomyelitis, anthrax and rabies, and suspicion must be reported to APHA immediately; HBLB Codes of Practice guide control of CEM, EVA, EHV, EIA and strangles, and yards use isolation, temperature monitoring and grouping to stop spread.
  • Laminitis is failure of the laminae holding the pedal bone, most often endocrinopathic from insulin dysregulation in EMS or PPID, and also sepsis-associated or supporting limb; it causes a leaning-back stance and strong digital pulses, is an emergency, and long-term control relies on testing for insulin dysregulation, weight loss, exercise and restricted grazing.
  • PPID is caused by loss of dopaminergic inhibition of the pituitary pars intermedia in older horses, raising ACTH; signs include hypertrichosis, muscle loss, laminitis and infections, diagnosis uses basal ACTH with seasonal reference ranges or a TRH stimulation test, and management combines a vet-prescribed dopamine agonist with good routine care.
  • Sweet itch is an allergy to Culicoides midges managed by avoiding them, mud fever and rain scald follow wet conditions and need dry, clean skin, ringworm is zoonotic, lice and chorioptic mites cause itching, photosensitisation may signal liver disease, and sarcoids are common, locally aggressive tumours that should not be interfered with without a vet.

Common mistakes in Equine Practice

  • Waiting to see if a colicking horse improves before calling the vet. Early assessment matters, because strangulating lesions need surgery within hours.
  • Treating equine asthma with medicines alone. Without reducing dust exposure, signs return when medication stops.
  • Thinking strangles must be reported to APHA. Strangles is not notifiable in the UK, but HBLB codes and good biosecurity should still be followed.
  • Thinking laminitis is mainly caused by eating too much grain. Most cases are endocrinopathic, linked to insulin dysregulation, so hormonal testing is essential.
  • Interpreting an autumn ACTH result using spring reference ranges. ACTH rises naturally in autumn, so season-specific ranges must be used.
  • Picking at or tying off a sarcoid. Interference can make sarcoids grow more aggressively, so they need veterinary treatment.

Lessons in this school

  1. Colic
    Colic means abdominal pain and is one of the most common equine emergencies.
  2. Equine respiratory disease
    Respiratory disease affects horses' welfare and performance.
  3. Equine infectious and notifiable diseases
    Some equine diseases spread rapidly or threaten trade, so they are notifiable.
  4. Laminitis and equine metabolic syndrome
    Laminitis is a painful, often recurring disease of the hoof, and most cases are linked to hormonal disease.
  5. Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction
    Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction, often called equine Cushing's disease, is common in older horses and ponies.
  6. Equine skin disease
    Skin disease is common in horses and often linked to the environment.
  7. Lameness diagnosis in the horse
    Lameness is the most common cause of lost performance in horses.
  8. Diagnostic analgesia
    Diagnostic analgesia uses local anaesthetic to find the source of pain in a lame horse.
  9. Common causes of lameness
    A small number of conditions cause most equine lameness.
  10. Wounds and synovial sepsis
    Horses often injure themselves, and wounds near joints or tendon sheaths can be life-threatening.
  11. Castration in the horse
    Castration is one of the most common surgical procedures in horses.
  12. Equine reproduction
    Breeding horses successfully needs an understanding of the mare's seasonal cycle and careful veterinary management.
  13. The neonatal foal
    The first days of a foal's life are critical, and problems can become emergencies within hours.
  14. Equine dentistry
    Dental disease is common in horses and affects eating, comfort and performance.
  15. Equine passports and medicines records
    Every horse in the UK must have a passport, which also controls which medicines it may receive.

What strangulating lesion is common in older horses?

A pedunculated lipoma.

What was severe equine asthma formerly called?

Recurrent airway obstruction, or heaves.

Which organism causes contagious equine metritis?

Taylorella equigenitalis.

Which hormone is raised in PPID?

ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone).
