Key terms and formulas at a glance

Palmar
The caudal surface of the forelimb distal to the carpus (plantar is the equivalent surface of the hindlimb distal to the tarsus)
Synsarcosis
The attachment of the forelimb to the trunk by muscles alone, without a bony joint, in species lacking a functional clavicle
Aponeurosis
A flat, sheet-like tendon spreading a muscle's force over a wide attachment
Brachial plexus
The network of spinal nerves C6–T2 that supplies the thoracic limb
Suspensory apparatus
The suspensory ligament, proximal sesamoid bones and distal sesamoidean ligaments, which support the fetlock
Reciprocal apparatus
The tendinous linkage of the equine stifle and hock (fibularis tertius and superficial digital flexor) that makes them move together
Digital cushion
Fat and connective tissue pads in the heel beneath the pedal bone that absorb concussion
Nucleus pulposus
The gelatinous centre of the intervertebral disc
Hypsodont
A tooth with a long reserve crown that erupts continuously as it wears (equine cheek teeth)
Menace response
A learned blink to a threatening gesture, testing CN II, the forebrain, the cerebellum and CN VII
Mediastinum
The partition between the two pleural sacs, containing the heart, oesophagus, trachea, great vessels and thymus
Greater omentum
The double fold of peritoneum hanging from the stomach's greater curvature, enclosing the omental bursa

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What do palmar and plantar describe?

The back surface of the forelimb below the carpus (palmar) and of the hindlimb below the tarsus (plantar). From “Anatomical terms of position and direction” →

In plain English: Veterinary anatomy uses a standard language, set out in the Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria, so that a radiograph report, a surgical note or a referral letter means the same thing to every reader. Key idea: Describe direction relative to the animal, not to the room or table: rostral in the head, cranial and caudal on the trunk and upper limbs, dorsal and palmar or plantar below the carpus and tarsus, axial and abaxial between paired digits.

Which spinal nerves form the brachial plexus?

C6 to T2. From “The thoracic limb of the dog and cat” →

In plain English: The carnivore forelimb carries about 60% of the body's weight and is attached to the trunk by muscle. Key idea: The radial nerve extends the elbow, carpus and digits, so it is the nerve that lets the forelimb bear weight; loss of it produces a dropped elbow and knuckling.

What is a Hansen type I disc extrusion?

Rupture of a degenerate, calcified nucleus pulposus through the annulus into the vertebral canal, typical of chondrodystrophic breeds. From “The vertebral column and spinal cord” →

In plain English: The vertebral column supports the body and protects the spinal cord, and it is the site of some of the commonest neurological problems in dogs. Key idea: Between about T2 and T10 the dog's rib heads are joined across the disc by an intercapital ligament, so disc extrusions are rare in the cranial thoracic spine; they cluster at T11–L2 and in the neck.

What is a tracheal bronchus?

A bronchus to the right cranial lung lobe that arises directly from the trachea, found in cattle and pigs. From “The thorax, heart and lungs” →

In plain English: The thorax holds the heart and lungs within the ribs and diaphragm. Key idea: Cattle and pigs have a tracheal bronchus to the right cranial lobe and lobulated lungs with poor collateral ventilation, which explains the cranioventral pattern of their bronchopneumonia.

Where do uroliths commonly lodge in a male dog?

Just caudal to the os penis, where the urethra runs in a bony groove. From “The urinary system” →

In plain English: The kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra are built on one plan, but kidney shape and the male urethra differ markedly between species. Key idea: The long, narrow male urethra is where stones lodge: in the cat's penile urethra, behind the dog's os penis, at the distal sigmoid flexure in the steer and in the urethral process of small ruminants. A male animal that strains without passing urine needs a vet urgently.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Comparative Anatomy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which term describes the surface of a dog's tail base that faces the ground? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ventral. Ventral means towards the belly side, and applies to the tail as to the trunk. Palmar and plantar are only for the distal limbs; caudal means towards the tail tip along the body's long axis.

  2. How many thoracic vertebrae does the horse normally have? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    18. The horse has 18 thoracic vertebrae and 18 pairs of ribs; the dog, cat, ox and sheep have 13 and the pig 14–15.

  3. What is a synovial bursa? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A fluid-filled sac that reduces friction where a tendon passes over bone. A bursa is a synovial sac at a pressure point. An aponeurosis is a flat tendon, a ligament joins bones, and endomysium wraps each fibre.

  4. A dog is lying on its back on the X-ray table and the beam enters the belly and leaves through the back. How is the thoracic radiograph named? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ventrodorsal. Projections are named entry to exit: the beam enters the ventral surface and exits dorsally, so it is ventrodorsal (VD). A dorsoventral (DV) view has the animal in sternal recumbency with the beam entering the back.

  5. On a cow's hind foot, a lesion lies on the side of the lateral claw that faces the interdigital space. How is it described? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    On the axial surface of the lateral claw. The axis of a ruminant limb runs between digits III and IV, so the claw surface facing the interdigital space is axial. Palmar would be wrong for a hind foot, where the term is plantar.

  6. Which joint is a synovial joint of the ball-and-socket (spheroidal) type? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The coxofemoral (hip) joint. The hip is a spheroidal synovial joint. The pelvic symphysis is cartilaginous, sutures are fibrous, and the atlantoaxial joint is a synovial pivot.

  7. A referral letter describes a wound "on the anterior aspect of the horse's knee". Which correction is most accurate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    On the dorsal aspect of the carpus. The equine "knee" is the carpus, and its front surface is called dorsal (the distal-limb convention begins at the carpus). The stifle is the true knee, so the most tempting distractor changes the joint.

  8. A five-month-old puppy is struck by a car and is lame on a hindlimb with swelling above the stifle; the ligaments are intact. Which injury is most likely on the basis of skeletal maturity? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A fracture through the distal femoral physis. In an immature animal the open physis is the weakest part of the limb, so trauma commonly separates it. Ligament ruptures are more typical once the physes have closed.

  9. An injection is to be given into a dog's thigh muscles. Why is the caudal thigh usually avoided? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The sciatic nerve runs there, and injecting near it risks nerve damage and lameness. The sciatic nerve passes caudal to the hip and down the caudal thigh between the hamstring muscles. Absorption is not the problem, and no statute names an injection site.

Key takeaways

  • Describe direction relative to the animal, not to the room or table: rostral in the head, cranial and caudal on the trunk and upper limbs, dorsal and palmar or plantar below the carpus and tarsus, axial and abaxial between paired digits.
  • One mammalian plan is modified for each species: no clavicle and a muscular forelimb attachment in the domestic mammals, a reduced number of digits in the horse and ruminants, and a species-specific number of thoracic and lumbar vertebrae.
  • Tendons transmit muscle force to bone; ligaments join bone to bone. Both are dense collagen with a poor blood supply, so they heal slowly and with scar tissue that is weaker than the original.
  • The radial nerve extends the elbow, carpus and digits, so it is the nerve that lets the forelimb bear weight; loss of it produces a dropped elbow and knuckling.
  • At the gallop the fetlock sinks under load, stretching the SDFT and suspensory ligament, which store and return elastic energy. The SDFT in its mid-metacarpal region is the commonest site of tendon strain in racehorses.
  • The cranial cruciate ligament prevents cranial translation and internal rotation of the tibia. In dogs it usually fails by progressive degeneration rather than a single injury, and once it fails the medial meniscus is at risk.

Common mistakes in Comparative Anatomy

  • Using anterior and posterior for the limbs or trunk of a quadruped. In veterinary anatomy they are reserved for the eye (the anterior chamber, the posterior segment) and a few human-derived terms; elsewhere they are ambiguous.
  • Forgetting the open physis. In a young animal it is mechanically weaker than the surrounding bone and ligaments, so trauma that would sprain an adult joint often fractures the growth plate instead (Salter–Harris fractures), with a risk of shortened or angular growth.
  • Treating the equine suspensory ligament as "just a ligament". It is a modified muscle (interosseous III) that still contains some muscle fibres, and it works with the flexor tendons as a passive support of the fetlock.
  • Assuming the cat's humerus is the same as the dog's. The feline supracondylar foramen carries the median nerve and brachial artery, so fracture repair of the distal humerus must avoid them.
  • Thinking the hoof wall is dead tissue with no function beyond protection. The wall is attached to living tissue by the laminae, transmits the horse's whole weight to the skeleton and grows continuously from the coronary band; its balance and integrity affect every structure above it.
  • Confusing the reciprocal apparatus with patellar locking. The reciprocal apparatus ties the stifle and hock together; patellar locking fixes the stifle, and only together do they hold the whole hindlimb. Failure to unlock is upward fixation of the patella.

Lessons in this school

  1. Anatomical terms of position and direction
    Veterinary anatomy uses a standard language, set out in the Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria, so that a radiograph report, a surgical note or a referral letter means the same thing to every reader.
  2. The skeleton and joints compared
    The skeletons of domestic mammals share one plan, modified for how each species moves and feeds.
  3. Muscles, tendons and fascia
    Skeletal muscles move the skeleton through tendons, while ligaments, fascia, bursae and tendon sheaths shape and protect that movement.
  4. The thoracic limb of the dog and cat
    The carnivore forelimb carries about 60% of the body's weight and is attached to the trunk by muscle.
  5. The equine distal limb and hoof
    The horse stands on a single digit, and most equine lameness arises below the carpus and tarsus.
  6. The pelvic limb and the equine stay apparatus
    The pelvic limb is the engine of propulsion, joined to the spine at the sacroiliac joint.
  7. The bovine foot and claw
    Cattle walk on two claws per foot, and lameness from claw lesions is one of the biggest welfare and economic problems in dairy herds.
  8. The vertebral column and spinal cord
    The vertebral column supports the body and protects the spinal cord, and it is the site of some of the commonest neurological problems in dogs.
  9. The skull, teeth and dental formulae
    The skull houses the brain and sense organs and carries the teeth, whose number, shape and growth reflect each species' diet.
  10. The brain and cranial nerves
    The brain's regions and the twelve pairs of cranial nerves are tested every time a vet examines a head.
  11. The thorax, heart and lungs
    The thorax holds the heart and lungs within the ribs and diaphragm.
  12. The carnivore digestive tract
    Dogs and cats have a simple stomach and a short intestine suited to a diet rich in protein and fat.
  13. The equine digestive tract
    The horse is a hindgut fermenter: a small stomach and a long small intestine feed a huge caecum and colon where microbes ferment fibre.
  14. The ruminant stomach
    Ruminants digest grass with the help of microbes in a four-chambered stomach.
  15. The urinary system
    The kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra are built on one plan, but kidney shape and the male urethra differ markedly between species.
  16. Male and female reproductive anatomy
    Reproductive anatomy varies more between domestic species than almost any other system, and the differences matter for breeding, neutering and obstetrics.
  17. Avian anatomy
    Birds are built for flight and egg-laying: a light, fused skeleton, a gut without teeth, a cloaca and, in the hen, a single functional ovary.
  18. Skin, hoof, horn and the mammary gland
    The skin and its derivatives, hair, glands, claws, horn and the mammary gland, protect the body, regulate temperature and in mammals feed the young.

Comparative Anatomy: questions students ask

What is the vertebral formula of the dog?

C7 T13 L7 S3, with about 20 caudal vertebrae.

What is the difference between a tendon and a ligament?

A tendon joins muscle to bone; a ligament joins bone to bone.

Where does the deep digital flexor tendon insert?

On the palmar (or plantar) surface of the distal phalanx (P3).

Which nerve's loss abolishes the patellar reflex?

The femoral nerve (L4–L6), which supplies the quadriceps femoris.
