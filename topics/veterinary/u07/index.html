Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the bioavailability of an intravenous drug?

100 percent. From “Pharmacokinetics” →

In plain English: Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug. Key idea: Pharmacokinetics covers absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion; bioavailability, volume of distribution, clearance and half-life describe drug handling, first-order drugs halve each half-life, and steady state takes about four to five half-lives.

What is a type A adverse drug reaction?

A predictable, dose-related exaggeration of a drug's normal effect. From “Adverse drug reactions and pharmacovigilance” →

In plain English: Every medicine can cause unwanted effects. Key idea: Adverse drug reactions may be predictable and dose-related (type A) or unpredictable (type B), the VMD runs UK veterinary pharmacovigilance, and vets should stop and treat, record details including batch number and report suspected adverse events, even when not proven.

What is the statutory minimum meat withdrawal under the cascade?

28 days. From “Withdrawal periods and food-producing animals” →

In plain English: Medicines given to food-producing animals can leave residues in meat, milk and eggs. Key idea: Residues are medicine left in food, MRLs set safe limits and withdrawal periods ensure residues fall below them; under the cascade, statutory minimums of 28 days for meat, 7 days for milk and eggs and 500 degree-days for fish apply, and farms must record, mark and discard appropriately.

Which receptor do macrocyclic lactones act on in worms?

Glutamate-gated chloride channels. From “Antiparasitic drugs” →

In plain English: Antiparasitic drugs control worms, flukes, protozoa and ectoparasites. Key idea: Benzimidazoles act on beta-tubulin, levamisole and macrocyclic lactones paralyse worms, praziquantel acts on tapeworms and isoxazolines block chloride channels in fleas and ticks; safety concerns include MDR1 dogs, permethrin in cats, neurological events and environmental harm, and choice depends on parasite, stage, species, resistance and route.

Which drug reverses alpha-2 agonist sedation?

Atipamezole. From “Sedatives and anaesthetic agents” →

In plain English: Sedation and general anaesthesia allow safe handling, imaging and surgery. Key idea: Alpha-2 agonists sedate reliably and are reversed by atipamezole, acepromazine calms without analgesia and cannot be reversed, and benzodiazepines suit sick patients; anaesthesia involves premedication, induction, maintenance and recovery, risk is graded by ASA status, and CEPSAF showed most deaths occur after surgery, so recovery monitoring is vital.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which process converts a drug into a more water-soluble form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Metabolism. Hepatic metabolism makes drugs easier to excrete in urine or bile.

  2. Which drug type binds a receptor without activating it? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Antagonist. Antagonists occupy the receptor and block agonists from acting.

  3. Which species is most at risk from permethrin spot-on products? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cats. Cats cannot metabolise permethrin effectively and develop tremors and seizures.

  4. Why does an oral drug sometimes have low bioavailability? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    First-pass metabolism by the liver. Oral drugs pass through the liver before reaching the systemic circulation.

  5. How does a competitive antagonist change an agonist's dose-response curve? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Shifts it right with the same maximum. More agonist can overcome competitive blockade, so the maximum is unchanged.

  6. Why can some oral antibiotics be dangerous to horses? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They disrupt hindgut bacteria and cause colitis. Upsetting the hindgut microbiota allows overgrowth of harmful bacteria such as clostridia.

  7. A cat with reduced kidney function is given a drug excreted unchanged in urine. What is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The half-life will lengthen. Reduced renal clearance slows elimination, so the drug persists longer.

  8. Drug P reaches a maximum effect of 60 percent at low concentrations, and drug Q reaches 100 percent at higher concentrations. Which statement is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    P is more potent but Q is more efficacious. Potency is about the amount needed; efficacy is the maximum effect.

  9. An owner asks if they can give their cat a human painkiller tablet. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No; see a vet for a safe option. Human painkillers such as paracetamol can be fatal to cats, so a vet must choose a safe drug.

Key takeaways

  • Pharmacokinetics covers absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion; bioavailability, volume of distribution, clearance and half-life describe drug handling, first-order drugs halve each half-life, and steady state takes about four to five half-lives.
  • Drugs act on receptors, enzymes, ion channels and transporters; agonists activate, partial agonists partly activate and antagonists block receptors, potency describes how much drug is needed while efficacy is the maximum effect, and the therapeutic index measures the safety margin.
  • Species differ in metabolism, anatomy and genetics: cats glucuronidate poorly and are poisoned by paracetamol and permethrin, ruminant and equine guts are disrupted by some oral antibiotics, and dogs with the MDR1 mutation react to macrocyclic lactones, so prescribers should use species-authorised products and the data sheet.
  • Adverse drug reactions may be predictable and dose-related (type A) or unpredictable (type B), the VMD runs UK veterinary pharmacovigilance, and vets should stop and treat, record details including batch number and report suspected adverse events, even when not proven.
  • The Veterinary Medicines Regulations 2013, amended in 2024, protect animal health, public health and the environment by controlling the authorisation, supply, use and recording of veterinary medicines, enforced by the VMD, with each authorised product described in its SPC and records kept for five years.
  • The cascade lets a vet use an unauthorised medicine when no authorised product exists for that condition in that species, following steps from other UK veterinary products, to UK human or imported veterinary medicines, to specially prepared medicines, with informed consent and extra residue rules for food-producing animals.

Common mistakes in Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics

  • Thinking half-life depends only on the liver. Half-life depends on both clearance and volume of distribution, and kidney excretion is often important.
  • Confusing potency with efficacy. A potent drug works at low amounts, but a less potent drug may still produce a bigger maximum effect.
  • Assuming that a drug safe in dogs is safe in cats. Cats metabolise many drugs differently and can be poisoned by products safe for dogs.
  • Waiting for proof before reporting. Pharmacovigilance relies on reports of suspected reactions; patterns only emerge when many reports are pooled.
  • Thinking the RCVS enforces medicines law. The VMD enforces the Veterinary Medicines Regulations; the RCVS regulates professional conduct.
  • Using the cascade because a human generic is cheaper. Cost is not a reason; a suitable authorised veterinary product must be used if one exists.

Lessons in this school

  1. Pharmacokinetics
    Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug.
  2. Pharmacodynamics
    Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body.
  3. Species differences in drug handling
    Drugs that are safe in one species can be dangerous in another.
  4. Adverse drug reactions and pharmacovigilance
    Every medicine can cause unwanted effects.
  5. The Veterinary Medicines Regulations
    The Veterinary Medicines Regulations control how veterinary medicines are made, authorised, supplied and used in the UK.
  6. The prescribing cascade
    When no authorised medicine exists for a condition in a species, vets may use the prescribing cascade.
  7. Prescription categories and controlled drugs
    Veterinary medicines are classified into legal categories that control who may prescribe and supply them, and some are controlled drugs with extra rules.
  8. Withdrawal periods and food-producing animals
    Medicines given to food-producing animals can leave residues in meat, milk and eggs.
  9. Responsible use of antimicrobials
    Vets have a legal and professional duty to use antimicrobials responsibly.
  10. Antimicrobials
    Antimicrobials kill or inhibit bacteria by acting on targets that human and animal cells lack.
  11. Antiparasitic drugs
    Antiparasitic drugs control worms, flukes, protozoa and ectoparasites.
  12. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
    NSAIDs are among the most widely used drugs in veterinary practice for pain and inflammation.
  13. Corticosteroids
    Corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive drugs.
  14. Opioids and analgesia
    Opioids are the most effective drugs for moderate to severe pain.
  15. Sedatives and anaesthetic agents
    Sedation and general anaesthesia allow safe handling, imaging and surgery.
  16. Cardiovascular drugs
    Drugs for heart failure and hypertension are widely used in dogs and cats.
  17. Drugs acting on the gut
    Gastrointestinal drugs treat vomiting, ulceration, diarrhoea and constipation.
  18. Drug calculations: principles
    Accurate calculations prevent medication errors.

Veterinary Pharmacology and Therapeutics: questions students ask

What is a partial agonist?

A drug that binds and activates a receptor but cannot produce the full maximum effect.

Why are cats sensitive to paracetamol?

They have low glucuronidation capacity.

Which UK body runs veterinary pharmacovigilance?

The Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD).

Which body enforces the Veterinary Medicines Regulations?

The Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD).
