Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does SOCRATES stand for?

Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Time course, Exacerbating and relieving factors, Severity. From “The medical history” →

In plain English: Most diagnoses are made, or strongly suggested, by the history. Key idea: The history is a diagnostic test with a high yield. Each question should either raise or lower the probability of a specific diagnosis, so learn which features discriminate, not just a list of questions.

What does SPIKES stand for?

Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions (empathy), Strategy and summary. From “Breaking bad news” →

In plain English: How bad news is given affects patients for years. Key idea: Patients remember how they were told long after they forget the details. Pace information to the patient, respond to emotion before giving more facts, and never leave without a plan and a named contact.

Which murmur is heard best with the patient sitting forward in expiration?

Aortic regurgitation (early diastolic, lower left sternal edge). From “The cardiovascular examination” →

In plain English: The cardiovascular examination looks for the signs of heart failure, valve disease, endocarditis and vascular disease, from the hands to the heart. Key idea: Murmurs are described by timing, site of maximal intensity, radiation, character, loudness and response to manoeuvres; the peripheral signs (pulse character, pulse pressure, apex) often tell you the severity.

What is premature closure?

Accepting a diagnosis before it is verified, so other possibilities aren't considered: the most common cognitive cause of diagnostic error. From “Clinical reasoning and diagnostic error” →

In plain English: Diagnosis is a reasoning process that can be studied and improved. Key idea: Most diagnostic errors involve both system factors and cognitive factors, particularly premature closure. The defences are a differential that includes can't-miss diagnoses, a pause to ask "what else?", and safety-netting with follow-up.

What is the approximate expected PaO₂ on 40% inspired oxygen?

About 30 kPa (roughly FiO₂ % minus 10). From “Arterial blood gas interpretation” →

In plain English: An arterial blood gas tells you about oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base status within minutes. Key idea: Never interpret a PaO₂ without the FiO₂. A "normal" PaO₂ on high-flow oxygen can hide severe lung disease.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Clinical Method and Reasoning

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which part of the history records the reaction a patient had to a drug? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Drug history, as the allergy and its reaction. Allergies are documented with the drug history, always with the nature of the reaction so allergy can be distinguished from intolerance.

  2. Which of these is part of closing a consultation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Safety-netting. Closing includes a summary, the plan and safety-netting advice about what to do if things change.

  3. Which three steps make up Elwyn's model of shared decision-making? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Team talk, option talk, decision talk. Elwyn et al. describe team talk, option talk and decision talk; the others are history-taking, SBAR and SPIKES.

  4. A patient says she is "allergic to amoxicillin" because it gave her loose stools. How should this be recorded? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    As an intolerance (diarrhoea), not an allergy, with the reaction described. Gastrointestinal upset is a predictable side effect, not an immune reaction. Labelling it as allergy would needlessly exclude penicillins; recording the reaction lets prescribers decide.

  5. A patient speaks little English and has brought her 12-year-old son to translate. What should you do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Arrange a professional interpreter. Family members, and especially children, may filter information, be distressed by it and compromise confidentiality; professional interpreting is the standard.

  6. A drug "halves the risk" of an event whose baseline risk is 2 in 1,000 over 5 years. What is the best way to explain this to a patient? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Without it about 2 in 1,000 people have the event over 5 years, and with it about 1 in 1,000. Absolute figures with a time frame and same denominator show the real benefit (ARR 0.1%, NNT 1,000), which the relative figure hides.

  7. Which question adds the most diagnostic value in a patient with a headache? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Did it reach its maximum intensity within a minute or so?. A thunderclap onset is a red flag for subarachnoid haemorrhage and changes management immediately. Discriminating questions shift probability; generic ones rarely do.

  8. Which is the best criticism of applying consultation models as rigid checklists in assessments? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Students may perform the steps mechanically without genuinely responding to the patient. The models describe skills to be used flexibly; ticking stages can produce formulaic empathy. Good assessment looks at whether the skills achieved their purpose.

  9. After Montgomery, which statement about disclosing a 1 in 10,000 risk of a serious complication is most accurate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It must be disclosed if a reasonable person in the patient's position, or this patient, would attach significance to it. Materiality depends on significance to the patient, not on a percentage threshold or professional practice; serious rare risks may well be material.

Key takeaways

  • The history is a diagnostic test with a high yield. Each question should either raise or lower the probability of a specific diagnosis, so learn which features discriminate, not just a list of questions.
  • Safety-netting closes every consultation: tell the patient what to expect, which symptoms mean they should seek help, how urgently and where, and when they'll next be reviewed.
  • Relative risk reductions sound large and stay the same whatever the baseline risk; absolute risks and NNT show how much difference treatment makes to this patient. Always give absolute figures.
  • Patients remember how they were told long after they forget the details. Pace information to the patient, respond to emotion before giving more facts, and never leave without a plan and a named contact.
  • Examination is targeted by the history: examine to confirm or refute your leading diagnoses, and always look for the signs that would change management.
  • NEWS2 is a trigger to look at the patient, not a diagnosis. A high score demands urgent senior assessment; a low score doesn't override clinical concern, and staff or relatives' worry is itself a reason to escalate.

Common mistakes in Clinical Method and Reasoning

  • Recording "allergic to penicillin" without the reaction. Diarrhoea or nausea is an intolerance; a rash or anaphylaxis is an allergy. The difference decides whether a patient can safely receive a whole class of antibiotics, and unverified labels lead to broader, less effective prescribing.
  • Thinking patient-centred means doing whatever the patient asks. It means understanding the patient's perspective and involving them in decisions; the doctor still gives honest advice and may decline requests that aren't in the patient's interest, such as antibiotics for a viral illness.
  • Thinking consent is a signature on a form. Consent is a process of dialogue in which the patient understands the options, material risks and alternatives; the form only records it.
  • Giving false reassurance to soften the blow ("I'm sure it will be fine"). It damages trust when the truth emerges and prevents patients planning; hope can be honest ("We'll do everything we can to control this").
  • Examining by rote without thinking. A complete routine examination is useful to learn, but in practice the purpose is to test hypotheses; findings should be interpreted against the history, not recorded and forgotten.
  • Missing the extra 2 points for oxygen, or using scale 2 for patients without confirmed hypercapnic respiratory failure. Scale 2 must be prescribed by a clinician; using it inappropriately hides hypoxaemia.

Lessons in this school

  1. The medical history
    Most diagnoses are made, or strongly suggested, by the history.
  2. Consultation models and patient-centred care
    Consultation models turn communication into teachable, observable skills.
  3. Communicating risk and shared decision-making
    Patients can only decide well if risks are explained in ways they understand.
  4. Breaking bad news
    How bad news is given affects patients for years.
  5. Principles of physical examination
    Examination is a skill of observation, technique and interpretation, built on consent and dignity.
  6. Vital signs and the early warning score (NEWS2)
    Deterioration is usually preceded by hours of abnormal observations.
  7. The cardiovascular examination
    The cardiovascular examination looks for the signs of heart failure, valve disease, endocarditis and vascular disease, from the hands to the heart.
  8. The respiratory examination
    The respiratory examination combines inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation to locate disease in the airways, lung tissue or pleura.
  9. The abdominal examination
    The abdominal examination detects organ enlargement, masses, fluid, tenderness and the signs of chronic liver disease.
  10. The neurological examination
    The neurological examination localises a lesion, which narrows the causes dramatically.
  11. Clinical reasoning and diagnostic error
    Diagnosis is a reasoning process that can be studied and improved.
  12. Interpreting tests: sensitivity, specificity and predictive values
    No test is perfect, and the same result means different things in different patients.
  13. Interpreting a full blood count and electrolytes
    The full blood count and urea and electrolytes are the most requested tests in hospital.
  14. Arterial blood gas interpretation
    An arterial blood gas tells you about oxygenation, ventilation and acid–base status within minutes.
  15. The 12-lead ECG: a systematic approach
    The ECG is cheap, fast and diagnostic for arrhythmias, ischaemia and electrolyte disturbance.
  16. Chest X-ray interpretation
    The chest X-ray is the most common imaging test in medicine.
  17. Presenting a patient and documenting care
    Clinical information only helps patients if it is communicated clearly.

Clinical Method and Reasoning: questions students ask

What are Neighbour's five checkpoints?

Connecting, summarising, handing over, safety-netting and housekeeping.

How is the number needed to treat calculated?

1 ÷ absolute risk reduction (as a proportion), rounded up.

When is NEWS2 SpO₂ scale 2 used?

For patients with confirmed hypercapnic respiratory failure with a clinician-prescribed target of 88–92%.

Where are the four classic auscultation areas?

Aortic: 2nd right intercostal space; pulmonary: 2nd left intercostal space; tricuspid: lower left sternal edge (4th space); mitral: the apex.
