Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is secondary active transport?

Transport of a substance against its gradient driven by the sodium gradient made by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, as in glucose uptake in the gut. From “The animal cell and its organelles” →

In plain English: Every tissue in the body is built from cells that share the same basic parts. Key idea: Each organelle has one main job: the nucleus stores the code, the RER and Golgi make and package proteins, the SER makes lipids and metabolises drugs, mitochondria make ATP, lysosomes digest, and cilia move fluid.

Name the three ketone bodies.

Acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. From “Lipid metabolism and ketogenesis” →

In plain English: Fat is the body's largest energy store, and how it is mobilised and burnt underlies several of the most important metabolic diseases in practice. Key idea: Ketone bodies form when fat is broken down fast and oxaloacetate is scarce because glucose is in demand: early lactation in cows, late pregnancy in ewes, and insulin deficiency in diabetic animals.

Why do housed piglets need iron soon after birth?

They are born with small iron stores, sow's milk contains little iron and they grow quickly, so they become anaemic without supplementation. From “Minerals, trace elements and vitamins” →

In plain English: Small amounts of minerals and vitamins keep enzymes, bones, blood and nerves working, and deficiencies and toxicities are common causes of disease in farm and companion animals. Key idea: Many deficiency diseases are regional or diet-induced: magnesium on spring grass, copper and cobalt on certain soils, selenium in deficient areas, iron in housed piglets. Sheep tolerate copper poorly, so a supplement safe for cattle can kill them.

What is narrow-sense heritability?

The proportion of phenotypic variance due to additive genetic variance (h² = VA/VP). From “Sex-linked and polygenic traits” →

In plain English: Some traits follow the sex chromosomes and many important ones are controlled by many genes. Key idea: X-linked recessive traits show mostly in males and pass from carrier mothers; birds are ZW females. Polygenic traits respond to selection in proportion to their heritability.

Why does a popular sire threaten a breed's genetic health?

He contributes a large share of the next generation's genes, spreading any hidden recessive alleles and reducing effective population size. From “Population genetics and inbreeding” →

In plain English: Breeds and herds are populations, and their health depends on how genes are shared within them. Key idea: For a rare recessive disease, carriers far outnumber affected animals (2pq is much larger than q²), so DNA testing of carriers is far more effective than removing affected animals.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which organelle makes ATP by aerobic respiration? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The mitochondrion. Mitochondria carry out the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation; the others package, digest and make proteins.

  2. Which level of protein structure is the sequence of amino acids? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Primary. The primary structure is the amino acid sequence encoded by the gene; folding gives the higher levels.

  3. Where does glycolysis take place? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    In the cytoplasm. Glycolysis is cytoplasmic and needs no oxygen; the Krebs cycle is in the matrix and the electron transport chain on the inner membrane.

  4. Glucose is absorbed from the gut lumen into enterocytes against its concentration gradient. Which mechanism is used? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Secondary active transport coupled to sodium. The sodium–glucose cotransporter uses the sodium gradient made by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which is why oral rehydration solutions contain both sodium and glucose.

  5. In an enzyme assay, a drug raises the apparent Km but Vmax is unchanged. What kind of inhibitor is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Competitive. A competitive inhibitor competes for the active site, so more substrate is needed for half-maximal rate, but at saturating substrate the full Vmax is still reached.

  6. A dog on long-term prednisolone has a markedly raised ALP but normal bile acids. What is the most likely explanation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Corticosteroid induction of the canine steroid-induced ALP isoenzyme. Dogs uniquely produce a steroid-induced isoenzyme of ALP; with normal bile acids, liver function is preserved.

  7. A Springer Spaniel puppy from a line with a known inherited disease develops progressive neurological signs, and neurones on biopsy contain swollen vacuoles. Which mechanism best explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Deficiency of a lysosomal enzyme, so undigested substrate accumulates in lysosomes. Vacuolated, swollen neurones in a young dog of a predisposed breed suggest a lysosomal storage disease such as fucosidosis.

  8. A Quarter Horse repeatedly develops stiffness, sweating and dark urine soon after starting exercise, with raised CK. Which explanation is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Polysaccharide storage myopathy from a GYS1 mutation causing abnormal muscle glycogen accumulation. Recurrent exertional rhabdomyolysis in a Quarter Horse suggests PSSM1, confirmed by a DNA test; management centres on diet low in starch and regular exercise, planned with a vet.

  9. An obese cat has eaten nothing for ten days and is now jaundiced. Which explanation is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Hepatic lipidosis from fat mobilised faster than the liver can oxidise or export it. Prolonged anorexia in an obese cat floods the liver with NEFA; fat accumulates in hepatocytes and causes cholestasis and jaundice.

Key takeaways

  • Each organelle has one main job: the nucleus stores the code, the RER and Golgi make and package proteins, the SER makes lipids and metabolises drugs, mitochondria make ATP, lysosomes digest, and cilia move fluid.
  • Enzymes lower activation energy at a specific active site; competitive inhibition raises Km and can be overcome by substrate, non-competitive inhibition lowers Vmax and cannot.
  • Glycolysis gives 2 ATP without oxygen and aerobic respiration about 30–32; the liver keeps blood glucose up by glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis; the pentose phosphate pathway protects red cells.
  • Ketone bodies form when fat is broken down fast and oxaloacetate is scarce because glucose is in demand: early lactation in cows, late pregnancy in ewes, and insulin deficiency in diabetic animals.
  • The liver detoxifies ammonia by making urea; when the liver is bypassed or fails, ammonia reaches the brain and causes hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Ruminants absorb VFAs, not glucose: acetate fuels tissues and milk fat, propionate makes glucose in the liver, butyrate becomes BHB. Rations are balanced on metabolisable energy.

Common mistakes in Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics

  • Assuming every cell has every organelle in the same amount. Mammalian red cells lose their nucleus and mitochondria, plasma cells are packed with RER, and hepatocytes with SER and mitochondria; structure follows function.
  • Reading a liver enzyme rise as liver failure. Enzymes measure cell damage or induction, not function; a dog with high ALT may have normal liver function, and an end-stage liver may have normal enzymes. Function is tested with bile acids, albumin, glucose, urea and clotting.
  • Assuming sugar-free means safe for pets. Xylitol is highly toxic to dogs even in small amounts; any ingestion needs prompt veterinary advice.
  • Assuming a fat animal can safely go without food. Obese cats, ponies and donkeys are the most at risk of fatal lipid mobilisation disorders when they stop eating; inappetence in these animals needs a vet promptly.
  • Thinking a high blood urea always means kidney disease. Urea also rises with a high-protein meal, gastrointestinal bleeding, dehydration and catabolism, and is low in liver failure because the urea cycle is impaired.
  • Treating ketosis, displaced abomasum, milk fever and metritis as separate problems. They share causes in the transition period and often occur in the same cows; prevention addresses the whole transition, planned with the herd's vet and nutritionist.

Lessons in this school

  1. The animal cell and its organelles
    Every tissue in the body is built from cells that share the same basic parts.
  2. Proteins and enzymes
    Proteins do most of the work of the body, and enzymes are the proteins that speed its chemical reactions.
  3. Carbohydrate metabolism
    Glucose is the fuel every cell can use, and many cannot do without it.
  4. Lipid metabolism and ketogenesis
    Fat is the body's largest energy store, and how it is mobilised and burnt underlies several of the most important metabolic diseases in practice.
  5. Protein and nitrogen metabolism
    Animals must dispose of the nitrogen from the protein they break down.
  6. Energy metabolism in the ruminant
    A cow lives on volatile fatty acids rather than glucose, yet her udder needs huge amounts of glucose for milk.
  7. Minerals, trace elements and vitamins
    Small amounts of minerals and vitamins keep enzymes, bones, blood and nerves working, and deficiencies and toxicities are common causes of disease in farm and companion animals.
  8. DNA, genes and gene expression
    Every inherited trait and inherited disease starts in DNA.
  9. Mendelian inheritance in domestic animals
    Many traits and diseases of domestic animals are controlled by single genes and follow Mendel's laws.
  10. Sex-linked and polygenic traits
    Some traits follow the sex chromosomes and many important ones are controlled by many genes.
  11. Inherited diseases and breed health schemes
    Selective breeding has concentrated inherited diseases in many breeds of dogs, cats, horses and cattle, and screening schemes aim to reduce them.
  12. Genetic testing in animals
    DNA tests now let owners, breeders and farmers know an animal's genotype for many diseases and traits, verify parentage and predict genetic merit.
  13. Population genetics and inbreeding
    Breeds and herds are populations, and their health depends on how genes are shared within them.
  14. Animal breeding and selection
    Breeding programmes change populations deliberately, for production, health and temperament, and sometimes create welfare problems by selecting too narrowly.
  15. Embryology and organogenesis
    Every organ, and many birth defects, can be traced to events in the first weeks of development.
  16. Congenital abnormalities
    Congenital abnormalities are defects present at birth, caused by genes, infections, toxins, drugs or nutrition acting on the developing embryo.

Biochemistry, Cell Biology and Genetics: questions students ask

What does a low Km mean?

The enzyme has a high affinity for its substrate, reaching half its maximum rate at a low substrate concentration.

Why is xylitol dangerous to dogs?

It triggers a large insulin release in dogs, causing severe hypoglycaemia, and can cause liver failure.

Which animals are most at risk of hyperlipaemia?

Ponies, donkeys and miniature horses, especially when fat, pregnant, lactating or ill and not eating.

In which organ is urea made?

The liver, by the urea cycle.
