Lessons in this school

  1. Pitch, the stave and clefs
    Western notation shows pitch by position on a five-line stave, and a clef fixes which line stands for which note.
  2. Note names, ledger lines and octave registers
    Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in every octave.
  3. Accidentals: sharps, flats and naturals
    An accidental raises or lowers a note by a semitone or two.
  4. Tones, semitones and enharmonic equivalents
    The semitone is the smallest step in Western tonal music, and two semitones make a tone.
  5. The alto and tenor clefs
    The C clefs place middle C on a chosen line so that instruments with a middle range can avoid ledger lines.
  6. Note values and rests
    Note values show how long each note lasts relative to the beat, and every note value has a matching rest for silence.
  7. Dotted notes and ties
    A dot after a note adds half its value, and a tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one sound.
  8. Pulse, beat and metre
    Most music has a regular pulse, and metre organises those beats into repeating patterns of strong and weak.
  9. Simple time signatures
    A time signature tells the performer how many beats are in each bar and what kind of note is the beat.
  10. Compound time signatures
    In compound time each beat is a dotted note that divides into three.
  11. Grouping notes and rests
    Beams and rests are grouped so that the beats of the bar are always visible.
  12. Triplets, duplets and irregular divisions
    Irregular divisions borrow a grouping from another metre: a triplet fits three notes into the time of two, a duplet two into the time of three.
  13. Syncopation, anacrusis and cross-rhythm
    Rhythm becomes interesting when it pulls against the metre.
  14. Irregular and changing metres
    Not all music fits two, three or four equal beats.
  15. Tempo and metronome marks
    Tempo is the speed of the beat, shown by words, usually Italian, and by metronome marks.
  16. Dynamics and articulation
    Dynamics show how loud music is, and articulation shows how each note is begun, held and connected.
  17. Ornaments
    Ornaments are signs that tell a performer to decorate a note with extra notes.
  18. Repeats, signs and score navigation
    Scores save space with repeat signs and directions that send the performer back or forward.

Key terms

Stave
Five horizontal lines and four spaces on which notes are written; position shows pitch
Octave
The interval between two notes with the same letter name, the upper at twice the frequency of the lower
Accidental
A sign before a note that raises or lowers it by one or two semitones or restores the natural note
Semitone
The smallest interval in Western tonal music: one-twelfth of an octave in equal temperament
Alto clef
A C clef with middle C on the middle line of the stave, standard for the viola
Crotchet
A quarter note: a filled notehead with a stem, worth two quavers
Dot
A sign after a note or rest that adds half of its value
Pulse
The regular series of beats underlying most music
Time signature
Two numbers at the start of a piece showing the number of beats per bar and the beat's note value
Compound time
Metre in which each beat is a dotted note dividing into three equal parts
Beam
A thick line joining the stems of quavers or shorter notes, replacing their flags and showing beat groupings
Triplet
Three notes of equal value played in the time of two of the same value
Syncopation
Accenting a weak beat or off-beat, or tying over or silencing a strong beat, against the expected metre
Irregular metre
A metre whose bar can't be divided into equal groups of two or three beats, such as 5/4 or 7/8
