Key terms and formulas at a glance

Stave
Five horizontal lines and four spaces on which notes are written; position shows pitch
Octave
The interval between two notes with the same letter name, the upper at twice the frequency of the lower
Accidental
A sign before a note that raises or lowers it by one or two semitones or restores the natural note
Semitone
The smallest interval in Western tonal music: one-twelfth of an octave in equal temperament
Alto clef
A C clef with middle C on the middle line of the stave, standard for the viola
Crotchet
A quarter note: a filled notehead with a stem, worth two quavers
Dot
A sign after a note or rest that adds half of its value
Pulse
The regular series of beats underlying most music
Time signature
Two numbers at the start of a piece showing the number of beats per bar and the beat's note value
Compound time
Metre in which each beat is a dotted note dividing into three equal parts
Beam
A thick line joining the stems of quavers or shorter notes, replacing their flags and showing beat groupings
Triplet
Three notes of equal value played in the time of two of the same value

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which line of the treble clef is G?

The second line from the bottom: the clef's curl circles it. From “Pitch, the stave and clefs” →

In plain English: Western notation shows pitch by position on a five-line stave, and a clef fixes which line stands for which note. Key idea: A clef turns a stave's lines into named pitches: the treble clef fixes G4 on the second line, the bass clef fixes F3 on the fourth line, and middle C sits on a ledger line between them.

What is a chromatic semitone?

A semitone using the same letter name twice, such as C–C♯ or B♭–B. From “Tones, semitones and enharmonic equivalents” →

In plain English: The semitone is the smallest step in Western tonal music, and two semitones make a tone. Key idea: E–F and B–C are the white-key semitones; a diatonic semitone changes letter, a chromatic one keeps it, and enharmonic notes sound alike but are spelt for their function.

What is the downbeat?

The first beat of a bar, normally the strongest. From “Pulse, beat and metre” →

In plain English: Most music has a regular pulse, and metre organises those beats into repeating patterns of strong and weak. Key idea: Metre groups beats into bars of strong and weak: duple (two), triple (three) or quadruple (four), with each beat dividing in two (simple) or three (compound).

Can a minim rest be used on beats 2–3 of a 4/4 bar?

No: write two crotchet rests so the middle of the bar is visible. From “Grouping notes and rests” →

In plain English: Beams and rests are grouped so that the beats of the bar are always visible. Key idea: Group notes and rests so every beat is visible: beam within the beat (by three in compound time), never hide the middle of a 4/4 bar, and complete each beat's rests before the next.

What does andante mean?

At a walking pace: moderately slow. From “Tempo and metronome marks” →

In plain English: Tempo is the speed of the beat, shown by words, usually Italian, and by metronome marks. Key idea: Tempo terms describe speed and character (largo, adagio, andante, moderato, allegro, presto); rall. and accel. change it gradually, and metronome marks give beats per minute.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Notation and Rhythm

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What are the notes on the lines of the treble clef, from the bottom? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    E G B D F. Treble lines spell E G B D F from the bottom; F A C E fills the spaces between them.

  2. What is the A above middle C in scientific pitch notation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A4. Octave numbers change at C, so the A just above C4 belongs to octave 4.

  3. Which accidental cancels an earlier sharp or flat? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The natural. A natural restores the letter-name note, cancelling any earlier accidental or key-signature sharp or flat.

  4. A note sits in the second space from the bottom of the bass clef. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    C (C3). Bass clef spaces from the bottom are A C E G, so the second space is C, an octave below middle C.

  5. A note sits on the first ledger line below the bass stave. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    E2. The bass stave's bottom line is G2, the space below it F2 and the first ledger line below E2.

  6. In one bar a B has a flat. Later in the same bar another B is written on the same line with no accidental. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    B♭. The flat lasts to the end of the bar on that line, so the second B is still B flat.

  7. A pianist reads a chord with one note in each stave. Why is the grand stave clearer than writing both notes on one stave? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It keeps notes near the five lines and makes middle C a shared reference, so both hands read with few ledger lines. The grand stave divides the piano's range at middle C so each hand reads close to its own stave instead of across many ledger lines.

  8. A violin melody climbs to notes needing six ledger lines above the treble stave for several bars. What would an experienced copyist most likely do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Write the passage an octave lower with 8va above it. Notation guides recommend an octave sign once ledger lines become hard to count; a bass clef would make high notes worse, not better.

  9. An editor adds a bracketed natural to an F in the bar after an F♯. A student says this is wrong because the barline already cancelled the sharp. Which is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The natural is a correct cautionary accidental: redundant by rule but helpful to performers. Cautionary accidentals don't change pitch; they prevent the common error of carrying an accidental over the barline.

Key takeaways

  • A clef turns a stave's lines into named pitches: the treble clef fixes G4 on the second line, the bass clef fixes F3 on the fourth line, and middle C sits on a ledger line between them.
  • Seven letter names repeat in every octave; octave numbers (middle C = C4) or Helmholtz primes (middle C = c′) say exactly which one is meant, and an octave always begins on C.
  • An accidental lasts until the end of the bar, for that line or space only; the barline cancels it, and a tie carries it only to the tied note.
  • E–F and B–C are the white-key semitones; a diatonic semitone changes letter, a chromatic one keeps it, and enharmonic notes sound alike but are spelt for their function.
  • Every C clef puts middle C on the line through its centre: the third line in alto clef (viola), the fourth line in tenor clef (upper cello, bassoon and trombone).
  • Each note value is half the previous one (semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver, semiquaver), every value has an equal rest, and the semibreve rest fills any whole silent bar.

Common mistakes in Notation and Rhythm

  • Reading bass clef as if it were treble. A note on the bottom line is E in treble clef but G in bass clef; learners who memorise only treble positions misread every bass note by a third (two letter names).
  • Assuming the octave number changes at A. In scientific pitch notation it changes at C: B3 is directly below C4, so the A just below middle C is A3 and the A just above is A4.
  • Carrying an accidental into the next bar. Players often keep a sharp from the previous bar; the rule is that the barline cancels it, which is exactly why editors add cautionary accidentals there.
  • Treating enharmonic spellings as interchangeable. In an exam a G♭ written where the key demands F♯ is marked wrong, because the spelling changes the interval and shows the wrong function.
  • Reading alto clef as treble shifted by one note. The shift is a seventh with an octave change, which is unreliable to apply mentally; anchor on middle C on the middle line instead.
  • Confusing the semibreve and minim rests. The semibreve rest hangs from the fourth line, the minim rest sits on the third; a memory aid is that the heavier, longer rest "hangs" and the lighter one "sits".

Lessons in this school

  1. Pitch, the stave and clefs
    Western notation shows pitch by position on a five-line stave, and a clef fixes which line stands for which note.
  2. Note names, ledger lines and octave registers
    Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in every octave.
  3. Accidentals: sharps, flats and naturals
    An accidental raises or lowers a note by a semitone or two.
  4. Tones, semitones and enharmonic equivalents
    The semitone is the smallest step in Western tonal music, and two semitones make a tone.
  5. The alto and tenor clefs
    The C clefs place middle C on a chosen line so that instruments with a middle range can avoid ledger lines.
  6. Note values and rests
    Note values show how long each note lasts relative to the beat, and every note value has a matching rest for silence.
  7. Dotted notes and ties
    A dot after a note adds half its value, and a tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one sound.
  8. Pulse, beat and metre
    Most music has a regular pulse, and metre organises those beats into repeating patterns of strong and weak.
  9. Simple time signatures
    A time signature tells the performer how many beats are in each bar and what kind of note is the beat.
  10. Compound time signatures
    In compound time each beat is a dotted note that divides into three.
  11. Grouping notes and rests
    Beams and rests are grouped so that the beats of the bar are always visible.
  12. Triplets, duplets and irregular divisions
    Irregular divisions borrow a grouping from another metre: a triplet fits three notes into the time of two, a duplet two into the time of three.
  13. Syncopation, anacrusis and cross-rhythm
    Rhythm becomes interesting when it pulls against the metre.
  14. Irregular and changing metres
    Not all music fits two, three or four equal beats.
  15. Tempo and metronome marks
    Tempo is the speed of the beat, shown by words, usually Italian, and by metronome marks.
  16. Dynamics and articulation
    Dynamics show how loud music is, and articulation shows how each note is begun, held and connected.
  17. Ornaments
    Ornaments are signs that tell a performer to decorate a note with extra notes.
  18. Repeats, signs and score navigation
    Scores save space with repeat signs and directions that send the performer back or forward.

Notation and Rhythm: questions students ask

In scientific pitch notation, what is middle C?

C4. The A above it is A4, tuned to 440 Hz.

What does a double flat do?

Lowers a note by two semitones; B double flat sounds the same as A.

Which pairs of white keys are a semitone apart?

E–F and B–C, which have no black key between them.

Where is middle C in the alto clef?

On the middle (third) line.
