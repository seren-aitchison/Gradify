Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How does 6/8 differ from 3/4?

6/8 has two dotted-crotchet beats, each dividing into three; 3/4 has three crotchet beats dividing into two. From “Hearing pulse and metre” →

In plain English: Every aural skill rests on feeling the beat. Key idea: Find the pulse, locate the strong beats to hear the grouping, then listen to how each beat divides: twos mean simple time, threes mean compound time.

Which syllable pairs are always semitones in a major scale?

Mi–fa and ti–do. From “Solfège and movable do” →

In plain English: Solfège gives each scale degree a syllable, so singers hear notes by their function in the key. Key idea: Movable do names notes by their scale degree, so patterns sound and feel the same in every key; minor can be la-based or do-based, and chromatic syllables and hand signs extend the system.

Which triad contains a tritone between its outer notes?

The diminished triad. From “Hearing chord quality” →

In plain English: Chords have characteristic colours. Key idea: Major and minor triads sound stable, diminished sounds tense and augmented unsettled; seventh chords add colour, and singing up from the bass and checking the fifth confirm the quality.

Why write the bass before the chords?

The bass usually determines the root or inversion, narrowing the choices. From “Harmonic dictation” →

In plain English: Harmonic dictation means writing down the chords of a progression. Key idea: Fix the key, write the bass and soprano, then choose Roman numerals that fit both, guided by the common patterns of tonal progressions and confirmed by chord quality.

What accidental signals C major moving to G major?

F♯. From “Hearing modulation” →

In plain English: Modulation is a change of key. Key idea: Notice the shift of home note, confirmed by a cadence and a consistent new accidental, then compare the new tonic with the old to name the modulation: dominant, subdominant or relative.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Ear Training and Musicianship

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A waltz is felt as strong–weak–weak. What is its metre? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Triple. Applying the standard result: Three beats per group is triple metre.

  2. What should you do on the first hearing of a rhythmic dictation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Find the pulse and metre. Applying the standard result: The metre gives the frame.

  3. What should you do before sight-singing a melody? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Establish the key with the tonic chord. Applying the standard result: Orientation fixes the tonal centre.

  4. Each beat in a piece divides into three equal parts. What kind of time is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Compound time. Division into three defines compound time.

  5. A beat in 6/8 sounds as "long-short". Which pattern is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A crotchet followed by a quaver. In compound time, long-short is crotchet plus quaver.

  6. A melody leaps from the tonic up to the fifth. What helps you sing it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Hearing it within the tonic chord. Applying the standard result: The fifth is a note of the tonic triad.

  7. A piece has six quavers per bar grouped 3 + 3. Which time signature fits best? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    6/8. Two groups of three quavers make compound duple.

  8. A student's bar in 4/4 adds up to four and a half beats. What is the likely error? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A note value is too long, perhaps a missed dot or tie. Applying the standard result: Each bar must total the time signature.

  9. Midway through a sight-singing test, you sing a wrong note. What is best practice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Keep the pulse going and recover. Continuity is valued over a single pitch.

Key takeaways

  • Find the pulse, locate the strong beats to hear the grouping, then listen to how each beat divides: twos mean simple time, threes mean compound time.
  • Fix the metre and mark the beats before filling in detail, hear each beat as a familiar pattern, and always check that each bar adds up to the time signature.
  • Establish the key with the tonic chord and scale, prepare the rhythm and difficult leaps, and sing with a steady pulse while hearing each note in relation to the tonic.
  • Movable do names notes by their scale degree, so patterns sound and feel the same in every key; minor can be la-based or do-based, and chromatic syllables and hand signs extend the system.
  • Recognise intervals by their quality, consonant or dissonant, bright or dark, use reference tunes as an early aid, and hear intervals as scale-degree relationships within a key.
  • Memorise before writing, fix the rhythm and anchor notes, then fill in using contour and scale degrees, and finish by singing and checking your answer.

Common mistakes in Ear Training and Musicianship

  • Confusing tempo with metre. A fast piece in 3/4 is still triple; speed changes the pulse rate, not the grouping.
  • Writing notes before fixing the metre. Without a frame of beats, durations are easy to misjudge.
  • Stopping to fix every wrong note. In sight-singing, as in performance, keeping the pulse matters more than a single pitch.
  • Mixing fixed and movable do. In movable do, do changes with the key; in fixed do, do is always C.
  • Relying on reference tunes for ever. They are slow under exam conditions; aim to hear each interval's character directly.
  • Writing note by note as the melody plays. This loses the shape of the phrase; memorising first is more reliable.

Ear Training and Musicianship: questions students ask

Why mark the beats before the notes?

It gives a frame that makes each duration easier to judge.

Why sing the tonic triad before a sight-singing test?

It fixes the key in your ear so each note can be related to the tonic.

Why practise descending intervals separately?

They sound different from ascending ones and need their own recognition.

Why is memorising the melody the first step?

Without holding it in memory, you cannot work out pitches and rhythm reliably.
