Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What three notes make up a triad?

The root, the third and the fifth. From “Triads: root, third and fifth” →

In plain English: A triad is a three-note chord built from a root, a third above it and a fifth above it. Key idea: A triad stacks two thirds above a root: root, third and fifth; whatever the spacing, the root is the note from which the other two form a third and a fifth.

Which three chords are the primary triads?

I, IV and V: tonic, subdominant and dominant. From “Diatonic triads and Roman numerals” →

In plain English: Each degree of a scale can carry a triad built from the notes of the key. Key idea: Diatonic triads in major are I ii iii IV V vi vii°; in minor (harmonic) i ii° III V vi vii° with III usually major; Roman numerals show both degree and quality.

What is ii7 in F major?

Gm7 (G–B♭–D–F). From “Diatonic seventh chords” →

In plain English: Adding a seventh to each diatonic triad gives the diatonic seventh chords of a key. Key idea: In major, diatonic sevenths are Imaj7 ii7 iii7 IVmaj7 V7 vi7 viiø7; in minor, V7 is dominant and vii°7 fully diminished; ii7–V7–I links roots falling by fifths with smooth voice leading.

How many diatonic triads contain any given melody note?

Three: with the note as root, third or fifth. From “Harmonising a melody” →

In plain English: Harmonising a melody means choosing chords that fit its notes and support its phrases. Key idea: Harmonise by planning cadences first, choosing chords that contain the melody's strong-beat notes, following functional progressions, and writing a bass in contrary motion while avoiding parallel fifths and octaves.

On which scale degree is the Neapolitan chord built?

The flattened second degree (♭2). From “The Neapolitan sixth” →

In plain English: The Neapolitan sixth is a major chord built on the flattened second degree, usually in first inversion, used as a dramatic predominant. Key idea: The Neapolitan is a major triad on ♭2, usually in first inversion, acting as a predominant that moves to V, often through a cadential 6/4.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chords and Harmony

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What interval separates the root and fifth of a triad? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A fifth. Two stacked thirds make a fifth from root to top note.

  2. Which triad is augmented? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    C–E–G♯. Two major thirds stacked give an augmented fifth from root to top.

  3. A G major triad has B in the bass. What inversion is it? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    First inversion. B is the third of G major, so the chord is in first inversion.

  4. A chord has the notes E, C and G spread across two octaves. What is its root? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    C. Rearranged into thirds, the notes form C–E–G, so C is the root.

  5. What is the quality of the triad F♯–A–C? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Diminished. F♯ to A is a minor third; F♯ to C is a diminished fifth.

  6. In D major, the chord A–D–F♯ has A in the bass. How is it labelled? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ic (I6/4). D–F♯–A is the tonic triad; A in the bass is the fifth, giving second inversion.

  7. A student says the chord with G in the bass and C and E above has root G. What is the error? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The bass note isn't always the root; arranged in thirds the notes are C–E–G, so the root is C. Inversions put the third or fifth in the bass; the root comes from the stack of thirds.

  8. A student writes G diminished as G–A♯–C♯. What is wrong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The letter names should form a stack of thirds: G–B♭–D♭. Thirds must use alternate letters; G–B♭–D♭ is the correct spelling.

  9. A student harmonises a melody using several second-inversion chords in a row. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Second inversions are unstable and are normally used only in patterns such as the cadential 6/4. Common-practice style restricts 6/4 chords to specific contexts.

Key takeaways

  • A triad stacks two thirds above a root: root, third and fifth; whatever the spacing, the root is the note from which the other two form a third and a fifth.
  • Major triads have a major third and perfect fifth; minor a minor third and perfect fifth; diminished a minor third and diminished fifth; augmented a major third and augmented fifth.
  • The bass note decides the inversion: root (a, 5/3), third (b, 6) or fifth (c, 6/4); second inversions are used mainly in fixed patterns such as the cadential 6/4.
  • Diatonic triads in major are I ii iii IV V vi vii°; in minor (harmonic) i ii° III V vi vii° with III usually major; Roman numerals show both degree and quality.
  • Chord symbols name the root and quality (C, Cm, Cdim, Caug), add sevenths and extensions (C7, Cmaj7), and use slashes for bass notes (C/E).
  • V7 adds a minor seventh to the dominant triad; its tritone resolves inwards, the leading note rising and the seventh falling, giving the strongest move to the tonic.

Common mistakes in Chords and Harmony

  • Assuming the lowest note is always the root. In inversions and spread chords, another note can be in the bass; rearrange the notes into thirds to find the root.
  • Changing letter names when altering a triad. C diminished is C–E♭–G♭, not C–D♯–F♯: keep the letter names of a stack of thirds and change only the accidentals.
  • Using second-inversion chords freely. In common-practice harmony, 6/4 chords are unstable and appear mainly as cadential, passing or pedal 6/4s.
  • Writing V as minor in a minor key. In tonal music the dominant in a minor key is normally major, using the raised leading note, so that it pulls strongly to the tonic.
  • Confusing C7 with Cmaj7. C7 has a minor seventh (B♭), the dominant seventh; Cmaj7 has a major seventh (B).
  • Letting the seventh rise or leap. In common-practice style the seventh of V7 resolves down by step.

Lessons in this school

  1. Triads: root, third and fifth
    A triad is a three-note chord built from a root, a third above it and a fifth above it.
  2. Major, minor, diminished and augmented triads
    Triads come in four qualities, depending on the size of their thirds and fifth.
  3. Inversions and figured bass symbols
    A triad can be arranged with its root, third or fifth in the bass.
  4. Diatonic triads and Roman numerals
    Each degree of a scale can carry a triad built from the notes of the key.
  5. Chord symbols in popular music
    Popular and jazz music uses chord symbols written above the melody instead of Roman numerals.
  6. The dominant seventh
    The dominant seventh chord, V7, adds a minor seventh to the dominant triad and is the strongest chord leading to the tonic.
  7. Types of seventh chord
    Adding a seventh to a triad creates a seventh chord.
  8. Diatonic seventh chords
    Adding a seventh to each diatonic triad gives the diatonic seventh chords of a key.
  9. Tonic, predominant and dominant function
    Chords in a key fall into functional groups: tonic chords give rest, predominant chords prepare the dominant, and dominant chords drive back to the tonic.
  10. Progressions by fifth and the circle of fifths sequence
    Root movement by falling fifth is the strongest and most common progression in tonal music.
  11. Harmonic rhythm
    Harmonic rhythm is the rate at which chords change.
  12. Harmonising a melody
    Harmonising a melody means choosing chords that fit its notes and support its phrases.
  13. Secondary dominants
    A secondary dominant is the dominant of a chord other than the tonic, borrowed from another key.
  14. Modulation to related keys
    Modulation is a change of key confirmed by a cadence.
  15. Modal mixture
    Modal mixture (borrowed chords) means using chords from the parallel minor in a major key, or vice versa.
  16. The Neapolitan sixth
    The Neapolitan sixth is a major chord built on the flattened second degree, usually in first inversion, used as a dramatic predominant.
  17. Augmented sixth chords
    Augmented sixth chords are chromatic predominants containing an augmented sixth that expands outwards to an octave on the dominant.
  18. The diminished seventh chord
    The diminished seventh is a four-note chord of stacked minor thirds, built on the leading note in minor keys.
  19. Extended chords: ninths, elevenths and thirteenths
    Extended chords add notes beyond the seventh by continuing to stack thirds.

Chords and Harmony: questions students ask

What intervals make up a minor triad?

A minor third and a perfect fifth above the root.

What decides a chord's inversion?

Only the bass note; the order of the upper notes doesn't matter.

What does C/E mean?

A C chord with E in the bass: first inversion.

How does the seventh of a dominant seventh chord normally resolve?

Down by step to the third of the tonic chord.
