Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is homorhythm?

A texture in which all the parts move together in the same rhythm. From “Monophony, homophony and polyphony” →

In plain English: Texture describes how the musical lines in a piece relate to each other. Key idea: Monophony is one line, polyphony combines independent lines and homophony supports one melody; homophony may be melody-dominated or homorhythmic, and composers often shift between textures for contrast and expression.

Why are parallel fifths and octaves forbidden in counterpoint?

They make the two lines lose independence and sound like one. From “Principles of species counterpoint” →

In plain English: Species counterpoint is a step-by-step method for learning to write independent lines that sound well together. Key idea: Species counterpoint, codified by Fux in 1725, teaches independent part-writing against a cantus firmus; intervals are classed as perfect consonances, imperfect consonances or dissonances, and contrary motion is preferred while parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden.

What is a neighbour tone?

A note that steps away from a consonance and returns to the same note. From “Third species” →

In plain English: Third species places four notes against each note of the cantus firmus. Key idea: Third species sets four notes against one, with the first beat consonant and other beats allowing passing tones, neighbour tones, double neighbours and the cambiata, producing a flowing line that must still avoid parallel perfect intervals between downbeats.

What is a round?

A canon at the unison that can be repeated indefinitely. From “Imitation and canon” →

In plain English: Imitation is the repetition of a melodic idea in another voice. Key idea: Imitation repeats an idea in another voice, freely or strictly; a canon is strict imitation throughout, from rounds at the unison to canons at other intervals and with transformations, as in Bach's Goldberg canons.

What does a sixth become under inversion at the octave?

A third. From “Invertible counterpoint” →

In plain English: Invertible counterpoint lets two lines swap places, upper becoming lower, and still sound correct. Key idea: Invertible counterpoint lets parts swap places; at the octave, intervals become 9 − n, so thirds and sixths stay consonant but fifths become dissonant fourths, while inversion at the tenth and twelfth brings other constraints.

  1. Which repertoire is the classic example of Western monophony? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Gregorian chant. Applying the standard result: Chant is a single unaccompanied line.

  2. A bagpipe holds a sustained note beneath the melody. What is this called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A drone. Applying the standard result: A sustained bass note is a drone.

  3. A passage has only a solo flute and a cello. How would you describe its density? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Thin. Applying the standard result: Few parts give a thin texture.

  4. A melody is accompanied by broken-chord figures in the left hand. What texture is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Melody-dominated homophony. Applying the standard result: A tune with distinct accompaniment.

  5. Gabrieli's music for two choirs answering each other across a church illustrates: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Antiphony. Applying the standard result: Alternating groups are antiphonal.

  6. Violins and flutes play the same tune an octave apart. What is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Doubling at the octave. Applying the standard result: The same line in octaves is a doubling.

  7. In which period did homophony become the dominant texture? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The Classical period. Clarity of melody and accompaniment defined Classical style.

  8. A melody passes from flute to violin to horn, one note each, changing colour. What technique is this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Klangfarbenmelodie. Applying the standard result: Timbre changes note by note.

  9. Which description of texture is most detailed? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    "A high violin melody over a low pedal, with a woodwind countermelody". Detail includes layers, register and roles.

Key takeaways

  • Monophony is one line, polyphony combines independent lines and homophony supports one melody; homophony may be melody-dominated or homorhythmic, and composers often shift between textures for contrast and expression.
  • Heterophony presents simultaneous varied versions of one melody and is central to many musical traditions; other textures include drones, ostinati, antiphony, call and response, monody and twentieth-century pointillist and layered textures.
  • Describing texture means naming its type and then its density, register, spacing and doublings, the roles of melody, bass, inner parts and countermelodies, and how the texture changes through the passage.
  • Species counterpoint, codified by Fux in 1725, teaches independent part-writing against a cantus firmus; intervals are classed as perfect consonances, imperfect consonances or dissonances, and contrary motion is preferred while parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden.
  • First species places one consonant note against each cantus firmus note, starting and ending on perfect consonances, using mostly imperfect consonances and contrary motion, avoiding parallel perfect intervals, with a singable line and a stepwise contrary cadence.
  • Second species sets two notes against one: strong beats must be consonant, weak beats may carry a stepwise passing dissonance, and parallel perfect intervals between successive strong beats remain forbidden.

Common mistakes in Counterpoint and Texture

  • Calling a hymn polyphonic because it has four parts. If the parts move together in rhythm, the texture is homophonic.
  • Treating heterophony as poor ensemble playing. In many traditions it is a deliberate, skilled texture.
  • Giving only a single word, such as "homophonic". Examiners expect detail about layers, register and change.
  • Treating the perfect fourth as consonant in two-part writing. In strict two-part counterpoint above the bass, it is a dissonance.
  • Ending with a third or sixth. In first species the final interval must be a unison or octave.
  • Placing a dissonance on the strong beat in second species. Only weak beats may be dissonant.

Lessons in this school

  1. Monophony, homophony and polyphony
    Texture describes how the musical lines in a piece relate to each other.
  2. Heterophony and other textures
    Not all music fits the three standard textures.
  3. Describing texture
    Exam questions often ask you to describe texture in detail.
  4. Principles of species counterpoint
    Species counterpoint is a step-by-step method for learning to write independent lines that sound well together.
  5. First species
    First species places one note against each note of the cantus firmus.
  6. Second species
    Second species places two notes against each note of the cantus firmus.
  7. Third species
    Third species places four notes against each note of the cantus firmus.
  8. Fourth species
    Fourth species uses syncopation to create suspensions, the most expressive dissonance of Renaissance and Baroque music.
  9. Fifth species
    Fifth species, or florid counterpoint, combines the rhythms of the earlier species into a free, varied line.
  10. Imitation and canon
    Imitation is the repetition of a melodic idea in another voice.
  11. The two-part invention
    Bach's fifteen two-part Inventions are miniature masterpieces of counterpoint written for teaching.
  12. Fugue
    A fugue is a contrapuntal composition built on a subject that enters in each voice in turn.
  13. Invertible counterpoint
    Invertible counterpoint lets two lines swap places, upper becoming lower, and still sound correct.
  14. Augmentation, diminution, inversion and retrograde
    Composers transform themes by changing their rhythm, direction or order.
  15. The chorale prelude
    The chorale prelude is an organ piece based on a Lutheran hymn tune.

Counterpoint and Texture: questions students ask

What is antiphony?

Alternation between two or more groups of performers.

What is a countermelody?

A secondary melody heard against the main melody.

How should a leap in a counterpoint line usually be treated?

It should be followed by a step in the opposite direction.

Which dissonance is allowed in second species?

The passing tone on the weak beat.
