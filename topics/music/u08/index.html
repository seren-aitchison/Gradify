Key terms and formulas at a glance

Frequency
The number of complete vibrations per second, measured in hertz
Fundamental
The lowest frequency of a vibrating system, which determines the perceived pitch
Standing wave
A wave pattern with fixed nodes and antinodes formed by two waves travelling in opposite directions
Beats
Periodic variations in loudness when two tones of slightly different frequency sound together
Decibel
A logarithmic unit comparing a sound level with a reference level
Reverberation time
The time for sound in a room to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops
Pizzicato
Plucking the strings instead of bowing
Double reed
Two blades of cane that vibrate against each other, as in the oboe and bassoon
Valve
A mechanism that diverts air through extra tubing to lower the pitch
Idiophone
An instrument whose own body vibrates to produce sound
Plectrum
A small pick that plucks a string, as in a harpsichord jack
Vocal folds
Folds of tissue in the larynx that vibrate to produce voiced sound

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What frequency is concert A (A4)?

440 Hz, by international standard. From “Sound waves: frequency, amplitude and pitch” →

In plain English: Musical sound is a pressure wave travelling through the air. Key idea: Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave; frequency in hertz determines pitch, with each octave doubling frequency, and amplitude mainly determines loudness.

How many decibels are added when the intensity doubles?

About 3 dB. From “Loudness, decibels and hearing” →

In plain English: Our ears respond to an enormous range of sound intensities, so loudness is measured on a logarithmic scale. Key idea: The decibel scale is logarithmic: +3 dB doubles the intensity and +10 dB sounds about twice as loud; hearing is most sensitive in the middle frequencies, and prolonged loud sound causes permanent damage.

How much does the second valve lower the pitch?

One semitone. From “The brass family” →

In plain English: Brass players make sound by buzzing their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece. Key idea: Brass players buzz their lips to select harmonics; valves and slides add tubing so the instruments can play chromatically; trumpet, horn, trombone and tuba give brilliance, warmth and weight.

What does a low-pass filter do in subtractive synthesis?

It removes higher harmonics, making the sound darker or softer. From “Electronic instruments and music technology” →

In plain English: Electronic instruments generate and shape sound with circuits and software. Key idea: From the theremin to software, electronic instruments create sound with circuits and code; subtractive synthesis shapes rich waveforms with filters and envelopes, and MIDI carries performance data, not sound.

Why are brass often marked one dynamic level lower than strings in tutti passages?

Brass are much more powerful, so they would otherwise cover the strings. From “Doubling and balance” →

In plain English: Doubling a line on more than one instrument changes its weight, colour and clarity. Key idea: Unison doubling blends and strengthens, octave doubling brightens or deepens, and balance depends on the different power of instruments; adjust dynamics and doubling so the main line stays clear.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Sound, Instruments and Orchestration

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the unit of frequency? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Hertz. Frequency is measured in hertz, vibrations per second.

  2. What is the frequency of the third harmonic of a 100 Hz fundamental? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    300 Hz. Harmonics are whole-number multiples: 3 × 100 = 300 Hz.

  3. Which change raises the pitch of a string? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Increasing its tension. Mersenne's laws: higher tension raises the frequency.

  4. Which physical property mainly determines how loud a sound seems? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Amplitude. Larger pressure variations carry more energy and are heard as louder.

  5. Which interval lies between the 4th and 5th harmonics? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A major third. The 5th and 4th harmonics are in the ratio 5:4, a pure major third.

  6. Why does a clarinet overblow a twelfth rather than an octave? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It behaves like a closed pipe, so the next harmonic is the 3rd. A closed cylinder lacks even harmonics, so the next mode is the 3rd harmonic, a twelfth above.

  7. Why are bass loudspeakers and instruments usually large? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Low notes have long wavelengths that need large radiating surfaces or air columns. A 55 Hz note has a wavelength over 6 m, so efficient low-frequency sound needs size.

  8. Why can a flute and an oboe playing the same pitch sound so different? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their harmonics have different strengths and their attacks differ. Same fundamental, but different spectra and envelopes give different timbres.

  9. Where are the nodes on a string vibrating in its fundamental mode? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    At each fixed end. The fixed ends cannot move, so they are nodes; the antinode is in the middle.

Key takeaways

  • Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave; frequency in hertz determines pitch, with each octave doubling frequency, and amplitude mainly determines loudness.
  • Every pitched note contains a fundamental and whole-number harmonics; their ratios give the pure intervals, and the strength of each harmonic, plus the envelope, creates timbre.
  • Strings and air columns sound through standing waves; string pitch rises with shorter length, higher tension and lower mass; closed pipes produce only odd harmonics and sound an octave lower than open pipes.
  • Beats occur at the difference of two close frequencies and are used for tuning; because pure intervals cannot all fit a twelve-note keyboard, systems from Pythagorean to equal temperament distribute the error differently.
  • The decibel scale is logarithmic: +3 dB doubles the intensity and +10 dB sounds about twice as loud; hearing is most sensitive in the middle frequencies, and prolonged loud sound causes permanent damage.
  • Listeners hear direct sound, early reflections and reverberation; reverberation time rises with room volume and falls with absorption, and good halls match reverberation to the music.

Common mistakes in Sound, Instruments and Orchestration

  • Thinking higher notes travel faster. All audible frequencies travel at the same speed in air; higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
  • Assuming harmonics match piano pitches exactly. Several, such as the 7th and 11th, are noticeably out of tune with equal temperament.
  • Saying the clarinet overblows an octave. As an approximately closed cylinder it overblows a twelfth, to the third harmonic.
  • Thinking equal temperament makes every interval pure. It makes every key equal by making every interval except the octave slightly impure.
  • Thinking two equal sources double the decibel reading. Two equal sources add about 3 dB, not double the number.
  • Thinking more reverberation is always better. Too much blurs fast music and speech; the ideal depends on the repertoire.

Lessons in this school

  1. Sound waves: frequency, amplitude and pitch
    Musical sound is a pressure wave travelling through the air.
  2. The harmonic series and timbre
    A single musical note contains many frequencies sounding together.
  3. Standing waves in strings and pipes
    Strings and air columns produce musical notes by forming standing waves.
  4. Beats, tuning and temperament
    When two nearly matching notes sound together they produce beats, which musicians use to tune.
  5. Loudness, decibels and hearing
    Our ears respond to an enormous range of sound intensities, so loudness is measured on a logarithmic scale.
  6. Room acoustics
    The room is part of the instrument: its size, shape and surfaces shape how music sounds.
  7. The string family
    Bowed strings form the core of the orchestra.
  8. The woodwind family
    Woodwind instruments make sound by setting an air column vibrating with an edge or a reed.
  9. The brass family
    Brass players make sound by buzzing their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece.
  10. Percussion
    Percussion instruments are struck, shaken or scraped and range from tuned timpani to unpitched cymbals.
  11. Keyboard instruments
    Keyboard instruments let one player control many notes at once.
  12. The voice
    The voice is the oldest instrument and the only one carried inside the body.
  13. Electronic instruments and music technology
    Electronic instruments generate and shape sound with circuits and software.
  14. Transposing instruments
    For some instruments, the written note is not the note that sounds.
  15. Ranges and registers
    Every instrument has a range of possible notes and distinct registers with different colours and strengths.
  16. The orchestra and its layout
    The symphony orchestra grew from a small Baroque ensemble into the large Romantic orchestra.
  17. Doubling and balance
    Doubling a line on more than one instrument changes its weight, colour and clarity.
  18. Writing for strings
    The string section is the most flexible part of the orchestra.
  19. Orchestral colour
    Orchestral colour is the art of combining instruments to create distinctive timbres.
  20. Reading a full score
    A full score shows every instrument's part, arranged in a standard order.
  21. Chamber ensembles
    Chamber music is played by small groups with one player to a part and usually no conductor.

Sound, Instruments and Orchestration: questions students ask

What two factors mainly determine timbre?

The harmonic spectrum (which harmonics and how strong) and the envelope, especially the attack.

What happens to the pitch of a string if its length is halved?

It rises by an octave (the frequency doubles).

What is a cent?

One hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone; there are 1200 cents in an octave.

At what daily exposure must employers provide hearing protection under the 2005 Regulations?

85 dB(A), the upper exposure action value.
