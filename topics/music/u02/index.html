Lessons in this school

  1. The major scale
    The major scale is the basis of most Western tonal music.
  2. Key signatures and the circle of fifths
    A key signature gathers a key's sharps or flats at the start of each stave.
  3. Scale degrees and their names
    Each note of a scale has a number and a technical name that describe its function in the key.
  4. Natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales
    Minor scales come in three forms that share a lower half but differ in their sixth and seventh degrees.
  5. Relative and tonic minor keys
    Every major key has a relative minor that shares its key signature, and a tonic minor that shares its tonic.
  6. Minor key signatures
    A minor key uses the key signature of its relative major.
  7. Finding the key of a melody
    Working out the key of a melody is a core skill in analysis and aural work.
  8. The chromatic scale
    The chromatic scale moves entirely by semitones through all twelve pitches in the octave.
  9. Pentatonic and blues scales
    Pentatonic scales have five notes and are among the most widespread scales in the world's music.
  10. Whole-tone and octatonic scales
    Whole-tone and octatonic scales are symmetrical: they divide the octave into equal or alternating steps.
  11. The modes
    The modes are seven-note scales built on each degree of the major scale.
  12. Interval number and quality
    An interval is the distance between two pitches.
  13. Major, minor and perfect intervals
    Every interval from the tonic up to a note of the major scale is either major or perfect.
  14. Augmented and diminished intervals
    Augmented and diminished intervals are a semitone larger or smaller than major, minor or perfect intervals.
  15. Compound intervals
    A compound interval is larger than an octave.
  16. Inverting intervals
    Inverting an interval means moving the lower note up an octave, or the upper note down.
  17. Consonance and dissonance
    Consonance and dissonance describe how stable or tense an interval or chord sounds.
  18. Transposition
    Transposing means writing or playing music at a different pitch while keeping its intervals.

Key terms

Tonic
The first and main note of a scale or key
Key signature
The sharps or flats at the start of each stave that apply throughout the music
Dominant
The fifth degree of a scale, second in importance to the tonic
Harmonic minor
A minor scale with a raised seventh degree, giving an augmented second between 6 and 7
Relative minor
The minor key sharing a major key's signature, starting on its sixth degree
Inner circle
The minor keys arranged by fifths, matching their relative majors on the circle of fifths
Modulation
A change of key within a piece, confirmed by a cadence in the new key
Chromatic scale
A scale of twelve notes, each a semitone apart
Pentatonic scale
A five-note scale; the major pentatonic uses degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6
Whole-tone scale
A six-note scale in which every step is a tone
Mode
A seven-note scale defined by its pattern of tones and semitones from a given tonic
Interval
The distance in pitch between two notes
Major interval
A second, third, sixth or seventh as found between the tonic and a note of its major scale
Augmented interval
A perfect or major interval enlarged by a semitone
