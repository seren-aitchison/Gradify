Key terms and formulas at a glance

Tonic
The first and main note of a scale or key
Key signature
The sharps or flats at the start of each stave that apply throughout the music
Dominant
The fifth degree of a scale, second in importance to the tonic
Harmonic minor
A minor scale with a raised seventh degree, giving an augmented second between 6 and 7
Relative minor
The minor key sharing a major key's signature, starting on its sixth degree
Inner circle
The minor keys arranged by fifths, matching their relative majors on the circle of fifths
Modulation
A change of key within a piece, confirmed by a cadence in the new key
Chromatic scale
A scale of twelve notes, each a semitone apart
Pentatonic scale
A five-note scale; the major pentatonic uses degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6
Whole-tone scale
A six-note scale in which every step is a tone
Mode
A seven-note scale defined by its pattern of tones and semitones from a given tonic
Interval
The distance in pitch between two notes

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Between which degrees of a major scale do the semitones fall?

Between 3 and 4, and between 7 and 8. From “The major scale” →

In plain English: The major scale is the basis of most Western tonal music. Key idea: A major scale is T T S T T T S, using each letter name once; the accidentals are whatever is needed to make that pattern from the chosen tonic.

Why does the melodic minor raise the sixth when rising?

To avoid the awkward augmented second between the sixth and the raised seventh. From “Natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales” →

In plain English: Minor scales come in three forms that share a lower half but differ in their sixth and seventh degrees. Key idea: Natural minor has a flattened 3, 6 and 7 relative to major; harmonic raises 7 for a leading note; melodic raises 6 and 7 going up and uses the natural form coming down.

How many different notes are in a chromatic scale before the octave?

Twelve. From “The chromatic scale” →

In plain English: The chromatic scale moves entirely by semitones through all twelve pitches in the octave. Key idea: The chromatic scale is twelve semitones; the harmonic spelling writes tonic and dominant once and every other letter twice.

What is the characteristic note of the Lydian mode?

The raised fourth. From “The modes” →

In plain English: The modes are seven-note scales built on each degree of the major scale. Key idea: Each mode is the major scale's notes from a different starting degree; it is best remembered as a major or minor scale with one characteristic note altered.

What simple interval is a twelfth equivalent to?

A fifth (12 − 7 = 5). From “Compound intervals” →

In plain English: A compound interval is larger than an octave. Key idea: A compound interval exceeds an octave; subtract 7 to find its simple equivalent, and keep the same quality.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Scales, Keys and Intervals

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the pattern of a major scale? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    T T S T T T S. The major scale has semitones between 3–4 and 7–8, giving T T S T T T S.

  2. What is the order of sharps? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    F C G D A E B. Sharps enter in fifths from F: F C G D A E B. The flat order is the reverse.

  3. Which degree is the mediant? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The third. The mediant is the third degree, halfway between tonic and dominant.

  4. Which note is in E♭ major? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A♭. E♭ major is E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D E♭: the fourth is A♭, making G–A♭ a semitone.

  5. A signature has four flats. What major key is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A♭ major. The four flats are B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭; the second-to-last is A♭.

  6. What is the subdominant of D major? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    G. D major is D E F♯ G A B C♯; the fourth degree is G.

  7. A student writes A major as A B C♯ D E F♯ G A. What is the error? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The seventh should be G♯, so that the scale ends with a semitone G♯–A. A major has three sharps: F♯, C♯ and G♯. Without G♯ the step 7–8 is a tone.

  8. Which pair of major keys are next to each other on the circle of fifths and so differ by only one accidental? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    E♭ major and B♭ major. E♭ (three flats) and B♭ (two flats) are a fifth apart. E and E♭ are far apart: four sharps against three flats.

  9. In E♭ major, which note is the leading note and why does it pull towards the tonic? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    D, because it lies a semitone below the tonic E♭. The leading note is the seventh degree, D, a semitone below E♭. D♭ would be the subtonic.

Key takeaways

  • A major scale is T T S T T T S, using each letter name once; the accidentals are whatever is needed to make that pattern from the chosen tonic.
  • Sharps enter in the order F C G D A E B and flats in reverse; neighbouring keys on the circle of fifths differ by one accidental and are closely related.
  • Scale degrees are named for their relationship to the tonic: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant and leading note.
  • Natural minor has a flattened 3, 6 and 7 relative to major; harmonic raises 7 for a leading note; melodic raises 6 and 7 going up and uses the natural form coming down.
  • The relative minor shares the key signature and starts on degree 6 of the major; the tonic minor shares the tonic but has three more flats or three fewer sharps.
  • A minor key uses its relative major's signature; the raised seventh, and where the music starts and ends, show which of the two keys it is.

Common mistakes in Scales, Keys and Intervals

  • Spelling with the wrong letter names. F major is F G A B♭ C D E F, not F G A A♯ C D E F: A♯ repeats the letter A and skips B.
  • Writing sharps or flats in a key signature out of order. B♭ major is always B♭ then E♭; writing E♭ then B♭ is incorrect even though the same two notes are flattened.
  • Thinking the subdominant is named because it is "just below the dominant". It is a fifth below the tonic, mirroring the dominant a fifth above; that it sits a step below the dominant is a consequence.
  • Writing the raised seventh in the key signature. Key signatures follow the natural minor; the raised notes of harmonic and melodic forms are written as accidentals.
  • Confusing "relative" with "parallel". C minor isn't the relative minor of C major; A minor is. C minor is its tonic (parallel) minor.
  • Assuming no key signature means C major. It could equally be A minor; look for G♯ and an A tonic.

Lessons in this school

  1. The major scale
    The major scale is the basis of most Western tonal music.
  2. Key signatures and the circle of fifths
    A key signature gathers a key's sharps or flats at the start of each stave.
  3. Scale degrees and their names
    Each note of a scale has a number and a technical name that describe its function in the key.
  4. Natural, harmonic and melodic minor scales
    Minor scales come in three forms that share a lower half but differ in their sixth and seventh degrees.
  5. Relative and tonic minor keys
    Every major key has a relative minor that shares its key signature, and a tonic minor that shares its tonic.
  6. Minor key signatures
    A minor key uses the key signature of its relative major.
  7. Finding the key of a melody
    Working out the key of a melody is a core skill in analysis and aural work.
  8. The chromatic scale
    The chromatic scale moves entirely by semitones through all twelve pitches in the octave.
  9. Pentatonic and blues scales
    Pentatonic scales have five notes and are among the most widespread scales in the world's music.
  10. Whole-tone and octatonic scales
    Whole-tone and octatonic scales are symmetrical: they divide the octave into equal or alternating steps.
  11. The modes
    The modes are seven-note scales built on each degree of the major scale.
  12. Interval number and quality
    An interval is the distance between two pitches.
  13. Major, minor and perfect intervals
    Every interval from the tonic up to a note of the major scale is either major or perfect.
  14. Augmented and diminished intervals
    Augmented and diminished intervals are a semitone larger or smaller than major, minor or perfect intervals.
  15. Compound intervals
    A compound interval is larger than an octave.
  16. Inverting intervals
    Inverting an interval means moving the lower note up an octave, or the upper note down.
  17. Consonance and dissonance
    Consonance and dissonance describe how stable or tense an interval or chord sounds.
  18. Transposition
    Transposing means writing or playing music at a different pitch while keeping its intervals.

Scales, Keys and Intervals: questions students ask

How many sharps does A major have?

Three: F♯, C♯ and G♯.

What is the supertonic?

The second degree of a scale, the note above the tonic.

What is raised in the harmonic minor scale?

The seventh degree, to create a leading note.

On which degree of a minor scale does its relative major begin?

The third degree, a minor third above the minor tonic.
