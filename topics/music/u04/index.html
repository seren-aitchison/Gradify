Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which two voices share the bass staff in SATB short score?

Tenor and bass. From “SATB layout and vocal ranges” →

In plain English: Four-part harmony is written for soprano, alto, tenor and bass voices. Key idea: SATB writing places soprano and alto on the treble staff and tenor and bass on the bass staff, with fixed stem directions, and keeps each voice within its comfortable range.

What is contrary motion?

Two voices moving in opposite directions. From “Principles of voice leading” →

In plain English: Good voice leading makes each part a singable melody while the parts together form clear harmony. Key idea: Smooth voice leading keeps common notes, moves upper voices by step, avoids augmented intervals and crossing, and resolves the leading note upwards and sevenths downwards.

How do the sixth and fourth above the bass resolve?

Down by step, to the fifth and third. From “The cadential six-four” →

In plain English: The cadential six-four is a tonic chord in second inversion that decorates the dominant at a cadence. Key idea: The cadential six-four places I in second inversion over the dominant on a strong beat; its sixth and fourth fall by step to V, so it acts as a decorated dominant.

Which chords form an interrupted cadence in a major key?

V to vi. From “The interrupted cadence” →

In plain English: The interrupted cadence sets up a perfect cadence then swerves to another chord, usually vi. Key idea: The interrupted cadence, V–vi (V–VI in minor), defeats the expected tonic; doubling the third of vi lets the leading note rise without consecutives, and the phrase is extended to a later perfect cadence.

In which direction does a standard suspension resolve?

Down by step. From “Suspensions” →

In plain English: A suspension holds a note over a change of harmony, creating a dissonance that then resolves down by step. Key idea: A suspension is prepared as a consonance, held as a dissonance on a strong beat, and resolved down by step; 4–3, 7–6, 9–8 and 2–3 are the common types.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Voice Leading and Cadences

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In SATB short score, which way do the alto's stems point? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Down. The alto shares the treble staff with the soprano and always has stems down.

  2. Which note should not be doubled in the dominant chord? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The leading note. The leading note needs to rise to the tonic, so doubling it causes faults.

  3. What are consecutive octaves? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Two voices an octave apart moving in parallel to another octave. Parallel motion between octaves merges the two lines.

  4. Which note is outside the usual tenor range of C3 to G4? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    B4. B4 is above the tenor's usual top note of G4.

  5. Which chord note may be omitted from a root-position triad? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The fifth. Omitting the fifth keeps the chord's quality, defined by the root and third.

  6. The bass moves F3 to G3 while the alto moves C4 to D4. What fault occurs? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Consecutive fifths. F–C and G–D are both perfect fifths, approached in parallel motion.

  7. A student writes a tenor part that sits on F4 and G4 for most of an exercise. What is the problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The tenor stays at the top of its range, which is tiring and strained. A part should mostly use the middle of its range; long stretches at the top strain the voice.

  8. A chord has the alto on C4 and the soprano on E5. What is wrong with the spacing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Soprano and alto are more than an octave apart. C4 to E5 is a tenth, wider than the octave limit for adjacent upper voices.

  9. In IV–V in root position, how can consecutive fifths and octaves be avoided most simply? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Move the three upper voices in contrary motion to the bass. The bass rises a step, so the upper voices falling to the nearest notes of V avoids parallels.

Key takeaways

  • SATB writing places soprano and alto on the treble staff and tenor and bass on the bass staff, with fixed stem directions, and keeps each voice within its comfortable range.
  • Double the root in root-position triads, never the leading note, and keep adjacent upper voices within an octave; the tenor and bass may be further apart.
  • Consecutive perfect fifths and octaves between any two voices are avoided because they destroy the independence of the lines; contrary motion against the bass is the usual cure.
  • Smooth voice leading keeps common notes, moves upper voices by step, avoids augmented intervals and crossing, and resolves the leading note upwards and sevenths downwards.
  • For roots a fourth or fifth apart keep one common note; for roots a third apart keep two; for roots a step apart move the upper voices in contrary motion to the bass.
  • First-inversion chords smooth the bass, soften the harmony and are doubled on the soprano or a primary note; ii6–V and passing vii°6 are common uses.

Common mistakes in Voice Leading and Cadences

  • Writing stems by note height, as in piano music. In SATB, stem direction shows which voice a note belongs to, so it never changes.
  • Doubling the third of a major chord in root position, especially the dominant. If the third is the leading note, it creates a strong fault; in other chords it thins the sound.
  • Checking only the outer voices. Consecutives can occur between any pair of the four voices; check all six pairs.
  • Leaping in every voice to reach a chord. The nearest available chord note usually gives the smoothest result.
  • Moving all voices in the same direction in IV–V. This almost always creates consecutive fifths and octaves.
  • Doubling the bass in vii°6 because it is the leading note's chord. In vii°6 the bass is the second degree, which is safe to double; the leading note must not be doubled.

Lessons in this school

  1. SATB layout and vocal ranges
    Four-part harmony is written for soprano, alto, tenor and bass voices.
  2. Doubling and spacing
    With four voices and three-note triads, one note must be doubled.
  3. Parallel fifths and octaves
    Consecutive perfect fifths and octaves are the most famous prohibitions in harmony.
  4. Principles of voice leading
    Good voice leading makes each part a singable melody while the parts together form clear harmony.
  5. Connecting root-position chords
    Root-position chords can be joined in three basic ways depending on the interval between their roots.
  6. Using first-inversion chords
    First-inversion chords give a smoother bass line and lighter harmonic weight.
  7. The cadential six-four
    The cadential six-four is a tonic chord in second inversion that decorates the dominant at a cadence.
  8. The perfect cadence
    A cadence is a harmonic punctuation mark at the end of a phrase.
  9. The imperfect cadence
    An imperfect cadence ends on the dominant, creating a pause like a comma or question mark.
  10. The plagal cadence
    The plagal cadence moves from IV to I.
  11. The interrupted cadence
    The interrupted cadence sets up a perfect cadence then swerves to another chord, usually vi.
  12. Cadences in minor keys and the tierce de Picardie
    Minor keys need extra care at cadences because of the raised leading note.
  13. Passing notes and auxiliary notes
    Non-chord notes add movement and colour to harmony.
  14. Suspensions
    A suspension holds a note over a change of harmony, creating a dissonance that then resolves down by step.
  15. Appoggiaturas, anticipations and pedal points
    Beyond passing notes and suspensions, composers use appoggiaturas, anticipations and pedal points for expressive and structural effect.
  16. Realising a figured bass
    Figured bass is a Baroque shorthand in which numbers under a bass line show the harmony.
  17. Harmonising a chorale melody
    Harmonising a chorale melody in the style of Bach brings together everything in this school.

Voice Leading and Cadences: questions students ask

Which voice carries the melody in SATB writing?

The soprano, the highest voice.

Why must one note be doubled in four-part writing of triads?

There are four voices but a triad has only three notes.

Why are consecutive fifths avoided?

They make two voices sound like one, destroying their independence.

How should the upper voices move in IV–V?

In contrary motion to the bass, to the nearest notes.
