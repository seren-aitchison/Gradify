Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is conjunct motion?

Melodic movement by step. From “Melodic contour and range” →

In plain English: A melody's shape and span give it character before harmony or rhythm are considered. Key idea: Contour describes a melody's shape; conjunct and disjunct motion, range and tessitura describe its movement and span; a single well-placed climax gives a melody direction.

What is diminution?

Shortening the note values of a melody. From “Melodic development” →

In plain English: Composers transform musical ideas to sustain interest while maintaining unity. Key idea: Melodic development transforms ideas by transposition, inversion, retrograde, augmentation, diminution, fragmentation and ornamentation, creating variety while preserving unity.

What does da capo mean?

From the beginning. From “Ternary form” →

In plain English: Ternary form, ABA, presents a section, a contrast and a return. Key idea: Ternary form presents a complete A, a contrasting B, and a return of A; unlike rounded binary, its first section ends in the tonic; the da capo aria is a Baroque ternary form with ornamented return.

What usually stays the same in a set of variations?

The harmonic structure and phrase lengths. From “Theme and variations” →

In plain English: In theme and variations, a theme is stated and then transformed in a series of variations. Key idea: Theme and variations states a theme and transforms it through melodic, rhythmic, harmonic, textural or modal changes, usually keeping its harmonic and phrase structure as a thread.

What happens in the development section?

Themes are fragmented, sequenced and moved through keys, building tension. From “Sonata form” →

In plain English: Sonata form is the most important instrumental form of the Classical and Romantic periods. Key idea: Sonata form sets out contrasting subjects in tonic and second key, develops them, and recapitulates both in the tonic, dramatising tonal conflict and resolution.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Melody and Form

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A melody rises to a high point and then falls. What contour is this? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An arch. Applying the standard result: An arch rises then falls.

  2. What usually ends an antecedent phrase? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A weaker cadence, often imperfect. The antecedent sounds open, prompting a consequent.

  3. What motif opens Beethoven's Fifth Symphony? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Three short notes and a long note. The short-short-short-long idea runs through the movement.

  4. A melody moves mostly by large leaps. How would you describe its motion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Disjunct. Applying the standard result: Movement by leap is disjunct.

  5. In a typical eight-bar sentence, how long is the continuation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Four bars. Applying the standard result: The sentence is usually 2 + 2 + 4.

  6. An idea is repeated a tone lower with every interval unchanged, introducing accidentals. What type of sequence is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A real sequence. Applying the standard result: Exact transposition is a real sequence.

  7. A student's melody reaches its top note five times. Why might this weaken it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Repeating the climax reduces its sense of arrival and direction. A climax is most effective when reached once.

  8. Two four-bar phrases begin identically; the first ends on V, the second on I. What is the structure? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A period. Matching openings with weak then strong cadences form a period.

  9. Why are sequences common in development sections? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They create momentum and help move through keys. Repeating ideas at new pitches drives the music and supports modulation.

Key takeaways

  • Contour describes a melody's shape; conjunct and disjunct motion, range and tessitura describe its movement and span; a single well-placed climax gives a melody direction.
  • Phrases end with cadences; a period pairs an antecedent and consequent with weaker then stronger cadences, while a sentence presents a basic idea, repeats it, and develops it in a continuation to a cadence.
  • Motifs are short recurring ideas that unify a piece; sequences repeat ideas at different pitches, either exactly (real) or adjusted to the key (tonal), creating momentum.
  • Melodic development transforms ideas by transposition, inversion, retrograde, augmentation, diminution, fragmentation and ornamentation, creating variety while preserving unity.
  • A good melody is planned in phrases with clear cadences, moves mainly by step with balanced leaps, uses repetition and sequence for unity, reaches one climax and ends on the tonic.
  • Binary form has two repeated sections; A modulates to the dominant or relative major and B returns home; rounded binary brings back the opening in the tonic within B.

Common mistakes in Melody and Form

  • Confusing range with tessitura. A melody may have a wide range but sit mostly in a narrow band of notes.
  • Assuming all phrases are four bars long. Composers frequently extend or shorten phrases for expressive effect.
  • Calling any repetition a sequence. A sequence repeats an idea at a different pitch level, usually several times in succession.
  • Confusing inversion with retrograde. Inversion flips the direction of intervals; retrograde reverses the order of notes.
  • Writing note by note without a plan. Planning phrases, cadences and climax first gives the melody direction.
  • Calling any two-part piece binary form. Binary form is defined by its two repeated sections and their key scheme.

Lessons in this school

  1. Melodic contour and range
    A melody's shape and span give it character before harmony or rhythm are considered.
  2. Phrases and periods
    Melodies are organised into phrases, which combine into larger units such as periods and sentences.
  3. Motif and sequence
    Short musical ideas, or motifs, give music unity, and sequences repeat them at different pitches.
  4. Melodic development
    Composers transform musical ideas to sustain interest while maintaining unity.
  5. Writing a melody
    Writing a good melody combines craft and imagination.
  6. Binary form
    Binary form divides a piece into two sections, each usually repeated.
  7. Ternary form
    Ternary form, ABA, presents a section, a contrast and a return.
  8. Strophic and through-composed forms
    Songs can repeat the same music for each verse or set the text with continuously new music.
  9. Rondo form
    Rondo form alternates a recurring main theme with contrasting episodes.
  10. Theme and variations
    In theme and variations, a theme is stated and then transformed in a series of variations.
  11. Minuet and trio, scherzo and trio
    The minuet and trio, and later the scherzo and trio, provided the dance-like third movement of many Classical works.
  12. Ground bass, ostinato and passacaglia
    Some music is built over a repeating bass or pattern.
  13. Sonata form
    Sonata form is the most important instrumental form of the Classical and Romantic periods.
  14. The concerto
    The concerto sets a soloist or group of soloists against an orchestra.
  15. The symphony
    The symphony became the most prestigious orchestral genre.
  16. Cyclic form and leitmotif
    Nineteenth-century composers sought unity across large works by bringing back themes.
