Key terms and formulas at a glance

Musical analysis
The close study of a piece to explain how its parts relate and function
Pivot chord
A chord common to two keys, used to smooth a modulation
Sentence
An eight-bar theme with a repeated two-bar basic idea followed by a four-bar continuation and cadence
Formal map
A table or diagram showing sections, bar numbers, themes, keys and cadences
Ursatz
Schenker's fundamental structure of a descending Urlinie over a I–V–I bass
Pitch class
All notes of the same name in any octave, including enharmonic equivalents
Thesis
The central claim that an essay argues for
Brief
A set of requirements that a composition must meet
Thematic transformation
Changing the character of a theme while keeping its identity, as in Liszt
Tessitura
The part of a voice's range where a piece mainly lies
Idiomatic
Suited to the natural techniques and strengths of an instrument
Pastiche
A piece composed in imitation of the style of another composer or period

What information belongs in a formal map?

Sections with bar numbers, thematic material, keys and cadences. From “Analysing form” →

In plain English: Formal analysis maps how a piece is divided into sections and how those sections relate in theme, key and function. Key idea: Formal analysis builds a map of sections, themes, keys and cadences with bar numbers, compares the piece with standard models to highlight deviations, and can consider formal function and form as process.

What catalogue abbreviation identifies works by J. S. Bach?

BWV. From “Writing about music” →

In plain English: Writing about music turns listening and analysis into clear argument supported by evidence. Key idea: Writing about music should present a clear argument with precise evidence from scores, recordings and sources, use accurate vocabulary and conventions, and reference all sources consistently.

What is the difference between syllabic and melismatic setting?

Syllabic has one note per syllable; melismatic has several notes on one syllable. From “Writing for voices” →

In plain English: Writing for voices requires attention to range, breath and especially the setting of words. Key idea: Writing for voices means choosing comfortable tessitura, setting text with natural stress, using syllabic or melismatic setting and word painting thoughtfully, and writing practical, breathable choral textures.

Which composer wrote Piano Phase (1967)?

Steve Reich. From “Contemporary compositional techniques” →

Quick quiz: test yourself on Composition and Analysis

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which analytical approach is designed mainly for atonal music? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pitch-class set analysis. Set theory was developed by Allen Forte and others to analyse post-tonal music.

  2. How would a dominant seventh in third inversion be labelled in UK practice? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    V7d. The letter d shows the seventh in the bass, which is third inversion.

  3. What is the contour of a melody? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Its overall rising and falling shape. Contour describes the shape of a melody as it rises and falls.

  4. What does motivic analysis mainly investigate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How small ideas generate a whole work. Motivic analysis traces how short ideas are varied and developed across a piece.

  5. In a modulation from C major to G major, which chord could act as a pivot? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A minor, vi in C and ii in G. A minor belongs to both keys, as vi of C and ii of G.

  6. In a sentence, what happens in the continuation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The material is fragmented and leads to a cadence. The continuation fragments and accelerates earlier material before the cadence.

  7. A scholar compares eight recordings of a Chopin nocturne, measuring tempo changes with software. What kind of analysis is this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Performance analysis. Measuring and comparing recorded performances is performance analysis.

  8. A passage in C has one F♯ leading to a G chord, then returns to C without a cadence in G. What is the best description? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A tonicisation of G with V/V. Without a cadence in G, the F♯ produces a secondary dominant that briefly tonicises G.

  9. A motif first heard in crotchets returns in the bass in minims. Which transformation is this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Augmentation. Doubling the note values is augmentation.

Key takeaways

  • Analysis explains how music works through approaches such as formal, harmonic, motivic, Schenkerian, set-theory and style analysis, each suited to different questions, and it is always an act of interpretation.
  • Harmonic analysis establishes the key, separates chord and non-chord notes, labels chords with numerals and inversions, identifies modulations and cadences, and then explains the role of harmonic rhythm and chromatic chords.
  • Melodic analysis describes contour, range, interval and rhythm, recognises sentence and period structures, and traces how motifs are transformed by sequence, inversion, augmentation and fragmentation.
  • Formal analysis builds a map of sections, themes, keys and cadences with bar numbers, compares the piece with standard models to highlight deviations, and can consider formal function and form as process.
  • Schenkerian analysis reduces tonal music to an Ursatz of Urlinie and I–V–I bass, shown at background, middleground and foreground levels connected by prolongation, though it is limited in repertoire and open to criticism of its assumptions.
  • Post-tonal analysis numbers pitch classes 0 to 11, groups sets into classes under transposition and inversion using normal and prime forms and interval-class vectors, and analyses twelve-tone rows through their 48 forms.

Common mistakes in Composition and Analysis

  • Treating analysis as labelling. Naming chords and sections is only a starting point; analysis should explain how features relate and what effect they have.
  • Labelling a modulation from one accidental. A single sharp may be a secondary dominant or a chromatic note; a modulation needs confirmation by a cadence in the new key.
  • Confusing a sentence with a period. A period has two parallel phrases with weak then strong cadences; a sentence repeats a basic idea and then develops it in a continuation.
  • Forcing a piece into a textbook form. Models are norms, not rules; describe what actually happens and treat departures as meaningful rather than errors.
  • Thinking a Schenkerian graph shows what the composer consciously planned. A graph is an interpretation of how the music's voice leading can be heard, not a record of composition.
  • Treating pitch-class sets as ordered. A set is unordered and ignores register; a twelve-tone row, by contrast, is an ordered series.

Lessons in this school

  1. Approaches to analysis
    Musical analysis explains how a piece works by examining its materials and how they relate.
  2. Harmonic analysis
    Harmonic analysis identifies the chords, keys and cadences of a passage and explains how they create direction.
  3. Analysing melody and motif
    Melodies are built from motifs grouped into phrases, and analysis can show how a whole movement grows from a few ideas.
  4. Analysing form
    Formal analysis maps how a piece is divided into sections and how those sections relate in theme, key and function.
  5. Schenkerian analysis
    Schenkerian analysis, developed by Heinrich Schenker, explains tonal music as the elaboration of a simple underlying structure.
  6. Post-tonal analysis and pitch-class sets
    Atonal and serial music needs different analytical tools from tonal music.
  7. Writing about music
    Writing about music turns listening and analysis into clear argument supported by evidence.
  8. Composing to a brief
    Composing to a brief means writing music that meets a set of requirements, as in examinations and professional commissions.
  9. Developing musical ideas
    A composition grows by developing a few ideas rather than piling up new ones.
  10. Writing for voices
    Writing for voices requires attention to range, breath and especially the setting of words.
  11. Writing for instruments
    Effective instrumental writing depends on knowing each instrument's range, registers, techniques and practical limits.
  12. Composing in a style
    Composing in a style, or pastiche, means writing music that follows the conventions of a composer, period or genre.
  13. Contemporary compositional techniques
    Since the early twentieth century composers have developed many new ways of organising sound beyond tonality.
  14. Composing with music technology
    Music technology lets composers create, edit and produce music in a digital audio workstation.
  15. Presenting a score
    A clear score lets performers play a composition as intended and examiners understand it.

Composition and Analysis: questions students ask

What confirms that a passage has modulated?

A cadence in the new key.

What is the antecedent of a period?

The first phrase, ending with a weaker cadence.

What is the bass of the Ursatz?

An arpeggiation of I–V–I.

How many forms of a twelve-tone row are possible?

48: P, I, R and RI on each of 12 starting pitches.
