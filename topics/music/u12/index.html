Lessons in this school

  1. Approaches to analysis
    Musical analysis explains how a piece works by examining its materials and how they relate.
  2. Harmonic analysis
    Harmonic analysis identifies the chords, keys and cadences of a passage and explains how they create direction.
  3. Analysing melody and motif
    Melodies are built from motifs grouped into phrases, and analysis can show how a whole movement grows from a few ideas.
  4. Analysing form
    Formal analysis maps how a piece is divided into sections and how those sections relate in theme, key and function.
  5. Schenkerian analysis
    Schenkerian analysis, developed by Heinrich Schenker, explains tonal music as the elaboration of a simple underlying structure.
  6. Post-tonal analysis and pitch-class sets
    Atonal and serial music needs different analytical tools from tonal music.
  7. Writing about music
    Writing about music turns listening and analysis into clear argument supported by evidence.
  8. Composing to a brief
    Composing to a brief means writing music that meets a set of requirements, as in examinations and professional commissions.
  9. Developing musical ideas
    A composition grows by developing a few ideas rather than piling up new ones.
  10. Writing for voices
    Writing for voices requires attention to range, breath and especially the setting of words.
  11. Writing for instruments
    Effective instrumental writing depends on knowing each instrument's range, registers, techniques and practical limits.
  12. Composing in a style
    Composing in a style, or pastiche, means writing music that follows the conventions of a composer, period or genre.
  13. Contemporary compositional techniques
    Since the early twentieth century composers have developed many new ways of organising sound beyond tonality.
  14. Composing with music technology
    Music technology lets composers create, edit and produce music in a digital audio workstation.
  15. Presenting a score
    A clear score lets performers play a composition as intended and examiners understand it.

Key terms

Musical analysis
The close study of a piece to explain how its parts relate and function
Pivot chord
A chord common to two keys, used to smooth a modulation
Sentence
An eight-bar theme with a repeated two-bar basic idea followed by a four-bar continuation and cadence
Formal map
A table or diagram showing sections, bar numbers, themes, keys and cadences
Ursatz
Schenker's fundamental structure of a descending Urlinie over a I–V–I bass
Pitch class
All notes of the same name in any octave, including enharmonic equivalents
Thesis
The central claim that an essay argues for
Brief
A set of requirements that a composition must meet
Thematic transformation
Changing the character of a theme while keeping its identity, as in Liszt
Tessitura
The part of a voice's range where a piece mainly lies
Idiomatic
Suited to the natural techniques and strengths of an instrument
Pastiche
A piece composed in imitation of the style of another composer or period
Total serialism
Applying serial ordering to durations, dynamics and attacks as well as pitch
MIDI
A protocol of performance instructions, such as note, timing and velocity, not audio
