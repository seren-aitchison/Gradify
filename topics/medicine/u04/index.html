Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is dead space ventilation?

Ventilation of alveoli that aren't perfused, so no gas exchange occurs. From “Ventilation, gas exchange and oxygen transport” →

In plain English: The lungs move air in and out, exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide with the blood, and haemoglobin carries oxygen to the tissues. Key idea: Gas exchange depends on matched ventilation and perfusion; oxygen is carried mainly by haemoglobin, whose sigmoid dissociation curve shifts right with acidosis, CO2, heat and 2,3-DPG.

Name three life-threatening features of acute asthma.

Any three of: PEF below 33%, SpO2 below 92%, silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, arrhythmia, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension. From “Asthma” →

In plain English: Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable symptoms and airflow obstruction. Key idea: Asthma needs objective diagnosis; adults now start on as-needed ICS–formoterol rather than SABA alone; acute asthma is graded by PEF and clinical features, and life-threatening features demand immediate escalation.

What does CURB-65 stand for?

Confusion, urea over 7 mmol/L, respiratory rate 30 or more, low blood pressure, age 65 or over. From “Community-acquired pneumonia” →

In plain English: Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is infection of the lung tissue acquired outside hospital. Key idea: CAP is most often caused by S. pneumoniae; CURB-65 (or CRB-65) guides where and how to treat; give antibiotics within 4 hours in hospital and escalate high-severity cases.

What advice is given about scuba diving after a pneumothorax?

Avoid it permanently unless definitive surgical treatment has been performed. From “Pneumothorax” →

In plain English: A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space, causing the lung to collapse. Key idea: Primary pneumothorax occurs in healthy lungs and may be managed conservatively if symptoms are minimal; secondary pneumothorax is more dangerous; tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis needing immediate decompression.

Which PE patients are given systemic thrombolysis?

Those with high-risk (massive) PE causing hypotension or shock, unless contraindicated. From “Pulmonary embolism” →

In plain English: Pulmonary embolism (PE) ranges from small, low-risk clots to massive PE causing shock. Key idea: PE causes dead space and right ventricular strain; risk is stratified by shock, RV dysfunction, troponin and PESI; high-risk PE needs thrombolysis, while low-risk PE can often be treated as an outpatient.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Respiratory Medicine

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What happens to oxygen release from haemoglobin when the curve shifts to the right? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Oxygen is released more easily to the tissues. A right shift lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, helping unloading in active tissues.

  2. Which spirometry pattern is typical of COPD? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reduced FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7. COPD causes airflow obstruction, reducing FEV1 more than FVC.

  3. Which condition typically causes type 1 respiratory failure? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pneumonia. Pneumonia causes V/Q mismatch and shunt without hypoventilation.

  4. In lobar pneumonia, consolidated alveoli are perfused but not ventilated. What is this called? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Shunt. Perfused but unventilated alveoli create a shunt, causing hypoxaemia that responds poorly to oxygen.

  5. A patient has FEV1 2.0 L, FVC 2.4 L and a reduced TLCO. Which condition is most consistent? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Interstitial lung disease. A ratio of 0.83 with low FVC suggests restriction; low TLCO suggests a disorder of the alveolar membrane such as fibrosis.

  6. A blood gas shows pH 7.38, PaCO2 7.2 kPa, bicarbonate 33 mmol/L and PaO2 7.6 kPa. What does it show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Chronic compensated type 2 respiratory failure. Raised CO2 with a normal pH and raised bicarbonate indicates chronic compensation.

  7. A patient on 60% oxygen has a PaO2 of 12 kPa. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Severe impairment of gas exchange, as this value is far below what 60% oxygen should give. On high inspired oxygen, PaO2 should be much higher; the result shows a large A–a gradient.

  8. An 80-year-old never-smoker without symptoms has an FEV1/FVC of 0.68. What is the most appropriate interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The fixed 0.7 cut-off may over-diagnose obstruction with ageing; compare with the lower limit of normal and clinical context. The ratio falls with age; using the lower limit of normal and the clinical picture avoids over-diagnosis.

  9. An acutely breathless woman with no lung disease has saturations of 85% on air. What is the immediate oxygen target? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    94–98%. Without risk factors for hypercapnia, the BTS target is 94–98%; start oxygen and escalate.

Key takeaways

  • Gas exchange depends on matched ventilation and perfusion; oxygen is carried mainly by haemoglobin, whose sigmoid dissociation curve shifts right with acidosis, CO2, heat and 2,3-DPG.
  • An FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7 shows obstruction; a low FVC with a normal ratio suggests restriction; peak flow variability, reversibility and FeNO support asthma; TLCO shows gas transfer.
  • Type 1 failure is low oxygen with normal or low CO2; type 2 adds raised CO2 from hypoventilation; prescribe oxygen to a target range (88–92% for those at risk of hypercapnia) and use NIV for persisting hypercapnic acidosis in COPD.
  • Asthma needs objective diagnosis; adults now start on as-needed ICS–formoterol rather than SABA alone; acute asthma is graded by PEF and clinical features, and life-threatening features demand immediate escalation.
  • COPD is diagnosed by post-bronchodilator obstruction in a person with risk factors; smoking cessation, vaccination and pulmonary rehabilitation come first, then inhaled therapy guided by asthmatic features; exacerbations need controlled oxygen and escalation when needed.
  • Bronchiectasis is permanent airway dilatation with chronic infection, diagnosed by HRCT and managed with airway clearance and targeted antibiotics; cystic fibrosis is an autosomal recessive CFTR disorder detected by newborn screening and now treated with CFTR modulators.

Common mistakes in Respiratory Medicine

  • Interpreting PaO2 without knowing the inspired oxygen. A PaO2 of 12 kPa is normal on air but shows a large gas exchange defect on 60% oxygen.
  • Labelling a restrictive pattern on spirometry as proven restrictive lung disease. Spirometry suggests restriction; full lung volumes (total lung capacity) are needed to confirm it.
  • Withholding oxygen from a hypoxic patient with COPD for fear of CO2 retention. Hypoxaemia kills; give controlled oxygen to the 88–92% target and monitor with blood gases.
  • Being reassured by a normal PaCO2 in acute asthma. In a severe attack, CO2 should be low from hyperventilation; a normal or raised level suggests exhaustion and near-fatal asthma.
  • Starting inhaled corticosteroids routinely in COPD. ICS increases pneumonia risk and is reserved for those with asthmatic or steroid-responsive features or frequent exacerbations.
  • Labelling chronic productive cough as COPD without considering bronchiectasis. Large daily sputum volumes, especially in a non-smoker or with recurrent infections, should prompt HRCT.

Lessons in this school

  1. Ventilation, gas exchange and oxygen transport
    The lungs move air in and out, exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide with the blood, and haemoglobin carries oxygen to the tissues.
  2. Lung function tests
    Lung function tests measure how much air the lungs hold and how quickly it can be moved.
  3. Respiratory failure: type 1 and type 2
    Respiratory failure occurs when the lungs fail to oxygenate the blood or remove carbon dioxide adequately.
  4. Asthma
    Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable symptoms and airflow obstruction.
  5. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
    COPD is a common, progressive lung disease with airflow obstruction, mainly caused by smoking.
  6. Bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis
    Bronchiectasis is permanent dilatation of the airways with chronic infection; cystic fibrosis is an inherited cause.
  7. Community-acquired pneumonia
    Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is infection of the lung tissue acquired outside hospital.
  8. Tuberculosis
    Tuberculosis (TB) is a bacterial infection, usually of the lungs, that remains a major global cause of death.
  9. Pleural effusion
    A pleural effusion is fluid in the pleural space.
  10. Pneumothorax
    A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space, causing the lung to collapse.
  11. Interstitial lung disease
    Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) are a group of conditions causing inflammation and scarring of the lung tissue.
  12. Sarcoidosis
    Sarcoidosis is a multisystem disease of unknown cause characterised by non-caseating granulomas.
  13. Pulmonary embolism
    Pulmonary embolism (PE) ranges from small, low-risk clots to massive PE causing shock.
  14. Pulmonary hypertension
    Pulmonary hypertension is raised pressure in the pulmonary arteries, which strains the right heart.
  15. Lung cancer
    Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in the UK.
  16. Obstructive sleep apnoea
    Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is repeated collapse of the upper airway during sleep, causing disturbed sleep and daytime sleepiness.

Respiratory Medicine: questions students ask

What FEV1/FVC ratio indicates airflow obstruction?

Below 0.7 (or below the lower limit of normal).

What blood gas values define type 2 respiratory failure?

PaO2 below 8 kPa with PaCO2 above about 6.0 kPa.

What is the recommended initial treatment for a newly diagnosed adult with asthma under the 2024 guideline?

As-needed low-dose ICS–formoterol (anti-inflammatory reliever therapy).

When is pulmonary rehabilitation offered in COPD?

To people functionally limited by breathlessness, usually MRC grade 3 or above, including after an exacerbation.
