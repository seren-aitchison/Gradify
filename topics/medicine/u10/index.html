Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does D stand for in ABCDE, and what must always be checked?

Disability: consciousness, pupils and blood glucose. From “The ABCDE assessment of the acutely ill patient” →

In plain English: The ABCDE approach is a systematic way to assess and treat an acutely unwell patient, dealing with life-threatening problems in order of urgency. Key idea: Assess and treat in order, A then B then C then D then E, reassessing after each step; use NEWS2 and clinical judgement to escalate, and hand over with SBAR.

When should paracetamol levels be taken after a single acute overdose?

At 4 hours after ingestion, or as soon as possible after that. From “Poisoning and overdose” →

In plain English: Poisoning is a common reason for hospital attendance, often after intentional self-harm. Key idea: Treat the patient with ABCDE, identify the poison and consult TOXBASE; treat paracetamol overdose with acetylcysteine guided by the nomogram or immediately if staggered or late; give naloxone for opioid toxicity; always offer psychosocial assessment.

What is the classic history of appendicitis?

Central abdominal pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, with anorexia, nausea and low-grade fever. From “Appendicitis” →

In plain English: Appendicitis is the commonest surgical emergency of the abdomen. Key idea: Appendicitis classically causes periumbilical pain moving to the right iliac fossa with anorexia and fever; use scores and appropriate imaging, and treat with appendicectomy or, in selected adults, antibiotics.

What does "no trace, wrong place" mean?

If capnography shows no CO2 trace after intubation, the tube is not in the trachea. From “Anaesthesia and airway management” →

In plain English: Anaesthesia makes surgery possible, and airway management is a core skill in anaesthesia and resuscitation. Key idea: Anaesthesia may be general, regional, local or sedation; airway management progresses from simple manoeuvres to supraglottic devices and intubation; capnography and the WHO checklist are key safety measures.

Who is invited for NHS breast screening?

Women aged 50 to 71, every three years. From “Breast disease” →

In plain English: Breast symptoms are common and most are benign, but breast cancer is the commonest cancer in the UK. Key idea: Triple assessment combines clinical examination, imaging and biopsy; most breast lumps are benign, but hard, irregular, fixed lumps need urgent referral; breast cancer treatment is guided by stage and receptor status.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Acute, Surgical and Palliative Care

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the first step in the ABCDE approach? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Assess the airway. Airway problems kill fastest, so they are assessed first.

  2. Which is an example of obstructive shock? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tension pneumothorax. Obstruction to venous return or outflow causes obstructive shock.

  3. What is the first-line drug for anaphylaxis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intramuscular adrenaline. Adrenaline reverses airway swelling, bronchospasm and hypotension.

  4. A patient has stridor and is struggling to breathe. What is the priority? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Call for urgent anaesthetic help and manage the airway. Stridor suggests upper airway obstruction, a life-threatening A problem.

  5. A shocked patient has distended neck veins and muffled heart sounds after a stab wound. What is likely? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cardiac tamponade. Raised JVP, hypotension and muffled heart sounds suggest tamponade.

  6. Symptoms persist five minutes after a first dose of IM adrenaline. What should happen? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Give a repeat dose of IM adrenaline. Guidance recommends repeating IM adrenaline after five minutes if there is no improvement.

  7. A patient with COPD and known hypercapnic respiratory failure is hypoxic. What oxygen saturation target is appropriate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    88 to 92%. Patients at risk of hypercapnia have a lower target to avoid worsening CO2 retention.

  8. What does a raised serum lactate in shock indicate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Tissue hypoperfusion with anaerobic metabolism. Lactate rises when tissues are not adequately perfused.

  9. A patient with anaphylaxis feels faint and has a BP of 75/40. How should they be positioned? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lying flat, with legs raised if possible. Standing or sitting can precipitate cardiovascular collapse.

Key takeaways

  • Assess and treat in order, A then B then C then D then E, reassessing after each step; use NEWS2 and clinical judgement to escalate, and hand over with SBAR.
  • Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion from hypovolaemic, distributive, cardiogenic or obstructive causes; recognise it early from tachycardia, poor perfusion and raised lactate, and treat the cause as well as supporting the circulation.
  • Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening airway, breathing or circulation problem after a trigger; give intramuscular adrenaline promptly, repeat after five minutes if needed, and escalate refractory cases.
  • Start high-quality CPR immediately, defibrillate VF or pVT promptly, give adrenaline early in non-shockable rhythms, minimise pauses and treat the 4 Hs and 4 Ts.
  • Treat the patient with ABCDE, identify the poison and consult TOXBASE; treat paracetamol overdose with acetylcysteine guided by the nomogram or immediately if staggered or late; give naloxone for opioid toxicity; always offer psychosocial assessment.
  • Trauma care starts with control of catastrophic bleeding, then ABCDE; major haemorrhage is treated with balanced blood products, early tranexamic acid and definitive control of bleeding in a major trauma centre.

Common mistakes in Acute, Surgical and Palliative Care

  • Moving on before fixing a problem. An airway or breathing problem must be treated before assessing circulation.
  • Waiting for hypotension before diagnosing shock. Young people can maintain blood pressure until they are severely shocked.
  • Giving an antihistamine first and waiting. Adrenaline is the only first-line treatment and must not be delayed.
  • Pausing CPR after a shock to check for a pulse. Resume compressions immediately and check the rhythm after two minutes.
  • Being reassured by a well patient after paracetamol overdose. Early symptoms are often absent despite risk of severe liver injury.
  • Focusing on a dramatic but non-life-threatening injury first. Follow <C>ABCDE so that the threats that kill fastest are treated first.

Lessons in this school

  1. The ABCDE assessment of the acutely ill patient
    The ABCDE approach is a systematic way to assess and treat an acutely unwell patient, dealing with life-threatening problems in order of urgency.
  2. Shock
    Shock is circulatory failure leading to inadequate oxygen delivery to the tissues.
  3. Anaphylaxis
    Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction.
  4. Cardiac arrest and advanced life support
    Cardiac arrest needs immediate, coordinated resuscitation.
  5. Poisoning and overdose
    Poisoning is a common reason for hospital attendance, often after intentional self-harm.
  6. Major trauma
    Major trauma is a leading cause of death in younger adults.
  7. Burns
    Burns range from minor scalds to life-threatening injuries with airway compromise and major fluid loss.
  8. The acute abdomen
    The acute abdomen is sudden, severe abdominal pain that may need urgent surgery.
  9. Appendicitis
    Appendicitis is the commonest surgical emergency of the abdomen.
  10. Bowel obstruction
    Bowel obstruction is a common surgical emergency in which intestinal contents cannot pass.
  11. Hernias
    A hernia is the protrusion of a structure through a defect in the wall that normally contains it.
  12. Preoperative assessment
    Preoperative assessment identifies and reduces risk before surgery and helps patients decide whether to proceed.
  13. Anaesthesia and airway management
    Anaesthesia makes surgery possible, and airway management is a core skill in anaesthesia and resuscitation.
  14. Postoperative complications
    Complications after surgery can be general or specific to the operation and tend to occur at characteristic times.
  15. Fluid prescribing
    Intravenous fluids are among the most commonly prescribed treatments, and errors cause real harm.
  16. Urological emergencies and urinary retention
    Several urological conditions need urgent recognition and treatment.
  17. Breast disease
    Breast symptoms are common and most are benign, but breast cancer is the commonest cancer in the UK.
  18. Frailty and comprehensive geriatric assessment
    Frailty is a state of reduced reserve that makes older people vulnerable to sudden decline after minor stresses.
  19. Falls
    Falls are common in older people and cause injury, fear and loss of independence.
  20. Palliative care and symptom control
    Palliative care aims to improve the quality of life of people with life-limiting illness and their families.
  21. Care in the last days of life
    Good care in the last days of life focuses on comfort, dignity and the needs of the person and those close to them.

Acute, Surgical and Palliative Care: questions students ask

Why can blood pressure be a misleading sign of shock in young people?

They compensate with tachycardia and vasoconstriction, keeping blood pressure normal until late.

Where is intramuscular adrenaline given in anaphylaxis?

Into the anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh.

What are the shockable rhythms?

Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

Within what time should tranexamic acid be given after injury?

As early as possible and within three hours.
