Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Where is vitamin B12 absorbed?

In the terminal ileum, bound to intrinsic factor. From “Digestion and absorption” →

In plain English: The gut breaks food down into molecules small enough to absorb, and different nutrients are absorbed at different sites. Key idea: Carbohydrates, proteins and fats are digested by luminal and brush-border enzymes; most nutrients are absorbed in the jejunum, iron and folate proximally, and B12 and bile salts in the terminal ileum.

Name two alarm features in a patient with reflux symptoms.

Two of: dysphagia, weight loss, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, iron-deficiency anaemia, upper abdominal mass. From “Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease” →

In plain English: Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is very common and usually managed in primary care. Key idea: GORD causes heartburn and regurgitation and is managed with lifestyle measures and PPIs, stepping down when controlled; alarm features need endoscopy; Barrett's oesophagus is a precursor of adenocarcinoma requiring surveillance.

Which part of the bowel is always involved in ulcerative colitis?

The rectum. From “Inflammatory bowel disease” →

In plain English: Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) comprises Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, chronic relapsing conditions often starting in young adults. Key idea: Crohn's affects any part of the gut with transmural, patchy inflammation; UC affects the colon continuously from the rectum; faecal calprotectin and colonoscopy aid diagnosis; acute severe colitis needs urgent admission.

What age range does bowel cancer screening in England cover?

50 to 74. From “Colorectal cancer and screening” →

In plain English: Colorectal cancer is one of the most common cancers in the UK. Key idea: Colorectal cancer develops through the adenoma–carcinoma sequence and is linked to age, genetics, IBD and lifestyle; FIT guides referral of symptomatic patients, and screening every two years from 50 to 74 aims to detect cancer and polyps early.

What weekly alcohol limit do the UK Chief Medical Officers advise not regularly exceeding?

14 units. From “Alcohol-related and metabolic liver disease” →

In plain English: Alcohol and metabolic dysfunction are the two leading causes of chronic liver disease in the UK. Key idea: Alcohol causes steatosis, hepatitis and cirrhosis; MASLD is fatty liver with cardiometabolic risk factors; FIB-4 and second-line tests stratify fibrosis risk to target referral.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Gastroenterology and Hepatology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where is iron mainly absorbed? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The duodenum and proximal jejunum. Iron absorption is concentrated in the proximal small intestine.

  2. When is parenteral nutrition indicated? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    When the gut cannot be used to meet nutritional needs. Parenteral nutrition is reserved for gut failure, given its risks.

  3. Coughing and nasal regurgitation when swallowing suggest which type of dysphagia? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Oropharyngeal. These features point to a problem starting the swallow, often neurological.

  4. Which enzyme deficiency causes bloating and diarrhoea after drinking milk? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Lactase. Lactase in the brush border breaks down lactose; deficiency leaves it to be fermented.

  5. A patient weighs 51 kg and is 1.70 m tall. What is their BMI? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    About 17.6 kg/m². Applying the standard result: 51 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) = 51 ÷ 2.89 = 17.6.

  6. Which investigation confirms achalasia? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    High-resolution oesophageal manometry. Manometry shows absent peristalsis and failed sphincter relaxation.

  7. A patient has had a total gastrectomy. Which deficiency should be anticipated and monitored? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Vitamin B12, due to loss of intrinsic factor. Without the stomach, intrinsic factor is absent, so B12 cannot be absorbed.

  8. A patient with anorexia nervosa is started on full feeding and becomes breathless with low phosphate on day 3. What has happened? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Refeeding syndrome, needing urgent senior review and electrolyte replacement. Electrolyte shifts on refeeding can cause cardiorespiratory failure.

  9. A 70-year-old smoker with heavy alcohol intake has progressive dysphagia to solids and weight loss. What is the most important concern? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Oesophageal cancer, needing urgent endoscopy. Progressive solid dysphagia with weight loss suggests malignant obstruction.

Key takeaways

  • Carbohydrates, proteins and fats are digested by luminal and brush-border enzymes; most nutrients are absorbed in the jejunum, iron and folate proximally, and B12 and bile salts in the terminal ileum.
  • MUST screening identifies malnutrition; support escalates from oral to enteral to parenteral, using the gut where possible; refeeding syndrome is prevented by identifying high-risk patients, starting slowly and monitoring electrolytes.
  • Oropharyngeal dysphagia is usually neurological; oesophageal dysphagia may be mechanical or motor; any new dysphagia warrants urgent endoscopy under NICE NG12 to exclude cancer.
  • GORD causes heartburn and regurgitation and is managed with lifestyle measures and PPIs, stepping down when controlled; alarm features need endoscopy; Barrett's oesophagus is a precursor of adenocarcinoma requiring surveillance.
  • Most peptic ulcers are caused by H. pylori or NSAIDs; test with breath or stool tests after stopping PPIs, eradicate according to guidance, stop NSAIDs, and act urgently on bleeding or perforation.
  • Upper GI bleeding is most often from peptic ulcers or varices; resuscitation and early escalation come first; the Glasgow-Blatchford score guides risk, and endoscopy should occur within 24 hours, or immediately if unstable.

Common mistakes in Gastroenterology and Hepatology

  • Assuming B12 deficiency always means dietary lack. Loss of intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia, gastrectomy) or terminal ileal disease are common causes.
  • Starting full feeding immediately in a starved patient. High-risk patients need cautious introduction, vitamin supplementation and electrolyte monitoring.
  • Attributing new dysphagia to reflux or anxiety without investigation. NICE recommends urgent endoscopy for dysphagia at any age.
  • Continuing high-dose PPIs indefinitely without review. Long-term use should be at the lowest effective dose and reviewed, given possible adverse effects.
  • Testing for H. pylori while the patient is still taking a PPI. This can give a false negative result; stop the PPI two weeks before.
  • Delaying resuscitation to arrange endoscopy. Stabilise the patient first, then endoscope.

Lessons in this school

  1. Digestion and absorption
    The gut breaks food down into molecules small enough to absorb, and different nutrients are absorbed at different sites.
  2. Nutrition, malnutrition and refeeding
    Malnutrition is common in hospital patients and worsens outcomes.
  3. Dysphagia and oesophageal disease
    Dysphagia, difficulty swallowing, can be the first sign of serious disease.
  4. Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease
    Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is very common and usually managed in primary care.
  5. Peptic ulcer disease and Helicobacter pylori
    Peptic ulcers are breaks in the lining of the stomach or duodenum.
  6. Upper gastrointestinal bleeding
    Upper GI bleeding is a common medical emergency.
  7. Coeliac disease
    Coeliac disease is an immune reaction to gluten that damages the small intestine.
  8. Inflammatory bowel disease
    Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) comprises Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, chronic relapsing conditions often starting in young adults.
  9. Irritable bowel syndrome
    Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a common disorder of gut–brain interaction causing pain and altered bowel habit.
  10. Diarrhoea and gastroenteritis
    Diarrhoea is among the most common reasons for seeking medical help.
  11. Colorectal cancer and screening
    Colorectal cancer is one of the most common cancers in the UK.
  12. Diverticular disease
    Diverticula, small pouches in the colon wall, are very common with age.
  13. Liver function tests and jaundice
    Liver blood tests and jaundice are common clinical problems.
  14. Viral hepatitis
    Viral hepatitis is a major cause of liver disease worldwide.
  15. Alcohol-related and metabolic liver disease
    Alcohol and metabolic dysfunction are the two leading causes of chronic liver disease in the UK.
  16. Cirrhosis and its complications
    Cirrhosis is the end stage of chronic liver injury, with scarring and nodules that disrupt liver structure.
  17. Gallstone disease
    Gallstones are very common and often silent, but they can cause pain, infection and pancreatitis.
  18. Acute and chronic pancreatitis
    Pancreatitis ranges from a self-limiting illness to a life-threatening emergency.

Gastroenterology and Hepatology: questions students ask

What three elements does MUST score?

BMI, unplanned weight loss and the effect of acute disease.

What is achalasia?

A motility disorder with failure of lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation and absent peristalsis.

What is Barrett's oesophagus?

Columnar metaplasia of the lower oesophagus caused by chronic reflux.

What are the two main causes of peptic ulcers?

Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAIDs.
