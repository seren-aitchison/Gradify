Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What causes the second heart sound?

Closure of the aortic and pulmonary (semilunar) valves. From “The cardiac cycle and cardiac output” →

In plain English: The cardiac cycle is the sequence of pressure and volume changes in one heartbeat, and cardiac output is the volume the heart pumps each minute. Key idea: Cardiac output is heart rate times stroke volume; stroke volume rises with preload (Frank–Starling) and contractility and falls with afterload.

What threshold of 10-year risk prompts a statin offer in NICE NG238?

A QRISK3 score of 10% or more, after discussing lifestyle and preferences. From “Atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk” →

In plain English: Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of arteries and the main cause of heart attack, stroke and peripheral arterial disease. Key idea: Atherosclerosis is inflammatory lipid deposition in the arterial wall; plaque rupture with thrombosis causes most acute events; treatment decisions use absolute 10-year risk from tools such as QRISK3.

What ejection fraction defines HFrEF?

40% or less. From “Heart failure: causes and classification” →

In plain English: Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs at normal filling pressures. Key idea: Heart failure is classified by EF (reduced, mildly reduced, preserved) and NYHA class; NT-proBNP triages who needs echocardiography and how urgently.

Which valve is commonly affected in people who inject drugs?

The tricuspid valve. From “Infective endocarditis” →

In plain English: Infective endocarditis is infection of the heart's inner lining, usually the valves. Key idea: Fever with a new murmur is endocarditis until proven otherwise; take multiple blood cultures before antibiotics, image the heart, and apply the Duke criteria.

What is a delta wave?

A slurred upstroke at the start of the QRS caused by pre-excitation through an accessory pathway, as in WPW. From “Tachyarrhythmias” →

In plain English: Tachyarrhythmias are fast heart rhythms. Key idea: Classify tachycardias by width and regularity; unstable patients need synchronised cardioversion; stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia is treated with vagal manoeuvres then adenosine; broad-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Cardiovascular Medicine

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is normally heard at the start of isovolumetric contraction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    S1, from closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves. Ventricular contraction closes the AV valves, producing S1, before the semilunar valves open.

  2. Which ion is mainly responsible for the plateau phase of the ventricular action potential? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Calcium entering through L-type channels. Calcium influx during phase 2 balances potassium efflux and triggers contraction.

  3. Which hormone is released by the heart when it is stretched and promotes sodium excretion? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Natriuretic peptide (ANP or BNP). Atrial and ventricular stretch release ANP and BNP, which promote natriuresis and vasodilation.

  4. A patient has heart rate 90/min, EDV 150 mL and ESV 90 mL. What is the cardiac output? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    5.4 L/min. SV = 150 − 90 = 60 mL; CO = 90 × 60 = 5,400 mL/min = 5.4 L/min (EF 40%).

  5. The R–R interval on a 25 mm/s ECG is 3 large squares. What is the heart rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    100/min. At 25 mm/s there are 300 large squares a minute: 300 ÷ 3 = 100 beats per minute.

  6. Blood pressure is 150/90 mmHg. What is the approximate mean arterial pressure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    110 mmHg. MAP ≈ 90 + ⅓(150 − 90) = 90 + 20 = 110 mmHg.

  7. A 75-year-old with long-standing hypertension has a fourth heart sound. What does it most likely indicate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Atrial contraction into a stiff, hypertrophied left ventricle. S4 occurs in late diastole when the atrium pushes blood into a non-compliant ventricle, as in left ventricular hypertrophy.

  8. A patient's QRS duration is 140 ms with an otherwise normal rhythm. What does this most likely indicate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Delayed ventricular conduction, such as a bundle branch block. A QRS of 120 ms or more shows slowed ventricular depolarisation, as in bundle branch block or ventricular rhythms.

  9. A young man has lost about 1 litre of blood after an injury. His blood pressure is 118/78 mmHg and heart rate 118/min. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    He is compensating with tachycardia and vasoconstriction; pressure may fall suddenly and he needs urgent assessment. Tachycardia is the early sign; hypotension is late. Escalate urgently using local pathways for major haemorrhage.

Key takeaways

  • Cardiac output is heart rate times stroke volume; stroke volume rises with preload (Frank–Starling) and contractility and falls with afterload.
  • The SA node's spontaneous depolarisation sets the rate; the AV node delays conduction; the ECG records atrial depolarisation (P), AV delay (PR), ventricular depolarisation (QRS) and repolarisation (T).
  • Pressure equals output times resistance; the baroreflex corrects changes in seconds, while the kidney, RAAS, ADH and natriuretic peptides control volume and set long-term pressure.
  • Atherosclerosis is inflammatory lipid deposition in the arterial wall; plaque rupture with thrombosis causes most acute events; treatment decisions use absolute 10-year risk from tools such as QRISK3.
  • Typical angina has three features; NICE uses CTCA first-line; management combines GTN, anti-anginal drugs and secondary prevention, with revascularisation for refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy.
  • ACS ranges from unstable angina to STEMI; ECG and serial high-sensitivity troponin make the diagnosis; STEMI needs immediate reperfusion, preferably primary PCI within NICE's time window.

Common mistakes in Cardiovascular Medicine

  • Equating ejection fraction with cardiac output. A patient can have a normal ejection fraction and still have heart failure (HFpEF), or a low EF with a resting output maintained by dilation and a fast rate.
  • Forgetting that the ECG shows electrical, not mechanical, activity. A normal-looking rhythm without a pulse (pulseless electrical activity) is a cardiac arrest.
  • Assuming a normal blood pressure rules out significant blood loss. Compensatory tachycardia and vasoconstriction can hold pressure up until a large loss, especially in young people, so falling pressure is a late sign.
  • Judging risk from cholesterol alone. Absolute risk depends on all risk factors together; a modest cholesterol in a smoker with diabetes can carry higher risk than a high cholesterol in a young non-smoker.
  • Being reassured by a normal resting ECG. Most people with stable angina have a normal ECG at rest; it doesn't exclude coronary artery disease.
  • Waiting for a troponin result before acting on ST elevation. STEMI is diagnosed from the ECG and symptoms; troponin can be normal early, and delay costs myocardium.

Lessons in this school

  1. The cardiac cycle and cardiac output
    The cardiac cycle is the sequence of pressure and volume changes in one heartbeat, and cardiac output is the volume the heart pumps each minute.
  2. Cardiac electrophysiology and conduction
    The heartbeat is generated and coordinated by specialised conducting tissue.
  3. Blood pressure regulation
    Blood pressure is regulated over seconds by the baroreceptor reflex and over hours to days by the kidney and hormones.
  4. Atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk
    Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of arteries and the main cause of heart attack, stroke and peripheral arterial disease.
  5. Stable angina
    Stable angina is chest discomfort caused by myocardial ischaemia on exertion, relieved by rest.
  6. Acute coronary syndromes
    Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) are unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI, caused by sudden reduction of coronary blood flow.
  7. Secondary prevention after myocardial infarction
    After a myocardial infarction, the aim is to prevent another event and help the person recover.
  8. Heart failure: causes and classification
    Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs at normal filling pressures.
  9. Managing chronic heart failure
    Treatment of chronic heart failure aims to relieve symptoms, reduce admissions and prolong life.
  10. Valvular heart disease
    Valve disease causes narrowing (stenosis) or leaking (regurgitation).
  11. Infective endocarditis
    Infective endocarditis is infection of the heart's inner lining, usually the valves.
  12. Cardiomyopathies and pericardial disease
    Cardiomyopathies are diseases of the heart muscle not explained by coronary disease or loading conditions; pericardial disease affects the sac around the heart.
  13. Atrial fibrillation
    Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia and a major cause of stroke.
  14. Bradyarrhythmias and heart block
    Bradyarrhythmias are slow heart rhythms, from sinus node disease to complete heart block.
  15. Tachyarrhythmias
    Tachyarrhythmias are fast heart rhythms.
  16. Hypertension
    Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for stroke, heart attack, heart failure and kidney disease.
  17. Peripheral arterial disease and aortic aneurysm
    Atherosclerosis in the leg arteries causes peripheral arterial disease; weakening of the aortic wall causes abdominal aortic aneurysm.
  18. Venous thromboembolism
    Venous thromboembolism (VTE) covers deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

Cardiovascular Medicine: questions students ask

What does the P wave represent?

Atrial depolarisation.

Where are the main arterial baroreceptors?

In the carotid sinus and the aortic arch.

What are foam cells?

Lipid-laden macrophages in the arterial intima, forming fatty streaks.

What three features define typical angina?

Constricting chest discomfort; brought on by exertion; relieved by rest or GTN within about 5 minutes.
