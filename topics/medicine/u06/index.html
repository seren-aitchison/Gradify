Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a typical GFR in a healthy young adult?

About 125 mL/min. From “Renal physiology and glomerular filtration” →

In plain English: The kidneys filter the blood, regulate fluids and electrolytes, and produce hormones. Key idea: Nephrons filter plasma at the glomerulus and adjust the filtrate along the tubule; GFR is about 125 mL/min and is estimated from creatinine; the kidneys also produce renin, erythropoietin and active vitamin D.

Which organism causes most community-acquired UTIs?

Escherichia coli. From “Urinary tract infection” →

In plain English: Urinary tract infections are among the most common bacterial infections. Key idea: Lower UTI causes urinary symptoms and pyelonephritis adds fever and loin pain; diagnostic approaches differ by age and group, with no dipsticks in over 65s; treatment follows NICE stewardship guidance, and signs of sepsis need urgent escalation.

What are the JBDS diagnostic criteria for DKA?

Ketones 3.0 mmol/L or above, glucose above 11.0 mmol/L or known diabetes, and bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L and/or pH below 7.3. From “Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state” →

In plain English: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) are life-threatening emergencies. Key idea: DKA is ketosis, high glucose and acidosis from insulin deficiency; HHS is severe hyperglycaemia and hyperosmolality without major ketosis; both need urgent fluids, monitoring and treatment of the cause, with insulin infusion in DKA.

What is the commonest cause of hypothyroidism in the UK?

Autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis. From “Hypothyroidism” →

In plain English: Hypothyroidism is common, especially in women, and easily treated once recognised. Key idea: Hypothyroidism, usually autoimmune in the UK, causes fatigue, weight gain and cold intolerance; primary disease shows high TSH and low free T4; levothyroxine is adjusted to TSH, and myxoedema coma is an emergency.

What is the screening test for primary hyperaldosteronism?

The aldosterone-to-renin ratio. From “Primary hyperaldosteronism and phaeochromocytoma” →

In plain English: Some cases of hypertension have endocrine causes that can be treated or cured. Key idea: Primary hyperaldosteronism is a common, often missed cause of hypertension screened with the aldosterone-to-renin ratio; phaeochromocytoma causes paroxysmal symptoms and is diagnosed by metanephrines, with careful pre-operative preparation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Renal, Endocrine and Metabolic Medicine

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where is most filtered sodium and glucose reabsorbed? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The proximal tubule. The proximal tubule reabsorbs the bulk of filtered solutes.

  2. Which is a pre-renal cause of AKI? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Severe dehydration. Applying the standard result: Reduced perfusion causes pre-renal AKI.

  3. What eGFR range defines CKD category G3b? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    30–44 mL/min/1.73 m². Applying the standard result: G3b is 30–44.

  4. Why do patients with chronic kidney disease often become anaemic? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reduced erythropoietin production. Damaged kidneys make less erythropoietin.

  5. Baseline creatinine is 80 µmol/L and current creatinine 250 µmol/L. What AKI stage is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stage 3. Applying the standard result: 250 ÷ 80 = 3.1 times baseline.

  6. What is the leading cause of CKD in the UK? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Diabetes. Diabetic kidney disease is the commonest cause.

  7. A very muscular athlete has a slightly raised creatinine but normal urine tests. Why should eGFR be interpreted cautiously? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    High muscle mass raises creatinine, so eGFR may underestimate true GFR. Creatinine reflects muscle mass as well as filtration.

  8. A man with AKI has a palpable, distended bladder. What is the most important immediate step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Relieve the obstruction, for example with a urinary catheter. Applying the standard result: Post-renal obstruction must be relieved.

  9. A patient has eGFR 70 and ACR 45 mg/mmol for 6 months. Why is this significant? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Severe albuminuria (A3) indicates high risk even with near-normal eGFR. Albuminuria is a strong predictor of progression and cardiovascular risk.

Key takeaways

  • Nephrons filter plasma at the glomerulus and adjust the filtrate along the tubule; GFR is about 125 mL/min and is estimated from creatinine; the kidneys also produce renin, erythropoietin and active vitamin D.
  • AKI is a rapid fall in kidney function defined by creatinine rise or low urine output, staged 1–3; causes are pre-renal, intrinsic or post-renal; management treats the cause, optimises fluids, stops nephrotoxic drugs and escalates complications.
  • CKD is kidney damage for over 3 months, classified by eGFR (G1–G5) and ACR (A1–A3); diabetes and hypertension are the main causes; management slows progression and cuts cardiovascular risk with blood pressure control, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors and statins.
  • Nephrotic syndrome is heavy proteinuria, low albumin and oedema; nephritic syndrome is haematuria, hypertension and declining function; rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis is an emergency needing same-day renal referral.
  • Lower UTI causes urinary symptoms and pyelonephritis adds fever and loin pain; diagnostic approaches differ by age and group, with no dipsticks in over 65s; treatment follows NICE stewardship guidance, and signs of sepsis need urgent escalation.
  • Hyperkalaemia of 6.5 mmol/L or above or with ECG changes is an emergency treated with calcium, insulin–glucose and salbutamol under monitoring; hyponatraemia is assessed by volume status and osmolality and corrected slowly to prevent osmotic demyelination.

Common mistakes in Renal, Endocrine and Metabolic Medicine

  • Treating a normal serum creatinine as proof of normal kidney function. Creatinine depends on muscle mass and rises only after substantial loss of GFR.
  • Continuing nephrotoxic medicines during AKI. NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, diuretics and some others should be reviewed and often paused.
  • Classifying CKD on eGFR alone. Albuminuria is a powerful predictor of risk and must be measured.
  • Treating haematuria and proteinuria with a rising creatinine as a simple UTI. These features may signal glomerulonephritis needing urgent assessment.
  • Diagnosing UTI in older people from a positive urine dipstick alone. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common, and confusion may have another cause.
  • Correcting chronic hyponatraemia too quickly. Rapid rises in sodium can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome, with permanent neurological damage.

Lessons in this school

  1. Renal physiology and glomerular filtration
    The kidneys filter the blood, regulate fluids and electrolytes, and produce hormones.
  2. Acute kidney injury
    Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function, common in hospital and often preventable.
  3. Chronic kidney disease
    Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is common, often silent, and increases cardiovascular risk.
  4. Glomerulonephritis and nephrotic syndrome
    Glomerular diseases damage the kidney's filters and present with nephrotic or nephritic patterns.
  5. Urinary tract infection
    Urinary tract infections are among the most common bacterial infections.
  6. Hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia
    Potassium and sodium disorders are common and can be life-threatening.
  7. Type 1 diabetes
    Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the pancreas stops producing insulin.
  8. Type 2 diabetes
    Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes and is closely linked with obesity and insulin resistance.
  9. Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state
    Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) are life-threatening emergencies.
  10. Hypoglycaemia
    Hypoglycaemia is the most common acute complication of diabetes treatment.
  11. Complications of diabetes
    Long-term high glucose damages small and large blood vessels.
  12. The hypothalamus and pituitary
    The hypothalamus and pituitary control much of the endocrine system.
  13. Hypothyroidism
    Hypothyroidism is common, especially in women, and easily treated once recognised.
  14. Hyperthyroidism
    Hyperthyroidism speeds up metabolism and can affect the heart, bones and eyes.
  15. Adrenal insufficiency
    Adrenal insufficiency means the adrenal glands do not make enough cortisol.
  16. Cushing's syndrome
    Cushing's syndrome is caused by prolonged exposure to excess cortisol.
  17. Primary hyperaldosteronism and phaeochromocytoma
    Some cases of hypertension have endocrine causes that can be treated or cured.
  18. Calcium disorders and the parathyroid glands
    Calcium is tightly regulated by parathyroid hormone and vitamin D.
  19. Osteoporosis and metabolic bone disease
    Osteoporosis weakens bones and causes fragility fractures, a major cause of disability in older people.
  20. Lipid disorders
    Raised cholesterol is a major modifiable cause of cardiovascular disease.

Renal, Endocrine and Metabolic Medicine: questions students ask

What creatinine rise within 48 hours defines AKI?

A rise of 26 µmol/L or more.

What are the three core features of nephrotic syndrome?

Heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia and oedema.

Should urine dipsticks be used to diagnose UTI in people over 65?

No; asymptomatic bacteriuria is common, so diagnosis is based on clinical features.

Why must chronic hyponatraemia be corrected slowly?

To avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.
