Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a thunderclap headache?

A severe headache reaching maximum intensity within a minute, requiring emergency assessment. From “Headache” →

In plain English: Headache is one of the commonest reasons for seeking medical help; most headaches are benign, but some signal serious disease. Key idea: Most headaches are primary, migraine, tension-type or cluster; frequent analgesic use can cause medication overuse headache; red flags such as thunderclap onset, fever with neck stiffness, focal signs or features of giant cell arteritis need urgent assessment.

Why must Parkinson's medicines be given on time in hospital?

Delays can cause rapid deterioration, including rigidity and inability to swallow. From “Parkinson's disease and movement disorders” →

In plain English: Parkinson's disease is the commonest movement disorder of later life. Key idea: Parkinson's disease causes bradykinesia with tremor, rigidity and later postural instability, plus non-motor symptoms; diagnosis is clinical and treatment is dopaminergic with therapy support; medication must be given on time, and dopamine blockers avoided.

Which vaccine are new university students offered?

MenACWY. From “Meningitis and encephalitis” →

In plain English: Meningitis and encephalitis are infections of the nervous system that can kill or disable within hours. Key idea: Meningitis causes fever, headache, neck stiffness and photophobia; a non-blanching rash suggests meningococcal septicaemia; both are emergencies needing immediate antibiotics and hospital care; herpes encephalitis needs prompt antivirals; vaccination prevents many cases.

What is the difference between bipolar I and bipolar II?

Bipolar I involves at least one manic episode; bipolar II involves hypomania and depression without full mania. From “Bipolar affective disorder” →

In plain English: Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania and depression. Key idea: Mania lasts at least a week with marked symptoms, and hypomania is milder; bipolar I includes mania and bipolar II hypomania; treatment uses antipsychotics for mania and lithium long-term, avoiding antidepressant monotherapy; lithium toxicity and valproate in pregnancy are major safety concerns.

When does alcohol withdrawal typically begin?

About 6 to 12 hours after the last drink. From “Alcohol and substance misuse” →

In plain English: Alcohol and drug misuse cause physical, psychological and social harm and often present in hospital. Key idea: AUDIT and dependence features guide assessment; alcohol withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium tremens and needs medically assisted withdrawal; Wernicke's encephalopathy needs urgent parenteral thiamine; opioid substitution therapy and naloxone reduce harm in opioid dependence.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Neurology and Psychiatry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which headache is typically bilateral and pressing, without nausea? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tension-type headache. Tension-type headache is mild to moderate and bilateral.

  2. What proportion of strokes are ischaemic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 85%. Most strokes are caused by blocked arteries.

  3. Which seizure involves both hemispheres from the onset? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A generalised seizure. Applying the standard result: Generalised seizures begin bilaterally.

  4. Which features suggest cluster headache? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Severe pain around one eye with tearing and nasal congestion on the same side. Autonomic features on the painful side are typical.

  5. Why is a CT scan needed before thrombolysis? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To exclude haemorrhage, in which thrombolysis would be dangerous. Applying the standard result: Thrombolysis could worsen a bleed.

  6. What should you do while someone is having a tonic–clonic seizure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cushion their head, keep them safe and time the seizure. Applying the standard result: Safety and timing are key.

  7. A 70-year-old has a new headache, scalp tenderness and jaw pain when chewing. What is the priority? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Same-day assessment for giant cell arteritis to prevent vision loss. Giant cell arteritis needs urgent treatment.

  8. A woman's arm weakness fully resolved after 20 minutes. What should happen? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Treat as TIA: start antiplatelet treatment unless contraindicated and arrange specialist assessment within 24 hours. Applying the standard result: TIA carries a high early risk of stroke.

  9. Why is valproate avoided in women of childbearing potential? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It carries a high risk of birth defects and developmental problems in pregnancy. Regulatory conditions apply because of fetal risk.

Key takeaways

  • Most headaches are primary, migraine, tension-type or cluster; frequent analgesic use can cause medication overuse headache; red flags such as thunderclap onset, fever with neck stiffness, focal signs or features of giant cell arteritis need urgent assessment.
  • Stroke is recognised with FAST and treated as an emergency; ischaemic stroke may receive thrombolysis within 4.5 hours and thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion; TIA needs immediate antiplatelet treatment and specialist review within 24 hours, then secondary prevention.
  • Seizures are focal or generalised; first seizures need specialist review within 2 weeks; first aid keeps people safe, and seizures of 5 minutes or more need emergency treatment; driving rules, SUDEP and valproate risks in pregnancy are key safety issues.
  • MS is an immune-mediated demyelinating disease, most often relapsing–remitting at onset, presenting with optic neuritis, sensory and motor symptoms; diagnosis requires lesions disseminated in space and time, and management combines disease-modifying therapy with symptom and rehabilitation support.
  • Parkinson's disease causes bradykinesia with tremor, rigidity and later postural instability, plus non-motor symptoms; diagnosis is clinical and treatment is dopaminergic with therapy support; medication must be given on time, and dopamine blockers avoided.
  • Dementia includes Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body and frontotemporal types; diagnosis combines history, cognitive testing and exclusion of reversible causes; management uses medication where appropriate, non-drug support, caution with antipsychotics and attention to capacity.

Common mistakes in Neurology and Psychiatry

  • Treating a sudden, severe "worst ever" headache as migraine. Thunderclap headache needs emergency investigation for subarachnoid haemorrhage.
  • Waiting to see whether stroke symptoms improve before calling 999. Delays reduce the benefit of time-critical treatment.
  • Putting something in the mouth of a person having a seizure. This can cause injury; keep them safe and time the seizure.
  • Diagnosing MS from a single episode without evidence of dissemination in space and time. Many conditions can cause one episode of neurological symptoms.
  • Delaying or omitting Parkinson's medicines in hospital. Missed doses can cause rapid deterioration, including inability to swallow.
  • Prescribing antipsychotics routinely for behavioural symptoms in dementia. They increase the risk of stroke and death and can cause severe reactions in Lewy body dementia.

Lessons in this school

  1. Headache
    Headache is one of the commonest reasons for seeking medical help; most headaches are benign, but some signal serious disease.
  2. Stroke and transient ischaemic attack
    Stroke is a medical emergency in which every minute counts.
  3. Epilepsy and seizures
    Epilepsy is a tendency to recurrent unprovoked seizures.
  4. Multiple sclerosis
    Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system and a leading cause of disability in young adults.
  5. Parkinson's disease and movement disorders
    Parkinson's disease is the commonest movement disorder of later life.
  6. Dementia
    Dementia is a progressive decline in cognitive function affecting daily life.
  7. Motor neurone disease
    Motor neurone disease (MND) is a progressive, fatal disease of the motor neurons.
  8. Peripheral neuropathy
    Peripheral neuropathy is damage to nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.
  9. Myasthenia gravis and muscle disease
    Myasthenia gravis affects the junction between nerves and muscles, causing fatigable weakness.
  10. Meningitis and encephalitis
    Meningitis and encephalitis are infections of the nervous system that can kill or disable within hours.
  11. Spinal cord compression and cauda equina syndrome
    Compression of the spinal cord or cauda equina can cause permanent paralysis and incontinence if not treated urgently.
  12. Raised intracranial pressure and head injury
    Head injuries are common, and some cause bleeding that raises pressure inside the skull.
  13. The psychiatric assessment and mental state examination
    The psychiatric assessment gathers a full history and examines the person's current mental state.
  14. Depression
    Depression is common in all clinical settings and can be life-threatening.
  15. Bipolar affective disorder
    Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania or hypomania and depression.
  16. Anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder
    Anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) are common and treatable.
  17. Schizophrenia and psychosis
    Schizophrenia is a severe mental illness involving psychosis.
  18. Self-harm and suicide risk assessment
    Self-harm is common and a strong predictor of suicide.
  19. Eating disorders
    Eating disorders are serious mental illnesses with high physical risk.
  20. Alcohol and substance misuse
    Alcohol and drug misuse cause physical, psychological and social harm and often present in hospital.
  21. Personality disorders
    Personality disorders are long-standing patterns of thinking, feeling and relating that cause distress and difficulty.
  22. Delirium
    Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, common in hospital and often missed.
  23. Psychiatry of childhood and adolescence
    Many mental disorders begin in childhood and adolescence.
  24. Mental health law in the UK
    Mental health law allows compulsory assessment and treatment in limited circumstances while protecting rights.

Neurology and Psychiatry: questions students ask

What does FAST stand for?

Face, Arm, Speech, Time to call 999.

When should you call 999 for a seizure?

If it lasts 5 minutes or more, repeats without recovery, or causes injury.

What is the commonest pattern of MS at onset?

Relapsing–remitting MS.

Which features suggest dementia with Lewy bodies?

Fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations and parkinsonism.
