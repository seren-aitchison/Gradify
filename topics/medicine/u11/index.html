Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why does serum creatinine fall in normal pregnancy?

Glomerular filtration rate rises by about 50%. From “Normal pregnancy and antenatal care” →

In plain English: Most pregnancies are healthy, and antenatal care aims to keep them so while detecting problems early. Key idea: Pregnancy causes higher cardiac output, plasma volume and GFR, and a prothrombotic state; care starts with booking by 10 weeks and a dating scan, and includes infection, haemoglobinopathy and anomaly screening, folic acid and vitamin D.

When does the established first stage of labour begin?

When there are regular painful contractions and the cervix is dilated to 4 cm or more. From “Labour and delivery” →

In plain English: Labour is the process by which the baby, placenta and membranes are delivered. Key idea: Labour has three stages, from cervical dilatation to birth to delivery of the placenta; progress is tracked on a partogram and the fetus by auscultation or CTG; delay may need oxytocin, assisted birth or caesarean birth.

Which is the only contraceptive method that also protects against STIs?

Condoms. From “Contraception” →

In plain English: Effective contraception allows people to plan pregnancies. Key idea: LARC methods are the most effective; UKMEC grades safety from 1 to 4, with oestrogen avoided in migraine with aura, older heavy smokers and VTE; the copper IUD is the most effective emergency contraception.

What is the difference between a median age and a limit age for a milestone?

Half of children reach a skill by the median age; failing to reach it by the limit age should prompt assessment. From “Child development and the developmental assessment” →

In plain English: Children develop skills in a predictable sequence, and delays can be the first sign of a medical, sensory or social problem. Key idea: Development is assessed across gross motor, fine motor and vision, speech and hearing, and social domains; limit ages and regression are red flags, prematurity is corrected until 2, and delay needs history, examination and referral.

What is the main complication of Kawasaki disease?

Coronary artery aneurysms. From “Childhood infections and immunisation” →

In plain English: Many childhood infections can be prevented by vaccination, and others need prompt recognition. Key idea: The UK programme starts at 8 weeks with the 6-in-1, MenB and rotavirus vaccines, with MMR at 1 year; measles and scarlet fever are notifiable; Kawasaki disease causes prolonged fever and risks coronary aneurysms, treated with IVIG.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Paediatrics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What daily dose of folic acid is advised for most women in early pregnancy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    400 micrograms. 400 micrograms reduces neural tube defects; higher-risk women take 5 mg.

  2. Where do most ectopic pregnancies implant? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The fallopian tube. Over 90% of ectopic pregnancies are tubal.

  3. Which drug is first line for hypertension in pregnancy in NICE guidance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Labetalol. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are avoided in pregnancy; labetalol is first line.

  4. Why does haemoglobin often fall in normal pregnancy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Plasma volume rises more than red cell mass. The dilutional effect causes a physiological anaemia.

  5. A woman has bleeding at 8 weeks, and the scan shows no intrauterine pregnancy or mass. What is the next step? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Serial serum hCG measurements 48 hours apart. A pregnancy of unknown location needs serial hCG to identify ectopic pregnancy.

  6. Which woman should be advised aspirin from 12 weeks? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    One with chronic kidney disease. Chronic kidney disease is a high risk factor for pre-eclampsia.

  7. A woman is RhD negative. What should be offered? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Anti-D prophylaxis. Anti-D prevents RhD sensitisation that could harm future pregnancies.

  8. A woman at 6 weeks has shoulder tip pain, tachycardia and hypotension. What is the priority? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Resuscitation and urgent surgical management. Shoulder tip pain and shock suggest a ruptured ectopic with intraperitoneal bleeding.

  9. A woman with pre-eclampsia has a seizure. Which drug is given? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Magnesium sulfate. Magnesium sulfate treats eclampsia and prevents further seizures.

Key takeaways

  • Pregnancy causes higher cardiac output, plasma volume and GFR, and a prothrombotic state; care starts with booking by 10 weeks and a dating scan, and includes infection, haemoglobinopathy and anomaly screening, folic acid and vitamin D.
  • Early pregnancy bleeding is usually miscarriage, but ectopic pregnancy must be excluded with a pregnancy test, transvaginal ultrasound and serial hCG; management may be expectant, medical or surgical.
  • Pre-eclampsia is new hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria or organ dysfunction; severe features include headache, visual change, epigastric pain and HELLP; aspirin prevents it in high-risk women, labetalol treats hypertension, magnesium sulfate treats eclampsia and delivery is definitive.
  • Diabetes in pregnancy raises the risk of macrosomia, shoulder dystocia and neonatal hypoglycaemia; GDM is diagnosed by OGTT (fasting 5.6 or 2-hour 7.8 mmol/L or more) and treated with diet, metformin and insulin, with lifelong screening afterwards.
  • Labour has three stages, from cervical dilatation to birth to delivery of the placenta; progress is tracked on a partogram and the fetus by auscultation or CTG; delay may need oxytocin, assisted birth or caesarean birth.
  • PPH is 500 mL or more, usually from uterine atony, and is treated with massage, uterotonics and tranexamic acid; shoulder dystocia is managed with McRoberts and suprapubic pressure; cord prolapse needs elevation of the presenting part and urgent birth.

Common mistakes in Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Paediatrics

  • Interpreting a falling haemoglobin in pregnancy as always pathological. Some fall is dilutional, though true iron deficiency anaemia is also common.
  • Assuming a woman with abdominal pain cannot be pregnant. Always perform a pregnancy test in women of reproductive age.
  • Assuming pre-eclampsia resolves immediately after birth. It can worsen or first appear postpartum, including eclampsia.
  • Thinking gestational diabetes has no long-term consequences. It greatly increases the later risk of type 2 diabetes.
  • Assuming every woman in labour needs continuous CTG. For low-risk women, intermittent auscultation is recommended.
  • Underestimating blood loss. Visual estimates are often too low, and young women may compensate until they suddenly decompensate.

Lessons in this school

  1. Normal pregnancy and antenatal care
    Most pregnancies are healthy, and antenatal care aims to keep them so while detecting problems early.
  2. Bleeding in early pregnancy
    Bleeding in the first trimester is common and usually due to miscarriage, but ectopic pregnancy is a life-threatening cause that must not be missed.
  3. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
    Hypertensive disorders are a major cause of maternal and fetal harm.
  4. Diabetes in pregnancy
    Diabetes in pregnancy, whether present before pregnancy or developing during it, increases risks for mother and baby.
  5. Labour and delivery
    Labour is the process by which the baby, placenta and membranes are delivered.
  6. Obstetric emergencies
    Obstetric emergencies can threaten the lives of both mother and baby within minutes.
  7. The postnatal period and perinatal mental health
    The weeks after birth bring physical recovery and major psychological adjustment.
  8. The menstrual cycle and its disorders
    The menstrual cycle is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, and its disorders are among the most common reasons women see a doctor.
  9. Contraception
    Effective contraception allows people to plan pregnancies.
  10. Subfertility
    Subfertility affects around 1 in 7 couples in the UK.
  11. The menopause
    The menopause is the permanent end of menstruation, and its symptoms can greatly affect quality of life.
  12. Gynaecological cancers
    Cancers of the cervix, uterus, ovary and vulva differ in their causes, presentation and prevention.
  13. Sexually transmitted infections
    Sexually transmitted infections are common and often have no symptoms, yet they can cause serious complications.
  14. Child development and the developmental assessment
    Children develop skills in a predictable sequence, and delays can be the first sign of a medical, sensory or social problem.
  15. The sick child
    Recognising a seriously ill child is one of the most important skills in paediatrics.
  16. The newborn
    The first days of life include important checks and screening, and some common problems such as jaundice need careful assessment.
  17. Respiratory illness in children
    Respiratory infections and wheeze are the most common reasons children see a doctor or are admitted to hospital.
  18. Childhood infections and immunisation
    Many childhood infections can be prevented by vaccination, and others need prompt recognition.
  19. Growth and nutrition in children
    Growth is a sensitive marker of a child's health.
  20. Congenital heart disease
    Congenital heart disease is the most common major birth defect.
  21. Safeguarding children
    Every doctor has a duty to protect children from abuse and neglect.
  22. Common paediatric emergencies
    Children can deteriorate quickly, and a structured approach saves lives.

Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Paediatrics: questions students ask

What is a threatened miscarriage?

Bleeding in early pregnancy with a closed cervix and a pregnancy that may continue.

What does HELLP stand for?

Haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets.

Which glucose values diagnose GDM according to NICE?

Fasting glucose of 5.6 mmol/L or more, or a 2-hour glucose of 7.8 mmol/L or more.

What are the four Ts of postpartum haemorrhage?

Tone, trauma, tissue and thrombin.
