Five sample flashcards

Who regulates doctors in the UK?

The General Medical Council (GMC). From “Good medical practice and professionalism” →

In plain English: The General Medical Council sets the standards every UK doctor must meet, and medical students are held to the same values. Key idea: The GMC regulates doctors through the register, standards, revalidation and fitness to practise; Good medical practice has four domains, and the same values of honesty, competence and respect apply to medical students.

Does an apology under the duty of candour admit legal liability?

No. From “Raising concerns and the duty of candour” →

In plain English: Doctors and students have a duty to act when patient safety is at risk and to be open when things go wrong. Key idea: Doctors and students must act on patient safety concerns, escalating through supervisors, incident systems and Freedom to Speak Up routes, and must be open, honest and apologise to patients when things go wrong.

When is the embryo most vulnerable to structural malformations?

During organogenesis, roughly weeks 3 to 8 after conception. From “Prescribing in pregnancy, children and older people” →

In plain English: Pregnancy, childhood and older age each change how medicines act and the harms they can cause. Key idea: In pregnancy weigh drug risks against untreated illness and avoid known teratogens such as valproate unless strict conditions are met; in children check weight-based doses in the BNF for Children; in older people review polypharmacy with STOPP/START and deprescribe where appropriate.

What does SBAR stand for?

Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. From “Teamwork, handover and leadership” →

In plain English: Safe care depends on how well people work together. Key idea: Effective teams share goals, communicate in a closed loop and value psychological safety; SBAR and structured, protected handover reduce errors, and every doctor shows leadership, followership and early escalation.

What is length-time bias?

Screening preferentially detects slow-growing disease that has a better prognosis. From “Screening: principles and criteria” →

In plain English: Screening aims to find disease or risk early in people without symptoms, but it can harm as well as help. Key idea: Screening should meet the Wilson and Jungner principles and UK NSC criteria, including RCT evidence that benefit outweighs harm; lead-time, length-time and overdiagnosis biases can mislead, and people should make an informed choice.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Professional Practice, Prescribing and Population Health

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which body keeps the UK register of medical practitioners? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The General Medical Council. The GMC holds the register and sets professional standards for doctors.

  2. Which principle concerns the fair distribution of limited healthcare resources? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Justice. Justice includes fairness in how scarce resources are allocated.

  3. Which is one of the five principles of the Mental Capacity Act 2005? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A person must be assumed to have capacity unless shown otherwise. The Act starts from a presumption of capacity for everyone aged 16 and over.

  4. A student posts a photo from theatre in which a patient's tattoo is visible. What is the main problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The patient could be identified without consent. Identifiable features breach confidentiality even when no name is given.

  5. A doctor argues that a rule against lying must be kept even if lying would comfort a patient. Which approach is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Deontology. Deontology judges acts by duties and rules rather than by their outcomes.

  6. Which risk would most clearly need discussing under the Montgomery test? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    One this patient would attach significance to. Materiality depends on what a reasonable person, or this patient, would consider significant.

  7. Which of these is most likely to lead to a student fitness to practise referral? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Forging a supervisor's signature on a logbook. Dishonesty such as forging signatures is among the most serious concerns.

  8. A ward has one side room and two patients who could use it. Which approach is most defensible? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Allocate by clinical need using clear criteria. Justice requires fair criteria based on need, applied consistently and transparently.

  9. A 14-year-old understands fully the risks and benefits of a proposed treatment and wants it. What applies? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She may consent if judged Gillick competent. Gillick competence allows under 16s with sufficient understanding to consent.

Key takeaways

  • The GMC regulates doctors through the register, standards, revalidation and fitness to practise; Good medical practice has four domains, and the same values of honesty, competence and respect apply to medical students.
  • The four principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice are prima facie duties that must be balanced; consequentialist, deontological and virtue approaches add perspective, and a structured framework helps work through cases.
  • Valid consent is voluntary, informed and given with capacity; Montgomery requires discussion of material risks and alternatives, the Mental Capacity Act sets a decision-specific test with best interests for those who lack capacity, and under 16s may consent if Gillick competent.
  • Confidential information may be disclosed with consent, when required by law or when justified in the public interest; share only the minimum, follow the Caldicott principles and data protection law, and seek advice when unsure.
  • Doctors and students must act on patient safety concerns, escalating through supervisors, incident systems and Freedom to Speak Up routes, and must be open, honest and apologise to patients when things go wrong.
  • A safe prescription identifies the patient and allergies and states the generic drug, dose, route, frequency, dates and indication, avoids abbreviations and trailing zeros, and meets extra legal rules for controlled drugs.

Common mistakes in Professional Practice, Prescribing and Population Health

  • Thinking professional standards only start at graduation. Students are expected to meet the values of Good medical practice, and concerns can affect provisional registration.
  • Treating beneficence as overriding autonomy. A person with capacity may refuse treatment even when doctors believe it would benefit them.
  • Assuming a patient lacks capacity because they make an unwise decision or have a diagnosis such as dementia. Capacity must be assessed for the specific decision at the time it is needed.
  • Believing that confidentiality ends when a patient dies. The duty of confidentiality continues after death.
  • Waiting for proof before raising a concern. You need only an honest, reasonable belief that patient safety may be at risk.
  • Leaving allergy status blank. Every chart should record allergy status, and "no known drug allergies" should be written when that is the case.

Lessons in this school

  1. Good medical practice and professionalism
    The General Medical Council sets the standards every UK doctor must meet, and medical students are held to the same values.
  2. Medical ethics: principles and frameworks
    Ethical reasoning helps doctors make and justify difficult decisions.
  3. Consent and capacity
    Valid consent and an understanding of capacity underpin every examination and treatment.
  4. Confidentiality and information governance
    Patients must be able to trust doctors with personal information.
  5. Raising concerns and the duty of candour
    Doctors and students have a duty to act when patient safety is at risk and to be open when things go wrong.
  6. Writing a safe prescription
    Prescribing errors are common and can cause serious harm.
  7. Adverse drug reactions and interactions
    Adverse drug reactions are a major cause of hospital admission and harm.
  8. Prescribing in renal and hepatic impairment
    The kidneys and liver clear most drugs, so impairment of either can lead to accumulation and toxicity.
  9. Prescribing in pregnancy, children and older people
    Pregnancy, childhood and older age each change how medicines act and the harms they can cause.
  10. Therapeutic drug monitoring
    For a small number of medicines, measuring blood levels helps keep treatment effective and safe.
  11. Patient safety and human factors
    Most harm in healthcare comes from flawed systems rather than careless individuals.
  12. Clinical governance and audit
    Clinical governance is the framework through which NHS organisations are accountable for improving quality.
  13. Teamwork, handover and leadership
    Safe care depends on how well people work together.
  14. Epidemiology: measures of disease frequency and association
    Epidemiology measures how common disease is and how strongly it is linked to exposures.
  15. Study design and critical appraisal
    Different research questions need different study designs, each with its own strengths and biases.
  16. Evidence-based medicine
    Evidence-based medicine combines the best research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values.
  17. Screening: principles and criteria
    Screening aims to find disease or risk early in people without symptoms, but it can harm as well as help.
  18. Health inequalities and the social determinants of health
    Health differs systematically between social groups, and much of that difference is avoidable.
  19. Health promotion and behaviour change
    Many leading causes of ill health are linked to behaviours such as smoking, inactivity and alcohol.
  20. The NHS and the organisation of healthcare
    Doctors work within a complex health system that shapes what care patients receive.

Professional Practice, Prescribing and Population Health: questions students ask

What does virtue ethics ask?

What a person of good character, such as a good doctor, would do.

What are the four parts of the functional test of capacity?

Understand, retain, use or weigh the information, and communicate a decision.

Who is the Caldicott guardian?

A senior person responsible for protecting patient information in an organisation.

Which 2013 inquiry highlighted a culture that ignored concerns?

The Francis Report into Mid Staffordshire NHS Foundation Trust.
