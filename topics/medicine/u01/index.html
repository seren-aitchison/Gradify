Key terms and formulas at a glance

Lysosomal storage disorder
An inherited deficiency of a lysosomal enzyme, so its substrate accumulates inside cells
Equilibrium potential
The membrane voltage at which there is no net flow of a given ion, calculated by the Nernst equation
Negative feedback
A response that opposes the initial change, returning a variable towards its set point
Osmolality
The concentration of osmotically active particles per kilogram of water
Anion gap
Na⁺ minus the sum of Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻: an estimate of unmeasured anions in plasma
Metaplasia
Reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another in response to chronic stress
Exudate
Protein-rich extravascular fluid produced by increased vascular permeability in inflammation
Granuloma
A focal collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with giant cells and lymphocytes, in chronic inflammation
Embolus
A detached mass (thrombus, fat, air, amniotic fluid, tumour) carried in the blood to lodge in a distant vessel
Metastasis
Spread of a malignant tumour to a site discontinuous with the primary
Oncogene
A mutated or over-expressed proto-oncogene whose gain of function drives cell growth
Pattern recognition receptor
A germline-encoded receptor, such as a Toll-like receptor, that detects conserved microbial or damage molecules

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which organelle is defective in Tay–Sachs and Gaucher disease?

The lysosome: each is an inherited deficiency of one lysosomal enzyme, so its substrate accumulates. From “Cell structure and function in health” →

In plain English: Every disease is ultimately a disorder of cells. Key idea: Rapidly dividing tissues (gut lining, bone marrow, hair follicles) are the first to show the effects of drugs that act on DNA synthesis or the mitotic spindle.

How is plasma osmolality calculated?

2 × Na⁺ + glucose + urea (all in mmol/L). From “Body fluids, osmolality and electrolytes” →

In plain English: Where water and salt sit in the body explains dehydration, oedema, hyponatraemia and how intravenous fluids behave. Key idea: The plasma sodium concentration tells you about water relative to sodium, not about how much sodium the body holds. A patient can be hyponatraemic and sodium-overloaded (heart failure) or hyponatraemic and depleted (diarrhoea).

Which cytokine is the main stimulus for CRP synthesis by the liver?

Interleukin-6 (IL-6). From “Acute inflammation” →

In plain English: Acute inflammation is the body's rapid, stereotyped response to infection and injury. Key idea: Every clinical sign and blood test of inflammation traces back to a mediator: fever and CRP to IL-1, IL-6 and TNF; redness and heat to vasodilators; swelling to leaky venules; pain to bradykinin and prostaglandins. NSAIDs relieve pain and fever by blocking cyclooxygenase.

State Knudson's two-hit hypothesis.

Both alleles of a tumour suppressor gene must be inactivated for cancer to develop; in hereditary cases one is inherited, so only one further somatic hit is needed. From “The hallmarks and genetics of cancer” →

In plain English: Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells, built up mutation by mutation. Key idea: Oncogenes are accelerators stuck on (one mutant copy is enough); tumour suppressors are brakes that fail only when both copies are lost. Inherited syndromes usually involve a tumour suppressor: you inherit the first hit.

Which antibody is most specific for rheumatoid arthritis?

Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP). From “Hypersensitivity and autoimmunity” →

In plain English: The immune system can harm the body it protects, by over-reacting to harmless antigens or attacking self. Key idea: The timing is the clue: minutes suggests IgE (type I); hours to days with tissue deposits suggests immune complexes (type III); 48–72 hours with lymphocytes suggests a T-cell reaction (type IV).

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Medical Science

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which organelle is the main site of ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Mitochondrion. Oxidative phosphorylation takes place on the inner mitochondrial membrane; the Golgi sorts proteins, the RER makes them and peroxisomes oxidise very-long-chain fatty acids.

  2. Which transporter do the gliflozin drugs inhibit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    SGLT2 in the proximal tubule. Gliflozins block sodium–glucose co-transporter 2, so glucose stays in the tubular fluid and is excreted; digoxin inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and proton pump inhibitors the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase.

  3. Which of these is an example of positive feedback? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Oxytocin release increasing uterine contractions during labour. Positive feedback amplifies the change: contractions release more oxytocin, which strengthens contractions. The others oppose the change (negative feedback).

  4. A drug used for gout and the vinca alkaloids used in chemotherapy share a target. What is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Tubulin, the protein of microtubules. Colchicine and vincristine both stop microtubule polymerisation: one reduces neutrophil migration, the other arrests dividing cells in mitosis.

  5. Why is the resting membrane potential of a neurone close to the K⁺ equilibrium potential rather than the Na⁺ one? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺. The membrane potential is a permeability-weighted average of the equilibrium potentials; at rest K⁺ leak channels dominate. The pump moves more Na⁺ (3) than K⁺ (2), and intracellular Na⁺ is low.

  6. A patient's TSH is high and free T4 is low. Where is the fault most likely to be? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The thyroid gland itself. Low T4 with an appropriately raised TSH means the pituitary is responding to a failing gland: primary hypothyroidism.

  7. A child inherits a mitochondrial DNA mutation. Which statement about the family is most accurate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The mother passes it to all her children, but severity varies with the proportion of mutant mitochondria. Mitochondria come from the egg, so transmission is maternal; heteroplasmy (a mix of normal and mutant copies) explains the variable severity.

  8. A patient with severe heart failure has a sodium of 126 mmol/L and ankle oedema. Which statement is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Total body sodium is increased but water is retained even more. Heart failure retains both sodium (hence oedema) and water; the reduced effective circulating volume drives ADH, so water exceeds sodium and the concentration falls.

  9. A gas shows pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 2.8 kPa and HCO₃⁻ 13 mmol/L. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A mixed disorder, such as metabolic acidosis with a primary respiratory alkalosis. Compensation rarely restores pH fully, so a normal pH with grossly abnormal values suggests two primary processes: the classic example is salicylate poisoning.

Key takeaways

  • Rapidly dividing tissues (gut lining, bone marrow, hair follicles) are the first to show the effects of drugs that act on DNA synthesis or the mitotic spindle.
  • Resting potential is mainly a potassium potential. Change plasma K⁺ and you change the excitability of every nerve and muscle cell, most dangerously the heart's.
  • In an endocrine axis, a hormone that is high together with a high stimulating hormone points to a problem above the gland or a stimulus the gland can't ignore; a hormone that is low with a high stimulating hormone points to failure of the gland itself.
  • The plasma sodium concentration tells you about water relative to sodium, not about how much sodium the body holds. A patient can be hyponatraemic and sodium-overloaded (heart failure) or hyponatraemic and depleted (diarrhoea).
  • Read a gas in order: pH (acidaemia or alkalaemia), then which of CO₂ or HCO₃⁻ explains it (the primary process), then whether the other has moved to compensate, then the anion gap for metabolic acidosis.
  • Leakage of cell contents is the basis of many blood tests: troponin from dead myocytes, ALT from hepatocytes, creatine kinase from muscle and amylase or lipase from the pancreas.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Medical Science

  • Assuming all body cells can divide to replace losses. Neurones and cardiac myocytes are essentially permanent (non-dividing) cells, so their loss is replaced by scar, not new tissue.
  • Thinking hyperkalaemia makes cells more excitable for long. A small depolarisation first brings cells nearer threshold, but sustained depolarisation inactivates Na⁺ channels, so the net effect in the heart is depressed conduction.
  • Calling any loop that ends a process "positive feedback". Positive feedback amplifies the original change; the event that stops it (such as delivery of the baby) is outside the loop.
  • Treating every low sodium as a sodium deficit. Hyponatraemia must be assessed with the patient's volume status, plasma and urine osmolality and urine sodium; giving saline to someone with fluid overload or SIADH can make things worse.
  • Calling a disorder "compensated" just because the PaCO₂ and bicarbonate are both abnormal. Check the direction: compensation moves the second value the same way as the first (both low in metabolic acidosis); opposite movements suggest a mixed disorder.
  • Calling dysplasia an adaptation. Dysplasia is disordered growth with atypical cells, a pre-malignant change, unlike metaplasia, which is an orderly switch of cell type.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cell structure and function in health
    Every disease is ultimately a disorder of cells.
  2. Membranes, transport and the resting potential
    Cells keep a different ionic composition from the fluid around them, and that difference powers nerve, muscle and heart.
  3. Homeostasis and negative feedback
    The body holds temperature, glucose, blood pressure and hormone levels within narrow limits by negative feedback.
  4. Body fluids, osmolality and electrolytes
    Where water and salt sit in the body explains dehydration, oedema, hyponatraemia and how intravenous fluids behave.
  5. Acid–base physiology
    Blood pH is held between 7.35 and 7.45 by buffers, the lungs and the kidneys.
  6. Cell injury, adaptation and death
    Cells under stress first adapt, then suffer reversible injury, and finally die.
  7. Acute inflammation
    Acute inflammation is the body's rapid, stereotyped response to infection and injury.
  8. Chronic inflammation and repair
    When injury persists, inflammation becomes chronic and repair runs alongside destruction.
  9. Thrombosis, embolism and infarction
    A thrombus is a clot in a living vessel; an embolus is anything that travels in the blood and lodges; infarction is the tissue death that follows.
  10. Neoplasia: benign and malignant tumours
    A neoplasm is an abnormal mass of tissue whose growth is uncoordinated and persists after the stimulus stops.
  11. The hallmarks and genetics of cancer
    Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells, built up mutation by mutation.
  12. Innate immunity
    Innate immunity is the fast, inborn defence that responds within minutes to hours and recognises broad classes of danger.
  13. Adaptive immunity
    Adaptive immunity recognises specific antigens with enormous diversity and remembers them.
  14. Hypersensitivity and autoimmunity
    The immune system can harm the body it protects, by over-reacting to harmless antigens or attacking self.
  15. Patterns of inheritance in clinical genetics
    Many diseases run in families in predictable ways.
  16. Pharmacodynamics: receptors and dose–response
    Pharmacodynamics is what a drug does to the body.
  17. Pharmacokinetics: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion
    Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to a drug.

Foundations of Medical Science: questions students ask

How many Na⁺ and K⁺ ions does the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase move per ATP?

3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.

Who named homeostasis, and what does it mean?

Walter Cannon (1932): keeping the internal environment stable despite external change.

Roughly how much CO₂ and non-volatile acid does the body make each day?

About 15,000 mmol of CO₂, removed by the lungs, and 50–100 mmol of non-volatile acid, removed by the kidneys.

Which type of necrosis occurs in a cerebral infarct?

Liquefactive necrosis.
