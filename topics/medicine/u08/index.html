Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why do Gram-negative bacteria stain pink?

Their thin peptidoglycan wall does not retain the crystal violet, so they take up the pink counterstain. From “Principles of microbiology” →

In plain English: Choosing the right investigation and treatment for an infection starts with basic microbiology. Key idea: Bacteria are classified by Gram stain, shape and oxygen needs; culture and sensitivity testing, reported using MIC breakpoints, guide the move from empirical to targeted treatment, supported by PCR and serology.

What is an HIV indicator condition?

A condition associated with undiagnosed HIV, such as tuberculosis or lymphoma, that should prompt an HIV test. From “HIV infection” →

In plain English: HIV is now a manageable long-term condition, but late diagnosis remains common. Key idea: HIV causes progressive loss of CD4 cells; offering testing widely, including for indicator conditions, prevents late diagnosis, and effective ART gives near-normal life expectancy and prevents transmission, alongside PrEP and timely PEP.

Within what time does NICE advise analgesia for a sickle cell painful crisis?

Within 30 minutes of arrival. From “Haemoglobinopathies: sickle cell disease and thalassaemia” →

In plain English: Inherited disorders of haemoglobin are common worldwide and in the UK. Key idea: Sickle cell disease and thalassaemia are inherited haemoglobin disorders; sickle crises need effective analgesia within 30 minutes, and acute chest syndrome, stroke, sequestration and infection are emergencies needing urgent specialist care.

What is the commonest childhood cancer?

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. From “Leukaemia” →

In plain English: Leukaemias are cancers of blood-forming cells. Key idea: Leukaemias are acute or chronic and myeloid or lymphoid; acute leukaemia causes bone marrow failure and needs urgent assessment, and NICE NG12 sets very urgent blood count and immediate referral criteria, especially for children.

What does the M in TNM describe?

Distant metastasis. From “Principles of cancer staging and treatment” →

In plain English: Cancer care depends on accurate diagnosis, staging and a plan agreed by a multidisciplinary team. Key idea: TNM describes tumour, nodes and metastases and combines into stages I–IV, distinct from grade; performance status, comorbidities and patient goals guide MDT decisions on curative or palliative treatment with surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy.

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Gram-positive cocci in clusters most likely indicate which organism? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Staphylococci. Applying the standard result: Clusters suggest staphylococci.

  2. Which is one of the five decisions at the 48–72 hour review? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Switch from IV to oral. Applying the standard result: IV to oral switch is a key option.

  3. How does Sepsis-3 define sepsis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated response to infection. Applying the standard result: Organ dysfunction is central.

  4. What does the minimum inhibitory concentration describe? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The lowest antibiotic concentration preventing visible growth. Applying the standard result: MIC measures susceptibility.

  5. Which antibiotics are most strongly associated with C. difficile infection? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Broad-spectrum agents such as cephalosporins and quinolones. Applying the standard result: Broad-spectrum agents disrupt gut flora.

  6. Within what time should high-risk patients with suspected sepsis receive antibiotics? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    One hour. Applying the standard result: NICE advises within the first hour.

  7. A single blood culture bottle grows coagulase-negative staphylococci in a well patient without a line. What is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Skin contamination. A single positive bottle with a skin commensal is often a contaminant.

  8. A patient reports "penicillin allergy" that was nausea as a child. What is the best approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Record it as intolerance and consider assessment or delabelling. Applying the standard result: Intolerance is not allergy.

  9. A patient with sepsis remains hypotensive after fluids, with lactate 5 mmol/L. What is the priority? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Urgent critical care review for vasopressor support. Applying the standard result: This suggests septic shock.

Key takeaways

  • Bacteria are classified by Gram stain, shape and oxygen needs; culture and sensitivity testing, reported using MIC breakpoints, guide the move from empirical to targeted treatment, supported by PCR and serology.
  • Choose antibiotics using local guidelines, patient factors and the narrowest effective spectrum; review at 48–72 hours to stop, switch, narrow or continue; and remember the harms of broad-spectrum use and mislabelled penicillin allergy.
  • Sepsis is infection-driven organ dysfunction; recognise it with NEWS2 and NICE criteria, and for high-risk patients take cultures and lactate, give antibiotics within the hour, resuscitate with fluids, control the source and escalate early to senior and critical care.
  • A focused travel history guides investigation; fever after travel to a malaria-endemic area is malaria until proven otherwise and needs same-day films, while dengue, enteric fever and common infections are also considered, and viral haemorrhagic fever needs risk assessment and isolation.
  • HIV causes progressive loss of CD4 cells; offering testing widely, including for indicator conditions, prevents late diagnosis, and effective ART gives near-normal life expectancy and prevents transmission, alongside PrEP and timely PEP.
  • Healthcare-associated infections include device-related infections, surgical site infection, MRSA, C. difficile and resistant Gram-negatives; prevention relies on standard and transmission-based precautions, device bundles and stewardship, with prompt isolation and NICE-guided management for C. difficile.

Common mistakes in Infection, Haematology and Oncology

  • Waiting for culture results before treating suspected sepsis. Take cultures quickly, but give antibiotics without delay.
  • Continuing IV antibiotics by default. Review at 48–72 hours and switch to oral when the patient meets criteria.
  • Being reassured by a normal temperature. Sepsis can occur without fever, especially in older or immunosuppressed people.
  • Dismissing malaria because the patient took prophylaxis. No prophylaxis is fully protective, and adherence is often incomplete.
  • Testing only people who seem to be at risk. Indicator conditions and routine opt-out testing find many people who would otherwise be missed.
  • Using alcohol gel alone during a C. difficile case. Spores are resistant; wash hands with soap and water.

Lessons in this school

  1. Principles of microbiology
    Choosing the right investigation and treatment for an infection starts with basic microbiology.
  2. Antimicrobial therapy and stewardship
    Antibiotics save lives, but every prescription selects for resistance.
  3. Sepsis
    Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection.
  4. Fever in the returning traveller
    Fever after travel may be a routine infection or something life-threatening.
  5. HIV infection
    HIV is now a manageable long-term condition, but late diagnosis remains common.
  6. Healthcare-associated infection and infection control
    Infections acquired in hospital harm patients and are often preventable.
  7. Vaccination and immunisation
    Immunisation prevents disease in individuals and populations.
  8. Anaemia
    Anaemia is a reduced haemoglobin concentration, and its cause must always be sought.
  9. Haemoglobinopathies: sickle cell disease and thalassaemia
    Inherited disorders of haemoglobin are common worldwide and in the UK.
  10. Haemostasis and bleeding disorders
    Normal haemostasis balances bleeding and clotting.
  11. Anticoagulation
    Anticoagulants prevent stroke and treat venous thromboembolism but carry a risk of bleeding.
  12. Blood transfusion
    Blood transfusion can save lives but carries risks, and many transfusions are avoidable.
  13. Leukaemia
    Leukaemias are cancers of blood-forming cells.
  14. Lymphoma
    Lymphomas are cancers of lymphocytes, usually presenting with enlarged lymph nodes.
  15. Myeloma
    Myeloma is a cancer of plasma cells in the bone marrow, often diagnosed late because its symptoms are non-specific.
  16. Myeloproliferative neoplasms
    Myeloproliferative neoplasms are chronic blood cancers in which the marrow makes too many blood cells.
  17. Principles of cancer staging and treatment
    Cancer care depends on accurate diagnosis, staging and a plan agreed by a multidisciplinary team.
  18. Systemic anticancer therapy and its toxicities
    Systemic anticancer therapy includes chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy, each with characteristic side effects.
  19. Oncological emergencies
    Cancer and its treatment can cause emergencies that need rapid recognition.
  20. Cancer screening programmes
    Screening aims to detect cancer or pre-cancer before symptoms appear.

Infection, Haematology and Oncology: questions students ask

Why does a false penicillin allergy label matter?

It leads to broader or less effective alternatives, increasing resistance and C. difficile risk.

Why measure lactate in suspected sepsis?

A raised lactate indicates tissue hypoperfusion and higher risk, guiding resuscitation.

How many negative malaria tests are needed to exclude malaria?

Three, taken over successive days.

Why is soap and water used for hand hygiene with C. difficile?

Alcohol gel does not kill C. difficile spores.
