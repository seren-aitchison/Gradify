Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the two main parts of most referencing systems?

In-text citations and a reference list or footnotes giving full details. From “Why we reference” →

In plain English: Referencing is more than a rule to avoid penalties: it is how scholars give credit, show their evidence and join an academic conversation. Key idea: Referencing gives credit, lets readers trace and check evidence, shows the range of your reading and places your work in a scholarly conversation; most systems pair short in-text citations with a full list or notes.

In Harvard, how is a reference list ordered?

Alphabetically by author surname. From “Harvard referencing” →

In plain English: Harvard is the most widely used referencing style in UK universities. Key idea: Harvard cites author and year in the text, adding page numbers for quotations and using et al. for several authors, with an alphabetical reference list whose entries follow a set pattern for each type of source.

How does OSCOLA treat punctuation in abbreviations?

It uses minimal punctuation, with no full stops after abbreviations. From “Footnote styles: OSCOLA and Chicago” →

In plain English: Law, history and some humanities use footnote styles, which put source details at the bottom of the page. Key idea: Footnote styles give source details in numbered notes, with a new number for each citation and short forms for repeat citations; OSCOLA is the standard for UK law with minimal punctuation, and Chicago notes and bibliography is common in history.

Does plagiarism have to be intentional to count as misconduct at most universities?

No; it usually counts whatever the intent, though intent may affect the penalty. From “Plagiarism and how to avoid it” →

In plain English: Plagiarism is the most common form of academic misconduct, and much of it is unintentional. Key idea: Plagiarism is presenting others' work or ideas as your own without acknowledgement, including copying, patchwriting and uncited paraphrase; it is misconduct even when unintentional, so careful note-making, referencing as you draft and checking work prevent it.

What did Weber-Wulff and colleagues (2023) find about AI detection tools?

They were neither accurate nor reliable. From “Text-matching software” →

In plain English: Most UK universities run submitted work through text-matching software such as Turnitin. Key idea: Text-matching tools like Turnitin highlight text that matches other sources and give a similarity score, but they do not detect plagiarism; reports must be interpreted, no percentage is safe or unsafe in itself, and AI detectors are unreliable.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Referencing and Academic Integrity

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a purpose of referencing? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Letting readers trace and check your evidence. References let readers find the sources behind your claims and judge them for themselves.

  2. Which of these usually needs a reference? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A specific statistic from a government report. Specific figures come from particular sources and must be referenced; widely known facts do not.

  3. Which style is an author-date style? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    APA. APA cites author surname and year in the text, as Harvard does; the others are numeric or note-based.

  4. A student cites (Jones, 2019) in her essay, but Jones is missing from the reference list. What is the problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Readers cannot find the full details of the source. Each in-text citation must match a full entry so that the source can be identified and checked.

  5. A student includes a diagram she found on a website in her report. What should she do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reference the diagram as a source like any other. Images, diagrams and figures are other people's work and must be referenced; redrawing still needs credit.

  6. A medical student's guide says to cite sources by number in order of first citation. Which style is this likely to be? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Vancouver. Vancouver is a numeric style widely used in medicine and health sciences.

  7. Why does referencing a strong source make an argument more persuasive? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Readers can see the claim rests on traceable, credible evidence. The persuasive force comes from evidence the reader can check, not from the number of references.

  8. A third-year student wants to reuse two paragraphs from his first-year essay in his dissertation. What should he do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Check the rules and acknowledge the earlier work if reuse is allowed. Unacknowledged reuse of your own assessed work can count as self-plagiarism under university rules.

  9. A student finds two websites giving slightly different Harvard formats. What should she do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Follow her own university's Harvard guide. Harvard exists in many versions, so the university's or department's guide sets the standard to follow.

Key takeaways

  • Referencing gives credit, lets readers trace and check evidence, shows the range of your reading and places your work in a scholarly conversation; most systems pair short in-text citations with a full list or notes.
  • Reference all words, ideas, data, images and other material that are not your own and not common knowledge; specific figures and interpretations need references, and when in doubt you should reference.
  • Referencing styles are author-date (Harvard, APA), numeric (Vancouver, IEEE) or note-based (OSCOLA, Chicago); disciplines prefer different styles, so check your module's requirement and apply it consistently.
  • Harvard cites author and year in the text, adding page numbers for quotations and using et al. for several authors, with an alphabetical reference list whose entries follow a set pattern for each type of source.
  • APA 7 is an author-date style that uses an ampersand between authors in brackets, et al. for three or more authors from the first citation, sentence-case titles, no publisher location and DOIs as https links.
  • Numeric styles number each source on first citation and reuse that number, with a reference list in numerical order; Vancouver suits medicine and uses round brackets or superscripts, while IEEE suits engineering and uses square brackets.

Common mistakes in Referencing and Academic Integrity

  • Seeing referencing only as a way to avoid plagiarism penalties. It is also what makes your evidence visible and your argument credible.
  • Assuming that information found online is common knowledge. Being freely available does not make something common knowledge; specific facts and ideas still need a source.
  • Mixing styles in one piece of work, for example using footnotes in some places and Harvard in others. Choose the required style and be consistent.
  • Writing the author's first name or full name in the in-text citation. Use the surname only, with the year.
  • Using Harvard rules in an APA assignment, such as including the place of publication or writing "and" inside brackets. Check the APA 7 rules specifically.
  • Giving a source a new number each time it is cited. A source keeps the number it was given when first cited.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why we reference
    Referencing is more than a rule to avoid penalties: it is how scholars give credit, show their evidence and join an academic conversation.
  2. What needs a reference
    Knowing when to reference is as important as knowing how.
  3. Referencing styles: an overview
    Universities use several referencing styles, and different departments may require different ones.
  4. Harvard referencing
    Harvard is the most widely used referencing style in UK universities.
  5. APA style
    APA style, from the American Psychological Association, is used in psychology, education, nursing and many social sciences.
  6. Numeric styles: Vancouver and IEEE
    Numeric styles are standard in medicine, health sciences, engineering and computing.
  7. Footnote styles: OSCOLA and Chicago
    Law, history and some humanities use footnote styles, which put source details at the bottom of the page.
  8. Secondary referencing
    Sometimes you read about one author's work in another author's book or article.
  9. Reference management software
    Reference managers store your sources and format citations automatically.
  10. Academic integrity and its values
    Academic integrity is the commitment to honest and responsible scholarship.
  11. Plagiarism and how to avoid it
    Plagiarism is the most common form of academic misconduct, and much of it is unintentional.
  12. Collusion and group work boundaries
    Working with others is encouraged at university, but collaboration has limits in individual assessments.
  13. Contract cheating and essay mills
    Contract cheating means getting someone else to complete assessed work for you.
  14. Text-matching software
    Most UK universities run submitted work through text-matching software such as Turnitin.
  15. Generative AI and academic integrity
    Generative AI tools such as chatbots raise new questions for academic integrity.
  16. Using generative AI responsibly in study
    Used well and within the rules, generative AI can support learning; used badly, it can replace the thinking that learning requires.
  17. Academic misconduct procedures
    If a university suspects academic misconduct, it follows a formal procedure.

Referencing and Academic Integrity: questions students ask

What is common knowledge?

Information widely known and found in many general sources without dispute.

In APA 7, when do you use "et al." in the text?

For works with three or more authors, from the first citation.

How is a numeric reference list ordered?

In numerical order of first citation.

What is a short form citation in a footnote style?

An abbreviated citation used for later references to a source already cited in full.
