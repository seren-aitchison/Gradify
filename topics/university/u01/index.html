Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many notional learning hours does one UK credit represent?

About 10 hours, so a 120-credit year is about 1,200 hours of learning. From “What is different about university study” →

In plain English: University study differs from school or college less in difficulty than in who is responsible for learning. Key idea: The biggest change at university is responsibility: you decide what, when and how to study for most of your working week, so planning and study habits matter as much as ability.

Name the six levels of the revised Bloom's taxonomy.

Remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create. From “Learning outcomes and the QAA level descriptors” →

In plain English: Every module states learning outcomes: what you should be able to do by the end. Key idea: Learning outcomes tell you what assessment will reward; read the verb, because describing when the outcome asks you to evaluate is the commonest reason for a mid-range mark.

What did Thomas (2012) find central to student retention and success?

A sense of belonging, built through relationships with staff and peers. From “Personal tutors and academic staff” →

In plain English: Most UK universities give each student a personal or academic tutor. Key idea: Personal tutors help you see the whole picture and connect you to support; bring evidence (feedback, marks, a plan) and a specific question to get the most from them.

What is a reasonable adjustment?

A change to a provision, criterion or practice that removes a substantial disadvantage for a disabled student, such as extra exam time. From “Support services and reasonable adjustments” →

In plain English: Universities offer a wide range of support, from academic skills advice to disability, wellbeing, money and careers services. Key idea: Register with support services early: adjustments, funding and advice take time to set up and work best before problems affect assessment.

What are the three phases of Zimmerman's self-regulation cycle?

Forethought, performance and self-reflection. From “Self-regulated learning” →

In plain English: Self-regulated learners plan, monitor and reflect on their own learning, adjusting as they go. Key idea: Self-regulation is a cycle you can run on any task: plan with a goal and strategy, monitor while working, then reflect and adjust. Attributing setbacks to strategy keeps you improving.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Starting University and Independent Learning

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes how university study differs from school? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Students take responsibility for most of their own learning time. Contact hours usually fall and much of the week is independent study; teaching guides rather than covers everything that will be assessed.

  2. Which best defines independent learning at university? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Taking responsibility for planning, carrying out and evaluating your own learning. Independence concerns who makes learning decisions; seeking help when needed is part of it.

  3. At which FHEQ level is the final year of a three-year honours degree in England? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Level 6. Undergraduate years run Levels 4, 5 and 6; Level 7 is master's.

  4. A 15-credit module has 30 contact hours. About how many hours of independent study does it expect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    About 120. 15 credits × 10 hours = 150 notional hours; 150 − 30 contact hours leaves about 120 hours of independent study.

  5. Which activity is self-directed rather than directed independent study? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Working through extra past questions on a topic you found hard. Directed tasks are set by tutors; extra practice you choose for yourself is self-directed.

  6. A student has passed 240 credits, 120 at Level 4 and 120 at Level 5, and leaves. Which award would they normally be eligible for? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A Diploma of Higher Education. A DipHE needs 240 credits with at least 90 at Level 5; an ordinary degree needs 300 credits.

  7. A student says: "University is just A level but harder." Which response is most accurate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The main change is responsibility for independent learning and new academic conventions, not only harder content. Content is more advanced, but the bigger adjustment is managing independent study and learning a discipline's ways of reading and arguing; deadlines certainly exist.

  8. A student memorises definitions for every lecture but can't answer questions that apply them. What is the best diagnosis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A surface approach: facts held in isolation, not related to each other or used. Isolated memorisation is the surface approach; it can be changed by tasks that require explaining and applying ideas.

  9. A student moving from a Scottish to an English university says Scottish degrees are longer "because they are easier". Which reply is best? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The SCQF honours degree spans four levels and 480 credits, starting at a different point; length reflects structure, not difficulty. Scottish honours degrees run from SCQF 7 to 10 over four years, reflecting a different school system and breadth; both frameworks place honours at the same broad level.

Key takeaways

  • The biggest change at university is responsibility: you decide what, when and how to study for most of your working week, so planning and study habits matter as much as ability.
  • Independent learning is managing your own learning cycle (what you need, how to get it and whether it worked), and that includes knowing when to ask for help.
  • Credit measures the amount of learning and level measures its complexity: an honours degree in England, Wales and Northern Ireland is 360 credits rising from Level 4 to Level 6.
  • Learning outcomes tell you what assessment will reward; read the verb, because describing when the outcome asks you to evaluate is the commonest reason for a mid-range mark.
  • Read the marking criteria before you start, not after you get your mark: they describe exactly what the next band looks like.
  • Act before the deadline: extensions and extenuating circumstances have time limits, and appeals rarely accept problems you could have reported at the time.

Common mistakes in Starting University and Independent Learning

  • Treating timetabled hours as the whole workload. A light timetable usually means more independent study is expected, not less work.
  • Thinking that asking a tutor or peer for help is "not independent". Choosing to seek help when you need it is part of self-direction.
  • Assuming the first year "doesn't count". It often carries no weight in the degree classification, but you must usually pass it to progress, and its credits count towards the award.
  • Ignoring the outcomes in the module handbook and studying only from the lecture slides. The outcomes, not the slides, define what you must be able to do.
  • Reading a mark of 65% as "a third of the work was wrong". UK percentage marks are banded judgements of quality, not proportions correct.
  • Assuming every university works the same way, or that a rule a friend at another university described applies to you. Always check your own regulations and handbook.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is different about university study
    University study differs from school or college less in difficulty than in who is responsible for learning.
  2. Independent learning
    Independent learning doesn't mean learning alone or without help.
  3. How degrees are structured: credits, levels and modules
    A UK degree is built from modules worth credits at defined levels.
  4. Learning outcomes and the QAA level descriptors
    Every module states learning outcomes: what you should be able to do by the end.
  5. Assessment at university: types, criteria and marks
    University assessment comes in many forms and is marked against published criteria.
  6. Academic regulations and the student handbook
    Every university has regulations that decide how you progress, what happens if you fail or fall ill, and how to appeal.
  7. Teaching formats: lectures, seminars, labs and tutorials
    Universities teach through several formats, each with a different purpose and a different job for you.
  8. Personal tutors and academic staff
    Most UK universities give each student a personal or academic tutor.
  9. Writing to academic staff
    Email is how most students contact lecturers, tutors and offices, and a clear, polite message gets a faster, more useful answer.
  10. The virtual learning environment and digital study
    The virtual learning environment (VLE) is where most modules live: materials, recordings, announcements and submission.
  11. The university library
    A university library is far more than a building with books: it is a gateway to databases, journals, e-books and specialist staff.
  12. Support services and reasonable adjustments
    Universities offer a wide range of support, from academic skills advice to disability, wellbeing, money and careers services.
  13. Self-assessment of study skills
    Knowing your strengths and gaps lets you spend effort where it matters.
  14. Setting goals for your degree
    Clear goals direct effort and help you persist.
  15. Motivation and self-efficacy
    Motivation isn't a fixed amount you either have or lack; it depends on what you believe about yourself and the task.
  16. Self-regulated learning
    Self-regulated learners plan, monitor and reflect on their own learning, adjusting as they go.
  17. Building study habits
    Much of what successful students do is habit, not willpower.
  18. Peer learning and study groups
    Learning with and from other students is one of the most effective and least used resources at university.
  19. Belonging and the transition to university
    Starting university is a major life transition, and doubts about fitting in are common and usually temporary.

Starting University and Independent Learning: questions students ask

What is the difference between directed and self-directed independent study?

Directed study is set by tutors (set reading, problem sheets); self-directed study is chosen by the learner (following up references, extra practice).

How many credits is a bachelor's degree with honours on the FHEQ?

Normally 360 credits, 120 per year, with at least 90 at Level 6.

What is constructive alignment?

Designing teaching activities and assessment so that both target the module's intended learning outcomes (Biggs, 1996).

What do external examiners do?

They are academics from other institutions who check that marking and standards are fair and comparable with other universities.
