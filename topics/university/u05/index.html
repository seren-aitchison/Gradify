Lessons in this school

  1. What academic writing is
    Academic writing has its own purposes, conventions and expectations, which differ from school, journalism and everyday writing.
  2. Analysing the question
    Many low marks come from answering a different question from the one set.
  3. Planning and outlining
    A plan turns research into a structure before you write.
  4. Drafting
    Drafting is where a plan becomes continuous prose.
  5. Editing and proofreading
    Good writing is usually rewritten writing.
  6. Essay structure
    An essay's structure makes its argument easy to follow.
  7. Introductions and conclusions
    Introductions and conclusions frame an essay and shape the marker's first and last impressions.
  8. Paragraphs and the PEEL model
    The paragraph is the basic unit of an argument.
  9. Report structure
    Many courses set reports as well as essays, and reports follow different conventions.
  10. Writing a literature review
    A literature review surveys and evaluates research on a topic, either as an assignment in its own right or as part of a dissertation.
  11. Reflective writing
    Many courses, especially in health, education and professional subjects, set reflective writing.
  12. Abstracts and summaries
    Abstracts and summaries condense a longer piece of work into a few sentences.
  13. Academic style and register
    Academic writing uses a particular register: formal, precise and objective in tone.
  14. Clarity and concision
    Clear, concise writing helps markers follow your argument and saves words for analysis.
  15. Hedging and stance
    Academic writers signal how confident they are in their claims.
  16. Using sources in your writing
    Using sources well means integrating them into your own argument rather than letting them take over.
  17. Signposting and cohesion
    Readers follow an argument more easily when writing is cohesive and clearly signposted.
  18. Grammar and punctuation for academic writing
    Errors in grammar and punctuation can obscure meaning and lower marks.
  19. Writing in your discipline
    Each discipline has its own expectations about evidence, structure and style.

Key terms

Academic literacies
The varied, discipline-specific practices of reading and writing in higher education
Instruction word
The word in a question that tells you what kind of thinking to do
Outline
A structured plan listing sections, paragraph points and evidence
First draft
An initial, unpolished version of a text
Editing
Revising content, structure and clarity of a text
Topic sentence
The sentence, usually first, that states a paragraph's main point
Thesis
The central position an essay argues
PEEL
Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link: a paragraph structure
Executive summary
A short overview of a report's purpose, findings and recommendations
Literature review
A critical survey of published research on a topic
Reflective writing
Writing that analyses one's own experience to learn and improve
Abstract
A short, self-contained summary of a whole piece of work
Register
The level of formality and style appropriate to a context
Concision
Expressing an idea in no more words than necessary
