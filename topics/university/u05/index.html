Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name four features of academic writing.

Any four of: clear argument, evidence, referencing, formal register, clear structure, cautious stance. From “What academic writing is” →

In plain English: Academic writing has its own purposes, conventions and expectations, which differ from school, journalism and everyday writing. Key idea: Academic writing presents a referenced, evidence-based argument in a clear structure and formal, cautious style; its conventions serve the purposes of checking, crediting and communicating complex ideas, and they vary between disciplines.

Which section do many writers draft last?

The introduction. From “Drafting” →

In plain English: Drafting is where a plan becomes continuous prose. Key idea: A first draft gets ideas into prose without perfecting them; separating drafting from editing, starting with the easiest section, writing in short regular sessions and leaving time before editing produce better work with less stress.

Which part of a PEEL paragraph do students most often leave out?

The explanation. From “Paragraphs and the PEEL model” →

In plain English: The paragraph is the basic unit of an argument. Key idea: Each paragraph should develop one point using PEEL: a topic sentence, cited evidence, explanation showing what the evidence means, and a link back to the question or onward to the next point.

Name the four main elements of an abstract.

Purpose, methods, main findings and conclusions. From “Abstracts and summaries” →

In plain English: Abstracts and summaries condense a longer piece of work into a few sentences. Key idea: An abstract is a self-contained summary of a whole piece of work, giving purpose, methods, main findings and conclusions in a tight word limit; it differs from an introduction because it includes results, so it is written last.

What is the difference between integral and non-integral citations?

Integral citations make the author part of the sentence; non-integral citations put the author in brackets. From “Using sources in your writing” →

In plain English: Using sources well means integrating them into your own argument rather than letting them take over. Key idea: Integrate sources as evidence for your own points, using integral or non-integral citations and well-chosen reporting verbs, prefer paraphrase to quotation, and keep your voice by leading with your point and evaluating every source.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Academic Writing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which feature is most characteristic of academic writing? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Claims supported by referenced evidence. Academic writing builds a reasoned argument from evidence that readers can trace through references.

  2. A question asks students to identify the similarities and differences between two theories of motivation. Which instruction word is it most likely to use? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Compare. The instruction word tells you the task, and identifying similarities and differences is what this instruction word asks for.

  3. What is freewriting? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Writing continuously without stopping to edit, to generate ideas. Freewriting is an idea-generation technique; the text is raw material, not a draft to submit.

  4. Which sentence is the most appropriate in academic style? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The data indicate a modest rise in attendance (Author, year). It is precise, cautious and referenced, without informal or exaggerated language.

  5. A question asks students to "evaluate the effectiveness of minimum unit pricing for alcohol in Scotland". Which answer plan fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Assess the evidence on its effects in Scotland and judge how effective it has been. Evaluate requires a judgement on effectiveness, limited to Scotland and to this specific policy.

  6. Which outline is most useful for writing an argumentative essay? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A thesis plus one point and its evidence for each paragraph. Paragraph points linked to a thesis give the essay a line of argument and show where evidence goes.

  7. Lea and Street (1998) argue that student writing is best seen as academic literacies. What does this imply for a joint-honours student? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She may need to adapt her writing to each subject's conventions. If conventions differ between disciplines, students working across subjects must learn the expectations of each.

  8. A student's essay on "the causes of the 2008 financial crisis in the UK" spends half its words on the US housing market. What is the main problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The balance drifts from the focus and limits set by the question. US events may be relevant as background, but giving them half the essay ignores the UK limit in the question.

  9. A student's outline has four body sections, but one covers a point unrelated to her thesis. What should she do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Cut or reframe the section so that it supports the thesis. Every part of the body should advance the argument; an unrelated section dilutes it and uses up words.

Key takeaways

  • Academic writing presents a referenced, evidence-based argument in a clear structure and formal, cautious style; its conventions serve the purposes of checking, crediting and communicating complex ideas, and they vary between disciplines.
  • Analyse a question by labelling its instruction, topic, focus and limiting words, interpreting the instruction word precisely, defining key terms and setting a scope that fits the limits and word count.
  • Plan by generating ideas, grouping them into themes, ordering them logically and writing a paragraph-by-paragraph outline tied to a thesis, with words allocated roughly 10 per cent each for introduction and conclusion and 80 per cent for the body.
  • A first draft gets ideas into prose without perfecting them; separating drafting from editing, starting with the easiest section, writing in short regular sessions and leaving time before editing produce better work with less stress.
  • Edit first for argument, structure and clarity, working from whole essay down to words; then proofread for spelling, grammar, punctuation, formatting and references, using time, reading aloud and focused passes.
  • An essay has an introduction with a thesis, a body of focused paragraphs and a conclusion; organise the body thematically, chronologically, comparatively or by argument and counter-argument according to the question, and prefer structures that build analysis into every paragraph.

Common mistakes in Academic Writing

  • Thinking that academic writing means long words and complicated sentences. Precision and clarity are valued more than complexity.
  • Treating "evaluate" or "to what extent" as "describe". These questions require a judgement supported by evidence, not just information.
  • Starting to write without a plan and hoping an argument will emerge. Write at least a brief outline with a thesis first.
  • Polishing every sentence before moving on. This slows drafting and fuels block; draft roughly first, then edit.
  • Relying on spellcheck as proofreading. It misses errors such as "their" for "there" and cannot check meaning or references.
  • Organising an essay by source, summarising one author per paragraph. Organise by points or themes, and use sources as evidence within them.

Lessons in this school

  1. What academic writing is
    Academic writing has its own purposes, conventions and expectations, which differ from school, journalism and everyday writing.
  2. Analysing the question
    Many low marks come from answering a different question from the one set.
  3. Planning and outlining
    A plan turns research into a structure before you write.
  4. Drafting
    Drafting is where a plan becomes continuous prose.
  5. Editing and proofreading
    Good writing is usually rewritten writing.
  6. Essay structure
    An essay's structure makes its argument easy to follow.
  7. Introductions and conclusions
    Introductions and conclusions frame an essay and shape the marker's first and last impressions.
  8. Paragraphs and the PEEL model
    The paragraph is the basic unit of an argument.
  9. Report structure
    Many courses set reports as well as essays, and reports follow different conventions.
  10. Writing a literature review
    A literature review surveys and evaluates research on a topic, either as an assignment in its own right or as part of a dissertation.
  11. Reflective writing
    Many courses, especially in health, education and professional subjects, set reflective writing.
  12. Abstracts and summaries
    Abstracts and summaries condense a longer piece of work into a few sentences.
  13. Academic style and register
    Academic writing uses a particular register: formal, precise and objective in tone.
  14. Clarity and concision
    Clear, concise writing helps markers follow your argument and saves words for analysis.
  15. Hedging and stance
    Academic writers signal how confident they are in their claims.
  16. Using sources in your writing
    Using sources well means integrating them into your own argument rather than letting them take over.
  17. Signposting and cohesion
    Readers follow an argument more easily when writing is cohesive and clearly signposted.
  18. Grammar and punctuation for academic writing
    Errors in grammar and punctuation can obscure meaning and lower marks.
  19. Writing in your discipline
    Each discipline has its own expectations about evidence, structure and style.

Academic Writing: questions students ask

Why does academic writing use referencing?

So readers can check the evidence and so that credit is given to the original authors.

What are the four kinds of words to look for when analysing a question?

Instruction words, topic words, focus words and limiting words.

What three processes did Flower and Hayes describe in writing?

Planning, translating and reviewing.

Why should drafting and editing be kept separate?

Editing while drafting slows writing down and can cause writer's block.
