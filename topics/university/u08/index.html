Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is maths anxiety?

A feeling of tension or fear that interferes with working with numbers. From “Maths anxiety and number confidence” →

In plain English: Many students feel anxious about numbers, which can hold them back. Key idea: Maths anxiety is common worry about numbers that uses up working memory and lowers performance; confidence improves through gradual practice, estimation, written steps and university support services.

Why should you round only the final answer?

To avoid rounding errors building up during calculations. From “Rounding, significant figures and estimation” →

In plain English: Rounding and estimation help you present numbers clearly and check your work. Key idea: Round using the "5 or more rounds up" rule, use significant figures for very large or small numbers, match precision to the data, round only final answers, and use estimation to check calculations.

Give an example of ratio data.

Weight, height, income or time. From “Types of data” →

In plain English: Different types of data need different summaries and analyses. Key idea: Categorical data may be nominal or ordinal; numerical data may be discrete or continuous and interval or ratio; data type determines suitable averages and charts.

Why should you check whether a graph's axis starts at zero?

Because a truncated axis can exaggerate differences. From “Reading graphs critically” →

In plain English: Graphs in the media and research can inform or mislead. Key idea: Read graphs critically by checking what is measured, scales, time periods, sources and what is missing; watch for truncated or dual axes, cherry-picked dates and cumulative totals; remember that graphs show measurements, not always reality or causes.

What does a p-value measure?

The probability of getting results at least as extreme as observed if the null hypothesis were true. From “Statistical significance in plain terms” →

In plain English: Research papers often report "statistically significant" results. Key idea: A p-value is the probability of results at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true, and p < 0.05 is often called significant; significance does not mean importance, so report effect sizes and confidence intervals too.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Numeracy and Data Skills

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where can students often get help with maths at university? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A maths and statistics support centre run by the university. Most universities offer free maths and statistics support.

  2. What is 30 out of 120 as a percentage? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    25%. 30 ÷ 120 × 100 = 25%, a quarter of the total.

  3. What is 45,678 rounded to two significant figures? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    46,000. The first two significant digits are 4 and 5; the next digit 6 rounds up.

  4. Why does maths anxiety lower performance? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Worry uses up working memory needed for calculations. Anxiety competes for limited mental resources.

  5. Unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which description is correct? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A rise of 1 percentage point, or 25% in relative terms. The difference is 1 point; 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25% relative.

  6. Why should a survey of 47 people not report 34.0426%? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The precision suggests more accuracy than such a small sample supports. Precision should match the accuracy of the data.

  7. Which strategy best builds number confidence? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Regular, low-pressure practice starting with familiar problems. Gradual practice builds skill and confidence.

  8. A price rises by 50% and then falls by 50%. What happens? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It ends lower than it started, at 75% of the original. The fall is 50% of a larger number than the rise.

  9. An answer of £1,850 seems wrong after estimating £175. What is the most likely error? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A misplaced decimal point in the calculation. Estimation reveals errors of scale, such as a decimal point in the wrong place.

Key takeaways

  • Maths anxiety is common worry about numbers that uses up working memory and lowers performance; confidence improves through gradual practice, estimation, written steps and university support services.
  • Percentages are parts per hundred; percentage change uses (new − old) ÷ old × 100; percentage points measure differences between percentages; ratios and proportions compare parts and wholes; always check the base and look at absolute numbers.
  • Round using the "5 or more rounds up" rule, use significant figures for very large or small numbers, match precision to the data, round only final answers, and use estimation to check calculations.
  • Use SI units and prefixes such as kilo, milli and micro; multiply to convert to smaller units and divide to larger ones, squaring for area and cubing for volume; keep units with numbers and check consistency to avoid errors.
  • Substitute values with units into formulae, follow BIDMAS for order of operations, and rearrange formulae by doing the same operation to both sides, checking with simple numbers.
  • Categorical data may be nominal or ordinal; numerical data may be discrete or continuous and interval or ratio; data type determines suitable averages and charts.

Common mistakes in Numeracy and Data Skills

  • Believing some people are just "not maths people". Number skills improve with practice and support.
  • Confusing percentage points with percentage change. A rise from 20% to 25% is 5 points but a 25% relative increase.
  • Reporting results with false precision. Too many decimal places suggest more accuracy than the data support.
  • Forgetting to square or cube conversion factors for area and volume. 1 m² is 10,000 cm², not 100 cm².
  • Ignoring the order of operations. 3 + 4 × 2 is 11, not 14, because multiplication comes first.
  • Calculating a mean for nominal data such as degree subject. Only counts and the mode make sense for nominal data.

Numeracy and Data Skills: questions students ask

How do you calculate percentage change?

(New value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.

What does BIDMAS stand for?

Brackets, Indices, Division and Multiplication, Addition and Subtraction.

What is the difference between nominal and ordinal data?

Nominal categories have no order; ordinal categories have a meaningful order.

How is the mean calculated?

Add all values and divide by the number of values.
