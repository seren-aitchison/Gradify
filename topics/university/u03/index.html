Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many hours of learning does one UK credit represent?

About 10 notional hours. From “How students use their time” →

In plain English: University gives students far more control over their time than school. Key idea: UK courses expect about 10 hours of learning per credit, roughly 35 to 40 hours a week for full-time study, much of it independent; students often report fewer hours and many work part-time, so a time audit helps them see where time goes and plan realistically.

Why should formative work not be neglected?

Its feedback helps improve later graded work. From “Prioritising tasks” →

In plain English: There is rarely time to do everything at once. Key idea: The urgent–important matrix sorts tasks into do now, schedule, minimise and drop, helping students protect time for important but non-urgent work; weighting tasks by their contribution to grades and setting three main daily tasks make priorities manageable.

What are mitigating circumstances?

A university process for considering events, such as illness, that affected a student's work. From “Balancing study, work and life” →

In plain English: Many students balance study with paid work, caring, commuting and wellbeing. Key idea: Students balance study with paid work, caring, commuting and other commitments; long work hours are linked with less study and more stress, so realistic limits, flexible jobs, planning around peaks, protecting wellbeing and using university financial, academic and wellbeing support help maintain balance.

What are the three sections of a Cornell page?

The cue column, the notes area and the summary. From “Note-making methods: linear, Cornell and maps” →

In plain English: Notes are tools for understanding and revision, not transcripts. Key idea: Note-making organises information into a selective, structured and personal record; linear notes suit sequential material, the Cornell method adds cue questions and summaries for self-testing, and mind maps show connections, with many students combining methods.

What is retrieval practice?

Recalling information from memory, for example by testing yourself. From “Reviewing and consolidating notes” →

In plain English: Notes are only useful if you return to them. Key idea: Memory fades quickly without review, as the forgetting curve shows, so reviewing notes within a day and using spaced retrieval practice, with summaries, questions, flashcards and maps, consolidates learning far better than rereading or highlighting.

Key takeaways

  • UK courses expect about 10 hours of learning per credit, roughly 35 to 40 hours a week for full-time study, much of it independent; students often report fewer hours and many work part-time, so a time audit helps them see where time goes and plan realistically.
  • Effective planning starts with a semester overview of deadlines, works backwards to set milestones, builds a weekly timetable around fixed commitments with study, preparation, review and buffer time, and breaks large tasks into scheduled steps reviewed each week.
  • The urgent–important matrix sorts tasks into do now, schedule, minimise and drop, helping students protect time for important but non-urgent work; weighting tasks by their contribution to grades and setting three main daily tasks make priorities manageable.
  • Procrastination is voluntary delay despite expected harm, often driven by task aversion, fear of failure, low confidence and immediate distractions, and functions as short-term mood repair; tiny first steps, implementation intentions, timed starts, reducing distractions and self-compassion help overcome it.
  • Multitasking on attention-demanding tasks means costly switching that reduces accuracy and learning, for users and those nearby; removing digital and environmental distractions and using time-blocking and Pomodoro-style focused sessions improve concentration.
  • Students balance study with paid work, caring, commuting and other commitments; long work hours are linked with less study and more stress, so realistic limits, flexible jobs, planning around peaks, protecting wellbeing and using university financial, academic and wellbeing support help maintain balance.

Common mistakes in Time Management, Lectures and Note-making

  • Treating timetabled classes as the whole of a course. Most learning at university happens in independent study.
  • Planning to start an assignment "when I have time". Without scheduled steps, work is pushed to the last minute.
  • Spending the day on urgent but unimportant tasks such as messages. Schedule important work first.
  • Believing you work best under last-minute pressure. Research links procrastination to more stress and poorer performance for most people.
  • Studying with notifications on, assuming you can ignore them. Each interruption costs time to refocus.
  • Taking on as many shifts as possible without considering study needs. Long hours can harm grades and wellbeing.

Time Management, Lectures and Note-making: questions students ask

Why should you work backwards from a deadline?

To set milestones and see when each stage must start.

How does procrastination differ from planned delay?

Procrastination is delay despite expecting to be worse off; planned delay is a deliberate decision to do something later for a good reason.

What is the Pomodoro Technique?

Working in 25-minute focused intervals with short breaks between them.

What is a hardship fund?

University financial support for students facing financial difficulty.
