Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is information literacy?

The ability to think critically and make balanced judgements about information we find and use. From “Information literacy” →

In plain English: Information literacy is the ability to find, evaluate and use information well. Key idea: Information literacy is the ability to find, evaluate and use information critically and ethically, through stages of identifying needs, searching, evaluating, managing, using and communicating information.

What is the difference between a catalogue and a discovery tool?

A catalogue lists items the library holds; a discovery tool searches the catalogue and many electronic resources at once. From “Library catalogues and discovery tools” →

In plain English: University libraries offer catalogues and discovery tools to find books, articles and other resources. Key idea: Catalogues list library holdings, while discovery tools search many resources at once; books are found by shelfmark and e-resources via login; interlibrary loan and access schemes provide items the library does not hold.

Which organisation produces the UK census?

The Office for National Statistics, in England and Wales. From “Grey literature and official statistics” →

In plain English: Not all useful research is published in journals. Key idea: Grey literature includes government, charity, think-tank and conference outputs, often timely but not always peer reviewed; official statistics come from the ONS, government departments and bodies like HESA; evaluate producers, methods, definitions and dates.

What is the difference between misinformation and disinformation?

Misinformation is shared without intent to deceive; disinformation is created deliberately to deceive. From “Recognising misinformation” →

In plain English: Misinformation spreads quickly online and can shape beliefs and behaviour. Key idea: Misinformation is false information shared without intent, disinformation is deliberate deception and malinformation is genuine information used to harm; bias, emotion and repetition spread it; lateral reading, reverse image search and SIFT help check claims.

When should a student collect data for a project involving people?

Only after receiving ethical approval. From “Research ethics for students” →

In plain English: Any research involving people must be ethical. Key idea: Research ethics rests on respect, beneficence, justice and integrity; informed consent, confidentiality and UK GDPR protect participants; student projects usually need ethical approval before data collection.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research and Information Literacy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which stage involves judging whether a source is reliable? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Evaluating the information found. Evaluation checks quality, relevance and bias.

  2. Which is a research question rather than a topic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    How does part-time work affect first-year students' grades?. A research question is specific and answerable.

  3. Which operator narrows a search so both terms must appear? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    AND. AND requires every term to be present, so results become more focused.

  4. Why does information literacy matter more today than in the past? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    There is far more information, and much of it is unreliable or misleading. Abundance makes critical judgement essential.

  5. Why are open "how" or "why" questions often better than yes or no questions? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They invite analysis and explanation rather than a one-word answer. Open questions encourage depth, analysis and explanation.

  6. Why are quotation marks used in "social media"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To search for the exact phrase rather than the separate words. Phrase searching keeps the words together in that exact order.

  7. A student finds a convincing blog post that supports her argument. What should she do next? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Check who wrote it, what evidence it uses and whether better sources exist. Evaluation comes before use, however well a source fits your argument.

  8. A student's question is "What is the effect of technology on society?" What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Narrow it by focusing on a specific technology, group, place and outcome. Broad questions are hard to answer in depth.

  9. A search returns 50,000 results. What is the best next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Add another concept with AND or use more specific terms and filters. Narrowing with more concepts, specific terms and filters makes results manageable.

Key takeaways

  • Information literacy is the ability to find, evaluate and use information critically and ethically, through stages of identifying needs, searching, evaluating, managing, using and communicating information.
  • A research question is a specific, answerable question within a broader topic; good questions are feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant; narrow topics by population, place, time and aspect.
  • Break questions into concepts and synonyms; use AND to narrow, OR to broaden, NOT carefully, truncation, phrase searching and brackets; refine with filters and subject headings and record your searches.
  • Catalogues list library holdings, while discovery tools search many resources at once; books are found by shelfmark and e-resources via login; interlibrary loan and access schemes provide items the library does not hold.
  • Subject databases index discipline-specific research with abstracts, headings and filters; examples include PubMed, PsycINFO, ERIC, Scopus, Web of Science, JSTOR, IEEE Xplore and Westlaw; choose databases to match the question and use subject headings and citation searching.
  • Google Scholar is easy and broad, with useful citation features, but has limited filters and quality control; use it alongside databases; judge web sources by authority, purpose, currency, accuracy and relevance, and use Wikipedia only as a starting point.

Common mistakes in Research and Information Literacy

  • Thinking information literacy just means being good at searching. Evaluating and using information ethically are equally important.
  • Choosing a question so broad it could fill a library. Focused questions lead to deeper, more manageable work.
  • Typing a whole sentence into a database. Databases work best with keywords combined by Boolean operators.
  • Assuming that if Google does not show the full text, the library cannot provide it. Library logins and interlibrary loan give access to much more.
  • Using only one database for an interdisciplinary topic. Different databases index different journals, so searching several finds more.
  • Citing Wikipedia as an academic source. Use it to find better sources, then read and cite those.

Research and Information Literacy: questions students ask

What does FINER stand for?

Feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant.

What does the Boolean operator OR do?

It broadens a search by finding records containing either term.

What is citation searching?

Following an article's references backwards and tracing later articles that cited it forwards.

What does the "Cited by" link in Google Scholar show?

Later works that have cited the article.
