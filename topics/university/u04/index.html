Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a monograph?

A book-length scholarly study of a single topic, usually by one or a few authors. From “Types of academic text” →

In plain English: University reading lists mix many kinds of text, from textbooks to journal articles, reports and grey literature. Key idea: Academic texts include textbooks, monographs, edited collections, journal articles and grey literature; sources can be primary, secondary or tertiary depending on the discipline, and strong work starts broad and then moves to the primary evidence.

What do the letters in SQ3R stand for?

Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review. From “Reading actively: SQ3R and questioning” →

In plain English: Passive reading, where the eyes move but little is learned, is common. Key idea: Active reading engages with a text through questions, prediction and self-testing, and SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) builds in retrieval practice, which helps understanding and memory far more than rereading or highlighting.

Name three reasons an academic text can be difficult.

Any three of: technical vocabulary, assumed background knowledge, complex argument structure, unfamiliar style or translation. From “Reading difficult texts” →

In plain English: Some academic texts are hard because of dense language, unfamiliar theory or complex arguments. Key idea: Texts are difficult because of vocabulary, assumed knowledge, complex arguments or style; reading background first, surveying structure, summarising in sections, keeping a glossary and rereading make hard texts manageable.

Why can an argument with true reasons still fail?

Because it may depend on a hidden assumption that is false. From “Identifying assumptions” →

In plain English: Arguments rest on assumptions that are not stated but must be true for the reasoning to work. Key idea: Assumptions are claims an argument relies on without stating; find them by asking what must be true for the reasons to support the conclusion, test them by negation, and judge whether they are supported and reasonable.

Who introduced the study of heuristics and biases in their 1974 paper?

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman. From “Cognitive biases in reasoning” →

In plain English: Even careful thinkers are affected by systematic biases in judgement. Key idea: Heuristics are useful shortcuts that create systematic biases, such as confirmation bias, anchoring, availability and motivated reasoning; critical thinkers reduce their effect by seeking disconfirming evidence, slowing down and making their reasoning explicit.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is usually a tertiary source? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An encyclopaedia entry. Tertiary sources compile and summarise existing knowledge, as encyclopaedias and handbooks do.

  2. What is scanning? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Searching quickly for a specific piece of information. Scanning looks for one item, such as a date, name or statistic, without reading every line.

  3. In which section of a research article do authors usually interpret their findings and admit limitations? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Discussion. The discussion relates results to previous work, interprets them and notes limitations and implications.

  4. A student wants to know the current state of research on a topic. Which text is the most efficient starting point? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A recent review article. A recent review article surveys and evaluates current research, so it maps the field quickly and points to key studies.

  5. A student has two days and a list of 15 further readings. What is the best approach? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Skim them against her essay question and read closely only the most relevant. Skimming against a clear purpose selects the most useful texts, leaving time for close reading where it matters.

  6. A student has 40 articles from a database search. What is the most efficient first step? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Read each title and abstract to judge relevance. A first pass through titles and abstracts quickly filters out irrelevant articles before deeper reading.

  7. A media historian studies how newspapers reported the 1984 miners' strike. For this project, what are the newspaper reports? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Primary sources. Whether a source is primary depends on the research question: here the reports themselves are the material being analysed.

  8. A student claims that speed reading lets him read every text fully in a third of the time. What does the evidence suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Big gains in speed usually come with a loss of comprehension. Rayner and colleagues (2016) found a trade-off between speed and accuracy: fast reading means understanding less.

  9. An abstract says a teaching method greatly improves grades, but the methods show 12 students at one school and no comparison group. What should the student conclude? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The claim is weakly supported and should be reported with caution. A tiny sample with no comparison group cannot show that the method caused the improvement, whatever the abstract says.

Key takeaways

  • Academic texts include textbooks, monographs, edited collections, journal articles and grey literature; sources can be primary, secondary or tertiary depending on the discipline, and strong work starts broad and then moves to the primary evidence.
  • Strategic reading starts with a purpose, uses skimming and scanning to select relevant texts and sections, and saves close reading for the texts that matter most.
  • Many articles follow IMRaD, and reading them in passes, from abstract and conclusion to introduction and discussion to methods and results, lets you judge relevance quickly and evaluate in depth when needed.
  • Active reading engages with a text through questions, prediction and self-testing, and SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) builds in retrieval practice, which helps understanding and memory far more than rereading or highlighting.
  • Reading notes are a selective, purpose-led record that starts with full references and keeps summary, quotation and your own comments clearly separate, with page numbers throughout.
  • Quotation uses exact words, paraphrase restates a passage in your own words and structure, and summary condenses the main ideas; all three must be cited, and swapping a few words is patchwriting, not paraphrase.

Common mistakes in Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking

  • Relying only on textbooks and websites for an essay. Use them to get started, then cite peer-reviewed articles and primary sources for your evidence.
  • Starting at page one of every book on the list. Set a purpose, check the contents and index, and read only what serves that purpose.
  • Reading only the abstract and citing the article as if you had read it. Abstracts compress and can overstate; read at least the discussion and, for key evidence, the methods.
  • Highlighting most of a page and calling it studying. Highlighting alone is passive; turn the text into questions and test yourself.
  • Copying sentences into notes without quotation marks and later pasting them into an essay as your own words. Mark every quotation clearly when you take it.
  • Thinking that a paraphrase does not need a citation because it is in your own words. The idea still belongs to the author, so cite it.

Lessons in this school

  1. Types of academic text
    University reading lists mix many kinds of text, from textbooks to journal articles, reports and grey literature.
  2. Reading strategically: purpose and selection
    No student can read everything on a long reading list, so reading has to be strategic.
  3. Reading a journal article
    Journal articles are the main way new research is reported, but their structure can be off-putting at first.
  4. Reading actively: SQ3R and questioning
    Passive reading, where the eyes move but little is learned, is common.
  5. Making notes from reading
    Notes turn reading into material you can use for seminars, essays and exams.
  6. Paraphrasing and summarising
    Paraphrasing and summarising let you use other writers' ideas in your own words.
  7. Reading difficult texts
    Some academic texts are hard because of dense language, unfamiliar theory or complex arguments.
  8. What critical thinking is
    Critical thinking is one of the most valued outcomes of a degree, but it is often misunderstood as being negative.
  9. Arguments: claims, reasons and conclusions
    In critical thinking, an argument is a set of reasons offered to support a conclusion, not a quarrel.
  10. Identifying assumptions
    Arguments rest on assumptions that are not stated but must be true for the reasoning to work.
  11. Evaluating evidence
    Arguments are only as strong as the evidence behind them.
  12. Flaws in reasoning
    Some arguments go wrong not because the evidence is false but because the reasoning is faulty.
  13. Cognitive biases in reasoning
    Even careful thinkers are affected by systematic biases in judgement.
  14. Comparing perspectives
    Academic debates involve competing theories, schools of thought and interpretations.
  15. Moving from description to analysis
    A common piece of feedback is "too descriptive".
  16. Building your own argument
    At university you are expected not just to evaluate other people's arguments but to make your own.

Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking: questions students ask

What is the difference between skimming and scanning?

Skimming reads quickly for the overall gist; scanning searches for a specific piece of information.

What does IMRaD stand for?

Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion.

What should be written at the top of every set of reading notes?

The full bibliographic details of the source.

What is the difference between a paraphrase and a summary?

A paraphrase restates a specific passage at similar length; a summary condenses the main ideas into a much shorter form.
