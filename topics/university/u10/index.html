Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does an open-book exam mainly test?

Understanding, analysis and the ability to find and apply information, rather than memory alone. From “Types of exam” →

In plain English: University exams come in many forms, each testing different skills. Key idea: University exams include unseen essays, MCQs, problem papers, open-book, take-home, practical and oral exams; each tests different skills, so check the format and practise in it.

Why can rereading create an illusion of competence?

Familiar material feels known, even though you may not be able to recall it. From “Revision techniques that work” →

In plain English: Some revision techniques are much more effective than others. Key idea: Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaboration and dual coding are effective; rereading and highlighting are weak and can create an illusion of competence; effective techniques often feel harder but work better.

What should you do if you run out of time on a question?

Write concise points or an outline showing what you would include. From “Exam technique: before and on the day” →

In plain English: Good preparation in the final days and on exam day helps you perform at your best. Key idea: Before exams, check logistics and rules, sleep and prepare equipment; at the start, read instructions and allocate time by marks; during the exam, answer the question asked, manage time, outline if short and check answers.

What are method marks?

Marks awarded for correct working even if the final answer is wrong. From “Problem-solving and calculation exams” →

In plain English: Problem-solving exams test the ability to apply methods under time pressure. Key idea: Solve problems systematically by reading carefully, sketching, choosing methods, substituting with units and checking; show working to earn method marks and follow-through; check with estimation and units, and manage time by moving on when stuck.

What mark is usually needed for a First-class degree in the UK?

70% or above. From “Results, resits and progression” →

In plain English: Understanding results and what happens next helps students plan. Key idea: Many UK universities use a 40% pass mark and classifications of First, 2:1, 2:2 and Third; failed modules may be resat, often capped at 40%; progression usually requires 120 credits per year, and regulations vary.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Exams and Revision

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which exam type uses selected answers from a list of options? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A multiple-choice question exam. MCQ exams ask students to choose answers from options.

  2. What should be the first step in planning revision? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Listing all topics and rating confidence in each. An audit shows what needs most attention.

  3. Which technique is most effective for long-term memory? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Testing yourself with practice questions over time. Retrieval practice spaced over time strengthens recall more than rereading or highlighting.

  4. Why are open-book exams not an excuse to skip revision? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    There is too little time to look everything up without good understanding. Understanding and organised notes are essential under time limits.

  5. Why is interleaving topics helpful? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It helps you learn to distinguish and choose methods across topics. Mixing topics strengthens flexible understanding.

  6. Why are effective techniques often described as desirable difficulties? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They feel harder but lead to stronger long-term learning. Effortful learning builds durable memory.

  7. Which preparation best suits an unseen essay exam? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Practising planning and writing timed essay answers. Practice in the exam format builds the needed skills.

  8. A student plans 12-hour revision days with no breaks. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Plan shorter sessions with breaks, sleep and rest to avoid burnout. Rest and sleep support memory and protect wellbeing, so long days without breaks backfire.

  9. A student says rereading feels effective because she recognises everything. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Test recall without notes, as recognition is not the same as recall. Recognition can create an illusion of competence.

Key takeaways

  • University exams include unseen essays, MCQs, problem papers, open-book, take-home, practical and oral exams; each tests different skills, so check the format and practise in it.
  • Plan revision by auditing topics and confidence, prioritising weaker and high-value topics, using short, spaced and interleaved sessions, scheduling past papers, and balancing revision with rest and flexibility.
  • Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaboration and dual coding are effective; rereading and highlighting are weak and can create an illusion of competence; effective techniques often feel harder but work better.
  • Use past papers and practice questions, progressing from untimed work with notes to timed answers and full mocks; compare with mark schemes and criteria, learn from feedback, and revise the whole syllabus rather than predicting questions.
  • Essay subjects need themes, evidence and timed essay practice; quantitative subjects need many problems and understanding of methods; practical and language subjects need rehearsal and regular active practice, all using retrieval and spacing.
  • Before exams, check logistics and rules, sleep and prepare equipment; at the start, read instructions and allocate time by marks; during the exam, answer the question asked, manage time, outline if short and check answers.

Common mistakes in Exams and Revision

  • Thinking open-book exams need no revision. Without good understanding and organised notes, there is not enough time to look everything up.
  • Leaving revision until the last few days. Cramming may help short-term recall but leads to weaker long-term memory and more stress.
  • Choosing techniques because they feel easy. Effortful techniques such as self-testing are more effective, even if they feel harder.
  • Trying to predict exam questions and revising only those topics. Exams may cover any part of the syllabus.
  • Revising maths by reading worked examples. Problem-solving skills develop by attempting problems yourself.
  • Spending too long on the first question. Allocating time by marks protects the marks available on later questions.

Exams and Revision: questions students ask

What is spaced practice?

Spreading study of a topic over several sessions with gaps between them.

What is retrieval practice?

Recalling information from memory, such as by self-testing with flashcards or practice questions.

How should past paper practice progress?

From untimed answers with notes, to answers without notes, to timed answers and full mock exams.

How should students revise for quantitative subjects?

By practising many problems, including unfamiliar ones, and understanding why methods work.
