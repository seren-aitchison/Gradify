Lessons in this school

  1. Structure of the heart
    The heart is two pumps side by side: the right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side to the body.
  2. The cardiac conduction system
    Each heartbeat starts in the heart itself and spreads along a specialised conduction pathway.
  3. The cardiac cycle
    The cardiac cycle is the sequence of filling and emptying in one heartbeat.
  4. Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate
    Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps each minute, the product of stroke volume and heart rate.
  5. Regulation of heart rate during exercise
    Heart rate is adjusted to demand by the cardiac control centre in the medulla, using information from receptors and acting through the autonomic nervous system and hormones.
  6. Heart rate responses to exercise
    Heart rate tells a story through every session: an anticipatory rise, a rapid climb, a steady state or a continued rise, and recovery.
  7. Blood vessels and blood pressure
    Arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules and veins each have a structure suited to their job.
  8. The vascular shunt and venous return
    In exercise, blood is redistributed from organs to working muscles, and the return of blood to the heart is boosted.
  9. Blood composition and oxygen transport
    Blood carries oxygen, carbon dioxide, fuel, heat and hormones.
  10. The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve
    The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve shows how saturated haemoglobin is at each partial pressure of oxygen.
  11. Structure of the respiratory system
    The respiratory system moves air to a huge, thin gas-exchange surface.
  12. Mechanics of breathing
    Breathing works by changing the volume of the chest cavity, so that pressure differences move air in and out.
  13. Lung volumes and capacities
    Lung volumes describe how much air moves in each breath and how much stays in the lungs.
  14. Gas exchange at the lungs and muscles
    Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion down partial pressure gradients, at the alveoli and at the muscles.
  15. Control of breathing during exercise
    Breathing is adjusted automatically to match the body's needs.
  16. Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max)
    VO2max is the highest rate at which a person can take in, transport and use oxygen, the classic measure of aerobic capacity.
  17. Cardiovascular drift
    In prolonged exercise at a steady workload, especially in the heat, heart rate creeps upwards while stroke volume falls.
  18. Cardiorespiratory adaptations to endurance training
    Months of endurance training remodel the heart, blood, vessels, lungs and muscles.

Key terms

Myogenic
Able to generate its own electrical impulse without nervous stimulation, as cardiac muscle does
Sinoatrial node
The heart's pacemaker in the right atrium wall, which initiates each heartbeat
Diastole
The relaxation phase of the cardiac cycle, when the chambers fill
Cardiac output
The volume of blood pumped by the left ventricle per minute (Q = SV × HR)
Cardiac control centre
The region of the medulla oblongata that regulates heart rate via the autonomic nervous system
Steady state
A plateau in heart rate (and oxygen uptake) when supply meets demand in submaximal exercise
Systolic blood pressure
The highest arterial pressure, during ventricular contraction
Vascular shunt
The redistribution of cardiac output from organs to working muscles during exercise
Haemoglobin
The iron-containing protein in red blood cells that binds and carries oxygen
Partial pressure
The pressure exerted by one gas in a mixture, which drives its diffusion
Alveoli
Tiny air sacs in the lungs where gas exchange occurs
Diaphragm
The dome-shaped sheet of muscle below the lungs, the main muscle of inspiration
Tidal volume
The volume of air breathed in or out in one normal breath
Diffusion
The net movement of molecules from an area of higher to lower partial pressure or concentration
