Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which blood vessel carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart?

The pulmonary vein. From “Structure of the heart” →

In plain English: The heart is two pumps side by side: the right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side to the body. Key idea: The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation); the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body (systemic circulation), which is why its wall is thicker.

State Starling's law of the heart.

The greater the stretch of the ventricle by filling, the greater the force of contraction and so the stroke volume. From “Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate” →

In plain English: Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps each minute, the product of stroke volume and heart rate. Key idea: Q = SV × HR; resting Q is similar for trained and untrained people, but training raises SV, lowers resting HR and greatly raises maximal Q.

Where is the vasomotor centre?

In the medulla oblongata. From “The vascular shunt and venous return” →

In plain English: In exercise, blood is redistributed from organs to working muscles, and the return of blood to the heart is boosted. Key idea: Exercise redirects blood to the muscles by vasodilation there and vasoconstriction elsewhere; venous return depends on the muscle pump, respiratory pump, valves and venoconstriction.

What is the role of surfactant?

To lower surface tension in the alveoli so they don't collapse. From “Structure of the respiratory system” →

In plain English: The respiratory system moves air to a huge, thin gas-exchange surface. Key idea: Air passes nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli; the alveoli give a huge, thin, moist, well-perfused surface for diffusion.

Which nerve stimulates the diaphragm?

The phrenic nerve. From “Control of breathing during exercise” →

In plain English: Breathing is adjusted automatically to match the body's needs. Key idea: The respiratory centre in the medulla increases breathing via the phrenic and intercostal nerves, driven mainly by rising CO₂ detected by chemoreceptors, with proprioceptors causing the fast initial rise.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which valve lies between the left atrium and the left ventricle? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Bicuspid (mitral) valve. The bicuspid or mitral valve is the left atrioventricular valve; the tricuspid is on the right, and the aortic and pulmonary valves are semilunar valves at the exits of the ventricles.

  2. Which structure delays the cardiac impulse between atria and ventricles? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The atrioventricular node. The AV node holds the impulse for about a tenth of a second so the atria can finish contracting before the ventricles start.

  3. During diastole, which valves are open? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The atrioventricular valves. In diastole the ventricles are relaxed and filling, so the AV valves are open and the semilunar valves closed to stop backflow from the arteries.

  4. Blood leaves the right ventricle. Where does it go next? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery to the lungs. The right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood via the pulmonary valve and artery to the lungs; the pulmonary veins return it to the left atrium.

  5. Why do the Purkinje fibres spread the impulse from the apex of the heart upwards? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    So the ventricles contract from the bottom up, pushing blood towards the arteries at the top. The ventricular exits (aortic and pulmonary valves) are at the top; contracting from the apex squeezes blood up and out efficiently.

  6. As heart rate rises from 70 to 180 beats per minute, which phase is shortened most? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Diastole. Systole shortens a little, but diastole shortens much more, which eventually limits filling time at very high heart rates.

  7. What makes the semilunar valves open during ventricular systole? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ventricular pressure rising above the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery. Valves are passive: they open when the pressure behind them exceeds the pressure in front.

  8. An athlete's maximal cardiac output rises after a year of endurance training. What is the main cause? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    An increase in maximal stroke volume. Training enlarges the left ventricle and blood volume, raising maximal SV; maximal HR doesn't rise, and a higher ESV would reduce SV.

  9. Why might a coach prefer a measured maximal heart rate to 220 − age when setting zones for an athlete? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Individual maxima can differ from the prediction by 10–20 bpm or more, making predicted zones wrong. The formulas describe population averages with a large spread; a measured maximum makes zones individual.

Key takeaways

  • The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation); the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body (systemic circulation), which is why its wall is thicker.
  • SA node → atria → AV node (delay) → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricles contract from the apex.
  • Diastole fills the heart and systole empties it; SV = EDV − ESV, and exercise raises SV by increasing filling and emptying.
  • Q = SV × HR; resting Q is similar for trained and untrained people, but training raises SV, lowers resting HR and greatly raises maximal Q.
  • The CCC in the medulla raises heart rate through the sympathetic cardiac accelerator nerve and lowers it through the parasympathetic vagus nerve, guided by chemoreceptors, baroreceptors and proprioceptors.
  • Submaximal exercise produces a steady state; maximal exercise produces a continuous rise to maximum; recovery is fast then slow.

Common mistakes in The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise

  • Saying all arteries carry oxygenated blood. Arteries carry blood away from the heart: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
  • Saying the brain starts each heartbeat. The SA node does; nerves and hormones only change how fast it fires.
  • Saying the valves open because the muscles pull them. Valves open and close passively because of pressure differences either side of them.
  • Thinking training increases maximal heart rate. Maximal heart rate is mostly set by age and genetics and is unchanged or slightly lower after training; the gain in maximal cardiac output comes from stroke volume.
  • Saying baroreceptors make heart rate rise in exercise. A rise in blood pressure detected by baroreceptors acts to slow the heart; in exercise their set point is reset upwards so heart rate can rise.
  • Treating 220 − age as exact. Its standard deviation is about 10–12 bpm, so a person's real maximum can be 20 bpm or more away from the prediction.

Lessons in this school

  1. Structure of the heart
    The heart is two pumps side by side: the right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side to the body.
  2. The cardiac conduction system
    Each heartbeat starts in the heart itself and spreads along a specialised conduction pathway.
  3. The cardiac cycle
    The cardiac cycle is the sequence of filling and emptying in one heartbeat.
  4. Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate
    Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps each minute, the product of stroke volume and heart rate.
  5. Regulation of heart rate during exercise
    Heart rate is adjusted to demand by the cardiac control centre in the medulla, using information from receptors and acting through the autonomic nervous system and hormones.
  6. Heart rate responses to exercise
    Heart rate tells a story through every session: an anticipatory rise, a rapid climb, a steady state or a continued rise, and recovery.
  7. Blood vessels and blood pressure
    Arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules and veins each have a structure suited to their job.
  8. The vascular shunt and venous return
    In exercise, blood is redistributed from organs to working muscles, and the return of blood to the heart is boosted.
  9. Blood composition and oxygen transport
    Blood carries oxygen, carbon dioxide, fuel, heat and hormones.
  10. The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve
    The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve shows how saturated haemoglobin is at each partial pressure of oxygen.
  11. Structure of the respiratory system
    The respiratory system moves air to a huge, thin gas-exchange surface.
  12. Mechanics of breathing
    Breathing works by changing the volume of the chest cavity, so that pressure differences move air in and out.
  13. Lung volumes and capacities
    Lung volumes describe how much air moves in each breath and how much stays in the lungs.
  14. Gas exchange at the lungs and muscles
    Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion down partial pressure gradients, at the alveoli and at the muscles.
  15. Control of breathing during exercise
    Breathing is adjusted automatically to match the body's needs.
  16. Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max)
    VO2max is the highest rate at which a person can take in, transport and use oxygen, the classic measure of aerobic capacity.
  17. Cardiovascular drift
    In prolonged exercise at a steady workload, especially in the heat, heart rate creeps upwards while stroke volume falls.
  18. Cardiorespiratory adaptations to endurance training
    Months of endurance training remodel the heart, blood, vessels, lungs and muscles.

The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise: questions students ask

Why is the wall of the left ventricle thicker than that of the right?

It must pump blood at higher pressure all round the systemic circulation, while the right ventricle pumps only to the nearby lungs.

What is the pacemaker of the heart?

The sinoatrial (SA) node in the wall of the right atrium.

What causes the "lub" heart sound?

Closure of the atrioventricular valves at the start of ventricular systole.

What is the equation for cardiac output?

Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.
