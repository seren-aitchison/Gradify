Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Whose definition describes skill as achieving pre-determined results with maximum certainty?

Barbara Knapp (1963). From “The nature of skill” →

In plain English: Skill is a learned ability to produce a chosen result consistently and efficiently. Key idea: Skill is a learned, goal-directed ability to produce pre-determined results consistently and efficiently, and most sports skills are perceptual-motor skills that combine reading the situation, deciding and moving.

What is negative transfer?

When learning one skill hinders the learning or performance of another. From “Transfer of skill” →

In plain English: Learning one skill can help, hinder or not affect the learning of another. Key idea: Transfer can be positive, negative, zero or bilateral; it depends on how similar the elements of skills are, and coaches maximise positive transfer by making practice realistic, ensuring skills are well learned and highlighting similarities and differences.

Name the four types of guidance.

Visual, verbal, manual and mechanical. From “Guidance” →

In plain English: Guidance is information given to help a learner perform a skill. Key idea: Guidance can be visual, verbal, manual or mechanical; visual suits beginners, verbal suits experienced performers, and manual and mechanical guidance support safety but can create dependence, so guidance should match the learner, skill and stage.

How many items can short-term memory hold?

About 7 ± 2 items. From “Memory in skill learning” →

In plain English: Memory allows performers to store and recall information and motor programmes. Key idea: The multi-store model has a sensory store, a limited short-term working memory and a large long-term memory; coaches aid retention through rehearsal, chunking, meaningful and simple information, and reinforcement.

In which style does the coach make all the decisions?

The command style. From “Coaching styles” →

In plain English: Coaches can use different styles depending on the learner, the skill and the situation. Key idea: Mosston's spectrum runs from command to problem-solving styles as decisions move from coach to learner, and autocratic or democratic leadership suits different groups, so coaches choose and blend styles to suit the learner, skill and situation.

  1. Which word best describes a skill? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Learned. Applying the standard result: Skills are learned through practice.

  2. Which skill is the most externally paced? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Receiving a serve in badminton. The receiver must react to the timing set by the server.

  3. Which of these is a gross motor ability? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Stamina. Stamina is a movement-related ability linked to fitness.

  4. A netball player reads a defender's position and passes into space. What type of skill is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Perceptual-motor. It combines perception, decision and movement.

  5. Why is a swimming stroke classed as low organisation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is easily broken down into arm and leg actions. Low organisation means the parts can be separated easily.

  6. What is the main difference between a skill and an ability? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Skills are learned; abilities are largely innate. Skills are learned on top of innate abilities.

  7. Which feature best shows that a gymnast is highly skilled? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She performs the routine fluently and consistently. Consistent, fluent and efficient performance indicates skill.

  8. A coach teaches a rugby pass in a game with defenders. Which classification best explains this choice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The pass is an open skill. Open skills need practice in changing environments.

  9. Why might a talent programme reassess players each year? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Maturation changes players' abilities over time. Players mature at different rates, so early assessments can mislead.

Key takeaways

  • Skill is a learned, goal-directed ability to produce pre-determined results consistently and efficiently, and most sports skills are perceptual-motor skills that combine reading the situation, deciding and moving.
  • Skills are placed on continua, including open–closed, gross–fine, discrete–serial–continuous, self-paced–externally paced, simple–complex and low–high organisation, and the placement guides how they should be practised.
  • Abilities are innate, stable traits, either psychomotor or gross motor, that underpin skills; they are specific rather than general and are used in talent identification, which must take account of maturation and relative age.
  • Practice can be massed or distributed, fixed or variable, and whole, part, progressive part or whole–part–whole; the choice depends on the skill's classification and the learner, and random practice tends to improve long-term retention more than blocked practice.
  • Transfer can be positive, negative, zero or bilateral; it depends on how similar the elements of skills are, and coaches maximise positive transfer by making practice realistic, ensuring skills are well learned and highlighting similarities and differences.
  • Fitts and Posner's stages are cognitive (understanding, many errors), associative (practising, fewer errors) and autonomous (automatic, consistent), and guidance and feedback should change to suit each stage.

Common mistakes in Skill Acquisition and Coaching

  • Using skill and ability as if they mean the same. Abilities are largely innate traits; skills are learned through practice and depend on those abilities.
  • Classifying a skill without a reason. A placement on a continuum must be justified by a feature of the skill, such as the stability of the environment.
  • Assuming a person with a high general sporting ability will be good at every sport. Abilities are specific, so a person may have the abilities for one sport but not another.
  • Using massed practice for beginners learning a complex skill. Distributed practice allows rest, feedback and mental rehearsal, which helps beginners.
  • Assuming that skills which look similar always transfer positively. If similar-looking skills need different responses, such as squash and tennis strokes, negative transfer can occur.
  • Thinking autonomous performers no longer need practice. Without practice, skills can decline, so performers may drift back to the associative stage.

Lessons in this school

  1. The nature of skill
    Skill is a learned ability to produce a chosen result consistently and efficiently.
  2. Classifying skills
    Skills can be classified on continua according to their features.
  3. Abilities
    Abilities are mostly innate traits that underpin the learning of skills.
  4. Types of practice
    How practice is organised affects how quickly and how well skills are learned.
  5. Transfer of skill
    Learning one skill can help, hinder or not affect the learning of another.
  6. Stages of learning
    Learners progress through recognisable stages as they acquire a skill.
  7. Learning curves and plateaus
    Learning curves show how performance changes with practice.
  8. Theories of learning
    Different theories explain how people learn movement skills.
  9. Guidance
    Guidance is information given to help a learner perform a skill.
  10. Feedback
    Feedback is information about a performance that helps the learner correct errors and stay motivated.
  11. Information-processing models
    Information-processing models describe how performers take in information, make decisions and produce movement.
  12. Reaction time and Hick's law
    Reaction time is the time from the onset of a stimulus to the start of a response.
  13. Memory in skill learning
    Memory allows performers to store and recall information and motor programmes.
  14. Schema theory
    Schema theory explains how performers adapt learned movements to new situations.
  15. Ecological and constraints-led approaches
    Ecological approaches see skill as emerging from the interaction between the performer, the task and the environment.
  16. The role of the coach
    Coaches have many roles beyond teaching technique.
  17. Coaching styles
    Coaches can use different styles depending on the learner, the skill and the situation.
  18. Planning a coaching session
    A well-planned session is safe, progressive and matched to participants' needs.
  19. Long-term athlete development
    Long-term athlete development models aim to match training to children's growth and maturation.
  20. Safeguarding and ethics in coaching
    Coaches have a duty to keep children and adults at risk safe and to behave ethically.
