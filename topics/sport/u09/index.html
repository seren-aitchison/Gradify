Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the overjustification effect?

Reduced interest in an enjoyable activity after an expected external reward is given for it. From “Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation” →

In plain English: Motivation is what energises and directs behaviour. Key idea: Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself; extrinsic from separate outcomes; expected tangible rewards can undermine interest in enjoyable tasks.

What kinds of goals improve performance most, according to Locke and Latham?

Specific and difficult goals. From “Goal-setting theory” →

In plain English: Goal-setting theory explains how specific, challenging goals improve performance. Key idea: Specific, difficult goals improve performance through attention, effort, persistence and strategy; commitment and feedback strengthen effects; goals can backfire if poorly designed.

What is cognitive reappraisal?

Reinterpreting a situation to change its emotional impact. From “Emotion regulation” →

In plain English: Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them. Key idea: Emotion regulation can occur at several points; reappraisal generally has better outcomes than suppression, but flexibility and context matter.

What is resilience?

Positive adaptation despite significant adversity. From “Resilience” →

In plain English: Resilience is the ability to adapt well in the face of adversity. Key idea: Resilience is common and arises from ordinary protective factors; it is both outcome and process; critics warn against using it to ignore harmful conditions.

What conditions make flow most likely?

High, well-matched challenge and skill, with clear goals and immediate feedback. From “Flow” →

In plain English: Flow is a state of deep absorption in a challenging activity. Key idea: Flow is deep absorption when high challenge matches high skill with clear goals and feedback; it supports engagement and wellbeing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Reading a book because you enjoy the story shows… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intrinsic motivation. Enjoyment of the activity itself is intrinsic.

  2. Maintaining a stable body temperature is an example of… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Homeostasis. Keeping internal conditions stable is homeostasis.

  3. Eating dessert after a full meal because it looks delicious is best explained by… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Incentive theory. The attractive dessert is an external incentive that pulls behaviour without hunger.

  4. Children promised an award for drawing later draw less in free time. This is… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The overjustification effect. Expecting an award reduced the children's later free-choice interest in drawing.

  5. A student performs best in an exam when moderately nervous, but freezes when very anxious. This fits… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Yerkes–Dodson law. Moderate arousal is optimal for performance, while very high arousal impairs it.

  6. A student values a career in medicine but believes they can't pass chemistry, so they give up. Expectancy-value theory says… (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Low expectancy reduces motivation. If either factor is low, motivation falls.

  7. Why might the same reward have different effects? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It can feel controlling or informative. Framing determines whether a reward undermines or supports motivation.

  8. Why do horror films challenge drive-reduction theory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    People seek increased tension. Seeking fear contradicts tension reduction.

  9. How does the wanting–liking distinction help explain addiction? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Craving outlasts pleasure. Wanting can grow while liking declines, so cravings persist without enjoyment.

Key takeaways

  • Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself; extrinsic from separate outcomes; expected tangible rewards can undermine interest in enjoyable tasks.
  • Drive theories explain motivation as reducing tension from unmet needs; arousal theory adds that people seek optimal stimulation; neither explains all motivation.
  • Incentive theories explain motivation by the pull of rewards; expectancy-value theory multiplies expected success by value; dopamine underlies wanting more than liking.
  • SDT proposes autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic needs; autonomous motivation supports persistence and wellbeing; autonomy-supportive environments help.
  • Specific, difficult goals improve performance through attention, effort, persistence and strategy; commitment and feedback strengthen effects; goals can backfire if poorly designed.
  • Achievement motivation is shaped by classroom goal structures, cultural beliefs and stereotypes; brief interventions can help in some contexts but effects vary.

Common mistakes in Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

  • Assuming rewards always increase motivation. For enjoyable tasks, expected tangible rewards can reduce interest.
  • Assuming all behaviour reduces tension. People often seek stimulation, novelty and challenge.
  • Thinking dopamine is the brain's "pleasure chemical". It is more closely linked to wanting and motivation.
  • Equating autonomy with independence. In SDT, autonomy means acting from self-endorsed values, which can include following others' guidance.
  • Assuming harder goals always help. Goals that are too difficult, narrow or high-stakes can reduce performance or encourage cheating.
  • Treating achievement motivation as only an individual trait. Classrooms and culture shape it strongly.

Lessons in this school

  1. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation
    Motivation is what energises and directs behaviour.
  2. Drive theories
    Drive theories explain motivation as a push to reduce internal tension caused by unmet needs.
  3. Incentive theories
    Incentive theories explain motivation by the pull of external goals and rewards.
  4. Self-determination theory
    Self-determination theory explains how social environments support or undermine motivation and wellbeing through three basic psychological needs.
  5. Goal-setting theory
    Goal-setting theory explains how specific, challenging goals improve performance.
  6. Achievement motivation in context
    Achievement motivation is shaped by context: classrooms, cultures, stereotypes and expectations.
  7. Theories of emotion: James–Lange and Cannon–Bard
    Early theories of emotion asked whether bodily changes cause emotional feelings or occur alongside them.
  8. The two-factor theory: Schachter–Singer
    Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory proposes that emotion depends on physiological arousal plus a cognitive label.
  9. Emotion regulation
    Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them.
  10. Stress
    Stress arises when demands are perceived to exceed resources.
  11. The physiology of stress
    Stress triggers bodily responses that prepare for action.
  12. Coping
    Coping is how people manage stressful demands.
  13. Resilience
    Resilience is the ability to adapt well in the face of adversity.
  14. Subjective wellbeing
    Subjective wellbeing is how people evaluate and experience their own lives.
  15. Happiness
    What makes people happy?
  16. Positive psychology in practice
    Positive psychology studies what helps people thrive and develops interventions to increase wellbeing.
  17. Flow
    Flow is a state of deep absorption in a challenging activity.
  18. Gratitude
    Gratitude is noticing and appreciating the good things in life, including what others do for us.
  19. Mindfulness research
    Mindfulness means paying attention to present-moment experience without judgement.
  20. Flourishing
    Flourishing means living well: feeling good and functioning well.

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing: questions students ask

What is a drive?

An unpleasant state of tension caused by an unmet need.

How do incentive theories differ from drive theories?

Incentives pull behaviour from outside; drives push from internal tension.

What are SDT's three basic needs?

Autonomy, competence and relatedness.

What is a mastery-oriented classroom?

One emphasising effort, improvement and understanding rather than comparison.
