Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does SAID stand for?

Specific adaptation to imposed demands. From “Specificity” →

In plain English: The principle of specificity states that training adaptations match the type of training performed. Key idea: Specificity means training must match the energy systems, muscles, fibre types, movements, speeds and environment of the sport and position, so that adaptations transfer to performance.

When does adaptation to training actually occur?

During recovery after training. From “Individual differences, variation and recovery” →

In plain English: People respond differently to the same training, and programmes need variety and rest to work well. Key idea: Training must be individualised because genetics, age, sex, history and lifestyle change the response, varied to avoid boredom, injury and plateaus, and balanced with recovery, when adaptation actually occurs.

Name three variables that can be changed in interval training.

Work duration and intensity, relief duration and type, and the number of repetitions or sets. From “Interval training and HIIT” →

In plain English: Interval training alternates periods of work with periods of rest or low-intensity recovery. Key idea: Interval training alternates work and relief, and the duration, intensity, ratio, repetitions and sets can be changed to target the ATP–PC, glycolytic or aerobic systems; HIIT is a time-efficient but demanding form of this.

Which type of stretching is best in a warm-up?

Dynamic stretching. From “Flexibility training” →

In plain English: Flexibility is the range of movement possible at a joint. Key idea: Flexibility is the range of movement at a joint, developed by static, dynamic, ballistic and PNF stretching; dynamic stretching suits warm-ups, while static and PNF stretching are best after exercise or in dedicated sessions.

How is session RPE load calculated?

RPE (0–10) multiplied by session duration in minutes. From “Monitoring training load” →

In plain English: Monitoring training load helps coaches check that athletes are training enough to improve but not so much that they become injured or ill. Key idea: External load is the work done and internal load is the athlete's response; session RPE (RPE × minutes) is a simple internal load measure, and the acute:chronic ratio flags spikes, though its link to injury is debated.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Training Principles and Programme Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which principle states that training should match the demands of the sport? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Specificity. Specificity links adaptations to the type of training performed.

  2. Which FITT component describes how hard training is? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intensity. Intensity refers to the effort or load of the training.

  3. Which phrase summarises the principle of reversibility? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Use it or lose it. Applying the standard result: Adaptations reverse when training stops.

  4. Which session is most specific for a 100 m sprinter? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    6 × 30 m maximal sprints with full recovery. Short maximal sprints with full recovery stress the ATP–PC system.

  5. A cyclist moves from two to three rides a week. Which FITT component has changed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Frequency. Frequency is how often training takes place.

  6. Which fitness adaptation is usually lost most quickly when training stops? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Aerobic adaptations such as VO2max. Aerobic changes such as plasma volume reverse within weeks.

  7. A swimmer's coach replaces half the pool sessions with running. What is the main concern? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Running adaptations transfer poorly to swimming. Movement patterns and muscles differ, so transfer to swimming is limited.

  8. A beginner increases running time, speed and frequency in the same week and develops shin pain. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reduce load and progress one variable at a time. Rapid multiple increases overload tissues; gradual single changes are safer.

  9. A runner with a stress fracture cannot run. Which plan best limits reversibility? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Pool running and cycling as advised by a physiotherapist. Cross-training maintains aerobic fitness without loading the injury.

Key takeaways

  • Specificity means training must match the energy systems, muscles, fibre types, movements, speeds and environment of the sport and position, so that adaptations transfer to performance.
  • Progressive overload means gradually increasing training demands beyond normal using frequency, intensity, time and type, changing one variable at a time and allowing recovery to avoid injury and plateaus.
  • Reversibility means fitness is lost when training stops or drops too far, with aerobic adaptations lost faster than strength, but keeping intensity high, cross-training and active rest limit the losses.
  • Training must be individualised because genetics, age, sex, history and lifestyle change the response, varied to avoid boredom, injury and plateaus, and balanced with recovery, when adaptation actually occurs.
  • The general adaptation syndrome has alarm, resistance and exhaustion stages, and the supercompensation model shows fitness dipping after training then rising above baseline, so the next session should arrive near that peak.
  • Continuous training is steady, moderate exercise for 20 minutes or more that develops aerobic endurance, with intensity set using heart rate zones such as the Karvonen formula; it is simple and safe but can be boring and lacks specificity for intermittent sports.

Common mistakes in Training Principles and Programme Design

  • Thinking any hard training improves all sports equally. Adaptations are specific, so a programme must reflect the demands of the particular sport and position.
  • Increasing frequency, intensity and time all at once. This raises injury risk and makes it hard to tell which change caused the response.
  • Assuming fitness is kept for months without training. Aerobic fitness can decline noticeably within a few weeks, so maintenance work matters.
  • Copying an elite athlete's programme exactly. Their training reflects their own history, genetics and recovery capacity, so it may overload or underload someone else.
  • Training hard every day to speed up progress. Without recovery the body moves towards the exhaustion stage rather than supercompensating.
  • Treating 220 − age as an exact maximum heart rate. It is only an estimate and may be wrong by more than 10 beats per minute for an individual.

Lessons in this school

  1. Specificity
    The principle of specificity states that training adaptations match the type of training performed.
  2. Progressive overload and FITT
    To keep improving, the body must be worked harder than it is used to, and the workload must rise gradually.
  3. Reversibility
    Fitness gains are lost when training stops or is greatly reduced.
  4. Individual differences, variation and recovery
    People respond differently to the same training, and programmes need variety and rest to work well.
  5. The general adaptation syndrome and supercompensation
    Training is a stress that the body responds to in a predictable sequence.
  6. Continuous training
    Continuous training involves steady exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes.
  7. Fartlek training
    Fartlek, Swedish for speed play, mixes changes of pace and terrain within one continuous session.
  8. Interval training and HIIT
    Interval training alternates periods of work with periods of rest or low-intensity recovery.
  9. Resistance training
    Resistance training uses weights, machines, bands or body weight to develop strength, power, muscular endurance and muscle size.
  10. Plyometrics
    Plyometric training uses jumps, bounds and throws to develop power.
  11. Flexibility training
    Flexibility is the range of movement possible at a joint.
  12. Circuit training
    Circuit training uses a series of exercises at stations, performed one after another with short rests.
  13. Speed, agility and quickness
    Speed, agility and quickness (SAQ) training develops fast movement, rapid changes of direction and quick reactions.
  14. Needs analysis
    A needs analysis is the first step in designing a training programme.
  15. Monitoring training load
    Monitoring training load helps coaches check that athletes are training enough to improve but not so much that they become injured or ill.
  16. Periodisation
    Periodisation is the planned division of training into phases so that athletes peak for important competitions.
  17. Tapering
    A taper is a planned reduction in training before a major competition to allow athletes to recover and perform at their best.
  18. Warm-up and cool-down
    A warm-up prepares the body and mind for exercise, and a cool-down helps the transition back to rest.

Training Principles and Programme Design: questions students ask

What do the letters FITT stand for?

Frequency, intensity, time and type.

What is the principle of reversibility?

Training adaptations are lost when training stops or is reduced too much.

What did the HERITAGE Family Study show?

People's VO2max responses to the same training vary widely, partly due to family (genetic) factors.

What is supercompensation?

A rise in fitness above the previous baseline after recovery from training.
