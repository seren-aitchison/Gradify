Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which enzyme breaks down ATP?

ATPase. From “ATP: the energy currency” →

In plain English: Every muscle contraction is powered by adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Key idea: ATP is the only direct energy source for contraction; stores last seconds, so the energy systems must resynthesise it continuously through coupled reactions.

What is the net ATP yield of anaerobic glycolysis from one glucose molecule?

2 ATP. From “The anaerobic glycolytic system” →

In plain English: When high-intensity exercise lasts longer than about 10 seconds, the anaerobic glycolytic system breaks down glucose without oxygen to resynthesise ATP quickly. Key idea: Anaerobic glycolysis breaks down glucose to pyruvate (PFK) and then lactate (LDH), yielding 2 ATP per glucose quickly but producing hydrogen ions that cause fatigue.

What is the crossover concept?

As intensity rises, carbohydrate supplies a greater and fat a smaller share of energy. From “Fuel use: carbohydrate and fat” →

In plain English: Muscles burn a mixture of carbohydrate and fat, and the mixture shifts with intensity, duration, training and diet. Key idea: Fat is plentiful and favoured at low intensity; carbohydrate is limited and favoured at high intensity; glycogen depletion limits prolonged hard exercise.

What blood lactate concentration defines OBLA?

4 mmol/L. From “Lactate threshold and OBLA” →

In plain English: As exercise intensity rises, blood lactate eventually climbs above resting levels and then accumulates rapidly. Key idea: The lactate threshold is where blood lactate starts to rise; OBLA is where it reaches 4 mmol/L; a higher threshold, as a percentage of VO2max, allows a faster sustainable pace.

What is one MET?

The resting energy rate: an oxygen uptake of 3.5 ml/kg/min, about 1 kcal/kg/h. From “Energy expenditure, METs and efficiency” →

In plain English: How much energy does an activity cost? Key idea: One MET is 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min, about 1 kcal/kg/h; moderate activity is 3–5.9 METs and vigorous 6 or more; economy and efficiency determine how much work a given energy produces.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Energy Systems and Exercise Metabolism

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What are the products of ATP breakdown? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    ADP, inorganic phosphate and energy. ATPase hydrolyses ATP to ADP and Pi, releasing energy; creatine and phosphate come from phosphocreatine breakdown.

  2. Where does the ATP–PC system take place? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The sarcoplasm. It is a single anaerobic reaction in the muscle cell's sarcoplasm.

  3. Where does anaerobic glycolysis take place? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The sarcoplasm. Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) of the muscle cell; the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain are in the mitochondria.

  4. During a 30-minute run, what happens to the concentration of ATP in the leg muscles? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It stays nearly constant because resynthesis matches breakdown. The energy systems resynthesise ATP as fast as it is used, so its concentration is defended while turnover rises enormously.

  5. Why is ATP described as the body's "energy currency"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    All energy-requiring processes in the cell are paid for with ATP, whichever fuel provided the energy. Like money, ATP is the common medium of exchange; its stores are small, which is why it must be constantly regenerated.

  6. Which activity relies predominantly on the ATP–PC system? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A shot put. A shot put lasts a second or two at maximal intensity; the others last minutes and rely mainly on glycolytic and aerobic systems.

  7. Which is a weakness of the ATP–PC system? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It yields only one ATP per molecule of PC. The system's yield and stores are small. It is anaerobic, fast and produces no lactate.

  8. Why is converting pyruvate to lactate useful to the muscle? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It regenerates NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep producing ATP. Glycolysis needs NAD⁺ to accept hydrogen; reducing pyruvate to lactate frees NAD⁺, letting fast ATP production continue.

  9. Cyanide blocks the electron transport chain. Why would exercise capacity collapse? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Most ATP from aerobic metabolism comes from the electron transport chain, so only anaerobic systems would remain. With the chain blocked, oxygen can't be used and the high-yield aerobic route fails; anaerobic systems can't sustain activity for long.

Key takeaways

  • ATP is the only direct energy source for contraction; stores last seconds, so the energy systems must resynthesise it continuously through coupled reactions.
  • The ATP–PC system breaks down phosphocreatine with creatine kinase to make ATP very fast, anaerobically, for up to about 10 seconds of maximal work.
  • Anaerobic glycolysis breaks down glucose to pyruvate (PFK) and then lactate (LDH), yielding 2 ATP per glucose quickly but producing hydrogen ions that cause fatigue.
  • The aerobic system (glycolysis, link reaction, Krebs cycle, electron transport chain) completely oxidises carbohydrate and fat for a very large ATP yield, but slowly.
  • All systems contribute at all times; intensity and duration decide which predominates, and in intermittent sports the aerobic system drives recovery between efforts.
  • Fat is plentiful and favoured at low intensity; carbohydrate is limited and favoured at high intensity; glycogen depletion limits prolonged hard exercise.

Common mistakes in Energy Systems and Exercise Metabolism

  • Saying food "gives muscles energy" directly. Food energy is used to resynthesise ATP; only ATP powers contraction.
  • Saying the ATP–PC system lasts 10 seconds in all exercise. It is the predominant source for maximal efforts up to about 10 s; in submaximal or intermittent exercise PC is used and restored repeatedly.
  • Saying "lactic acid causes muscle soreness the next day". Lactate is cleared within an hour or so; delayed-onset soreness is caused by muscle damage, not lactate.
  • Saying the Krebs cycle produces most of the ATP. Most ATP is made by the electron transport chain; the Krebs cycle mainly releases CO₂ and supplies hydrogen carriers.
  • Saying the systems "switch on and off" in sequence. They overlap from the start of exercise; only their relative contributions change.
  • Assuming low-intensity exercise is best for fat loss because it burns a higher percentage of fat. Total energy expenditure and energy balance matter more for body fat than the fuel mix during the session.

Lessons in this school

  1. ATP: the energy currency
    Every muscle contraction is powered by adenosine triphosphate (ATP).
  2. The ATP–PC system
    The ATP–PC (phosphocreatine) system resynthesises ATP fastest of all, powering maximal efforts of up to about 10 seconds.
  3. The anaerobic glycolytic system
    When high-intensity exercise lasts longer than about 10 seconds, the anaerobic glycolytic system breaks down glucose without oxygen to resynthesise ATP quickly.
  4. The aerobic system
    The aerobic system uses oxygen to break down carbohydrate and fat completely, giving a very large ATP yield for prolonged exercise.
  5. The energy continuum
    All three energy systems work all the time; what changes is their relative contribution.
  6. Fuel use: carbohydrate and fat
    Muscles burn a mixture of carbohydrate and fat, and the mixture shifts with intensity, duration, training and diet.
  7. Oxygen deficit
    At the start of exercise, oxygen uptake lags behind demand.
  8. Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC)
    After exercise, oxygen uptake stays above resting levels while the body recovers.
  9. Lactate threshold and OBLA
    As exercise intensity rises, blood lactate eventually climbs above resting levels and then accumulates rapidly.
  10. Restoring phosphocreatine and glycogen
    Recovery means rebuilding fuel stores.
  11. Indirect calorimetry and the respiratory exchange ratio
    Measuring oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output reveals both how much energy someone is using and which fuel they are burning.
  12. Energy expenditure, METs and efficiency
    How much energy does an activity cost?
  13. Causes of fatigue
    Fatigue is a fall in the ability to produce force or power.
  14. Metabolic adaptations to training
    Training changes the metabolic machinery of muscle.
