Key terms and formulas at a glance

Physical activity
Any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that uses energy
Moderate-intensity activity
Activity that raises heart rate and breathing, but still allows conversation
Sedentary behaviour
Waking behaviour at 1.5 METs or less while sitting, reclining or lying
All-cause mortality
Death from any cause, used to measure overall health risk
Cardiac output
The volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute: heart rate × stroke volume
VO2 max
The maximum rate at which the body can take up and use oxygen
ATP
Adenosine triphosphate, the molecule that directly supplies energy for muscle contraction
Cardiorespiratory endurance
The ability of the heart, lungs and blood vessels to supply oxygen during sustained activity
Validity
The extent to which a test measures what it is intended to measure
Active Lives survey
Sport England's national survey of physical activity among adults and children
Sarcopenia
Age-related loss of muscle mass and strength
Exercise referral scheme
A programme in which health professionals refer people to supervised physical activity

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the talk test?

At moderate intensity you can talk but not sing; at vigorous intensity you can say only a few words. From “Physical activity, exercise and fitness” →

In plain English: Physical activity, exercise and fitness are related but different ideas. Key idea: Physical activity is any movement that uses energy; exercise is structured activity for fitness; intensity can be described by METs or the talk test, and FITT describes how much activity someone does.

Can a person meet the activity guidelines and still be highly sedentary?

Yes: they can exercise for 30 minutes but sit for most of the rest of the day. From “Sedentary behaviour” →

In plain English: Sedentary behaviour is sitting or lying with low energy use while awake, and it carries health risks separate from a lack of exercise. Key idea: Sedentary behaviour is prolonged low-energy sitting or lying; it is linked to disease even in people who exercise, so guidelines advise breaking up sitting as well as being active.

What is the principle of specificity?

Training adaptations are specific to the type of exercise performed. From “Long-term adaptations to training” →

In plain English: Regular training causes lasting changes in the body that improve health and performance. Key idea: Training produces a stronger heart, lower resting heart rate, more capillaries and mitochondria, bigger stronger muscles and denser bones; gains depend on specificity, progressive overload and recovery and reverse without training.

Which test measures flexibility?

The sit-and-reach test. From “Measuring fitness” →

In plain English: Fitness tests measure components of fitness so that progress can be tracked and training planned. Key idea: Fitness tests measure specific components; results are useful only if tests are valid, reliable and conducted safely under standardised conditions.

What is pulmonary rehabilitation?

A supervised programme of exercise and education for people with lung conditions such as COPD. From “Activity with long-term conditions” →

In plain English: Physical activity helps prevent and manage many long-term conditions, but people may need tailored advice. Key idea: Physical activity is a core part of managing most long-term conditions; supervised programmes help, and people should start gradually, follow their healthcare team's advice and stop if they have warning symptoms.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Physical Activity

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an example of physical activity that isn't exercise? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cycling to work. Active travel is physical activity, though not planned exercise for fitness.

  2. How often should adults do muscle-strengthening activity? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    At least 2 days a week. The guidelines recommend strength activity on at least two days a week.

  3. Which is a sedentary behaviour? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Watching television while sitting. Seated screen time uses very little energy, 1.5 METs or less.

  4. About how many METs is a moderate-intensity activity? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    3–6 METs. Moderate activities use about three to six times resting energy.

  5. A man runs (vigorous) for 25 minutes three times a week and does nothing else. Does he meet the aerobic guideline? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Yes: 75 minutes of vigorous activity meets it. 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week meets the aerobic target; strength work is still needed.

  6. What is the difference between sedentary behaviour and physical inactivity? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Sedentary behaviour is time spent sitting; inactivity is not meeting activity guidelines. Someone can be active enough and still sit for long periods, so the two are distinct.

  7. A person can comfortably sing while walking. What does this suggest about the intensity? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is light, so walking faster would bring it into the moderate range. Being able to sing indicates light intensity; moderate activity makes breathing faster so singing is difficult.

  8. A public health team wants the biggest health gain for its budget. Which group should it target? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The least active people, since moving from none to some activity brings the largest benefits. The dose–response curve is steepest at low levels of activity.

  9. A company wants to reduce health risks for desk-based staff who mostly already exercise after work. What is the best intervention? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Encourage regular breaks from sitting, standing desks and walking meetings. Breaking up prolonged sitting addresses the separate risk of sedentary time.

Key takeaways

  • Physical activity is any movement that uses energy; exercise is structured activity for fitness; intensity can be described by METs or the talk test, and FITT describes how much activity someone does.
  • Adults need 150 minutes of moderate (or 75 of vigorous) activity a week plus strength work on 2 days; children need an average of 60 minutes a day; older adults add balance; some activity is better than none.
  • Sedentary behaviour is prolonged low-energy sitting or lying; it is linked to disease even in people who exercise, so guidelines advise breaking up sitting as well as being active.
  • Regular activity lowers the risk of early death, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, some cancers and depression, and improves sleep, mood and independence in older age.
  • Exercise rapidly raises heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output and ventilation, redistributes blood to muscles and raises body temperature, controlled by the nervous system, hormones and chemoreceptors.
  • Training produces a stronger heart, lower resting heart rate, more capillaries and mitochondria, bigger stronger muscles and denser bones; gains depend on specificity, progressive overload and recovery and reverse without training.

Common mistakes in Physical Activity

  • Thinking only sport or gym exercise counts. All movement counts towards activity guidelines, including active travel, housework and play.
  • Forgetting the strength recommendation. Many people who meet the aerobic target don't do muscle-strengthening activity, which is a separate guideline.
  • Assuming a daily workout cancels out a sedentary day. High sitting time carries risk unless offset by large amounts of activity; reducing and breaking up sitting matters too.
  • Expecting exercise alone to produce large weight loss. Activity has many health benefits even without weight loss; for weight loss, diet changes usually matter more.
  • Thinking heart rate rises only once exercise starts. Adrenaline causes an anticipatory rise before exercise begins.
  • Increasing training load too quickly. Big jumps raise the risk of injury and overtraining; progress gradually and include rest.

Lessons in this school

  1. Physical activity, exercise and fitness
    Physical activity, exercise and fitness are related but different ideas.
  2. The UK physical activity guidelines
    The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 guidelines set out how much activity people of different ages need.
  3. Sedentary behaviour
    Sedentary behaviour is sitting or lying with low energy use while awake, and it carries health risks separate from a lack of exercise.
  4. The benefits of physical activity
    Regular physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases and improves mental health and quality of life.
  5. Short-term responses to exercise
    When exercise starts, the body responds within seconds to deliver more oxygen to working muscles.
  6. Long-term adaptations to training
    Regular training causes lasting changes in the body that improve health and performance.
  7. Energy systems
    Muscles get energy from ATP, which is resupplied by three energy systems.
  8. Components of fitness
    Fitness is made up of several components, some linked mainly to health and others to skill and performance.
  9. Measuring fitness
    Fitness tests measure components of fitness so that progress can be tracked and training planned.
  10. Physical activity in children and young people
    Physical activity supports children's growth, development, health and wellbeing, but fewer than half meet the guidelines.
  11. Physical activity in older adults
    Staying active in later life helps maintain independence, prevent falls and protect mental health.
  12. Activity with long-term conditions
    Physical activity helps prevent and manage many long-term conditions, but people may need tailored advice.
  13. Barriers to physical activity
    Many people want to be more active but face barriers.
  14. Active environments and active travel
    The places where people live, work and travel strongly affect how active they are.

Physical Activity: questions students ask

How many minutes of moderate activity a week do the UK guidelines recommend for adults?

At least 150 minutes, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a combination.

What energy level defines sedentary behaviour?

1.5 METs or less while sitting, reclining or lying awake.

How does physical activity reduce type 2 diabetes risk?

It improves insulin sensitivity and helps control blood glucose and weight.

What is the anticipatory rise in heart rate?

An increase in heart rate before exercise starts, caused by adrenaline.
