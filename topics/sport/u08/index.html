Key terms and formulas at a glance

Free sugars
Sugars added to foods plus those in honey, syrups and fruit juice
Essential amino acid
An amino acid the body can't make, which must be obtained from food
Saturated fat
Fat with no carbon–carbon double bonds, mostly solid at room temperature
Fat-soluble vitamin
A vitamin stored in body fat and the liver: A, D, E and K
Haem iron
Iron from animal sources bound in haemoglobin and myoglobin, which is well absorbed
Dehydration
A deficit of body water that impairs physiological function
Eatwell Guide
The UK's visual model of a healthy, balanced diet for people aged 2 and over
Basal metabolic rate
The energy the body uses at complete rest to maintain basic functions
Nutrition declaration
The mandatory table of energy and nutrients per 100 g or 100 ml on pre-packed food
Reformulation
Changing the recipe of a food product to make it healthier, such as reducing salt or sugar
Ultra-processed food
An industrial food formulation made mostly from substances extracted from foods or synthesised, with additives
Exclusive breastfeeding
Feeding an infant only breast milk, with no other food or drink except medicines and vitamins

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the SACN recommendation for free sugars?

No more than 5% of daily energy intake, about 30 g a day for adults. From “Carbohydrates and dietary fibre” →

In plain English: Carbohydrates are the body's main energy source, and dietary fibre is the part of plant foods the small intestine can't digest. Key idea: Most energy should come from starchy, fibre-rich carbohydrates; free sugars should be under 5% of energy, and adults should aim for 30 g of fibre a day.

How much vitamin D does SACN recommend for everyone over 1 year?

10 micrograms a day. From “Vitamins” →

In plain English: Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small amounts for health. Key idea: Vitamins A, D, E and K are fat-soluble and stored; B vitamins and C are water-soluble and need regular intake; UK advice targets vitamin D and folic acid.

What BMI range is classed as healthy for most adults?

18.5 to 24.9. From “Energy balance” →

In plain English: Body weight changes when energy intake and energy expenditure are out of balance. Key idea: Weight changes when intake and expenditure differ; daily needs are about BMR × PAL; BMI and waist-to-height ratio are screening tools with known limits.

Why shouldn't babies under 12 months have honey?

It can contain bacteria that cause infant botulism. From “Nutrition in pregnancy and infancy” →

In plain English: Good nutrition before and during pregnancy and in the first years of life supports healthy growth and development. Key idea: Take folic acid and vitamin D, avoid alcohol and specific risky foods in pregnancy; breastfeed exclusively for around 6 months if possible, and introduce solids at around 6 months.

What three factors does MUST score?

BMI, unplanned weight loss and acute disease effect. From “Malnutrition” →

In plain English: Malnutrition means an imbalance of energy or nutrients, including undernutrition and overnutrition. Key idea: Malnutrition includes undernutrition and overnutrition; MUST screens adults for undernutrition risk; refeeding severely malnourished people needs careful supervision.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Sport and Exercise Nutrition

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these counts as a free sugar? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The sugar in unsweetened orange juice. Juicing releases sugars from the fruit's cells, so they count as free sugars; sugars in whole fruit and plain milk don't.

  2. Which food contains all essential amino acids in good proportions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Eggs. Animal proteins such as eggs are complete; rice and beans each lack some amino acids.

  3. Which food is a rich source of omega-3 fatty acids? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Mackerel. Oily fish such as mackerel, salmon and sardines are the best sources of long-chain omega-3s.

  4. An adult eats about 18 g of fibre a day. Which change would increase it most? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Switching white bread and rice to wholegrain versions and adding a portion of beans. Wholegrains and pulses are rich in fibre; juice, cheese, eggs and yoghurt contain little or none.

  5. What is the protein RNI for an adult weighing 60 kg? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    45 g a day. Using the RNI of 0.75 g per kg: 0.75 × 60 = 45 g of protein a day.

  6. Which swap best reduces saturated fat intake? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cooking with rapeseed oil instead of butter. Rapeseed oil is mostly unsaturated; butter, coconut oil and pastry are high in saturated fat.

  7. A cereal is labelled "no added sugar" but is sweetened with concentrated fruit juice. How should it be judged? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its sweetness still comes from free sugars, so it may not be a lower-sugar choice. Concentrated fruit juice counts as free sugars; check the "of which sugars" line on the label.

  8. A vegan eats rice and lentils regularly but worries about protein quality. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Combining cereals and pulses across the day supplies all essential amino acids. Cereals (low in lysine) and pulses (low in methionine) complement each other.

  9. A man cuts butter and cream but replaces them with sugary snacks and white bread. Why might his heart disease risk not fall? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrate doesn't lower risk; unsaturated fats should replace it. Benefit comes from replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated or monounsaturated fat.

Key takeaways

  • Most energy should come from starchy, fibre-rich carbohydrates; free sugars should be under 5% of energy, and adults should aim for 30 g of fibre a day.
  • Nine amino acids are essential; animal proteins and complementary plant proteins supply them all; adults need about 0.75 g per kg a day, which most UK adults exceed.
  • Saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol and heart disease risk; replace it with unsaturated fats, limit it to 10% of energy, and eat oily fish weekly.
  • Vitamins A, D, E and K are fat-soluble and stored; B vitamins and C are water-soluble and need regular intake; UK advice targets vitamin D and folic acid.
  • Calcium and iron are minerals of particular concern; iron absorption depends on its form and on other foods eaten with it.
  • Most adults need around 6–8 glasses of fluid a day plus water from food; urine colour, thirst and dizziness help judge hydration, and older people and young children are most at risk.

Common mistakes in Sport and Exercise Nutrition

  • Treating all sugar as the same. The sugars in whole fruit and plain milk come with fibre or other nutrients and aren't counted as free sugars; fruit juice and honey are.
  • Assuming more protein always builds more muscle. Beyond requirements, extra protein is used for energy or excreted; muscle growth depends on resistance training and overall energy intake.
  • Assuming "low fat" always means healthier. Some low-fat products replace fat with sugar or refined starch; replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrate doesn't lower heart disease risk.
  • Assuming high-dose supplements are always safe. Fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate; excess vitamin A in pregnancy is harmful, so pregnant women should avoid liver and vitamin A supplements.
  • Thinking iron deficiency only affects people who eat no meat. Low intakes are common in teenage girls and women of childbearing age because of menstrual losses; heavy periods and some gut conditions also cause deficiency.
  • Believing tea and coffee dehydrate you. In usual amounts, caffeinated drinks contribute to fluid intake; their mild diuretic effect doesn't outweigh the water they provide.

Lessons in this school

  1. Carbohydrates and dietary fibre
    Carbohydrates are the body's main energy source, and dietary fibre is the part of plant foods the small intestine can't digest.
  2. Protein
    Proteins are made of amino acids and are needed for growth, repair, enzymes, hormones and immunity.
  3. Fats
    Fats provide energy, fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids, but the type matters.
  4. Vitamins
    Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small amounts for health.
  5. Minerals
    Minerals are inorganic elements needed for structure, fluid balance, nerve function and enzyme activity.
  6. Water and hydration
    Water makes up about 60% of adult body weight and is essential for nearly every body process.
  7. The Eatwell Guide
    The Eatwell Guide is the UK government's model of a healthy, balanced diet.
  8. Energy balance
    Body weight changes when energy intake and energy expenditure are out of balance.
  9. Reading food labels
    Food labels give the information people need to compare products and make healthier choices.
  10. Salt, sugar and saturated fat
    Salt, sugar and saturated fat are the three nutrients UK diets most often contain too much of.
  11. Ultra-processed food
    Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) make up more than half of the energy in UK diets.
  12. Nutrition in pregnancy and infancy
    Good nutrition before and during pregnancy and in the first years of life supports healthy growth and development.
  13. Nutrition in childhood and adolescence
    Children and teenagers need energy and nutrients for growth and development, but many UK children eat too much sugar and too little fibre.
  14. Nutrition in older age
    Older adults need less energy but as many or more nutrients, and are at risk of both undernutrition and dehydration.
  15. Vegetarian and vegan diets
    Plant-based diets are increasingly common in the UK.
  16. Malnutrition
    Malnutrition means an imbalance of energy or nutrients, including undernutrition and overnutrition.
  17. Diet and chronic disease
    Diet is one of the leading modifiable causes of chronic disease.
  18. Food insecurity
    Food insecurity is a lack of reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
  19. Eating disorders
    Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions affecting eating, weight and body image.
