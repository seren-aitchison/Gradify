Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which plane and axis does a forward somersault use?

The sagittal plane, about the frontal (medio-lateral) axis. From “Anatomical language: planes, axes and positions” →

In plain English: Anatomy has a precise shared language so that a movement can be described the same way by any coach, physiotherapist or scientist. Key idea: Each plane pairs with the axis that pierces it at 90°: sagittal with frontal, frontal with sagittal, transverse with longitudinal.

Which two bones form the shoulder girdle?

The clavicle and the scapula. From “The axial and appendicular skeleton” →

In plain English: The 206 bones of the adult skeleton divide into the axial skeleton, the central core that protects and supports, and the appendicular skeleton, the limbs and girdles that move. Key idea: The axial skeleton protects and supports; the appendicular skeleton provides the levers for movement; the girdles trade mobility (shoulder) for stability (pelvis).

What is circumduction?

A cone-shaped movement of a limb combining flexion, abduction, extension and adduction. From “Movement terminology” →

In plain English: Each joint action has a precise name. Key idea: Flexion decreases and extension increases a joint angle; abduction moves away from and adduction towards the midline; rotation turns about a long axis.

What is the role of calcium in muscle contraction?

It binds to troponin, which moves tropomyosin off the myosin-binding sites on actin so cross-bridges can form. From “The sliding filament theory” →

In plain English: Muscles shorten because actin and myosin filaments slide past each other, driven by repeated cycles of myosin cross-bridges. Key idea: Calcium switches contraction on by moving troponin and tropomyosin; ATP powers the cycle by detaching and re-cocking myosin heads.

Which muscles make up the hamstrings?

Biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus. From “The major muscles used in sport” →

In plain English: Analysing movement needs a working knowledge of the major muscles: where they are, what they do and when sport uses them. Key idea: Many muscles cross two joints: rectus femoris (hip and knee), hamstrings (hip and knee) and gastrocnemius (knee and ankle), so their action depends on the position of both joints.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In the anatomical position, which way do the palms face? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Forwards. The anatomical position has the palms facing forwards (anteriorly); this places the radius and ulna side by side, which the directional terms for the forearm assume.

  2. In an adult, where is most red bone marrow found? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    In flat and irregular bones such as the sternum, ribs and pelvis. In adults, red marrow persists mainly in flat and irregular bones and the ends of long bones; the shafts of long bones fill with fatty yellow marrow.

  3. Which bone is a sesamoid bone? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Patella. The patella develops within the quadriceps tendon, the defining feature of a sesamoid bone; the scapula is flat, the talus short and the radius long.

  4. A goalkeeper dives sideways to save a shot, moving the whole body away from the midline. In which plane is the movement? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Frontal. Sideways movement that divides front from back happens in the frontal plane, rotating about the sagittal axis. "Longitudinal" is an axis, not a plane.

  5. A discus thrower spins before release. Which axis is the body rotating about? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The longitudinal axis. Spinning on the spot is rotation about the vertical, head-to-toe longitudinal axis, in the transverse plane.

  6. Which function of the skeleton most directly helps an endurance runner deliver oxygen to working muscle? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Production of red blood cells. Red blood cells carry oxygen on haemoglobin; more red cells (as at altitude) raise oxygen-carrying capacity.

  7. Why are shoulder dislocations far more common in sport than hip dislocations? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The shoulder girdle's loose attachment and shallow socket favour mobility over stability. The pectoral girdle attaches to the trunk only at the sternoclavicular joint and the glenoid is shallow, so the joint is mobile but easily displaced; the deep acetabulum and fused pelvic girdle make the hip very stable.

  8. A 70-year-old wants exercise to protect against osteoporotic fracture. Which advice best reflects the evidence? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Progressive resistance and, where safe, impact training, combined with balance work. Resistance and impact loading preserve bone, and balance training reduces falls, the immediate cause of most fractures. Swimming is good for fitness but barely loads bone; rest causes bone loss. Educational only: older adults should get individual advice.

  9. Which structure most directly prevents the tibia sliding forwards on the femur during a sudden stop? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The anterior cruciate ligament. Ligaments resist unwanted movement between bones; the ACL specifically resists anterior translation of the tibia, which is why sudden deceleration and cutting can rupture it.

Key takeaways

  • Each plane pairs with the axis that pierces it at 90°: sagittal with frontal, frontal with sagittal, transverse with longitudinal.
  • Mechanical functions (support, protection, movement, attachment) and physiological functions (blood cell production, mineral and fat storage) both contribute to performance.
  • Compact bone resists bending in the shaft; spongy bone, aligned to stress, keeps the ends strong but light; cartilage and periosteum protect and connect.
  • The axial skeleton protects and supports; the appendicular skeleton provides the levers for movement; the girdles trade mobility (shoulder) for stability (pelvis).
  • Bone responds to strain: high, dynamic, varied loading increases density, and unloading or low energy availability causes loss.
  • Fibrous joints are immovable, cartilaginous slightly movable and synovial freely movable; greater mobility means less stability.

Common mistakes in Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System

  • Matching a plane with the axis of the same name. The sagittal plane's movements turn about the frontal axis, and frontal-plane movements about the sagittal axis. Note that OCR and some texts call the frontal axis "transverse"; check your specification's terms.
  • Writing "the skeleton produces blood". It produces blood cells, in red marrow; plasma is mostly water and is not made in bone.
  • Calling the patella a short bone. It is a sesamoid bone, formed within the quadriceps tendon.
  • Placing the fibula on the medial side or treating it as the main weight-bearing bone. The tibia is medial and bears most of the load; the fibula is lateral and mainly anchors muscles.
  • Assuming all exercise builds bone. Swimming and cycling barely load the skeleton, so athletes in them may need impact or resistance work for bone health.
  • Calling the intervertebral joints immovable. They are cartilaginous and slightly movable; the facet joints between vertebral arches are in fact small synovial joints.

Lessons in this school

  1. Anatomical language: planes, axes and positions
    Anatomy has a precise shared language so that a movement can be described the same way by any coach, physiotherapist or scientist.
  2. Functions of the skeleton
    The skeleton is far more than a frame.
  3. Bone structure and types of bone
    Bones are living organs built from compact and spongy tissue, cartilage, marrow and a covering membrane.
  4. The axial and appendicular skeleton
    The 206 bones of the adult skeleton divide into the axial skeleton, the central core that protects and supports, and the appendicular skeleton, the limbs and girdles that move.
  5. Bone remodelling and the effects of exercise on bone
    Bone is constantly broken down and rebuilt, and it adapts to the loads placed on it.
  6. Classification of joints
    A joint (articulation) is where two or more bones meet.
  7. Synovial joints
    Synovial joints are the freely movable joints of sport.
  8. Movement terminology
    Each joint action has a precise name.
  9. Joint analysis of sporting actions
    Exam questions and real coaching both ask you to break a movement into joints, actions, planes and muscles.
  10. Ligaments, tendons and cartilage
    Connective tissues hold the musculoskeletal system together and transmit its forces.
  11. Skeletal muscle structure
    Skeletal muscle is organised in nested bundles, from the whole muscle down to the protein filaments that generate force.
  12. The sliding filament theory
    Muscles shorten because actin and myosin filaments slide past each other, driven by repeated cycles of myosin cross-bridges.
  13. Muscle fibre types
    Skeletal muscle contains fibres that differ in speed, force and fatigue resistance.
  14. Agonists, antagonists, fixators and synergists
    Muscles rarely work alone.
  15. Types of muscle contraction
    Muscles can produce force while shortening, lengthening or staying the same length.
  16. The major muscles used in sport
    Analysing movement needs a working knowledge of the major muscles: where they are, what they do and when sport uses them.
  17. Motor units and the all-or-none law
    Muscles are controlled by motor units, each a motor neurone and the fibres it supplies.
  18. Gradation of force: recruitment, summation and tetanus
    A muscle can produce anything from a gentle touch to maximal force.
  19. Proprioceptors and the stretch reflex
    Proprioceptors tell the nervous system the position, length and tension of muscles and joints.

Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System: questions students ask

Which bones protect the heart and lungs?

The ribs and sternum (the thoracic cage), with the thoracic vertebrae behind.

What covers the ends of long bones where they form a joint?

Articular (hyaline) cartilage.

How many vertebrae are in each region of the spine?

7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral and about 4 fused coccygeal.

What does Wolff's law state?

Bone adapts to the loads placed upon it: loaded bone strengthens and unloaded bone weakens.
