Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What part of the brain controls body temperature?

The hypothalamus. From “Thermoregulation during exercise” →

In plain English: Exercising muscles produce large amounts of heat, which the body must lose to stay safe. Key idea: Exercise releases most energy as heat; the hypothalamus controls core temperature near 37 °C using vasodilation and sweating, and heat is transferred by conduction, convection, radiation and evaporation, with evaporation the main route during exercise but less effective in high humidity.

Why is there less oxygen available at altitude?

Barometric pressure is lower, so the partial pressure of oxygen is lower. From “Altitude and hypoxia” →

In plain English: At altitude, the air contains less oxygen pressure, challenging aerobic performance. Key idea: Altitude lowers barometric pressure and therefore the partial pressure of oxygen, causing hypoxia; the body responds with increased breathing and heart rate, endurance performance falls while explosive events may benefit, and altitude illness is prevented by gradual ascent and treated by descent and medical help.

What is the main stimulus for hypertrophy?

Mechanical tension. From “Muscle hypertrophy” →

In plain English: Over longer periods, resistance training increases muscle size. Key idea: Hypertrophy increases muscle fibre cross-sectional area as protein synthesis exceeds breakdown, driven mainly by mechanical tension and supported by satellite cells; volume, effort near failure, frequency and progressive overload drive growth, while adequate protein, energy and sleep support it.

What is sarcopenia?

Age-related loss of muscle mass and function. From “Exercise and ageing” →

In plain English: Ageing brings changes in muscle, bone, the heart and balance, but regular exercise slows many of them. Key idea: Ageing reduces muscle mass and power, VO₂max, bone density and balance, but regular aerobic, resistance and balance exercise slows these declines; the UK CMOs advise older adults to do 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly and strength and balance activities on two days a week.

What is energy availability?

Dietary energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, relative to fat-free mass. From “Relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S)” →

In plain English: Athletes who do not eat enough to fuel training can develop serious health and performance problems. Key idea: Low energy availability, when intake does not cover exercise and basic functions, causes RED-S, affecting reproductive, bone, immune, metabolic and psychological health and performance in both sexes; warning signs should prompt medical assessment, and management involves a multidisciplinary team increasing energy intake and adjusting training.

  1. What is the main way heat is lost during exercise in hot conditions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Evaporation of sweat. Applying the standard result: Evaporation removes most heat.

  2. Which sign suggests exertional heat stroke rather than heat exhaustion? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Confusion or collapse. Nervous system signs indicate heat stroke.

  3. Which response produces heat in the cold? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Shivering. Applying the standard result: Muscle activity produces heat.

  4. A cyclist feels a cooling breeze. Which mechanism is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Convection. Applying the standard result: Moving air carries heat away.

  5. Which adaptation results from heat acclimatisation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Increased plasma volume. Applying the standard result: Acclimatisation expands plasma.

  6. What should be done first for suspected exertional heat stroke? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Call 999 and start rapid whole-body cooling. Applying the standard result: It is a medical emergency.

  7. Why can conduction, convection and radiation add heat on a very hot day? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The surroundings are hotter than the skin. Applying the standard result: Heat moves from hot to cool.

  8. Why does heart rate rise during prolonged exercise in the heat? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Stroke volume falls as blood volume drops and more blood goes to the skin. Applying the standard result: Cardiovascular drift raises heart rate.

  9. What does the RNLI advise for someone experiencing cold water shock? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Float on your back to recover your breathing. Applying the standard result: Floating helps control gasping.

Key takeaways

  • Exercise releases most energy as heat; the hypothalamus controls core temperature near 37 °C using vasodilation and sweating, and heat is transferred by conduction, convection, radiation and evaporation, with evaporation the main route during exercise but less effective in high humidity.
  • Heat reduces endurance performance through cardiovascular drift and rising core temperature; heat exhaustion needs rest, cooling and fluids, while exertional heat stroke is an emergency requiring a 999 call and rapid cooling; acclimatisation over 1–2 weeks raises plasma volume and sweating, and pre-cooling, hydration and scheduling reduce risk.
  • In the cold, vasoconstriction and shivering conserve and produce heat, while wind and water increase heat loss; cold reduces muscle power and dexterity and can trigger bronchoconstriction, and hypothermia, frostbite and cold water shock are prevented by layers, staying dry and appropriate planning.
  • Altitude lowers barometric pressure and therefore the partial pressure of oxygen, causing hypoxia; the body responds with increased breathing and heart rate, endurance performance falls while explosive events may benefit, and altitude illness is prevented by gradual ascent and treated by descent and medical help.
  • Altitude increases EPO, red blood cells and haemoglobin mass over several weeks; models include live high–train high, live high–train low and simulated altitude, and evidence suggests average benefits from live high–train low but large individual variation, placebo concerns and risks such as poor sleep and iron deficiency.
  • Early strength gains come mainly from neural adaptations, such as increased motor unit recruitment and firing rate, better synchronisation, reduced antagonist co-activation and reduced inhibition, with hypertrophy contributing more later; these adaptations are specific to the movements trained.

Common mistakes in Exercise Physiology: Environment, Adaptation and Populations

  • Thinking sweating cools the body by itself. Cooling comes from the evaporation of sweat, not from sweat dripping off.
  • Assuming a collapsed athlete in the heat is just tired. Confusion or collapse may mean heat stroke, which needs emergency treatment and rapid cooling.
  • Rubbing frostbitten skin to warm it up. Rubbing can damage tissue further; seek medical help.
  • Saying there is less oxygen percentage at altitude. The percentage is the same; the partial pressure is lower.
  • Expecting every athlete to improve after altitude training. Responses vary widely between individuals.
  • Assuming early strength gains mean muscles have grown. The nervous system drives most early improvement.

Lessons in this school

  1. Thermoregulation during exercise
    Exercising muscles produce large amounts of heat, which the body must lose to stay safe.
  2. Exercise in the heat and acclimatisation
    Hot conditions reduce performance and increase the risk of heat illness.
  3. Exercise in the cold
    Cold environments challenge the body to conserve heat.
  4. Altitude and hypoxia
    At altitude, the air contains less oxygen pressure, challenging aerobic performance.
  5. Altitude training
    Many endurance athletes train at altitude hoping to improve performance at sea level.
  6. Neuromuscular adaptations to strength training
    Early strength gains happen before muscles grow much larger.
  7. Muscle hypertrophy
    Over longer periods, resistance training increases muscle size.
  8. Overtraining and underperformance
    Training too hard without enough recovery can reduce performance and harm health.
  9. Detraining
    When training stops or is greatly reduced, fitness gains are gradually lost.
  10. Exercise and ageing
    Ageing brings changes in muscle, bone, the heart and balance, but regular exercise slows many of them.
  11. Children and young athletes
    Children are not small adults: they differ in physiology, growth and maturation.
  12. Female athletes and the menstrual cycle
    Female athletes have been under-represented in sport science research.
  13. Relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S)
    Athletes who do not eat enough to fuel training can develop serious health and performance problems.
  14. Exercise in pregnancy
    Physical activity during pregnancy benefits both parent and baby.
  15. Exercise for long-term conditions
    Exercise is an important part of managing many long-term conditions.
