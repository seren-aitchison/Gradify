Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between distance and displacement?

Distance is the total path length; displacement is the straight-line change in position in a given direction. From “Linear motion: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration” →

In plain English: Linear motion is movement in a straight or curved line where all parts of the body move the same distance in the same direction at the same time. Key idea: Distance and speed are scalars, displacement and velocity are vectors with direction, and acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time, measured in m/s².

Where does the centre of mass lie in a standing person?

Roughly in the pelvis, at about 55–57% of standing height. From “Centre of mass and stability” →

In plain English: The centre of mass is the point at which the mass of a body can be considered to be concentrated. Key idea: The centre of mass shifts with body position, and stability increases with a lower centre of mass, a wider base of support, a central line of gravity, more mass and more points of contact.

What does the area under a force–time graph represent?

Impulse. From “Impulse and momentum” →

In plain English: Momentum is the quantity of motion a body has, and impulse is how a force changes it over time. Key idea: Momentum is mass × velocity, impulse is force × time and equals the change in momentum, and athletes increase contact time to generate speed or to cushion impact forces.

What is the vertical velocity of a projectile at the peak of flight?

Zero. From “Projectile motion” →

In plain English: A projectile is any body released into the air that is acted on only by weight and air resistance. Key idea: A projectile in flight is acted on by weight and air resistance; its release velocity splits into a constant horizontal component and a vertical component changed by gravity, giving a parabola when weight dominates.

Where must a ball be struck to produce backspin?

Below its centre of mass. From “Spin and the Magnus effect” →

In plain English: Spinning balls curve in flight because of the Magnus effect. Key idea: An eccentric strike makes a ball spin, and the Magnus effect causes it to move towards the side of lower pressure: topspin dips, backspin floats and sidespin swerves, with spin also changing the bounce.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Biomechanics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these quantities is a vector? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Displacement. Displacement has both size and a stated direction.

  2. On a distance–time graph, what does the gradient represent? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Speed. Gradient is distance divided by time, which is speed.

  3. About which axis does a figure skater rotate during an upright spin? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Longitudinal. The longitudinal axis runs from head to toe.

  4. A swimmer completes 100 m in a 50 m pool in 60 s, finishing at the start wall. What is the average velocity? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0 m/s. Displacement is zero because she ends where she began, so velocity is zero.

  5. A velocity–time graph shows a straight line sloping downwards to the time axis. What is the performer doing? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Decelerating at a constant rate. A negative gradient on a velocity–time graph is a steady deceleration.

  6. A diver leaves the board with the reaction force passing behind her centre of mass. What happens? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    She rotates because the force is eccentric. A force outside the centre of mass creates a torque and rotation.

  7. A cyclist rides round a circular velodrome at a constant 15 m/s. Which statement is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She is accelerating because her direction changes. Velocity is a vector, so a change of direction is an acceleration even at constant speed.

  8. Two sprinters reach the same peak velocity, but A wins. Which velocity–time feature most likely explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A's line falls less in the final phase. Holding velocity late in the race reduces time lost to fatigue.

  9. Which movement involves rotation mainly about the frontal axis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A cartwheel. A cartwheel rotates in the frontal plane about the front-to-back axis.

Key takeaways

  • Distance and speed are scalars, displacement and velocity are vectors with direction, and acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time, measured in m/s².
  • On a distance–time graph the gradient is speed; on a velocity–time graph the gradient is acceleration and the area under the line is the distance travelled.
  • Angular motion is rotation about an axis caused by an eccentric force (torque), described by angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration, about the longitudinal, transverse or frontal axis.
  • The centre of mass shifts with body position, and stability increases with a lower centre of mass, a wider base of support, a central line of gravity, more mass and more points of contact.
  • Newton's first law describes inertia, the second law gives F = ma, and the third law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction acting on a different body.
  • Forces can start, stop, speed up, slow down, redirect or deform a body; the main external forces are weight, reaction, friction and air resistance, and only unbalanced forces cause acceleration.

Common mistakes in Biomechanics

  • Using speed and velocity as if they always mean the same thing. They are equal only when the path is a straight line in one direction; velocity depends on displacement, not distance.
  • Reading a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph as standing still. A horizontal line on a velocity–time graph means constant velocity; only a horizontal line on a distance–time graph means stationary.
  • Thinking any force makes a body spin. Only a force acting outside the centre of mass produces rotation; a force through the centre of mass gives linear motion.
  • Assuming athletes always want maximum stability. Sprint and swim starts deliberately reduce stability so the athlete can move off quickly.
  • Thinking action and reaction forces cancel out. They act on different bodies, so each can still cause acceleration of the body it acts on.
  • Confusing mass and weight. Mass is the amount of matter in kg; weight is the force of gravity on that mass in N.

Lessons in this school

  1. Linear motion: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration
    Linear motion is movement in a straight or curved line where all parts of the body move the same distance in the same direction at the same time.
  2. Distance–time and velocity–time graphs
    Graphs show how motion changes during a performance.
  3. Angular motion
    Angular motion is movement around an axis, as when a gymnast somersaults or a limb rotates at a joint.
  4. Centre of mass and stability
    The centre of mass is the point at which the mass of a body can be considered to be concentrated.
  5. Newton's laws of motion
    Newton's three laws describe how forces change motion.
  6. Types of force in sport
    Forces are pushes or pulls that can change the motion or shape of a body.
  7. Free-body diagrams
    Free-body diagrams show all the external forces acting on a body at one instant.
  8. Impulse and momentum
    Momentum is the quantity of motion a body has, and impulse is how a force changes it over time.
  9. Friction
    Friction is the force that opposes two surfaces sliding over each other.
  10. Levers
    The skeleton and muscles work together as lever systems to produce movement.
  11. Mechanical advantage of lever systems
    Different lever classes favour either force or speed and range of movement.
  12. Projectile motion
    A projectile is any body released into the air that is acted on only by weight and air resistance.
  13. Factors affecting projectile distance
    The horizontal distance a projectile travels depends on its speed, angle and height of release, together with aerodynamic factors.
  14. Fluid mechanics: drag
    Drag is the force that opposes a body moving through air or water.
  15. The Bernoulli principle and lift
    Lift is a force that acts at right angles to the airflow and can keep projectiles in the air for longer or press vehicles onto the ground.
  16. Spin and the Magnus effect
    Spinning balls curve in flight because of the Magnus effect.
  17. Moment of inertia and angular momentum
    Moment of inertia is a body's resistance to changes in rotation, and angular momentum is the quantity of rotation it has.
  18. Conservation of angular momentum
    Once a performer leaves the ground, their angular momentum stays the same unless an external torque acts.
  19. Biomechanical analysis and technology
    Coaches and scientists use qualitative and quantitative methods to analyse movement and improve technique.

Biomechanics: questions students ask

What does a horizontal line on a distance–time graph show?

The performer is stationary.

What type of force produces angular motion?

An eccentric force, applied outside the centre of mass.

What is the line of gravity?

A vertical line from the centre of mass down to the ground.

What is inertia?

The resistance of a body to a change in its state of motion, which depends on mass.
