Lessons in this school

  1. Constructing historical enquiries
    An A level historical investigation begins with an enquiry: a question worth asking, about a debate historians care about, which the available evidence can answer.
  2. Developing research questions
    The wording of a research question shapes the whole investigation.
  3. Selecting primary sources
    An investigation needs primary sources that are relevant, varied and reliable enough to support an argument.
  4. Evaluating provenance in depth
    At A level, evaluating a source means going beyond "it is biased" to ask who made it, when, why, for whom and in what circumstances, and what that means for its value to a particular question.
  5. Comparing interpretations at A level
    At A level, comparing interpretations means explaining why historians reach different conclusions, not just stating that they do.
  6. Historiographical debate
    Historiography is the history of how historians have written history.
  7. Using academic literature
    An A level investigation must engage with what historians have written, not just with primary sources.
  8. Constructing an argument
    A historical investigation succeeds when it makes a clear argument, supports it with evidence and deals with objections.
  9. Structuring an extended essay
    A 4,000-word investigation needs a structure that carries the argument from introduction to judgement.
  10. Citation and referencing
    Historians cite their sources so that readers can check the evidence and see whose ideas are being used.
  11. Avoiding anachronism
    Anachronism means placing something in the wrong time: using ideas, words or standards that did not exist then, or assuming people knew what would happen next.
  12. Evaluating historical significance
    Deciding what is historically significant is itself a historical judgement, made with criteria.
  13. Writing a sustained historical judgement
    The highest-level historical writing reaches a clear, consistent and well-supported judgement, rather than simply listing views.

Key terms

Enquiry
A historical question that drives research and argument
Open question
A question inviting argument and judgement rather than a single fact
Primary source
Evidence created at or close to the time being studied
Provenance
The origin and history of a source: who made it, when, why and for whom
Revisionism
An interpretation that challenges an established view
Historiography
The study of how history has been written and interpreted
Monograph
A specialist book on a focused topic
Thesis
The overall answer an argument puts forward
Thematic structure
Organising an essay by factors or arguments
Footnote
A note at the foot of a page giving the source of a claim
Anachronism
Placing something in the wrong period of time
Historical significance
The importance attributed to an event, person or development, judged by criteria
Sustained judgement
A clear, consistent answer maintained and developed throughout an essay
