What four features make a strong historical enquiry?

A genuine debate, a clear focus, available evidence and a demand for judgement. From “Constructing historical enquiries” →

In plain English: An A level historical investigation begins with an enquiry: a question worth asking, about a debate historians care about, which the available evidence can answer. Key idea: A strong enquiry addresses a genuine debate, has a clear focus, can be answered with available evidence and demands judgement; broad topics must be narrowed into focused questions before research begins.

Since when has Hansard recorded parliamentary debates verbatim?

Since 1909. From “Selecting primary sources” →

In plain English: An investigation needs primary sources that are relevant, varied and reliable enough to support an argument. Key idea: Historians use official records, personal sources, the press, visual sources, statistics and oral history, found in archives such as The National Archives and digitised collections such as the Old Bailey Online (1674 to 1913); a strong selection is varied and tests the argument.

What did Butterfield criticise in 1931?

Whig history, which told the past as progress towards the present. From “Historiographical debate” →

In plain English: Historiography is the history of how historians have written history. Key idea: Whig, Marxist, Annales, social, cultural and postmodern approaches ask different questions of the past; the Fischer controversy from 1961 shows how new evidence, questions and contexts drive historiographical debate.

What is a qualification in an argument?

A refinement that limits a claim or makes it more precise. From “Constructing an argument” →

In plain English: A historical investigation succeeds when it makes a clear argument, supports it with evidence and deals with objections. Key idea: A historical argument has a thesis, a line of argument, evidence, counter-arguments and qualification; on the 1832 Reform Act, the threat of disorder explains its passage while the Whigs' aims explain its content, a judgement strengthened by facing objections.

What is presentism?

Judging the past only by present-day values. From “Avoiding anachronism” →

In plain English: Anachronism means placing something in the wrong time: using ideas, words or standards that did not exist then, or assuming people knew what would happen next. Key idea: Anachronism includes wrong terms, hindsight, presentism and teleology; it distorts understanding by making past actions seem foolish or inevitable, and historians should explain past values in context before judging by the alternatives available at the time.

Quick quiz: test yourself on A Level Historical Investigation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which historian argued that history advances by asking questions of evidence? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    R. G. Collingwood. He set out the view in The Idea of History in 1946.

  2. Which of these is an open research question? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    How far was Chartism driven by economic distress?. Open questions invite argument and judgement rather than a fact.

  3. Which digitised collection contains accounts of London trials from 1674 to 1913? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Old Bailey Proceedings Online. It records nearly 200,000 trials at London's central criminal court.

  4. Why is "Why did the British Empire fall?" a weak enquiry? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is too broad to answer properly within 4,000 words. A workable enquiry needs a defined period, place and aspect.

  5. Why should a question about "success" define its terms? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Without a definition, success cannot be measured against evidence. A defined term gives the investigation a clear test.

  6. Why should an investigation include sources that challenge its argument? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Testing the argument against contrary evidence makes it stronger and more convincing. An argument is only convincing if it survives contrary evidence.

  7. Which revision best improves the enquiry "the 1832 Reform Act"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    How far was the Great Reform Act of 1832 a response to the threat of revolution?. A focused question with a debate demands an argued answer.

  8. Which is the best refinement of "Was the New Deal a success?" (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    How far did the New Deal reduce unemployment in the United States between 1933 and 1941?. A good refinement defines success and sets a period.

  9. Which judgement about a selection of only Poor Law Commissioners' reports is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is unbalanced, because every source comes from those who designed and ran the system. Balance needs sources from several perspectives.

Key takeaways

  • A strong enquiry addresses a genuine debate, has a clear focus, can be answered with available evidence and demands judgement; broad topics must be narrowed into focused questions before research begins.
  • Research questions should be open, inviting judgement; causation, consequence, change, significance and interpretation questions shape investigations differently, and questions should be refined to define terms and periods as research develops.
  • Historians use official records, personal sources, the press, visual sources, statistics and oral history, found in archives such as The National Archives and digitised collections such as the Old Bailey Online (1674 to 1913); a strong selection is varied and tests the argument.
  • Provenance analysis examines a source's nature, origin, purpose, audience, context, content and tone; the forged Zinoviev letter of 1924 is worthless as evidence of Soviet plans but valuable evidence of British fears and press power, showing that value depends on the question.
  • Interpretations of appeasement moved from Guilty Men (1940) to revisionists using released records and counter-revisionists such as Parker (1993); they differ because of context, evidence and the questions asked, and must be tested against evidence.
  • Whig, Marxist, Annales, social, cultural and postmodern approaches ask different questions of the past; the Fischer controversy from 1961 shows how new evidence, questions and contexts drive historiographical debate.

Common mistakes in A Level Historical Investigation

  • Choosing a topic rather than a question. Without a question, research becomes description.
  • Keeping the first version of a question unchanged. Research questions usually need refining as evidence is found.
  • Choosing only sources that support the argument. A strong investigation also uses sources that challenge it.
  • Dismissing a biased or forged source as useless. Even a forgery is valuable evidence for some questions.
  • Saying historians differ "because of bias" without explanation. Explain how context, evidence and questions shape each interpretation.
  • Treating historiography as a list of names. Explain what each approach asks and why it produces particular interpretations.

Lessons in this school

  1. Constructing historical enquiries
    An A level historical investigation begins with an enquiry: a question worth asking, about a debate historians care about, which the available evidence can answer.
  2. Developing research questions
    The wording of a research question shapes the whole investigation.
  3. Selecting primary sources
    An investigation needs primary sources that are relevant, varied and reliable enough to support an argument.
  4. Evaluating provenance in depth
    At A level, evaluating a source means going beyond "it is biased" to ask who made it, when, why, for whom and in what circumstances, and what that means for its value to a particular question.
  5. Comparing interpretations at A level
    At A level, comparing interpretations means explaining why historians reach different conclusions, not just stating that they do.
  6. Historiographical debate
    Historiography is the history of how historians have written history.
  7. Using academic literature
    An A level investigation must engage with what historians have written, not just with primary sources.
  8. Constructing an argument
    A historical investigation succeeds when it makes a clear argument, supports it with evidence and deals with objections.
  9. Structuring an extended essay
    A 4,000-word investigation needs a structure that carries the argument from introduction to judgement.
  10. Citation and referencing
    Historians cite their sources so that readers can check the evidence and see whose ideas are being used.
  11. Avoiding anachronism
    Anachronism means placing something in the wrong time: using ideas, words or standards that did not exist then, or assuming people knew what would happen next.
  12. Evaluating historical significance
    Deciding what is historically significant is itself a historical judgement, made with criteria.
  13. Writing a sustained historical judgement
    The highest-level historical writing reaches a clear, consistent and well-supported judgement, rather than simply listing views.

A Level Historical Investigation: questions students ask

What is the difference between an open and a closed question?

An open question invites argument; a closed question has a single factual answer.

Where are the records of central government in England kept?

The National Archives at Kew.

Why is the Zinoviev letter valuable despite being forged?

Its publication and reception reveal British fears of communism and the power of the press in 1924.

What did Guilty Men argue in 1940?

That Chamberlain and his colleagues were to blame for weakness and folly.
