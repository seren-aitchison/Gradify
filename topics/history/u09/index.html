Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What was the "mutilated victory"?

The nationalist claim that Italy had been cheated of the territory promised for joining the war. From “Post-war Italy” →

In plain English: Italy was on the winning side in 1918, yet many Italians felt cheated by the peace and frightened by unrest at home. Key idea: Disappointment with the peace (the "mutilated victory"), economic crisis and the unrest of the biennio rosso weakened Liberal Italy and frightened the propertied classes, creating openings for Fascism.

Who were the "November criminals"?

The name nationalists gave to the politicians who agreed the Armistice and Versailles. From “The Treaty of Versailles and Germany” →

In plain English: For many Germans, the new republic was born with the humiliation of Versailles. Key idea: Germans resented Versailles as a diktat; the reparations crisis led to the Ruhr occupation in 1923, and Stresemann's diplomacy from 1924 to 1929 revised the treaty peacefully but fragilely.

When was Hitler appointed chancellor?

On 30 January 1933. From “Hitler's rise to power” →

In plain English: Adolf Hitler went from a failed putsch in 1923 to chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933. Key idea: After 1923 Hitler sought power through elections; the Depression, propaganda and the SA made the Nazis the largest party, but conservatives around Hindenburg made him chancellor on 30 January 1933.

When did the Nazi book burnings take place?

On 10 May 1933. From “Propaganda and censorship in Nazi Germany” →

In plain English: Joseph Goebbels ran a Ministry of Propaganda that tried to control everything Germans read, heard and saw. Key idea: Goebbels's ministry controlled the press, radio, film and the arts, burned banned books and staged rallies; propaganda worked best when it reinforced existing beliefs and real successes.

What was the T4 programme?

The Nazi programme, begun in 1939, of murdering disabled people. From “Persecution of minorities” →

In plain English: Nazi racial ideology targeted many groups besides Jews: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, gay men and others judged a threat to the national community. Key idea: Nazi racial and eugenic ideas led to the sterilisation of around 400,000 people and the persecution of Roma and Sinti, gay men and others; from 1939 the T4 programme murdered disabled people, a step towards genocide.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Rise of Fascism and Nazism

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which port did D'Annunzio seize in September 1919? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Fiume. He held Fiume with volunteers until December 1920.

  2. Which king appointed Mussolini prime minister in 1922? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Victor Emmanuel III. Victor Emmanuel III refused martial law and invited Mussolini to form a government.

  3. Which Socialist deputy was murdered by Fascists in 1924? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Giacomo Matteotti. His murder caused the Aventine Secession.

  4. Why did the factory occupations of 1920 help the Fascists? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They frightened property owners, who backed the Fascist squads. Fear of revolution drove support for anti-socialist violence.

  5. Why did many landowners and industrialists tolerate the Fascist squads? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The squads attacked socialists and trade unions they feared. Property owners saw the squads as protection against the left.

  6. Why was the Lateran Treaty important to Mussolini? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It won him wide support among Catholics. Reconciliation with the Church broadened his support.

  7. Which judgement about post-war Italy is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Fear of revolution, more than revolution itself, gave Fascism its opportunity. The propertied classes turned to Fascism out of fear.

  8. Which judgement about Mussolini's appointment is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    He was appointed by the king and the elite under the threat of force, not by a seizure of power. The March on Rome was a successful threat.

  9. Which judgement about Fascist Italy is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A dictatorship with totalitarian aims but more limited control than Nazi Germany. The king, Church and army kept independence.

Key takeaways

  • Disappointment with the peace (the "mutilated victory"), economic crisis and the unrest of the biennio rosso weakened Liberal Italy and frightened the propertied classes, creating openings for Fascism.
  • Mussolini founded the Fascist movement in 1919, built it through squad violence, and was appointed prime minister by Victor Emmanuel III in October 1922 after the threat of the March on Rome.
  • Through the Acerbo Law, the 1924 election, the Matteotti crisis and the laws of 1925–1926, Mussolini built a dictatorship, made peace with the Church in 1929 and claimed a corporate state, but its control was less complete than its claims.
  • The Weimar Republic, proclaimed on 9 November 1918, had a highly democratic constitution (adopted August 1919), but proportional representation and Article 48 made it vulnerable in a crisis.
  • Germans resented Versailles as a diktat; the reparations crisis led to the Ruhr occupation in 1923, and Stresemann's diplomacy from 1924 to 1929 revised the treaty peacefully but fragilely.
  • The republic survived the Spartacists, the Kapp Putsch, political murders and the Munich Putsch, and enjoyed relative stability from 1924 to 1929, but depended on institutions that did not fully support democracy.

Common mistakes in The Rise of Fascism and Nazism

  • Thinking Italy was on the losing side in the war. Italy won, but many Italians felt the peace denied them what they had been promised.
  • Believing the March on Rome overthrew the government by force. The king appointed Mussolini legally; the march was a threat that worked.
  • Assuming Mussolini had absolute power from 1922. He led a coalition until the dictatorship was built in 1925–1926, and the king and Church remained independent forces.
  • Blaming the constitution alone for Weimar's collapse. Its weaknesses mattered most in combination with economic crisis and the choices of its leaders.
  • Thinking Germany paid reparations in full. Payments were reduced by the Dawes and Young Plans and suspended in the Depression.
  • Calling 1924–1929 a truly golden age. Prosperity depended on loans, farmers struggled, and coalitions stayed unstable.

Lessons in this school

  1. Post-war Italy
    Italy was on the winning side in 1918, yet many Italians felt cheated by the peace and frightened by unrest at home.
  2. Mussolini
    Benito Mussolini moved from revolutionary socialism to nationalism during the First World War, founded the Fascist movement in 1919 and became prime minister in 1922.
  3. The development of Italian Fascism
    Mussolini took office as head of a coalition in 1922 and built a dictatorship by 1926.
  4. The Weimar Republic
    Germany became a democratic republic in November 1918, as the war ended in defeat.
  5. The Treaty of Versailles and Germany
    For many Germans, the new republic was born with the humiliation of Versailles.
  6. Political instability in Weimar Germany
    Between 1919 and 1923 the Weimar Republic faced uprisings from left and right, political murders and an attempted putsch by Hitler.
  7. Hyperinflation
    In 1923 the German mark collapsed: by November one US dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks.
  8. The Great Depression in Germany
    The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 plunged Germany into an economic and political crisis.
  9. Hitler's rise to power
    Adolf Hitler went from a failed putsch in 1923 to chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933.
  10. Nazi ideology
    Nazism was built on a set of racial and nationalist ideas that shaped everything the regime did.
  11. Nazi consolidation of power
    In January 1933 Hitler led a coalition cabinet; by August 1934 he was Führer of a one-party state.
  12. The police state
    The Nazi regime controlled Germans through the SS, the Gestapo, concentration camps and courts.
  13. Propaganda and censorship in Nazi Germany
    Joseph Goebbels ran a Ministry of Propaganda that tried to control everything Germans read, heard and saw.
  14. Nazi economic policy
    Unemployment in Germany fell from about six million in 1933 to near full employment by 1938.
  15. Youth and education in Nazi Germany
    The Nazis saw young people as the future of the national community and set out to shape them through schools and youth organisations.
  16. Women and family policy in Nazi Germany
    Nazi ideology assigned women the role of wives and mothers serving the national community.
  17. Persecution of minorities
    Nazi racial ideology targeted many groups besides Jews: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, gay men and others judged a threat to the national community.
  18. Antisemitism
    Antisemitism was at the centre of Nazi ideology.
  19. The Holocaust
    Between 1941 and 1945 the Nazi regime and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews.
  20. Resistance to Nazi rule
    Opposition to Nazi rule ranged from grumbling and nonconformity to attempts to kill Hitler.

The Rise of Fascism and Nazism: questions students ask

When and where did Mussolini found the Fascist movement?

In Milan in March 1919.

What did the Acerbo Law do?

It gave two-thirds of the seats to the party with the largest share of the vote, if it won at least a quarter.

What was Article 48?

The clause that allowed the president to rule by emergency decree in a crisis.

How was the Kapp Putsch defeated?

By a general strike of workers in March 1920.
