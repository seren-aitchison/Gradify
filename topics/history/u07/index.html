Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What was Weltpolitik?

Wilhelm II's world policy: colonies, a great navy and a global role for Germany. From “European power politics” →

In plain English: In 1914 Europe was dominated by a handful of Great Powers whose rivalries had been building for decades. Key idea: German unification in 1871 upset the balance of power; Bismarck contained the risks, but Wilhelm II's Weltpolitik after 1890 pushed Russia and Britain towards France.

What was Pan-Slavism?

The belief that Slav peoples belonged together, with Russia as their protector. From “Nationalism” →

In plain English: Nationalism, the belief that each nation should have its own state and that loyalty to it comes first, both strengthened the Great Powers and threatened to tear Austria-Hungary apart. Key idea: Nationalism strengthened the Great Powers' rivalry but, in the Balkans, threatened Austria-Hungary's survival, making Vienna determined to crush Serbia and Russia unwilling to abandon it.

What was no man's land?

The ground between the opposing front-line trenches. From “Trench warfare” →

In plain English: From late 1914 the war in the west was fought from trenches. Key idea: Trench systems, machine guns, wire and artillery made defence stronger than attack; gas and tanks did not end the stalemate alone, but combined methods did by 1918.

When did conscription begin in Britain?

With the Military Service Act of January 1916. From “The British Home Front, 1914–1918” →

In plain English: The First World War changed life in Britain: government took new powers, men were conscripted, food was rationed and civilians were bombed. Key idea: DORA, conscription from 1916, rationing in 1918 and air raids brought the war home; the state took unprecedented powers, many removed after 1918, with some lasting effects.

What did Germany do with troops freed by Brest-Litovsk?

It moved them west for the Spring Offensive of March 1918. From “Russia leaves the war” →

In plain English: Revolution in 1917 took Russia out of the war. Key idea: The Provisional Government's decision to keep fighting helped the Bolsheviks take power; Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) took Russia out of the war at a heavy price and freed German troops for the Spring Offensive.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The First World War

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In which year was Germany unified? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1871. Germany was unified in 1871 after the Franco-Prussian War.

  2. Which admiral drove Germany's naval expansion from 1898? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tirpitz. Admiral Tirpitz led the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900.

  3. Which agreement of 1904 settled colonial disputes between Britain and France? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Entente Cordiale. The Entente Cordiale was signed in 1904; it settled colonial disputes but was not a military alliance.

  4. Why did Bismarck try to keep France isolated? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To prevent a war on two fronts against France and Russia together. A France allied to Russia would threaten Germany from both sides.

  5. Why did the Schlieffen Plan make British involvement more likely? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It required German troops to invade neutral Belgium. Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality.

  6. Why did Italy not join the war on Germany's side in 1914? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It argued Austria-Hungary's war was not defensive, so the alliance didn't apply. The Triple Alliance was defensive; Italy joined the Entente in 1915.

  7. Which judgement about Weltpolitik is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It helped create the encirclement that Germany's leaders came to fear. Its effects on Russia and Britain made Germany's position worse.

  8. Which judgement about war plans in 1914 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They constrained leaders' choices and sped escalation, but leaders still chose war. Plans mattered but did not remove decisions.

  9. Which is the best judgement about alliances and the outbreak of war? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They explain how a Balkan crisis spread, but not why war began. Alliances spread the war; decisions began it.

Key takeaways

  • German unification in 1871 upset the balance of power; Bismarck contained the risks, but Wilhelm II's Weltpolitik after 1890 pushed Russia and Britain towards France.
  • The naval race turned Britain against Germany, and rigid mobilisation plans, above all the Schlieffen Plan, made a local crisis likely to become a general war once mobilisation began.
  • The Triple Alliance and Triple Entente divided Europe by 1907; the alliances were defensive, and they explain how a Balkan crisis spread more than why war began.
  • The Moroccan crises strengthened the Entente against Germany, while Ottoman decline made the Balkans a flashpoint where Austria-Hungary and Russia would not back down twice.
  • Nationalism strengthened the Great Powers' rivalry but, in the Balkans, threatened Austria-Hungary's survival, making Vienna determined to crush Serbia and Russia unwilling to abandon it.
  • Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb armed with help from the Black Hand, killed Franz Ferdinand and Sophie in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914; it became the trigger for war because of the decisions that followed.

Common mistakes in The First World War

  • Thinking the Great Powers were divided into two fixed camps for decades. The alignments of 1914 formed gradually, and as late as the 1890s Britain was at odds with France and Russia over empire.
  • Thinking militarism means only that countries had armies. The point is the arms race, the influence of military planning on policy, and plans that left little time for diplomacy.
  • Assuming Britain was bound by alliance to fight in 1914. The ententes were understandings; Britain entered over the invasion of Belgium.
  • Treating imperial rivalry as only about overseas colonies. The most dangerous rivalry was in the Balkans, on Europe's own doorstep.
  • Treating nationalism as one force. It united some states, threatened others and took different forms in each country.
  • Thinking the assassination made war inevitable. It was a trigger; whether it led to war depended on choices made in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg.

Lessons in this school

  1. European power politics
    In 1914 Europe was dominated by a handful of Great Powers whose rivalries had been building for decades.
  2. Militarism
    Before 1914 the Great Powers built ever larger armies and navies and planned for rapid offensive war.
  3. Alliances
    By 1914 Europe was divided between the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente.
  4. Imperial rivalry
    Competition for colonies and influence produced repeated crises between the Great Powers before 1914.
  5. Nationalism
    Nationalism, the belief that each nation should have its own state and that loyalty to it comes first, both strengthened the Great Powers and threatened to tear Austria-Hungary apart.
  6. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand
    On 28 June 1914 a young Bosnian Serb shot the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne in Sarajevo.
  7. The July Crisis
    In the five weeks after Sarajevo the Great Powers moved from a Balkan crisis to a European war.
  8. The outbreak of war
    In August 1914 millions of men marched to war expecting it to be over quickly.
  9. Trench warfare
    From late 1914 the war in the west was fought from trenches.
  10. The Western Front
    The Western Front saw the war's most famous battles: Verdun, the Somme and Passchendaele.
  11. Gallipoli
    In 1915 the Allies tried to knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war by forcing the Dardanelles.
  12. The Eastern Front, 1914–1917
    In the east the war was more mobile than in the west, with huge advances and retreats.
  13. The British Home Front, 1914–1918
    The First World War changed life in Britain: government took new powers, men were conscripted, food was rationed and civilians were bombed.
  14. Total war
    The First World War demanded the mobilisation of entire societies and economies.
  15. Propaganda in the First World War
    Governments in the First World War used propaganda on an unprecedented scale to recruit soldiers, sustain morale and win neutral opinion.
  16. Women's wartime roles
    The war drew women into new work, from munitions factories to nursing near the front.
  17. Russia leaves the war
    Revolution in 1917 took Russia out of the war.
  18. The United States enters the war
    The United States stayed neutral until April 1917.
  19. The Armistice
    Germany came close to victory in spring 1918 and was defeated by the autumn.
  20. The Treaty of Versailles
    The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, set the terms of peace with Germany.
  21. The consequences of the First World War
    The First World War killed millions, destroyed four empires and reshaped politics, economies and memory across the world.
