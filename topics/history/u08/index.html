Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What was the Okhrana?

The tsarist secret police, which watched and arrested opponents. From “Tsarist Russia” →

In plain English: At the start of the twentieth century Russia was a vast empire ruled by an autocrat, with a largely peasant population and a fast-growing industry. Key idea: Around 1900 Russia was a vast multinational empire ruled by an autocratic tsar through the bureaucracy, police and Church; emancipation in 1861 and Witte's industrialisation of the 1890s created pressures that autocracy struggled to contain.

Why was it a mistake for Nicholas to take command of the army in 1915?

He was blamed personally for later defeats, and left government to Alexandra. From “The First World War and Russia” →

In plain English: The First World War placed unbearable strain on Russia's army, economy and government. Key idea: Russia suffered defeat at Tannenberg (1914) and great losses in 1915; Nicholas took command in 1915, shortages and inflation hit the cities, and Alexandra and Rasputin's influence discredited the government, preparing the way for revolution.

What was the Sovnarkom?

The Council of People's Commissars, the Bolshevik government led by Lenin. From “The October Revolution” →

In plain English: On 24 and 25 October 1917 the Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd. Key idea: The Bolsheviks seized Petrograd on 24 to 25 October 1917, formed the Sovnarkom, issued decrees on peace and land, set up the Cheka and dissolved the Constituent Assembly in January 1918; historians debate whether October was a coup or a popular revolution.

What did Lenin's Testament say about Stalin?

That he was too rude and should be removed as General Secretary. From “Stalin's rise to power” →

In plain English: When Lenin died in January 1924, few expected Joseph Stalin to succeed him. Key idea: After Lenin's death in 1924, Stalin used his post as General Secretary, the Lenin Enrolment, the policy of Socialism in One Country and shifting alliances to defeat Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin by 1929.

Whose murder in December 1934 did Stalin use to justify arrests?

Sergei Kirov, the Leningrad party leader. From “The Great Purge” →

In plain English: In 1936 to 1938 Stalin's secret police arrested well over a million people and shot hundreds of thousands in the Great Purge, or Great Terror. Key idea: After Kirov's murder in 1934, the show trials, the army purge and Yezhov's mass operations of 1937 to 1938 killed about 680,000 people; archives confirm Stalin's direction, while historians debate how far local pressures drove its scale.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When were the serfs emancipated in Russia? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1861. Alexander II freed the serfs, but they had to make redemption payments for land.

  2. Where was the Russian fleet destroyed by Japan in May 1905? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tsushima. The defeat ended Russia's hopes in the war with Japan.

  3. Roughly what share of Russia's population were peasants around 1900? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About four-fifths. Russia remained an overwhelmingly peasant country.

  4. Why did Witte's industrialisation create new pressures for the tsar? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It created a large, discontented urban working class. Fast industrial growth brought poor conditions and unrest in the cities.

  5. Why did rumours about Rasputin damage the monarchy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They suggested a disreputable peasant controlled the royal family. Scandal undermined respect for the tsar and tsarina.

  6. Why did the Socialist Revolutionaries win peasant support? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They promised to give the land to the peasants. Land was the peasants' central demand, and the SRs promised it.

  7. Which judgement about tsarist rule around 1900 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Strong in its means of control, but facing pressures autocracy could not absorb. Control and pressure existed side by side before 1905.

  8. Which judgement about Nicholas II's responsibility is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    He inherited serious problems, but his choices turned crisis into collapse. Structure and personal choice both mattered in 1917.

  9. Which judgement about the opposition before 1905 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small and divided parties, but voicing grievances that could produce revolt. Grievance mattered more than organisation.

Key takeaways

  • Around 1900 Russia was a vast multinational empire ruled by an autocratic tsar through the bureaucracy, police and Church; emancipation in 1861 and Witte's industrialisation of the 1890s created pressures that autocracy struggled to contain.
  • Nicholas II (1894 to 1917) was devoted to autocracy but indecisive; his son's haemophilia gave Rasputin influence, defeat by Japan and the First World War undermined him, and he abdicated in 1917.
  • Around 1900 peasants, about four-fifths of the population, were poor and burdened by redemption payments, workers lived in harsh conditions, and a small elite held power; the Socialist Revolutionaries, Social Democrats and liberals voiced their grievances.
  • Bloody Sunday (January 1905) sparked strikes, mutinies and peasant risings; the October Manifesto promised a Duma and split the opposition, the army crushed the rest, and Stolypin combined repression with land reform.
  • Russia suffered defeat at Tannenberg (1914) and great losses in 1915; Nicholas took command in 1915, shortages and inflation hit the cities, and Alexandra and Rasputin's influence discredited the government, preparing the way for revolution.
  • Bread protests and strikes in Petrograd from 23 February 1917 became revolution when the garrison mutinied on 27 February; Duma leaders and the Petrograd Soviet emerged, and Nicholas abdicated on 2 March 1917.

Common mistakes in The Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union

  • Thinking the Russian Empire was mainly Russian. Fewer than half its people were Russians in 1897.
  • Thinking Rasputin ran the government. He influenced Alexandra and some appointments, but rumour exaggerated his power.
  • Confusing the Bolsheviks with all Russian socialists. They were one faction of the Social Democrats, alongside the Mensheviks and the Socialist Revolutionaries.
  • Thinking the October Manifesto made Russia a constitutional monarchy. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 kept supreme power with the tsar.
  • Thinking the Russian army failed only for lack of courage. Shortages of weapons, supplies, transport and leadership were central.
  • Thinking the Bolsheviks led the February Revolution. Lenin was in exile, and the rising was largely spontaneous.

Lessons in this school

  1. Tsarist Russia
    At the start of the twentieth century Russia was a vast empire ruled by an autocrat, with a largely peasant population and a fast-growing industry.
  2. Nicholas II
    Nicholas II, the last tsar, reigned from 1894 to 1917.
  3. Russian society and inequality
    Most Russians around 1900 were poor peasants, while a small elite held land, wealth and power.
  4. The 1905 Revolution
    In 1905 strikes, mutinies and peasant risings swept Russia after troops fired on peaceful protesters.
  5. The First World War and Russia
    The First World War placed unbearable strain on Russia's army, economy and government.
  6. The February Revolution
    In February 1917 strikes and bread protests in Petrograd turned into a revolution when soldiers joined them, and within days Nicholas II abdicated.
  7. The Provisional Government
    The Provisional Government ruled Russia from March to October 1917 but shared power with the Petrograd Soviet and lost support month by month.
  8. Lenin and the Bolsheviks
    Vladimir Lenin built the Bolsheviks into a disciplined party and, after returning to Russia in April 1917, set them on course to seize power.
  9. The October Revolution
    On 24 and 25 October 1917 the Bolsheviks seized power in Petrograd.
  10. The Russian Civil War
    From 1918 to 1921 the Bolsheviks fought a brutal civil war against the Whites, nationalist movements and foreign intervention.
  11. War Communism
    From 1918 to 1921 the Bolsheviks ran the economy by force to win the Civil War, taking grain from peasants and nationalising industry.
  12. The New Economic Policy
    In March 1921 Lenin replaced War Communism with the New Economic Policy, allowing peasants to sell surplus grain and small traders to operate.
  13. Stalin's rise to power
    When Lenin died in January 1924, few expected Joseph Stalin to succeed him.
  14. Collectivisation
    From 1929 Stalin forced Soviet peasants into collective farms, destroying the peasantry's independence and causing a catastrophic famine.
  15. Soviet industrialisation
    In the 1930s Stalin drove the Soviet Union to industrialise at breakneck speed, building new cities, factories and dams.
  16. The Five-Year Plans
    Stalin's Five-Year Plans set targets for every part of Soviet industry from 1928.
  17. The Great Purge
    In 1936 to 1938 Stalin's secret police arrested well over a million people and shot hundreds of thousands in the Great Purge, or Great Terror.
  18. Soviet propaganda
    Stalin's regime used posters, film, newspapers, schools and art to build loyalty and a cult of the leader.
  19. Life under Stalin
    Life in Stalin's Soviet Union combined new opportunities in education and work with shortages, crowded housing, surveillance and fear.
  20. The Soviet Union in the Second World War
    The Soviet Union suffered the heaviest losses of any country in the Second World War and played the largest part in defeating Nazi Germany.

Father Georgi Gapon, on 9 January 1905.

Soldiers of the garrison mutinied and joined the protesters.
