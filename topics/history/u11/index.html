Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What was containment?

The US policy of stopping the spread of communism, set out by George Kennan in 1946. From “Origins of the Cold War” →

In plain English: Within two years of defeating Germany together, the United States and the Soviet Union had become rivals. Key idea: Disputes over eastern Europe and Germany broke the wartime alliance; containment, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan in 1947 formalised the rivalry, and historians still debate where blame lies.

How did Stalin respond to the speech?

He compared Churchill to Hitler and called the speech a call to war. From “The Iron Curtain” →

In plain English: In March 1946 Winston Churchill declared that an "iron curtain" had descended across Europe. Key idea: Churchill's speech at Fulton on 5 March 1946 described an iron curtain across Europe, called for Anglo-American partnership and drew a fierce response from Stalin; the phrase came to define the division of Europe.

What was the "missile gap"?

The false American belief in the late 1950s that the Soviet Union had more missiles. From “The arms race” →

In plain English: From 1949 the United States and the Soviet Union competed to build ever more powerful nuclear weapons and the means to deliver them. Key idea: The Soviet atomic bomb (1949), hydrogen bombs (1952–1953) and Sputnik (1957) drove an arms race in warheads and missiles, fuelled by mutual fear and often exaggerated estimates.

How was the crisis resolved?

Khrushchev removed the missiles in return for an American no-invasion pledge and, secretly, the removal of US missiles from Turkey. From “The Cuban Missile Crisis” →

In plain English: For thirteen days in October 1962 the world came closer to nuclear war than at any other time. Key idea: Khrushchev placed missiles in Cuba in 1962; after thirteen days, Kennedy's quarantine and negotiation led to their removal on 28 October, with a no-invasion pledge and a secret deal on the Turkish missiles.

What were glasnost and perestroika?

Openness and restructuring: Gorbachev's reforms of Soviet society, the economy and government. From “Reagan and Gorbachev” →

In plain English: In the 1980s Ronald Reagan's confrontational policies and Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms transformed superpower relations. Key idea: Reagan's build-up and SDI, and Gorbachev's glasnost, perestroika and new thinking, led to summits and the INF Treaty of 1987; most historians see Gorbachev's choices as decisive, with Reagan's readiness to negotiate essential.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Cold War

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When was the Truman Doctrine announced? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    12 March 1947. Truman announced it to Congress, beginning with aid for Greece and Turkey.

  2. Which system had a single ruling party? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Soviet Union under the Communist Party. The Communist Party allowed no rival parties or free elections.

  3. Which communist leader broke with Stalin in 1948? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tito of Yugoslavia. Yugoslavia went its own way after 1948, outside the Soviet bloc.

  4. Why did Stalin refuse Marshall Plan aid for the Soviet bloc? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He saw it as a way to extend American influence into eastern Europe. He feared American economic influence would weaken Soviet control.

  5. Why did American leaders believe Soviet expansion had to be resisted early? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They believed appeasement in the 1930s showed that aggression grows if not stopped. The lesson of appeasement shaped US thinking.

  6. How did the Red Army's presence help communists take power? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It backed communist control of police and pressure on rival parties. Soviet power underpinned communist takeovers.

  7. Which view of the Cold War's origins do most historians now hold? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Mutual suspicion, with Stalin's methods in eastern Europe a central cause. Archival research has supported a post-revisionist balance.

  8. Which judgement about ideology and security is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They reinforced each other, so neither alone explains the conflict. Each shaped how the other was understood.

  9. Which judgement about the division of Europe is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Stalin was determined to control the region, but pace and form were shaped by circumstance and Western moves. Historians weigh intention and circumstance.

Key takeaways

  • Disputes over eastern Europe and Germany broke the wartime alliance; containment, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan in 1947 formalised the rivalry, and historians still debate where blame lies.
  • American capitalism and democracy faced Soviet communism and one-party rule; ideological rivalry and security fears, rooted in recent history, reinforced each other.
  • Communists took power across eastern Europe by 1948 with Soviet backing, Yugoslavia broke away, and in 1949 Germany became two states; Stalin's aims and Western responses together divided Europe.
  • Churchill's speech at Fulton on 5 March 1946 described an iron curtain across Europe, called for Anglo-American partnership and drew a fierce response from Stalin; the phrase came to define the division of Europe.
  • Stalin blockaded West Berlin from 24 June 1948 to stop a West German state; the airlift supplied the city until he gave up in May 1949, strengthening the West and confirming Germany's division.
  • NATO (4 April 1949), with its Article 5 guarantee, and the Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955), after West Germany joined NATO, divided Europe into two armed camps.

Common mistakes in The Cold War

  • Thinking the Cold War began on a single day. It developed between 1945 and 1947 through a series of decisions on both sides.
  • Thinking communism and capitalism were only economic systems. Each was also a set of political beliefs about how society should be run.
  • Thinking every eastern European communist state took orders from Moscow. Tito's Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948.
  • Assuming Churchill was prime minister when he gave the speech. He had lost the 1945 election and spoke as leader of the opposition.
  • Thinking the blockade led to fighting. Neither side fired on the other; the airlift avoided direct conflict.
  • Thinking the Warsaw Pact was formed at the same time as NATO. It came six years later, after West Germany joined NATO.

Lessons in this school

  1. Origins of the Cold War
    Within two years of defeating Germany together, the United States and the Soviet Union had become rivals.
  2. Capitalism and communism
    The Cold War was a conflict of ideas as well as of power.
  3. The division of Europe
    Between 1945 and 1949 Europe split into a Soviet-controlled east and an American-backed west.
  4. The Iron Curtain
    In March 1946 Winston Churchill declared that an "iron curtain" had descended across Europe.
  5. The Berlin Blockade
    From June 1948 to May 1949 the Soviet Union cut all land routes into West Berlin, and the Western powers supplied the city by air.
  6. NATO and the Warsaw Pact
    The Cold War divided Europe into two military alliances.
  7. The Korean War
    In June 1950 communist North Korea invaded South Korea, and the Cold War became a hot war.
  8. The arms race
    From 1949 the United States and the Soviet Union competed to build ever more powerful nuclear weapons and the means to deliver them.
  9. Nuclear deterrence
    Nuclear weapons were built in the hope that they would never be used.
  10. The Hungarian Uprising
    In October 1956 Hungarians rose against Soviet-backed communist rule, and within days Soviet tanks crushed them.
  11. The Berlin Wall
    On 13 August 1961 East German forces sealed the border through Berlin, and the Berlin Wall became the most famous symbol of the Cold War.
  12. The Cuban Missile Crisis
    For thirteen days in October 1962 the world came closer to nuclear war than at any other time.
  13. Vietnam and containment
    The Vietnam War was the longest and most divisive application of containment.
  14. Détente
    In the late 1960s and 1970s the superpowers tried to reduce tension through arms control, trade and agreements.
  15. Soviet intervention in Afghanistan
    In December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan to save a communist government.
  16. Reagan and Gorbachev
    In the 1980s Ronald Reagan's confrontational policies and Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms transformed superpower relations.
  17. The fall of the Berlin Wall
    On the night of 9 November 1989 East Berliners poured through the Berlin Wall.
  18. The collapse of the Soviet Union
    In December 1991 the Soviet Union ceased to exist.
  19. The end of the Cold War
    Between 1989 and 1991 the Cold War ended without a war between the superpowers.

The Cold War: questions students ask

What was the Soviet economic system?

A planned economy with state ownership of industry and farms.

When were the two German states founded?

In 1949: West Germany in May and East Germany in October.

Where and when did Churchill give the Iron Curtain speech?

At Fulton, Missouri, on 5 March 1946.

What triggered the Berlin Blockade?

The introduction of the Deutschmark in the western zones in June 1948, seen by Stalin as a step towards a West German state.
