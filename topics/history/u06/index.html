Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

When did Parliament abolish the British slave trade?

In 1807. From “The British Empire in the nineteenth century” →

In plain English: In the nineteenth century the British Empire became the largest the world had seen, covering about a quarter of the world's land by 1900. Key idea: The nineteenth-century British Empire combined settler colonies, India, colonies and informal influence; Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery from 1834, compensating owners, and took direct rule of India in 1858.

What was the Midlothian campaign?

Gladstone's speeches to huge crowds in 1879 to 1880, a new style of mass campaigning. From “The development of democracy” →

In plain English: As the electorate grew, politics changed: parties built national organisations, leaders campaigned to mass audiences, and the elected Commons won out over the Lords. Key idea: After 1867 parties built mass organisations and leaders such as Gladstone campaigned to mass audiences; the Parliament Act of 1911 made the Commons supreme, and the Acts of 1918 and 1928 completed adult suffrage.

Who led the Irish Parliamentary Party in the 1880s?

Charles Stewart Parnell. From “Irish nationalism” →

In plain English: Irish nationalists sought self-government, some through Parliament and some through revolution. Key idea: Irish nationalism took revolutionary, constitutional and cultural forms; Home Rule Bills failed in 1886 and 1893, and the 1912 bill passed in 1914 but was suspended, while Ulster unionists' armed opposition made all-Ireland Home Rule impossible.

Which African states remained independent in 1914?

Ethiopia and Liberia. From “The Scramble for Africa” →

In plain English: Between about 1880 and 1914 European powers divided almost the whole of Africa among themselves. Key idea: Between 1880 and 1914 Europe partitioned almost all of Africa, with rules set at the Berlin Conference (1884 to 1885); rivalry, economic and strategic interests and technology such as the Maxim gun drove it, and Britain took Egypt, Sudan, Nigeria, Kenya and southern Africa.

Who led the NUWSS?

Millicent Fawcett. From “Women's suffrage” →

In plain English: Women campaigned for the parliamentary vote for over half a century before winning it in 1918 and 1928. Key idea: The NUWSS (1897) campaigned peacefully and the WSPU (1903) militantly; the government answered with force-feeding and the Cat and Mouse Act (1913); women over 30 with property gained the vote in 1918 and all women at 21 in 1928.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Nineteenth-Century Britain

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who received the £20 million compensation under the Slavery Abolition Act? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Slave owners. Compensation went to owners, not to the people who had been enslaved.

  2. Which prime minister encouraged Victoria to take the title Empress of India? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Benjamin Disraeli. Disraeli used the monarchy to promote empire and loyalty.

  3. Which prime minister passed the Third Reform Act in 1884? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    William Gladstone. It extended household suffrage to the counties.

  4. Why does the resistance of enslaved people matter to the history of abolition? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Rebellions such as Jamaica's in 1831 to 1832 increased pressure to end slavery. Resistance made slavery harder and costlier to maintain.

  5. Why did a republican movement grow in the early 1870s? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Victoria's long withdrawal after Albert's death made the monarchy seem useless. Her absence led critics to question the cost and role of the Crown.

  6. Why did the Ballot Act reduce bribery and intimidation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Voters could no longer be seen voting, so they could not be checked. Secret voting made it impossible to know if a bribed voter kept his side of a deal.

  7. Which judgement about how the empire was held together is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Local collaboration, backed by the threat and use of force. Collaboration and coercion worked together across the empire.

  8. Which judgement about Victorian monarchy is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    By 1901 it had lost power but gained popularity, largely because of that change. Withdrawal from politics made the Crown a national symbol.

  9. Which judgement about democracy by 1885 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Britain had moved a long way towards democracy, but was not yet one. Progress was real, but large groups remained excluded.

Key takeaways

  • The nineteenth-century British Empire combined settler colonies, India, colonies and informal influence; Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery from 1834, compensating owners, and took direct rule of India in 1858.
  • Victoria (1837 to 1901) saw the monarchy lose direct political power but gain a symbolic role; after Albert's death in 1861 she withdrew, but Disraeli, the title of Empress (1876) and the jubilees of 1887 and 1897 restored its popularity.
  • The Second Reform Act (1867) gave the vote to borough householders, the Ballot Act (1872) made voting secret, the 1883 Act curbed corruption, and the Third Reform Act (1884) and Redistribution Act (1885) gave most men the vote in fairer constituencies.
  • After 1867 parties built mass organisations and leaders such as Gladstone campaigned to mass audiences; the Parliament Act of 1911 made the Commons supreme, and the Acts of 1918 and 1928 completed adult suffrage.
  • New Unionism brought unskilled workers into unions after 1888, Keir Hardie's ILP (1893) and the LRC (1900) sought working-class MPs, and Taff Vale (1901) and the 1903 pact with the Liberals helped Labour win 29 seats in 1906.
  • The Act of Union (1801) brought Ireland into the UK; O'Connell won Catholic Emancipation in 1829; the Great Famine (1845 to 1852) killed about a million and drove over a million to emigrate; Gladstone's reforms of 1869 to 1881 addressed some grievances.

Common mistakes in Nineteenth-Century Britain

  • Thinking the empire was a single system. Settler colonies, India, crown colonies and informal influence were governed very differently.
  • Thinking Victoria had no influence. She could still be consulted, encourage and warn, and she tried to influence ministers.
  • Thinking all men could vote after 1884. Around four in ten adult men, and all women, still could not.
  • Dating British democracy to a single act. It developed in stages from 1867 to 1928, and some would add 1948 or 1969.
  • Thinking Labour was formed as a revolutionary party. It was a coalition of unions and socialists seeking representation in Parliament.
  • Thinking the famine was caused only by potato blight. Poverty, dependence on one crop, land ownership and limited relief all made it worse.

Lessons in this school

  1. The British Empire in the nineteenth century
    In the nineteenth century the British Empire became the largest the world had seen, covering about a quarter of the world's land by 1900.
  2. Victorian monarchy
    Queen Victoria reigned from 1837 to 1901, longer than any previous British monarch.
  3. Parliamentary reform, 1867–1884
    Between 1867 and 1885 Parliament extended the vote to most working men and changed how elections were run.
  4. The development of democracy
    As the electorate grew, politics changed: parties built national organisations, leaders campaigned to mass audiences, and the elected Commons won out over the Lords.
  5. The growth of the Labour movement
    Between the 1880s and 1906 unskilled workers formed new unions and the labour movement created its own political party.
  6. Ireland and the Union
    The Act of Union of 1800 made Ireland part of the United Kingdom, but Ireland's Catholic majority remained poor and largely excluded from power.
  7. Irish nationalism
    Irish nationalists sought self-government, some through Parliament and some through revolution.
  8. Imperial expansion
    Britain added vast territories to its empire in the nineteenth century, from Asia to Africa.
  9. Colonial administration
    Britain governed hundreds of millions of people with relatively few British officials.
  10. The Scramble for Africa
    Between about 1880 and 1914 European powers divided almost the whole of Africa among themselves.
  11. Imperial resistance
    Colonised peoples resisted British rule throughout the nineteenth century, in rebellions, wars and, increasingly, political movements.
  12. The Boer Wars
    Britain fought two wars against the Boer republics of southern Africa, in 1880 to 1881 and 1899 to 1902.
  13. Women's suffrage
    Women campaigned for the parliamentary vote for over half a century before winning it in 1918 and 1928.
  14. The Liberal welfare reforms
    Between 1906 and 1914 Liberal governments introduced pensions, school meals, labour exchanges and national insurance.
  15. Britain's changing international position
    In the mid-nineteenth century Britain avoided binding alliances, confident in its navy and empire.

Nineteenth-Century Britain: questions students ask

What did the Government of India Act of 1858 do?

It transferred the rule of India from the East India Company to the Crown.

What did Bagehot mean by the "dignified" parts of the constitution?

Parts such as the monarchy that won loyalty, as distinct from the "efficient" parts that governed.

What did the Second Reform Act of 1867 do?

Gave the vote to male householders in boroughs and £10 lodgers, roughly doubling the electorate.

What did the dockers win in 1889?

The "tanner", sixpence an hour.
