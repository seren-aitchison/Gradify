Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What did Upton Sinclair's The Jungle expose?

Filthy conditions in Chicago's meatpacking plants, in 1906. From “Progressive reform” →

In plain English: Between about 1900 and 1920 American reformers tried to curb the power of big business, clean up politics and protect consumers. Key idea: Progressive reformers from about 1900 to 1920 attacked trusts, corruption and unsafe food; Roosevelt and Wilson expanded federal power, and amendments brought income tax, direct election of senators, Prohibition and women's suffrage, but Black Americans gained little.

What did the Agricultural Adjustment Act do?

Paid farmers to produce less, to raise prices. From “The New Deal” →

In plain English: Roosevelt's New Deal of 1933 to 1938 used federal power to provide relief, recovery and reform. Key idea: The New Deal (1933 to 1938) stabilised banks, created jobs through the CCC and WPA, paid farmers through the AAA, and created Social Security and union rights in 1935; critics attacked it from both sides, and unemployment stayed high until the war.

What did Brown v. Board of Education rule in 1954?

That segregation in public schools was unconstitutional. From “The Civil Rights Movement” →

In plain English: From the 1950s to the late 1960s Black Americans and their allies challenged segregation and won landmark laws. Key idea: Black Americans challenged Jim Crow through Brown v. Board (1954), Little Rock, sit-ins, Freedom Rides, Birmingham, the March on Washington (1963) and Selma, winning the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965), though inequality persisted.

Who led the Women's Political Council during the boycott?

Jo Ann Robinson. From “The Montgomery Bus Boycott” →

In plain English: The Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955 to 1956 was one of the first great mass protests of the Civil Rights Movement. Key idea: Rosa Parks's arrest on 1 December 1955 began a 381-day boycott organised by the Women's Political Council, churches and the MIA under King; Browder v. Gayle (1956) ended bus segregation in Montgomery.

What did supply-side economics argue?

That lower taxes and less regulation encourage work, saving, investment and growth. From “Reaganomics” →

In plain English: Ronald Reagan, president from 1981 to 1989, promised to shrink government, cut taxes and revive the economy. Key idea: Reaganomics combined tax cuts (1981, 1986), deregulation, higher defence spending and a firm line against unions; after the recession of 1981 to 1982 came strong growth and low inflation, but also large deficits, a tripled national debt and rising inequality.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The United States in the Twentieth Century

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which president created the Federal Reserve in 1913? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Woodrow Wilson. The Federal Reserve became the central bank of the United States.

  2. Which tariff of 1930 provoked retaliation and shrank world trade? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Smoot-Hawley Tariff. Other countries raised tariffs against American goods in response.

  3. In which year did Roosevelt propose his court-packing plan? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1937. Congress rejected the plan after his re-election.

  4. Why did Progressives attack the trusts? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Giant corporations controlled markets and could fix prices. Monopoly power threatened consumers and competitors.

  5. Why did bank failures deepen the Depression? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Depositors lost their savings, and credit for businesses dried up. Failures spread fear and reduced spending and lending.

  6. Why did Roosevelt avoid challenging segregation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He needed the votes of Southern Democrats in Congress. Political calculation limited his civil rights record.

  7. Which judgement about Progressive reform is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It expanded federal power, but served some Americans far more than others. Reform was real but uneven in who it helped.

  8. Which judgement about Hoover's response is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    More than his reputation suggests, but too little and too late. Hoover acted, but within the narrow limits of his beliefs.

  9. Which judgement about Roosevelt's leadership is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A transformative leader whose political skill sometimes outran his principles. His achievements and compromises both matter.

Key takeaways

  • Progressive reformers from about 1900 to 1920 attacked trusts, corruption and unsafe food; Roosevelt and Wilson expanded federal power, and amendments brought income tax, direct election of senators, Prohibition and women's suffrage, but Black Americans gained little.
  • Speculation on the margin led to the crash of October 1929; overproduction, inequality, bank failures and tariffs turned it into the Great Depression, with about a quarter unemployed by 1933, and Hoover's limited response failed.
  • Roosevelt, elected in 1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944, restored confidence through his inaugural address and fireside chats and built a broad coalition, but his court-packing plan of 1937 failed; he died in April 1945.
  • The New Deal (1933 to 1938) stabilised banks, created jobs through the CCC and WPA, paid farmers through the AAA, and created Social Security and union rights in 1935; critics attacked it from both sides, and unemployment stayed high until the war.
  • The United States moved from the Neutrality Acts to Lend-Lease (1941) and war after Pearl Harbor (December 1941); war production ended the Depression, women and Black Americans gained new work, and about 120,000 Japanese Americans were interned.
  • The GI Bill, the baby boom, defence spending and highways drove post-war prosperity; suburbs, cars and television created a consumer society, but McCarthyism, racial exclusion and persistent poverty marked its limits.

Common mistakes in The United States in the Twentieth Century

  • Thinking Progressive reform helped all Americans equally. Segregation deepened in the same years.
  • Thinking the crash alone caused the Depression. Weak banks, inequality, overproduction and policy mistakes turned a crash into a depression.
  • Thinking Roosevelt opposed segregation. He avoided challenging it to keep the support of Southern Democrats.
  • Thinking the New Deal ended the Depression. Unemployment was still around 15% in 1940; war spending ended it.
  • Thinking the war brought lasting equality for women. Most women war workers left or were pushed out of their jobs after 1945.
  • Thinking post-war prosperity reached everyone. Harrington found tens of millions still poor in 1962.

Lessons in this school

  1. Progressive reform
    Between about 1900 and 1920 American reformers tried to curb the power of big business, clean up politics and protect consumers.
  2. The Great Depression
    The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 was followed by the worst economic depression in American history.
  3. Franklin D. Roosevelt
    Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected president four times and led the United States through the Depression and most of the Second World War.
  4. The New Deal
    Roosevelt's New Deal of 1933 to 1938 used federal power to provide relief, recovery and reform.
  5. The Second World War and America
    The Second World War ended the Depression and made the United States the world's leading military and economic power.
  6. Post-war prosperity
    After 1945 the United States enjoyed a long boom of rising incomes, suburban homes, cars and television.
  7. The Civil Rights Movement
    From the 1950s to the late 1960s Black Americans and their allies challenged segregation and won landmark laws.
  8. Martin Luther King Jr.
    Martin Luther King Jr.
  9. Malcolm X
    Malcolm X rejected integration and non-violence as the answer to racism, and became the most powerful voice of Black nationalism in the early 1960s.
  10. The Montgomery Bus Boycott
    The Montgomery Bus Boycott of 1955 to 1956 was one of the first great mass protests of the Civil Rights Movement.
  11. The Vietnam War and American society
    The Vietnam War divided American society more deeply than any conflict since the Civil War.
  12. The Watergate scandal
    The Watergate scandal began with a burglary in 1972 and ended with the only resignation of a president in American history.
  13. Reaganomics
    Ronald Reagan, president from 1981 to 1989, promised to shrink government, cut taxes and revive the economy.
  14. The United States and the end of the Cold War
    In the 1980s the Cold War moved from a dangerous new confrontation to its peaceful end in 1991.
  15. The changing role of the United States
    After the Cold War the United States was the world's only superpower, but its role changed through globalisation, the attacks of 11 September 2001, long wars and the financial crisis of 2008.
