Key terms and formulas at a glance

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

When did Augustine's mission arrive in Kent?

In 597. From “Anglo-Saxon England” →

In plain English: Between the end of Roman rule in the early fifth century and 1066, England was formed out of several Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. Key idea: Anglo-Saxon England grew from rival kingdoms into a single kingdom with shires, a national coinage and taxation, shaped by Christian conversion from 597.

When was William crowned king of England?

On Christmas Day 1066, in Westminster Abbey. From “The Norman Conquest” →

In plain English: In 1066 England had three kings in one year. Key idea: Edward's death in January 1066 left rival claims; Harold was crowned, defeated Hardrada at Stamford Bridge, and was killed by William at Hastings, who was crowned on Christmas Day.

Why was it called Domesday Book?

Because, like the Last Judgement, its verdicts could not be appealed (Richard FitzNigel, around 1179). From “The Domesday Book” →

In plain English: In 1086 William's commissioners surveyed most of England in a few months. Key idea: Domesday Book, from William's 1086 survey, records land, resources and values in 1066 and 1086, showing a near-total change of landholders above much continuity in village life.

When and where was Becket murdered?

In Canterbury Cathedral on 29 December 1170. From “Thomas Becket” →

In plain English: Thomas Becket rose from royal chancellor to Archbishop of Canterbury, quarrelled with Henry II over the rights of the Church, and was murdered in his cathedral in 1170. Key idea: Becket defended the Church's rights against Henry II's Constitutions of Clarendon (1164); his murder in 1170 made him a saint and forced Henry to back down over criminous clerks.

What caused the Black Death?

Plague, caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. From “The Black Death” →

In plain English: The Black Death reached England in 1348 and killed a large share of the population within two years. Key idea: The Black Death, plague caused by Yersinia pestis, killed perhaps a third to a half of England's people from 1348; labour shortage shifted power towards peasants despite the Statute of Labourers.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Medieval Britain

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which kingdom eventually unified England under its kings? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Wessex. The kings of Wessex, from Alfred's family, led the creation of a single English kingdom.

  2. In which year did the Great Heathen Army land in England? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    865. It landed in East Anglia in 865, beginning a campaign of conquest rather than raiding.

  3. Who wrote a Life of King Alfred in 893? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Asser. Asser, a Welsh bishop at Alfred's court, wrote it during the king's lifetime.

  4. What does the Sutton Hoo ship burial suggest about early Anglo-Saxon kings? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They were wealthy and connected to trade across Europe and beyond. Its gold, silver and imported items show wealth and wide connections.

  5. Why were monasteries common targets for Viking raiders? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They were wealthy and had little defence. Treasure, food and people could be taken from undefended religious houses.

  6. Why did Alfred divide the fyrd into two halves? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    So that one half could serve while the other farmed, keeping a force always ready. Rotation kept an army in the field without ruining agriculture.

  7. Which judgement about England in 1066 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It had advanced administration, but powerful earls and a disputed succession made it vulnerable. The evidence shows sophisticated government alongside political divisions.

  8. Which is the best explanation for changing views of the Vikings? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    New evidence, especially archaeology, added trade and settlement to the chroniclers' picture of violence. Excavations such as Coppergate broadened the evidence base beyond hostile monastic sources.

  9. Which is the most balanced view of Asser's Life as evidence? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Very useful for how Alfred wanted to be seen, but flattering and needing corroboration. Closeness and purpose cut both ways: value and limits must be weighed.

Key takeaways

  • Anglo-Saxon England grew from rival kingdoms into a single kingdom with shires, a national coinage and taxation, shaped by Christian conversion from 597.
  • Viking activity moved from raids (from 793) to conquest and settlement (from 865), creating the Danelaw and leaving a lasting mark on language and place names.
  • Alfred saved Wessex at Edington in 878 and strengthened it with burhs, military reform and a revival of learning, but much of his reputation rests on sources from his own court.
  • Edward's death in January 1066 left rival claims; Harold was crowned, defeated Hardrada at Stamford Bridge, and was killed by William at Hastings, who was crowned on Christmas Day.
  • William won at Hastings on 14 October 1066 by breaking a shield wall that held for most of the day, through cavalry, archers and feigned retreats, helped by Harold's tired army and Harold's death.
  • William secured England through castles, the crushing of revolts (above all the Harrying of the North), the replacement of the English elite and Church leaders, and by keeping effective Anglo-Saxon government.

Common mistakes in Medieval Britain

  • Picturing the Anglo-Saxons as primitive. Late Anglo-Saxon England had sophisticated government, coinage, law and art.
  • Thinking Vikings were only raiders. Many were farmers, traders and settlers whose communities became part of English society.
  • Remembering Alfred mainly for the story of the burnt cakes. The story first appears in later sources, long after his death.
  • Assuming William's victory proves his claim was the strongest. Victory decided the throne, not the legal merits.
  • Assuming the Bayeux Tapestry is a straightforward picture of what happened. It is a Norman narrative, made later, with its own purposes.
  • Thinking Norman control was complete after Hastings. Serious revolts continued until about 1071.

Lessons in this school

  1. Anglo-Saxon England
    Between the end of Roman rule in the early fifth century and 1066, England was formed out of several Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.
  2. The Viking invasions
    From the late eighth century Scandinavian raiders attacked Britain; from 865 a large army set out to conquer.
  3. Alfred the Great
    Alfred, king of Wessex from 871 to 899, survived near-defeat by the Vikings and laid foundations for a united England.
  4. The Norman Conquest
    In 1066 England had three kings in one year.
  5. The Battle of Hastings
    The battle fought on 14 October 1066 decided the English throne.
  6. William I and Norman control
    Winning at Hastings was not the same as controlling England.
  7. Feudalism
    Textbooks often describe Norman England as a "feudal pyramid".
  8. The Domesday Book
    In 1086 William's commissioners surveyed most of England in a few months.
  9. Medieval monarchy
    Medieval kings ruled personally, but not without limits.
  10. Henry II and legal reform
    Henry II (1154–1189) restored order after civil war and made royal justice available across England.
  11. Thomas Becket
    Thomas Becket rose from royal chancellor to Archbishop of Canterbury, quarrelled with Henry II over the rights of the Church, and was murdered in his cathedral in 1170.
  12. King John and Magna Carta
    King John (1199–1216) lost Normandy, quarrelled with the Pope and taxed heavily, until rebel barons forced him to agree Magna Carta in 1215.
  13. The development of Parliament
    Parliament grew out of the king's great councils in the thirteenth century.
  14. Medieval society
    Medieval people described their society as made up of those who prayed, those who fought and those who worked.
  15. The Black Death
    The Black Death reached England in 1348 and killed a large share of the population within two years.
  16. The Peasants' Revolt
    In June 1381 rebels from Essex and Kent marched on London, burned the property of the king's ministers and met the fourteen-year-old Richard II.
  17. The Wars of the Roses
    Between 1455 and 1487 the houses of Lancaster and York fought for the English crown.
  18. The rise of the Tudor dynasty
    Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485 with a weak hereditary claim.
