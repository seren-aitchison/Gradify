Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who led the Gunpowder Plot?

Robert Catesby; Guy Fawkes was arrested guarding the gunpowder. From “James I and the Union of the Crowns” →

In plain English: In 1603 James VI of Scotland became James I of England, ruling two kingdoms, and Ireland, as one monarch. Key idea: James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, uniting the crowns but not the kingdoms; Parliament rejected fuller union in 1607, and the Gunpowder Plot of 1605 hardened anti-Catholic feeling.

What did Laud order to be done with communion tables?

Moved to the east end of churches and railed off as altars. From “Religious tensions under the early Stuarts” →

In plain English: Religion divided Stuart England: Puritans wanted further reform, Catholics were feared, and Charles I backed a new style of churchmanship under Archbishop Laud. Key idea: Puritans sought reform, Catholics were feared after 1605, and Charles I's promotion of Arminianism and Laud, archbishop from 1633, alarmed many Protestants, making religion central to the conflict.

When was Strafford executed?

On 12 May 1641, after an act of attainder. From “The Long Parliament” →

In plain English: The Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, dismantled the Personal Rule within a year, but by 1642 the country had divided. Key idea: The Long Parliament executed Strafford and abolished the instruments of the Personal Rule in 1641, but the Irish rebellion, the Grand Remonstrance and the attempt on the five members divided the nation, and war began in August 1642.

Who commanded the New Model Army?

Sir Thomas Fairfax, with Oliver Cromwell commanding the cavalry. From “Key campaigns and battles of the Civil War” →

In plain English: The First Civil War lasted from 1642 to 1646 and ended in victory for Parliament. Key idea: The First Civil War moved from indecisive Edgehill (1642) to Marston Moor (1644), where the Scottish alliance won the north, and Naseby (1645), where the New Model Army won the war; Charles surrendered in 1646.

What was the Instrument of Government?

Britain's first written constitution, of December 1653, creating the Protectorate. From “The Protectorate” →

In plain English: From 1653 to 1659 England was governed by a Lord Protector under a written constitution. Key idea: The Protectorate (1653 to 1659) ruled under the Instrument of Government and the Humble Petition and Advice; the Major-Generals (1655 to 1657) were unpopular, and after Cromwell's death in 1658 the regime collapsed, leading to Monck's intervention in 1660.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Stuart Britain and the Civil War

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which title did James proclaim for himself in 1604? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    King of Great Britain. Parliament never made the title law, so it remained a proclamation.

  2. Which book by James I set out the theory of divine right in 1598? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The True Law of Free Monarchies. James wrote it in 1598, while he was still King of Scotland only.

  3. Which Parliament of 1614 passed no laws and granted no money? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Addled Parliament. It was dissolved after a few weeks with nothing achieved.

  4. Why did the English Parliament reject full union in 1607? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It feared Scottish influence and changes to English law. MPs defended English law and interests against Scottish influence.

  5. Why did lawyers believe in an ancient constitution? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They held that the king ruled through common law and with Parliament. The idea limited the king through law and consent.

  6. Why did Charles I raise a Forced Loan in 1626? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Parliament would not grant enough money for his wars. War costs drove him to raise money without Parliament.

  7. Which judgement about James I is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A capable king whose difficulties were partly inherited. Recent work, such as Croft's, has revised older criticism of James.

  8. Which judgement about ideas of kingship is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ideas mattered most when attached to disputes over money and religion. Ideas and practical disputes worked together.

  9. Which judgement about the Petition of Right is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Important as a principle, but weak as a practical restraint at the time. Its importance as a statement of principle grew in later crises.

Key takeaways

  • James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, uniting the crowns but not the kingdoms; Parliament rejected fuller union in 1607, and the Gunpowder Plot of 1605 hardened anti-Catholic feeling.
  • James I's theory of divine right said kings were answerable only to God, while lawyers such as Coke defended an ancient constitution of law and Parliament; historians debate how far these ideas really clashed before 1640.
  • Inflation and war costs left the Stuart crown dependent on Parliament; impositions, the failed Great Contract, the Forced Loan and the Petition of Right of 1628 marked disputes that ended with the dissolution of 1629.
  • Puritans sought reform, Catholics were feared after 1605, and Charles I's promotion of Arminianism and Laud, archbishop from 1633, alarmed many Protestants, making religion central to the conflict.
  • Charles I, king from 1625, was devout, inflexible and loyal to Buckingham; failed wars at Cadiz and Île de Ré, financial disputes and religious fears led him to dissolve Parliament in 1629.
  • From 1629 to 1640 Charles ruled without Parliament, raising money through revived rights and Ship Money, governing through Thorough and the prerogative courts; historians debate whether this was tyranny or effective rule.

Common mistakes in Stuart Britain and the Civil War

  • Thinking the Union of the Crowns in 1603 created one United Kingdom. The parliaments of England and Scotland were not united until 1707.
  • Thinking divine right meant James claimed he could ignore the law whenever he liked. He usually said a good king would rule by law.
  • Thinking the Petition of Right stopped Charles I. He ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640.
  • Thinking Laud was a Catholic. He was a Protestant churchman, but his reforms looked Catholic to his critics.
  • Blaming everything on Charles's personality. He inherited a weak financial system and deep religious divisions.
  • Thinking it was illegal for Charles to rule without Parliament. Kings could legally choose when to summon it.

Lessons in this school

  1. James I and the Union of the Crowns
    In 1603 James VI of Scotland became James I of England, ruling two kingdoms, and Ireland, as one monarch.
  2. The divine right of kings
    The early Stuarts believed that kings were appointed by God and answerable only to Him.
  3. Parliament and taxation
    The crown could not pay its way without Parliament, and arguments over money ran through the reigns of James I and Charles I.
  4. Religious tensions under the early Stuarts
    Religion divided Stuart England: Puritans wanted further reform, Catholics were feared, and Charles I backed a new style of churchmanship under Archbishop Laud.
  5. Charles I
    Charles I became king in 1625 and within four years had quarrelled so badly with Parliament that he ruled without it.
  6. The Personal Rule
    From 1629 to 1640 Charles I ruled without Parliament, raising money through old royal rights and reforming the Church through Laud.
  7. The Bishops' Wars
    Charles I's attempt to impose a new Prayer Book on Scotland in 1637 provoked a national rebellion and two wars.
  8. The Long Parliament
    The Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, dismantled the Personal Rule within a year, but by 1642 the country had divided.
  9. Causes of the English Civil War
    Why did England go to war with itself in 1642?
  10. Royalists and Parliamentarians
    In 1642 people had to choose between king and Parliament, or try to stay neutral.
  11. Key campaigns and battles of the Civil War
    The First Civil War lasted from 1642 to 1646 and ended in victory for Parliament.
  12. The trial and execution of Charles I
    On 30 January 1649 Charles I was executed outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, after a trial by a court created by a purged House of Commons.
  13. The Commonwealth
    From 1649 to 1653 England was a republic, governed by the Rump Parliament and a Council of State.
  14. Oliver Cromwell
    Oliver Cromwell rose from a Huntingdon gentleman to Lord Protector of England, Scotland and Ireland.
  15. The Protectorate
    From 1653 to 1659 England was governed by a Lord Protector under a written constitution.
  16. The Restoration
    In May 1660 Charles II returned to England, and the monarchy, the House of Lords and the Church of England were restored.
  17. The Glorious Revolution
    In 1688 the Catholic James II was driven from the throne and replaced by his Protestant daughter Mary and her husband William of Orange.
  18. The Bill of Rights
    The Bill of Rights of 1689 set limits on royal power that still form part of the British constitution.

Stuart Britain and the Civil War: questions students ask

What did James I say about kings in his 1610 speech to Parliament?

That kings "are justly called gods".

What were impositions?

Extra customs duties raised by the crown without Parliament's consent.

What was agreed at the Hampton Court Conference of 1604?

A new translation of the Bible, published in 1611, but few other reforms.

Who was Charles I's favourite in the 1620s?

George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham, assassinated in 1628.
