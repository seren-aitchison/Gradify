Lessons in this school

  1. James I and the Union of the Crowns
    In 1603 James VI of Scotland became James I of England, ruling two kingdoms, and Ireland, as one monarch.
  2. The divine right of kings
    The early Stuarts believed that kings were appointed by God and answerable only to Him.
  3. Parliament and taxation
    The crown could not pay its way without Parliament, and arguments over money ran through the reigns of James I and Charles I.
  4. Religious tensions under the early Stuarts
    Religion divided Stuart England: Puritans wanted further reform, Catholics were feared, and Charles I backed a new style of churchmanship under Archbishop Laud.
  5. Charles I
    Charles I became king in 1625 and within four years had quarrelled so badly with Parliament that he ruled without it.
  6. The Personal Rule
    From 1629 to 1640 Charles I ruled without Parliament, raising money through old royal rights and reforming the Church through Laud.
  7. The Bishops' Wars
    Charles I's attempt to impose a new Prayer Book on Scotland in 1637 provoked a national rebellion and two wars.
  8. The Long Parliament
    The Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, dismantled the Personal Rule within a year, but by 1642 the country had divided.
  9. Causes of the English Civil War
    Why did England go to war with itself in 1642?
  10. Royalists and Parliamentarians
    In 1642 people had to choose between king and Parliament, or try to stay neutral.
  11. Key campaigns and battles of the Civil War
    The First Civil War lasted from 1642 to 1646 and ended in victory for Parliament.
  12. The trial and execution of Charles I
    On 30 January 1649 Charles I was executed outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, after a trial by a court created by a purged House of Commons.
  13. The Commonwealth
    From 1649 to 1653 England was a republic, governed by the Rump Parliament and a Council of State.
  14. Oliver Cromwell
    Oliver Cromwell rose from a Huntingdon gentleman to Lord Protector of England, Scotland and Ireland.
  15. The Protectorate
    From 1653 to 1659 England was governed by a Lord Protector under a written constitution.
  16. The Restoration
    In May 1660 Charles II returned to England, and the monarchy, the House of Lords and the Church of England were restored.
  17. The Glorious Revolution
    In 1688 the Catholic James II was driven from the throne and replaced by his Protestant daughter Mary and her husband William of Orange.
  18. The Bill of Rights
    The Bill of Rights of 1689 set limits on royal power that still form part of the British constitution.

Key terms

Union of the Crowns
One monarch ruling England and Scotland from 1603, while the kingdoms stayed separate
Divine right
The belief that monarchs are appointed by God and answerable only to Him
Subsidy
A tax granted by Parliament to the crown
Arminianism
A theology rejecting predestination and stressing ceremony and the sacraments
Favourite
A courtier who enjoyed the monarch's special trust and patronage
Ship Money
A levy for the navy, extended by Charles I to the whole country from 1635
National Covenant
The 1638 Scottish pledge to defend the Scottish Church against Charles's reforms
Act of attainder
A law declaring a person guilty of a crime without a trial in the courts
Revisionism
The approach, from the 1970s, that rejected long-term explanations of the Civil War
Royalist
A supporter of the king in the Civil War
Solemn League and Covenant
The 1643 alliance between Parliament and the Scots
Regicide
The killing of a king, or a person who took part in it
Commonwealth
England's republic from 1649 to 1653
Lord Protector
The title of the head of state under the Protectorate, from 1653
