Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What were the four crops in the Norfolk rotation?

Wheat, turnips, barley and clover. From “Agricultural change” →

In plain English: Between about 1700 and 1850 English farming produced far more food with a smaller share of the workforce. Key idea: Enclosure, the Norfolk rotation, new tools and selective breeding raised English farm output between 1700 and 1850, feeding a growing population and releasing labour for industry; historians debate how revolutionary the change was.

Where did most of Britain's raw cotton come from by the early nineteenth century?

The plantations of the American South, worked by enslaved people. From “The textile industry” →

In plain English: Cotton was the leading industry of the Industrial Revolution, transformed by a series of inventions and supplied with raw cotton grown by enslaved people in the Americas. Key idea: The flying shuttle, spinning jenny, water frame, mule and power loom transformed cotton, which concentrated in Lancashire and became Britain's largest export, supplied by enslaved labour in the American South and resisted by the Luddites.

What did the 1851 census show about where people lived?

For the first time, more than half of Britain's people lived in towns. From “Urbanisation” →

In plain English: In 1851 Britain became the first country in which more people lived in towns than in the countryside. Key idea: Britain's towns grew rapidly with industry and migration, and by 1851 more than half the population lived in towns; growth outpaced housing, water, drainage and local government.

What was the Great Stink?

The smell of sewage in the Thames in the summer of 1858, which forced Parliament to fund new sewers. From “Public health” →

In plain English: Cholera, typhus and typhoid killed thousands in Britain's industrial towns. Key idea: Cholera and other diseases killed thousands in industrial towns; Chadwick's report (1842) led to the weak Public Health Act of 1848, Snow linked cholera to water in 1854, and the Great Stink of 1858 and the Act of 1875 brought effective action.

List three of the six points of the People's Charter.

Votes for all men over 21, the secret ballot, no property qualification for MPs, payment of MPs, equal constituencies and annual parliaments. From “Chartism” →

In plain English: Chartism was the largest working-class political movement of nineteenth-century Britain, demanding the vote for all men. Key idea: The People's Charter of 1838 demanded six points, including votes for all men and the secret ballot; petitions in 1839, 1842 and 1848 were rejected and the movement was divided, but five of its six points were later achieved.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Industrial Revolution

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who used selective breeding to raise heavier sheep and cattle from the 1760s? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Robert Bakewell. Bakewell's New Leicester sheep became famous across Britain.

  2. Who introduced vaccination against smallpox in 1796? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Edward Jenner. Vaccination reduced deaths from one major killer.

  3. Which machine did Arkwright patent in 1769? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The water frame. The water frame needed water power, and so a mill.

  4. Why did clover matter in the Norfolk rotation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It restored nitrogen to the soil, so land did not need to lie fallow. Clover kept the soil fertile while producing animal feed.

  5. Why did Wrigley and Schofield stress rising fertility? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Parish registers showed people married younger and had more children. Their reconstruction of parish records showed earlier marriage.

  6. Why did employers bring workers into factories? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Large, costly machines and the need to control quality and output. Machines and supervision favoured one central workplace.

  7. Which judgement about the agricultural revolution is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A long, gradual transformation that accelerated after 1750. Historians stress gradual change with acceleration after 1750.

  8. Which judgement about population and industrialisation is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They reinforced each other rather than one simply causing the other. Population growth and industrial change each encouraged the other.

  9. Which judgement about the factory system by 1850 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The symbol of the age, but for most workers change came more slowly. The factory as symbol and the typical worker's experience differed.

Key takeaways

  • Enclosure, the Norfolk rotation, new tools and selective breeding raised English farm output between 1700 and 1850, feeding a growing population and releasing labour for industry; historians debate how revolutionary the change was.
  • Britain's population rose from about 10.5 million in 1801 to about 21 million by 1851, driven mainly by rising fertility as people married younger, and it both supplied and was encouraged by industrialisation.
  • Factories, pioneered by Arkwright's Cromford Mill (1771), replaced the domestic system in textiles by bringing machines, power and workers under one discipline, though most workers in 1851 still worked outside factories.
  • The flying shuttle, spinning jenny, water frame, mule and power loom transformed cotton, which concentrated in Lancashire and became Britain's largest export, supplied by enslaved labour in the American South and resisted by the Luddites.
  • Newcomen's engine (1712) pumped mines, Watt's separate condenser (1769) and rotary motion (1781) made steam efficient and able to drive machinery; steam spread gradually, with its biggest effect on productivity after 1830.
  • Darby's coke smelting (1709) and Cort's puddling and rolling (1784) made iron plentiful, coal supplied energy that did not depend on land, and canals such as the Bridgewater (1761) cut the cost of carrying them.

Common mistakes in The Industrial Revolution

  • Crediting a few famous innovators with transforming farming. Change depended on thousands of farmers and spread slowly.
  • Assuming population grew mainly because people lived longer. Wrigley and Schofield found that rising fertility mattered more.
  • Thinking most British workers worked in factories by 1850. Agriculture, domestic service and small workshops still employed more people.
  • Thinking the Luddites simply hated machines. They protested against low wages and the loss of skilled work.
  • Thinking Watt invented the steam engine. He greatly improved Newcomen's engine of 1712.
  • Thinking coal mining became safe after the Davy lamp. Explosions and accidents continued to kill many miners.

Lessons in this school

  1. Agricultural change
    Between about 1700 and 1850 English farming produced far more food with a smaller share of the workforce.
  2. Population growth
    England's population grew faster in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries than ever before.
  3. The factory system
    The factory brought workers, machines and power together under one roof and one discipline.
  4. The textile industry
    Cotton was the leading industry of the Industrial Revolution, transformed by a series of inventions and supplied with raw cotton grown by enslaved people in the Americas.
  5. Steam power
    The steam engine freed industry from dependence on water and muscle.
  6. Coal and iron
    Coal and iron were the foundations of industrial Britain, supplying energy and the material for machines, bridges and rails.
  7. Railways
    Railways transformed travel, trade and everyday life in Britain from the 1830s.
  8. Urbanisation
    In 1851 Britain became the first country in which more people lived in towns than in the countryside.
  9. Industrial working conditions
    Industrial work meant long hours, harsh discipline and danger, but also regular wages.
  10. Child labour
    Children worked in mills, mines and workshops throughout the Industrial Revolution, and campaigns to protect them produced Britain's first effective factory laws.
  11. Public health
    Cholera, typhus and typhoid killed thousands in Britain's industrial towns.
  12. Housing and sanitation
    Working families in industrial towns crowded into back-to-back houses, courts and cellars, with shared privies and little clean water.
  13. Industrial capitalism
    The Industrial Revolution was driven by private investment, markets and the search for profit.
  14. Trade unions
    Workers in industrial Britain organised trade unions to protect wages and conditions, despite laws against them.
  15. Chartism
    Chartism was the largest working-class political movement of nineteenth-century Britain, demanding the vote for all men.
  16. The Great Reform Act
    The Reform Act of 1832 changed who could vote and which places sent MPs to Parliament, after a political crisis that some feared would end in revolution.
  17. Early Victorian social reform
    In the 1830s and 1840s Parliament passed a wave of reforms on poverty, work, town government, trade and health.
  18. The changing British economy
    By 1851 Britain was the "workshop of the world", but by the 1880s rivals were catching up.
