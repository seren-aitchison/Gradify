Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What happened at Canossa in 1077?

Emperor Henry IV stood in the snow to seek Pope Gregory VII's forgiveness. From “Medieval Christianity” →

In plain English: In medieval Western Europe the Catholic Church shaped belief, learning, law and daily life, and the papacy claimed authority over kings. Key idea: The medieval Church taught salvation through the sacraments and shaped daily life; popes fought emperors in the Investiture Controversy (Canossa 1077), Innocent III wielded great power, and the Avignon papacy and Great Schism (1378 to 1417) weakened papal authority.

What began the Thirty Years' War in 1618?

The Defenestration of Prague by Protestant Bohemian nobles. From “Religious conflict in Europe” →

In plain English: The divisions of the Reformation led to more than a century of religious wars in Europe, from the French Wars of Religion to the Thirty Years' War. Key idea: The French Wars of Religion (1562 to 1598), with the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572), the Dutch Revolt (from 1568) and the Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) combined religion and politics; the Peace of Westphalia (1648) settled religion by territory.

What is Mill's harm principle?

That the state may restrict liberty only to prevent harm to others. From “The development of political thought” →

In plain English: Ideas about power, authority and liberty developed dramatically between the Renaissance and the nineteenth century. Key idea: Machiavelli (1513) and Hobbes (1651) stressed power and order, Locke (1689) and Mill (1859) consent and liberty, and Burke (1790) founded conservatism against Paine's radicalism; each responded to crises and shaped later politics.

What did the Forster Education Act of 1870 do?

Set up school boards to provide elementary schools where none existed. From “Education and literacy” →

In plain English: Literacy in Britain rose from a skill of the clergy and elite to near-universal by 1900. Key idea: Printing, the Reformation and Sunday schools spread literacy before the state acted; the Acts of 1870, 1880, 1891, 1902 and 1944 created universal state education, and historians measure past literacy mainly through signatures, which underestimate reading.

What did Thompson mean by saying class "happens"?

That class forms when people come to feel and express shared interests against others. From “Social class and identity” →

In plain English: People have understood their place in society through ideas of rank, class, gender, race, religion and region. Key idea: Ideas of social order moved from estates and "sorts" to class in the nineteenth century; Thompson (1963) saw class as something people make through shared experience, while later historians added gender, race and other identities.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Religion, Culture and Society

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which pope called the First Crusade in 1095? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Urban II. He preached the crusade at the Council of Clermont.

  2. Who founded the Jesuits in 1540? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ignatius of Loyola. The Jesuits built schools and sent missionaries worldwide.

  3. When did the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre begin? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    24 August 1572. Thousands of Huguenots were killed in Paris and other cities.

  4. Why did belief in purgatory shape medieval religious practice? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    People paid for prayers and masses to shorten the suffering of the dead. Concern for the dead drove gifts to the Church.

  5. Why did printing help the Reformation spread? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pamphlets and Bibles reached large numbers of readers quickly. Print carried reformers' ideas faster than censors could act.

  6. Why did Catholic France fight the Catholic Habsburgs? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It feared Habsburg power encircling France. Political interest outweighed shared religion.

  7. Which judgement about papal power is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It peaked around 1200 and then declined, while the Church's hold on belief remained strong. Power and belief followed different paths.

  8. Which judgement about the Catholic Reformation is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It halted and partly reversed Protestant expansion, but could not reunite the Church. Its gains were real but limited to parts of Europe.

  9. Which judgement about the wars of religion is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Religion and politics were inseparable; religion gave intensity, politics shaped alliances. Both religion and politics drove the wars.

Key takeaways

  • The medieval Church taught salvation through the sacraments and shaped daily life; popes fought emperors in the Investiture Controversy (Canossa 1077), Innocent III wielded great power, and the Avignon papacy and Great Schism (1378 to 1417) weakened papal authority.
  • Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (1517) against indulgences led to a doctrine of faith alone; print and princes spread Protestantism, Calvin shaped Reformed churches, and the Council of Trent (1545 to 1563) and the Jesuits led a Catholic revival.
  • The French Wars of Religion (1562 to 1598), with the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572), the Dutch Revolt (from 1568) and the Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) combined religion and politics; the Peace of Westphalia (1648) settled religion by territory.
  • Enlightenment thinkers such as Kant, Voltaire, Montesquieu and Beccaria championed reason, tolerance and reform; their ideas spread through print and salons and influenced enlightened absolutists, but often excluded women and enslaved people.
  • Copernicus (1543), Kepler, Galileo and Newton's Principia (1687) transformed astronomy and physics, Vesalius (1543) and Harvey (1628) the study of the body, and Bacon and the Royal Society (1660) promoted experiment; historians debate the term "Scientific Revolution".
  • Machiavelli (1513) and Hobbes (1651) stressed power and order, Locke (1689) and Mill (1859) consent and liberty, and Burke (1790) founded conservatism against Paine's radicalism; each responded to crises and shaped later politics.

Common mistakes in Religion, Culture and Society

  • Thinking the medieval Church was unchanging and unchallenged. It was reformed repeatedly and criticised by figures such as Wycliffe and Hus.
  • Thinking Luther meant to split the Church in 1517. He sought reform; division followed from conflict over authority.
  • Treating the wars of religion as purely religious. Catholic France fought the Catholic Habsburgs for political reasons.
  • Treating the Enlightenment as a single agreed programme. Its thinkers disagreed about religion, politics and human nature.
  • Assuming early scientists rejected religion. Many, including Newton, were deeply religious.
  • Reading political thinkers outside their context. Each wrote in response to the crises of their own time.

Lessons in this school

  1. Medieval Christianity
    In medieval Western Europe the Catholic Church shaped belief, learning, law and daily life, and the papacy claimed authority over kings.
  2. The European Reformation
    In 1517 Martin Luther's protest against indulgences began a movement that split Western Christianity.
  3. Religious conflict in Europe
    The divisions of the Reformation led to more than a century of religious wars in Europe, from the French Wars of Religion to the Thirty Years' War.
  4. The Enlightenment
    In the eighteenth century the thinkers of the Enlightenment argued that reason, rather than tradition or religious authority, should guide human affairs.
  5. Scientific change
    Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries European thinkers transformed understanding of the heavens, the body and nature.
  6. The development of political thought
    Ideas about power, authority and liberty developed dramatically between the Renaissance and the nineteenth century.
  7. The growth of secularism
    Over the last two centuries religion has lost much of its public role in Europe.
  8. Women's changing roles
    Women's legal rights, work and public roles changed profoundly between the early modern period and the late twentieth century.
  9. Education and literacy
    Literacy in Britain rose from a skill of the clergy and elite to near-universal by 1900.
  10. The development of mass media
    Newspapers, radio, cinema, television and the internet have each transformed how people receive news, entertainment and propaganda.
  11. Art and cultural movements
    Art and culture reflect and shape the societies that produce them.
  12. Social class and identity
    People have understood their place in society through ideas of rank, class, gender, race, religion and region.
  13. Historical memory
    Societies remember the past in ways that differ from what historians write.
  14. Commemoration and contested heritage
    Memorials, statues and museums express what a society chooses to honour, and they have become fiercely contested.
