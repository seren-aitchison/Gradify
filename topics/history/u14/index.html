Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What was the millet system?

A system allowing religious communities to run many of their own affairs. From “The Ottoman Empire” →

In plain English: The Ottoman Empire lasted from around 1300 until 1922 and at its height ruled south-eastern Europe, the Middle East and North Africa. Key idea: The Ottoman Empire expanded from around 1300, took Constantinople in 1453 and peaked under Suleiman; it governed through the millet system and devshirme, reformed through the Tanzimat, and ended after the First World War, with Turkey founded in 1923.

What did the Treaty of Kanagawa do?

Opened Japanese ports to American ships, in 1854. From “The rise of modern Japan” →

In plain English: In the half-century after 1853 Japan transformed itself from an isolated feudal state into an industrial and imperial power. Key idea: Perry's arrival (1853) and the Treaty of Kanagawa (1854) opened Japan; the Meiji Restoration (1868) abolished feudal domains, built a national army, industry and a constitution (1889), and victories over China (1895) and Russia (1905) made Japan an imperial power.

Which was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery?

Brazil, on 13 May 1888. From “Abolition and emancipation” →

In plain English: Between the 1780s and 1888 the Atlantic slave trade and then slavery itself were abolished across the Americas. Key idea: The British campaign from 1787 used petitions, boycotts and Parliament to abolish the trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833; slavery ended across the Americas by 1888, and historians debate the weight of campaigning, economics and resistance.

What did Frantz Fanon argue in The Wretched of the Earth?

That violence could liberate the colonised. From “Anti-colonial resistance” →

In plain English: In the twentieth century colonised peoples built mass movements that brought down European empires. Key idea: Anti-colonial movements drew on nationalism, Pan-Africanism and socialism; methods ranged from Gandhi's satyagraha and Nkrumah's mass politics to armed struggle in Kenya, Algeria and Vietnam, shaped by settlers and colonial intransigence.

What was the Long March?

The Communists' retreat of 1934 to 1935 to Yan'an, during which Mao became leader. From “The Chinese Revolution” →

In plain English: Between 1911 and 1949 China moved from the fall of its last dynasty through warlordism, civil war and Japanese invasion to communist victory. Key idea: After 1911 China fragmented under warlords; the GMD under Chiang turned on the CCP in 1927, the Long March (1934 to 1935) made Mao leader, war with Japan from 1937 weakened the Nationalists, and Mao proclaimed the People's Republic on 1 October 1949.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Global History and Empires

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which sultan captured Constantinople in 1453? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Mehmed II. Constantinople became the Ottoman capital, Istanbul.

  2. Which Persian ruler sacked Delhi in 1739? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nadir Shah. The sack showed how weak Mughal power had become.

  3. When did the Manchus take Beijing and found the Qing dynasty? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1644. The Qing ruled China until the last emperor abdicated in 1912.

  4. Why do recent historians question the idea of Ottoman decline? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The empire adapted and reformed and survived for centuries. Its long survival suggests adaptation rather than simple decline.

  5. Why did Akbar include Hindu Rajput nobles in government? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To win the loyalty of powerful Hindu rulers in a diverse empire. Inclusion strengthened his rule over a Hindu majority.

  6. Why did the Boxer Rebellion humiliate the dynasty? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It was crushed by foreign armies that occupied Beijing. Foreign intervention showed the dynasty's weakness.

  7. Which judgement about Ottoman decline is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It lost relative power to Europe, but "decline" understates its adaptation. Relative weakness and resilience both appear in the evidence.

  8. Which explanation of Mughal weakening is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Overstretch and the rise of regional powers, not simply religious policy. Overstretch, succession disputes and rivals combined after 1707.

  9. Which explanation of the Qing fall is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    External humiliation and internal crisis together destroyed its claim to rule. Pressures from inside and outside combined.

Key takeaways

  • The Ottoman Empire expanded from around 1300, took Constantinople in 1453 and peaked under Suleiman; it governed through the millet system and devshirme, reformed through the Tanzimat, and ended after the First World War, with Turkey founded in 1923.
  • Babur founded the Mughal Empire at Panipat in 1526; Akbar governed through tolerance and the mansabdari system; Aurangzeb expanded it but strained it; after 1707 regional powers rose and the empire fragmented.
  • The Qing (1644 to 1912) reached a peak in the eighteenth century, but the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, defeat by Japan and the Boxer Rebellion weakened it, and the revolution of 1911 ended the dynasty in 1912.
  • Perry's arrival (1853) and the Treaty of Kanagawa (1854) opened Japan; the Meiji Restoration (1868) abolished feudal domains, built a national army, industry and a constitution (1889), and victories over China (1895) and Russia (1905) made Japan an imperial power.
  • Portugal and Spain led European expansion from 1492, conquering the Aztec and Inca empires; disease and forced labour devastated Indigenous peoples, and the Columbian exchange transformed the world's crops, animals and economy.
  • About 12.5 million Africans were embarked on the Atlantic slave trade and about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage; enslaved people worked on plantations and resisted in many ways, and historians combine ship data and first-hand accounts to study the trade.

Common mistakes in Global History and Empires

  • Assuming the Ottoman Empire was in decline for three centuries. Recent historians stress its adaptation and reform.
  • Blaming the Mughal decline on Aurangzeb's religious policies alone. Overstretch, succession disputes and regional rivals mattered at least as much.
  • Thinking China was weak and isolated throughout Qing rule. In the eighteenth century it was one of the world's largest and most prosperous states.
  • Thinking Japan simply copied the West. It adapted Western models selectively, keeping imperial ideology and its own traditions.
  • Explaining conquest only by European weapons. Disease and Indigenous allies, such as Cortés's Tlaxcalan allies, were crucial.
  • Thinking enslaved people were passive victims. They resisted on ships and plantations through revolt, escape and keeping their cultures.

Lessons in this school

  1. The Ottoman Empire
    The Ottoman Empire lasted from around 1300 until 1922 and at its height ruled south-eastern Europe, the Middle East and North Africa.
  2. The Mughal Empire
    The Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent from the sixteenth to the early eighteenth century and was one of the richest states in the world.
  3. The Qing dynasty
    The Qing dynasty ruled China from 1644 to 1912, presiding over a golden age in the eighteenth century and a century of crisis afterwards.
  4. The rise of modern Japan
    In the half-century after 1853 Japan transformed itself from an isolated feudal state into an industrial and imperial power.
  5. European colonialism
    From the late fifteenth century European states built empires across the Americas, Africa and Asia.
  6. The Atlantic slave trade
    Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas.
  7. Abolition and emancipation
    Between the 1780s and 1888 the Atlantic slave trade and then slavery itself were abolished across the Americas.
  8. The Haitian Revolution
    Between 1791 and 1804 enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue overthrew slavery and founded Haiti, the first state created by formerly enslaved people.
  9. Latin American independence
    Between 1808 and 1826 most of Spain's and Portugal's American colonies became independent.
  10. The Scramble for Africa: African experiences
    The partition of Africa looks different when seen from African perspectives rather than European capitals.
  11. Anti-colonial resistance
    In the twentieth century colonised peoples built mass movements that brought down European empires.
  12. Indian independence
    India won independence from Britain on 15 August 1947 after decades of nationalist campaigning.
  13. Partition
    The independence of India and Pakistan in August 1947 was accompanied by partition, mass violence and one of the largest migrations in history.
  14. The Chinese Revolution
    Between 1911 and 1949 China moved from the fall of its last dynasty through warlordism, civil war and Japanese invasion to communist victory.
  15. Mao and the People's Republic of China
    Mao Zedong ruled China from 1949 until his death in 1976, launching campaigns that transformed the country and caused immense suffering.
  16. Post-colonial states
    Dozens of new states emerged from the European empires between 1945 and 1975.
  17. Globalisation and historical change
    Globalisation, the growing connection of the world's economies and societies, has transformed life since 1945.
