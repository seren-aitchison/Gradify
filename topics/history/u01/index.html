Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between the past and history?

The past is everything that happened; history is an account of it built from surviving evidence. From “What is history?” →

In plain English: History is the study of the human past through the evidence it left behind. Key idea: History is not the past itself but an evidence-based argument about it: historians select, interpret and explain, so their accounts can differ while still respecting the facts.

What does NOP stand for in source analysis?

Nature, origin and purpose. From “Source provenance” →

In plain English: Provenance is the origin of a source: who made it, when, where, why and for whom. Key idea: Provenance (nature, origin and purpose) explains why a source says what it says, and tells the historian what it can reliably be used for.

How does an interpretation differ from a source?

An interpretation is a later argued account of the past; a source is a trace from the past itself. From “Historical interpretations” →

In plain English: An interpretation is a historian's (or film-maker's, or museum's) considered view of the past. Key idea: Interpretations are argued accounts of the past; they differ because of evidence, questions, context and perspective, and are judged by how well they fit the evidence.

Why did Magna Carta's significance grow after 1215?

It was reissued, entered law in 1225 and was later used as a symbol of the rule of law and limits on royal power. From “Significance” →

In plain English: Not everything in the past is equally significant. Key idea: Significance is a reasoned judgement about importance, made with criteria, and it can change over time as an event is reinterpreted.

Which three years saw poll taxes before the Peasants' Revolt?

1377, 1379 and 1381. From “Essay construction” →

In plain English: A history essay answers a question with a sustained argument. Key idea: A strong essay sets out a line of argument at the start, sustains it through analytical paragraphs that weigh factors, and ends with a justified judgement.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Historical Thinking and Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes history as a discipline? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An evidence-based account of the past that answers questions. History is enquiry: questions answered with evidence. It is selective, not complete, and interpretations are judged by evidence.

  2. Which of these is a primary source for life in a Victorian workhouse? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An 1850s workhouse admission register. The register was produced at the time by the institution itself; the others are later interpretations.

  3. Which year was the Domesday survey carried out? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1086. William I ordered the survey at Christmas 1085 and it was carried out in 1086.

  4. Two historians use the same documents but reach different conclusions about why a war began. What is the most likely reason? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They weigh the evidence and its significance differently. Interpretation involves judging which evidence matters most, so careful historians can disagree without either being dishonest.

  5. A historian studies how the Second World War was remembered in 1960s Britain. How should she treat a history of the war published in 1965? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    As a primary source for 1960s memory of the war. For an enquiry into 1960s memory, a 1965 history is itself evidence from that time.

  6. When is a historian's book a primary source? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    When the question is about how history was written at that time. Whether a source is primary depends on the question being asked.

  7. Which is the strongest reply to the claim that history is "just facts and dates"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Facts matter, but history selects and explains them to answer questions, which involves argument. Facts constrain history, but explaining and judging significance requires argument; the reply that keeps both is strongest.

  8. Why is the Bayeux Tapestry valuable but problematic evidence for the Norman Conquest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is near-contemporary and detailed, but made after the events from the Norman side with a purpose. Its closeness to events gives value; its later making and Norman perspective mean it must be read critically.

  9. Why is the absence of London from Domesday Book not evidence that London was unimportant? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its absence reflects how the survey was compiled, not London's significance. Silence in a source must be explained; other evidence shows London was England's largest town.

Key takeaways

  • History is not the past itself but an evidence-based argument about it: historians select, interpret and explain, so their accounts can differ while still respecting the facts.
  • Primary sources come from the period studied; secondary sources interpret it later. Which one a source is depends on the question being asked.
  • Sources become evidence when used to answer a question; historians make inferences from them and strengthen claims by corroborating them with other evidence.
  • Provenance (nature, origin and purpose) explains why a source says what it says, and tells the historian what it can reliably be used for.
  • Reliability asks whether a source is accurate; usefulness asks what it can show for a particular question. Even an unreliable source can be highly useful.
  • All sources have a perspective; bias is when that perspective distorts. Historians detect both through language, selection and provenance, and test them by corroboration.

Common mistakes in Historical Thinking and Methods

  • Thinking that because historians disagree, any opinion about the past is as good as any other. Interpretations must be supported by evidence and can be judged by how well they account for it.
  • Assuming primary sources are always more reliable than secondary ones. A primary source can be mistaken, partial or propaganda; a good secondary source may weigh hundreds of primary sources.
  • Treating a single source as proof. One source supports an inference; a claim becomes secure when several independent sources point the same way.
  • Writing "the source is biased" and stopping. Provenance analysis should explain how the author's position shapes the content and what the source is still useful for.
  • Concluding that a biased source is useless. Bias is often what makes a source useful, because it reveals attitudes and intentions.
  • Treating "bias" as a reason to dismiss a source. Instead, explain the perspective and ask what it reveals about the author and their world.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is history?
    History is the study of the human past through the evidence it left behind.
  2. Primary and secondary sources
    Primary sources were produced at or close to the time being studied; secondary sources are later accounts that interpret the past.
  3. Historical evidence
    Sources become evidence when they are used to answer a question.
  4. Source provenance
    Provenance is the origin of a source: who made it, when, where, why and for whom.
  5. Reliability and usefulness
    Reliability asks whether a source's account can be trusted; usefulness asks what it can show for a particular enquiry.
  6. Bias and perspective
    Every source is written from a perspective.
  7. Historical interpretations
    An interpretation is a historian's (or film-maker's, or museum's) considered view of the past.
  8. Cause and consequence
    Explaining why things happened is central to history.
  9. Continuity and change
    Historians ask not only what changed but what stayed the same, how fast, how far and for whom.
  10. Significance
    Not everything in the past is equally significant.
  11. Chronology and periodisation
    Chronology is the ordering of events in time; periodisation divides the past into named periods.
  12. Historical argument
    A historical argument makes a claim and supports it with evidence and reasoning, while dealing with counter-arguments.
  13. Essay construction
    A history essay answers a question with a sustained argument.
  14. Evidence selection
    Good history uses the right evidence, not the most evidence.
  15. Evaluating historical claims
    Popular history is full of claims that sound plausible but don't survive examination.
  16. Comparing interpretations
    Comparing interpretations means identifying how two accounts differ, explaining why, and judging which is more convincing.
