Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What four steps make up a strong source analysis?

Content, provenance, context and a judgement about usefulness. From “Source analysis questions” →

In plain English: Source questions test whether you can use evidence from the past to answer a historical question. Key idea: Source questions require analysis of content, provenance and context to reach a judgement about usefulness for the specific question; the Kitchener image of September 1914 is useful evidence of recruitment methods, less so of men's motives.

How did the alliance system turn a local crisis into a general war?

Allies were drawn in one after another: Germany backed Austria-Hungary, Russia backed Serbia, and Germany's plans brought in France and Britain. From “Causation questions” →

In plain English: Causation questions ask why something happened. Key idea: Causation answers identify long-term, short-term and trigger causes, link them, and prioritise with criteria; the assassination of 28 June 1914 triggered a crisis that alliances, war plans and leaders' decisions turned into a general war by 4 August 1914.

Name two criteria for judging significance.

Effects at the time, long-term impact and what the event reveals. From “Significance questions” →

In plain English: Significance questions ask why an event, person or development mattered. Key idea: Significance answers use criteria, consider different groups and periods and reach a judgement; the Battle of Britain (July to October 1940) prevented invasion and kept Britain in the war, though the war was decided mainly elsewhere.

What is weighing in a history essay?

Comparing the importance of factors and explaining their relationships. From “Extended historical arguments” →

In plain English: A level essays reward a sustained argument that weighs factors and reaches a substantiated judgement. Key idea: An extended argument has a line of argument, analytical paragraphs, weighing and a substantiated judgement; on October 1917, Provisional Government failures and the Kornilov affair created the opportunity, and Bolshevik leadership and organisation seized it.

What is best-fit marking?

Placing an answer in the level whose description it matches most closely overall. From “Mark-scheme interpretation” →

In plain English: Understanding how examiners mark helps students write answers that meet the criteria. Key idea: History answers are marked by levels using best fit, assessing objectives set out in the Department for Education's subject content of 2014; higher levels move from description to explanation, analysis and substantiated judgement.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Historical Examination and Revision

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Who drew the image of Lord Kitchener used in 1914 recruitment? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Alfred Leete. It first appeared on the cover of London Opinion.

  2. Which book of 1961 presented British generals as callous incompetents? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Donkeys by Alan Clark. Clark focused on the losses of battles such as the Somme.

  3. When did Britain enter the First World War? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4 August 1914. Germany's invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war.

  4. Why should contextual knowledge be used in a source answer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It supports, challenges or explains what the source shows. Context helps judge what the source can show.

  5. Why do Clark's and Sheffield's views of Haig differ? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They wrote in different contexts, used different evidence and asked different questions. Context, evidence and questions shape interpretations.

  6. Why does the trigger alone not explain the war? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It explains the timing, but not why a local crisis became a general war. Other causes explain the scale of the conflict.

  7. Which judgement about the Kitchener image is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Useful evidence of recruitment methods, less useful as evidence of why men joined. Usefulness depends on the question asked.

  8. Which judgement about the "learning curve" interpretation is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Convincing about military improvement, less so as a defence of every decision. Evaluation weighs what it explains against what it overlooks.

  9. Which judgement about the causes of war in 1914 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Leaders made decisions within a system of alliances and war plans that made escalation likely. Decisions and structures together explain the war.

Key takeaways

  • Source questions require analysis of content, provenance and context to reach a judgement about usefulness for the specific question; the Kitchener image of September 1914 is useful evidence of recruitment methods, less so of men's motives.
  • Interpretation questions require identifying the view, explaining how it was formed, testing it against evidence and judging; on Haig, Clark's The Donkeys (1961) and the "learning curve" view differ because of context, evidence and questions.
  • Causation answers identify long-term, short-term and trigger causes, link them, and prioritise with criteria; the assassination of 28 June 1914 triggered a crisis that alliances, war plans and leaders' decisions turned into a general war by 4 August 1914.
  • Consequence answers distinguish short-term and long-term, intended and unintended effects; the Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) weakened Germany as intended, but its resentment helped destabilise Weimar, though the Depression mattered more to Hitler's rise.
  • Change and continuity answers analyse extent, pace and direction, identify turning points and recognise continuity; public health in Britain changed substantially between 1848 and 1875, but slowly and unevenly, with poor housing persisting.
  • Significance answers use criteria, consider different groups and periods and reach a judgement; the Battle of Britain (July to October 1940) prevented invasion and kept Britain in the war, though the war was decided mainly elsewhere.

Common mistakes in Historical Examination and Revision

  • Writing "the source is biased so it is not useful". A biased source is useful evidence of its maker's aims and methods.
  • Simply agreeing or disagreeing with an interpretation. Explain why it says what it does, then test it.
  • Listing causes in separate paragraphs without linking them. Show how causes interacted to produce the event.
  • Assuming the Treaty of Versailles alone caused the Second World War. Many later events, including the Depression, intervened.
  • Describing events in order without analysing change. Use extent, pace and direction to judge how much changed.
  • Writing only about what happened in an event. Significance answers explain why it mattered, using criteria.

Lessons in this school

  1. Source analysis questions
    Source questions test whether you can use evidence from the past to answer a historical question.
  2. Interpretation analysis questions
    Interpretation questions ask you to explain and evaluate how historians and others have presented the past.
  3. Causation questions
    Causation questions ask why something happened.
  4. Consequence questions
    Consequence questions ask what happened as a result of an event, and how important those results were.
  5. Change and continuity questions
    Change and continuity questions ask how far things changed over a period, and how quickly.
  6. Significance questions
    Significance questions ask why an event, person or development mattered.
  7. Comparison questions
    Comparison questions ask you to identify and explain similarities and differences between two events, people or periods.
  8. Evidence-based essays
    Examiners reward essays that support every point with precise, relevant evidence.
  9. Extended historical arguments
    A level essays reward a sustained argument that weighs factors and reaches a substantiated judgement.
  10. Planning examination answers
    A few minutes of planning in an exam produces a clearer, better-argued answer.
  11. Time management
    Good history answers are wasted if the exam time runs out.
  12. Mark-scheme interpretation
    Understanding how examiners mark helps students write answers that meet the criteria.
  13. GCSE practice examinations
    Practising full questions under exam conditions is the best preparation for GCSE History.
  14. A level practice examinations
    A level examinations test sustained argument, source evaluation and the analysis of interpretations.
