Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who did Henry VII marry in 1486, and why did it matter?

Elizabeth of York, uniting the houses of Lancaster and York. From “Henry VII and dynastic security” →

In plain English: Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485 with a weak claim and spent his reign making it secure. Key idea: Henry VII secured a weak claim won at Bosworth in 1485 through marriage to Elizabeth of York, defeating the pretenders Simnel and Warbeck, financial control of the nobility, peaceful diplomacy and dynastic marriages.

What did the Act of Supremacy of 1534 do?

It declared Henry Supreme Head of the Church of England. From “The English Reformation” →

In plain English: In the 1530s Henry VIII broke with the Roman Catholic Church and made himself head of the Church in England. Key idea: Henry VIII broke with Rome to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, through the Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) and the Act of Supremacy (1534), but his church stayed largely Catholic in doctrine, and historians debate how fast England became Protestant.

What did England lose in January 1558?

Calais, its last possession in France. From “Mary I and Catholic restoration” →

In plain English: Mary I, queen from 1553 to 1558, restored Catholicism and the Pope's authority, and burned around 280 Protestants. Key idea: Mary I defeated Lady Jane Grey in 1553, restored Catholicism and papal authority, married Philip of Spain, and burned around 280 Protestants; her reign ended in 1558 without an heir, and historians debate whether it failed.

What did the Treaty of Nonsuch of 1585 do?

Committed Elizabeth to send an army to help the Dutch rebels against Spain. From “The Spanish Armada” →

In plain English: In 1588 Philip II of Spain sent a great fleet, the Armada, to escort an army to invade England. Key idea: Religion, the Netherlands, privateering and Mary Queen of Scots' execution caused war; the Armada of 1588 was scattered by fireships off Calais, damaged at Gravelines and wrecked by storms.

Who were the gentry?

Landowners below the nobility, who grew in number and wealth in the sixteenth century. From “Tudor culture and society” →

In plain English: Tudor society was a strict hierarchy, but the sixteenth century also saw a growing gentry, rising literacy and a flowering of drama. Key idea: Tudor society was a hierarchy with a growing gentry; grammar schools, print and new theatres such as the Globe (1599) produced a cultural flowering, though the golden age image hides the hardship of the 1590s.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Tudor England

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. At which battle was Lambert Simnel's rising defeated in 1487? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Stoke. The Battle of Stoke, 16 June 1487, ended the Simnel rising.

  2. Who was Henry VIII's chief minister from about 1532 to 1540? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Thomas Cromwell. Cromwell steered through the break with Rome.

  3. Which law of 1533 stopped appeals to Rome? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Act in Restraint of Appeals. It let English courts decide Henry's marriage.

  4. Why did Henry VII use bonds and recognisances? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To guarantee the nobles' loyalty with the threat of heavy payments. Financial pressure kept powerful nobles obedient.

  5. Why was the Amicable Grant of 1525 abandoned? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It met widespread resistance from those asked to pay. Resistance forced Henry and Wolsey to back down.

  6. Why did Pope Clement VII refuse the annulment? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He was under the influence of Catherine's nephew, Emperor Charles V. Charles V's power in Italy constrained the Pope.

  7. Which judgement about Tudor security in 1509 is best supported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Real but fragile: a peaceful succession, but with one male heir. The evidence supports security with lasting weaknesses.

  8. How far should Elton's view of Cromwell's reforms be accepted? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Cromwell made lasting changes, but "revolution" overstates their speed. Historians accept change while questioning the word revolution.

  9. How quickly did the English Reformation change religion in England? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The political break was fast, but religious change in parishes took decades. Historians separate the political break from slow religious change.

Key takeaways

  • Henry VII secured a weak claim won at Bosworth in 1485 through marriage to Elizabeth of York, defeating the pretenders Simnel and Warbeck, financial control of the nobility, peaceful diplomacy and dynastic marriages.
  • Wolsey dominated government from 1515 until his failure to secure the annulment in 1529; Cromwell, chief minister from about 1532 to 1540, carried through the break with Rome, and historians debate Elton's claim of a revolution in government.
  • Henry VIII broke with Rome to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, through the Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) and the Act of Supremacy (1534), but his church stayed largely Catholic in doctrine, and historians debate how fast England became Protestant.
  • Cromwell's survey of 1535 prepared the dissolution of the smaller monasteries in 1536 and the larger ones by 1540, driven by royal finance, religious reform and the supremacy, and provoking the Pilgrimage of Grace in 1536.
  • Under Edward VI (1547–1553), protectors Somerset and Northumberland led a Protestant reformation, with Prayer Books in 1549 and 1552, which provoked the Prayer Book and Kett's Rebellions of 1549.
  • Mary I defeated Lady Jane Grey in 1553, restored Catholicism and papal authority, married Philip of Spain, and burned around 280 Protestants; her reign ended in 1558 without an heir, and historians debate whether it failed.

Common mistakes in Tudor England

  • Thinking Henry VII's throne was safe after Bosworth. He faced rebellions and pretenders for over a decade.
  • Thinking Henry VIII ran the daily business of government himself. His ministers did most of the work.
  • Thinking Henry VIII made England Protestant. He rejected the Pope's authority but kept much Catholic doctrine.
  • Thinking the dissolution made the crown permanently rich. Much of the land was sold quickly to pay for war.
  • Thinking Kett's Rebellion was mainly religious. Its main grievances were enclosure and local government.
  • Relying only on Foxe for Mary's reign. His Book of Martyrs was Protestant propaganda as well as a record.

Lessons in this school

  1. Henry VII and dynastic security
    Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485 with a weak claim and spent his reign making it secure.
  2. Henry VIII's government
    Henry VIII ruled from 1509 to 1547 through powerful ministers, above all Thomas Wolsey and Thomas Cromwell.
  3. The English Reformation
    In the 1530s Henry VIII broke with the Roman Catholic Church and made himself head of the Church in England.
  4. The dissolution of the monasteries
    Between 1536 and 1540 Henry VIII closed every monastery, nunnery and friary in England and Wales and took their wealth.
  5. Edward VI and Protestant reform
    Edward VI became king at nine in 1547, and in his six-year reign England's church became clearly Protestant.
  6. Mary I and Catholic restoration
    Mary I, queen from 1553 to 1558, restored Catholicism and the Pope's authority, and burned around 280 Protestants.
  7. Elizabeth I and the religious settlement
    Elizabeth I's religious settlement of 1559 created a Protestant Church of England that tried to include as many people as possible.
  8. Elizabethan government
    Elizabeth I ruled from 1558 to 1603 through a small Privy Council, Parliament and unpaid local officials.
  9. The Spanish Armada
    In 1588 Philip II of Spain sent a great fleet, the Armada, to escort an army to invade England.
  10. Elizabethan exploration
    Elizabethan sailors crossed the Atlantic, raided Spanish America and sailed around the world.
  11. Poverty and social policy
    Poverty grew in the sixteenth century as population and prices rose.
  12. Tudor culture and society
    Tudor society was a strict hierarchy, but the sixteenth century also saw a growing gentry, rising literacy and a flowering of drama.
  13. Tudor foreign policy
    Tudor monarchs balanced the two great powers of France and Spain, fought Scotland and struggled to control Ireland.
  14. Succession and the end of the Tudor dynasty
    The Tudors were repeatedly troubled by the question of who would succeed.

Tudor England: questions students ask

Why did Wolsey fall in 1529?

He failed to persuade the Pope to annul Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon.

Why did Henry VIII want an annulment?

His marriage to Catherine of Aragon had produced no surviving son.

What was the Valor Ecclesiasticus?

The 1535 survey of church wealth used to prepare the dissolution.

What did the 1549 Prayer Book replace?

The Latin mass, with a service in English.
