Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is hedging in academic writing?

Using cautious language to match the strength of evidence. From “Academic writing style” →

In plain English: Academic writing at postgraduate level is precise, evidence-based and clear. Key idea: Academic style is clear, precise, appropriately hedged, balanced and formal; avoid long sentences, vague words, overclaiming and unexplained jargon, and follow disciplinary conventions on voice.

What does the given-new principle mean?

Placing known information before new information in sentences. From “Paragraphs and signposting” →

In plain English: Well-structured paragraphs and signposting guide readers through complex arguments. Key idea: Coherent paragraphs have a topic sentence, development and a linking sentence, with one main idea; signposting at thesis, chapter and paragraph levels and cohesive links guide readers.

What is the difference between methodology and methods?

Methodology is the overall approach and its justification; methods are the specific techniques. From “Writing the methodology chapter” →

In plain English: The methodology chapter explains and justifies how the research was done. Key idea: The methodology chapter explains the overall approach and specific methods, covering paradigm, design, sampling, data collection, analysis, ethics, rigour, limitations and reflexivity, with justification for choices and enough detail to evaluate the study.

Why do examiners often read the conclusion early?

To judge the thesis's overall argument and contribution. From “Writing the conclusion” →

In plain English: The conclusion draws the thesis together and leaves a lasting impression. Key idea: The conclusion restates aims, answers research questions, summarises contribution, gives recommendations and future research and ends clearly; it is concise, confident, free of new evidence and linked to the introduction.

What is synthesis in academic writing?

Combining and comparing sources to build an argument. From “Using sources and avoiding plagiarism” →

In plain English: Integrating sources well shows critical engagement and avoids plagiarism. Key idea: Use quotations sparingly, paraphrase in your own words and structure, and synthesise sources by theme; avoid plagiarism, including patchwriting and undisclosed AI use, through careful notes and following institutional policies.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Academic Writing and the Thesis

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which sentence shows appropriate hedging? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The findings suggest a link between sleep and memory. Hedging reflects the real strength of the evidence.

  2. Which habit tends to increase writing productivity? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Writing in short, regular scheduled sessions. Regular sessions build momentum and steady progress.

  3. Which is a component of an academic argument? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reasoning that links evidence to claims. Reasoning shows why evidence supports the claim.

  4. Which change most improves clarity? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Splitting a long sentence into two shorter ones. Shorter sentences with one idea each are easier to follow.

  5. What is a benefit of joining a writing group? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It provides accountability and support. Shared commitment helps writers keep going.

  6. What does a topic sentence do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    States the main claim of a paragraph. Topic sentences guide readers through each step.

  7. A draft says "This proves the theory is correct in all cases". What is the main problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It overclaims beyond what evidence can show. Single studies rarely prove theories in all cases.

  8. A student cannot start writing because she wants every sentence to be perfect. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Write a rough draft first and edit later. Separating drafting from editing reduces perfectionism.

  9. Why should a thesis address counter-arguments? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It shows awareness of alternatives and strengthens the case. Responding to objections makes arguments more persuasive.

Key takeaways

  • Academic style is clear, precise, appropriately hedged, balanced and formal; avoid long sentences, vague words, overclaiming and unexplained jargon, and follow disciplinary conventions on voice.
  • Productive writing comes from short, regular sessions, scheduled time, specific goals, accountability and separating drafting from editing; free writing and rough drafts help overcome blocks and perfectionism.
  • An academic argument combines a central claim, supporting claims, evidence, reasoning and responses to counter-arguments, structured with topic sentences and signposting to create a red thread through the thesis.
  • Coherent paragraphs have a topic sentence, development and a linking sentence, with one main idea; signposting at thesis, chapter and paragraph levels and cohesive links guide readers.
  • The traditional thesis has introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion and conclusion; alternatives include thesis by publication, thematic and practice-based structures, and all must follow regulations and present a coherent contribution.
  • The introduction establishes context, identifies a gap, states aims, questions and contribution, sets scope and outlines chapters; Swales' CARS model moves from territory to niche to occupying the niche, and it should be revised at the end.

Common mistakes in Academic Writing and the Thesis

  • Confusing complex language with good writing. Clear, direct prose communicates expertise better.
  • Waiting for inspiration or large blocks of time. Regular short sessions are more productive.
  • Listing sources without making claims. Literature should support an argument, not replace it.
  • Writing very long paragraphs with several ideas. Split them so each paragraph makes one point.
  • Assuming every thesis must use the same structure. Disciplines and projects vary, so choose a structure that suits the argument.
  • Writing an introduction that promises more than the thesis delivers. Revise it at the end to match the final argument.
