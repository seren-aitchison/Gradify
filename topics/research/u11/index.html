What is a preprint?

A version of a paper shared publicly before peer review. From “The academic publishing system” →

In plain English: Academic publishing shares research findings and builds careers. Key idea: Academic outputs include articles, books, conference papers and preprints; papers pass through submission, editor screening, peer review, decision and revision before publication, managed by publishers, editors and reviewers.

How many main contributions should one article usually make?

One. From “Writing a journal article” →

In plain English: A journal article is shorter and more focused than a thesis chapter. Key idea: Articles usually follow IMRaD: introduction, methods, results and discussion; adapt thesis work by focusing on one contribution, cutting background, shortening methods and following author and reporting guidelines.

What is DOAJ?

The Directory of Open Access Journals, which lists vetted open access journals. From “Predatory journals” →

In plain English: Predatory journals exploit researchers by charging fees without proper peer review. Key idea: Predatory journals charge fees without genuine peer review, often using spam, fast-acceptance promises, hidden fees, fake boards and false metrics; check venues with Think. Check. Submit., DOAJ, COPE and indexing.

What is a common poster size requirement?

A0 or A1. From “Academic posters” →

In plain English: Posters present research visually and invite conversation. Key idea: Posters communicate a key message quickly and invite conversation; use clear titles, logical flow, minimal text, large visuals, white space and QR codes, and stand by the poster with a short summary.

What does the REF inform?

The allocation of quality-related (QR) research funding. From “The Research Excellence Framework” →

In plain English: The Research Excellence Framework assesses the quality of research in UK universities. Key idea: The REF assesses UK university research to inform QR funding and accountability; REF 2021 assessed outputs, impact and environment through expert panels, and REF 2029 increases attention to research culture.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Publishing, Presenting and Impact

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which output is shared before peer review? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A preprint. Preprints are posted before formal review.

  2. Which is the most important factor in choosing a journal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Whether the paper fits the journal's scope and audience. Fit with scope and audience is essential.

  3. Which section of IMRaD interprets findings? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Discussion. The discussion explains what findings mean and why they matter.

  4. What usually happens after a paper is submitted to a journal? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The editor screens it for scope and quality. Editors decide whether to send papers for review.

  5. Which tool helps check whether a journal is legitimate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Think. Check. Submit.. The tool offers checklists for assessing journals.

  6. How should a thesis chapter be adapted into an article? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Focus on one contribution and cut background. Articles need a tighter focus on one contribution and much less background.

  7. Why has the academic publishing system been criticised? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    High costs, slow timelines and incentives favouring positive results. Critics highlight cost, speed and publication bias.

  8. A paper is rejected by a first-choice journal. What is a good next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Revise using any feedback and submit to the next journal on the list. A ranked plan allows quick resubmission after revisions.

  9. Why should authors follow a journal's author guidelines? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Papers that ignore them may be returned or rejected. Editors expect manuscripts to meet requirements.

Key takeaways

  • Academic outputs include articles, books, conference papers and preprints; papers pass through submission, editor screening, peer review, decision and revision before publication, managed by publishers, editors and reviewers.
  • Choose journals by scope, audience, quality, open access, speed and cost; assess them by reading recent issues, checking indexing and legitimacy, and plan a ranked list of options.
  • Articles usually follow IMRaD: introduction, methods, results and discussion; adapt thesis work by focusing on one contribution, cutting background, shortening methods and following author and reporting guidelines.
  • Peer review uses experts to assess quality, validity, originality and significance; it may be single-anonymous, double-anonymous, open or post-publication, and good reviews are constructive, specific, confidential and conflict-free.
  • Interpret decisions carefully, list every reviewer comment, plan changes with co-authors and write a polite response letter that quotes each comment, explains changes with locations and respectfully justifies any disagreement.
  • Predatory journals charge fees without genuine peer review, often using spam, fast-acceptance promises, hidden fees, fake boards and false metrics; check venues with Think. Check. Submit., DOAJ, COPE and indexing.

Common mistakes in Publishing, Presenting and Impact

  • Expecting publication to be quick. Review and revision often take many months.
  • Choosing a journal on prestige alone. A poor fit leads to desk rejection and wasted time.
  • Submitting a thesis chapter almost unchanged. Articles need a tighter focus and different framing.
  • Writing a harsh, vague review. Constructive, specific comments help authors improve their work.
  • Ignoring reviewer comments you disagree with. Explain your reasoning respectfully instead.
  • Trusting a journal because it claims an impact factor. Predatory journals often make false claims.

Publishing, Presenting and Impact: questions students ask

What is desk rejection?

Rejection by the editor without sending the paper for peer review.

What does IMRaD stand for?

Introduction, methods, results and discussion.

What is double-anonymous review?

Review in which neither authors nor reviewers know each other's identities.

What should a response letter include?

A reply to each comment, explaining changes and where they are.
