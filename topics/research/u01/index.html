Key terms and formulas at a glance

Research
A process of investigation leading to new insights, effectively shared (REF definition)
Original contribution to knowledge
New knowledge, method, evidence or synthesis that extends a discipline, required of a doctorate
Interdisciplinary research
Research that integrates concepts, methods or theories from two or more disciplines
UKRI
UK Research and Innovation: the body, created by the Higher Education and Research Act 2017, that houses the seven research councils, Innovate UK and Research England
MPhil
A research Master's examined by thesis and viva; often the initial registration for doctoral students
Professional doctorate
A level 8 doctorate combining taught elements with research in or about professional practice, such as the EdD or DBA
Doctoral Training Partnership
A UKRI council's block award to a university or consortium to recruit and train doctoral students across a broad area
Upgrade (transfer, confirmation)
The formal review at which a probationary or MPhil-registered student is assessed for continuation to PhD
Vitae RDF
The Researcher Development Framework: four domains, twelve sub-domains and 63 descriptors of effective researchers' knowledge, behaviours and attributes
Principal supervisor
The supervisor with lead responsibility for a research student's academic guidance and progress
Supervision record
A written note of a supervision meeting's decisions and actions, shared by the student
Code of practice
A university's document setting out how research degree supervision, progression and support work, and each party's responsibilities

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How does the REF define research?

A process of investigation leading to new insights, effectively shared. From “What is research?” →

In plain English: Research is systematic, original investigation that produces new knowledge and shares it so others can check and use it. Key idea: Research is distinguished by method, novelty and sharing: a systematic investigation whose result was not known in advance and is reported so that others can check it.

Which council funds social science doctoral training?

The Economic and Social Research Council (ESRC), part of UKRI. From “The UK research system and its funders” →

In plain English: UK university research is funded through a "dual support" system: block funding allocated by research assessment, and competitive project grants from UK Research and Innovation, charities, government and industry. Key idea: As a postgraduate researcher you are part of this system: your studentship, your supervisor's grants, your department's REF submission and your funder's terms all shape what you can do and what is expected of you.

What is a CDT?

A Centre for Doctoral Training: a focused, cohort-based doctoral programme, common in EPSRC areas. From “Doctoral training: DTPs, CDTs and studentships” →

In plain English: Much UK doctoral study is funded through studentships, many awarded by UKRI's councils through Doctoral Training Partnerships and Centres for Doctoral Training. Key idea: A studentship is a contract: in return for funding you accept the funder's terms, which typically cover full-time study, training, submission expectations, open access to outputs and acknowledging the funder.

What should you send after each supervision meeting?

A short written record of decisions and action points with dates. From “Managing your supervisor” →

In plain English: "Managing your supervisor" means taking responsibility for making supervision work: preparing meetings, asking for the feedback you need and keeping a record. Key idea: A written record of supervision meetings protects both student and supervisor and is often required by the university's code of practice; it is also invaluable when writing up.

What four things does a risk register record for each risk?

Likelihood, impact, mitigation and an owner or trigger. From “Research project management” →

In plain English: A research degree is a long, uncertain project with a fixed deadline, so project management skills matter as much as subject knowledge. Key idea: Managing a research project means controlling scope and time while keeping enough flexibility for the unexpected; the thesis is the deliverable, and every work package should lead to part of it.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Research Landscape and Research Degrees

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which element is part of the REF definition of research? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The new insights must be effectively shared. The REF definition has three parts: investigation, new insights and effective sharing. Funding source, method type and setting are not part of it.

  2. At what FHEQ level is a Master's degree in England? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Level 7. Master's degrees are level 7; bachelor's degrees with honours are level 6 and doctorates level 8.

  3. In Becher and Trowler's terms, what does a "hard" discipline have? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Relatively high consensus on theory and method. Hard and soft describe the degree of paradigm consensus, not difficulty or size.

  4. A team studies how a protein folds, hoping the knowledge will eventually help design drugs. Which category fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Use-inspired basic research (Pasteur's quadrant). The work seeks fundamental understanding but is motivated by a possible use, which is exactly Stokes's Pasteur's quadrant.

  5. A doctoral student applies an established theory of organisational learning to NHS emergency departments, where it has not been tested. What kind of originality is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    New application of known theory to a new setting. Using known theory in a new context is one of Phillips and Pugh's recognised forms of originality.

  6. Researchers in health, economics and computer science combine clinical data, cost models and machine learning into one model of hospital demand. Which term fits? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Interdisciplinary. The methods are integrated into a single new model, which is the defining feature of interdisciplinary work. Transdisciplinary would require co-production with non-academic partners.

  7. A Master's student compiles every published study on remote working into an annotated list, without a stated search method or synthesis. A supervisor says it is "not yet research". Which reason is strongest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It gathers existing knowledge without a systematic, reproducible method or a new synthesis. Secondary work can be research (systematic reviews are), but only when a stated method produces a new insight. Primary data are not required, and topic or examination status is irrelevant.

  8. Which contribution statement would an examiner find easiest to defend? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    "The first evidence, from a three-year cohort, on how placement years affect graduate employment in engineering". A specific, bounded statement names the new evidence and design and can be checked against the literature; sweeping or vague claims cannot be defended.

  9. An interdisciplinary PhD student worries about the viva. Which step most reduces the risk of an unfair examination? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Agree early which disciplinary conventions the thesis follows and seek examiners who understand both fields. Examiners judge against conventions; agreeing them and choosing suitably experienced examiners addresses the main risk. The other options increase it.

Key takeaways

  • Research is distinguished by method, novelty and sharing: a systematic investigation whose result was not known in advance and is reported so that others can check it.
  • Originality is usually incremental: a doctorate typically adds a clearly defined piece to existing knowledge, not a revolution. Examiners look for a contribution you can state in a sentence and defend.
  • Learning your discipline's conventions (its key journals, accepted methods, citation style and thesis format) is part of research training; examiners judge your work against them.
  • As a postgraduate researcher you are part of this system: your studentship, your supervisor's grants, your department's REF submission and your funder's terms all shape what you can do and what is expected of you.
  • The difference between the degrees is the scale and independence of the research and the originality required; word limits and durations vary, so always check your own university's regulations.
  • Every UK doctorate, whatever its route, must meet the FHEQ level 8 descriptor; what differs is the structure and the setting of the contribution.

Common mistakes in The Research Landscape and Research Degrees

  • Treating "finding information" as research. Collecting what is already known, however thoroughly, is study; research must add something that wasn't known, by a method others could follow.
  • Equating originality with size. A narrow, well-defined contribution that is clearly new and rigorously established is safer than a broad claim that cannot be supported.
  • Assuming interdisciplinary means "using two literatures". Integration is the test: show how concepts or methods from each field change the answer.
  • Thinking universities' research is funded mainly by tuition fees. Research funding comes chiefly from public grants, charities and industry, although universities do cross-subsidise research from other income, which has become a growing concern.
  • Assuming all PhDs are three years. Funded UK studentships are often three and a half to four years, many students need longer, and part-time doctorates commonly take six to seven years. Regulations set maximum registration periods.
  • Assuming a thesis by papers is quicker. Journal review times are unpredictable, papers repeat background material, and the integrative chapters must still argue for a single coherent contribution.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is research?
    Research is systematic, original investigation that produces new knowledge and shares it so others can check and use it.
  2. An original contribution to knowledge
    A doctorate must make an original contribution to knowledge; a Master's dissertation usually needs to show advanced understanding and some independent analysis.
  3. Disciplines and interdisciplinarity
    Every researcher works within a discipline's questions, methods and standards of evidence.
  4. The UK research system and its funders
    UK university research is funded through a "dual support" system: block funding allocated by research assessment, and competitive project grants from UK Research and Innovation, charities, government and industry.
  5. Research degrees: Master's, MPhil and PhD
    UK postgraduate study includes taught Master's degrees with a dissertation, research Master's (MRes, MPhil) and doctorates.
  6. Professional doctorates and the PhD by publication
    Not every doctorate is a traditional PhD by thesis.
  7. Doctoral training: DTPs, CDTs and studentships
    Much UK doctoral study is funded through studentships, many awarded by UKRI's councils through Doctoral Training Partnerships and Centres for Doctoral Training.
  8. Progression: upgrade and confirmation
    Doctoral students are formally reviewed during their degree, most importantly at the upgrade (also called transfer or confirmation), when an independent panel decides whether the project is on track for a PhD.
  9. Researcher development and the Vitae RDF
    The Vitae Researcher Development Framework (RDF) describes the knowledge, behaviours and attributes of effective researchers in four domains.
  10. The supervisory relationship
    The relationship with your supervisors is the strongest single influence on a research degree experience.
  11. Managing your supervisor
    "Managing your supervisor" means taking responsibility for making supervision work: preparing meetings, asking for the feedback you need and keeping a record.
  12. Regulations, responsibilities and expectations
    Every research degree is governed by the awarding university's regulations and code of practice, which set out registration periods, supervision, progression, examination and the responsibilities of s
  13. Research culture and community
    Research culture is the behaviours, values, expectations and norms of research communities, and it shapes how research is done and who thrives.
  14. Research project management
    A research degree is a long, uncertain project with a fixed deadline, so project management skills matter as much as subject knowledge.
  15. Writing a research proposal
    A research proposal persuades a reader (an admissions panel, a funder or an upgrade panel) that a question matters, can be answered and can be answered by you in the time available.
  16. Planning time: milestones and Gantt charts
    A research degree has a fixed end date and many dependent tasks.
  17. Wellbeing and resilience in research
    Research degrees can be intellectually rewarding and personally demanding.

The Research Landscape and Research Degrees: questions students ask

At which FHEQ level are doctorates?

Level 8 (Master's degrees are level 7).

What distinguishes interdisciplinary from multidisciplinary research?

Integration: interdisciplinary work combines concepts or methods to reach a new understanding; multidisciplinary work places disciplines side by side.

What is a "1+3" studentship?

A funded route with a one-year research Master's (often an MRes) followed by a three-year PhD.

What links the papers in a PhD by publication?

A substantial critical commentary or integrative chapters explaining their coherence and contribution.
