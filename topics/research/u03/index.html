Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is ontology?

The study of the nature of being and reality. From “Ontology” →

In plain English: Ontology is the study of what exists and what reality is like. Key idea: Ontology concerns the nature of reality; realist and objectivist positions see reality and social phenomena as existing independently of us, while relativist and constructionist positions see them as shaped by human understanding and interaction, and these assumptions shape research design.

Who named positivism?

Auguste Comte. From “Positivism” →

In plain English: Positivism holds that knowledge should be based on observable, measurable facts. Key idea: Positivism, named by Comte and developed by the logical positivists, assumes an objective, measurable reality, tests hypotheses through operationalised variables and seeks causal, generalisable, value-free findings, but is criticised for the unverifiable verification principle, theory-laden observation and neglect of meaning.

Who wrote The Social Construction of Reality?

Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, in 1966. From “Social constructionism” →

In plain English: Social constructionism holds that much of what we take as natural or obvious is created through social processes. Key idea: Social constructionism holds that knowledge and social categories are produced through interaction, language and culture and are historically and culturally specific; examples include childhood, gender and race, constructed categories can have real effects, and research often uses discourse and narrative analysis.

Where did critical theory develop?

In the Frankfurt School. From “Critical and transformative paradigms” →

In plain English: Some researchers see research as a way to challenge injustice. Key idea: Critical and transformative paradigms, from the Frankfurt School's critical theory to feminist, critical race, disability, decolonial and transformative approaches, aim to expose power and promote justice; participatory methods involve communities as co-researchers, though balancing advocacy and rigour and managing power are challenges.

What is the demarcation problem?

The problem of distinguishing science from non-science. From “Popper and falsification” →

In plain English: Karl Popper argued that science advances by trying to prove theories wrong. Key idea: Popper argued that theories cannot be verified but can be falsified, so science advances through bold conjectures and attempted refutations, with falsifiability marking science from non-science; critics point to the Duhem–Quine problem, scientists' retention of theories despite anomalies and Lakatos's research programmes.

Key takeaways

  • Ontology concerns the nature of reality; realist and objectivist positions see reality and social phenomena as existing independently of us, while relativist and constructionist positions see them as shaped by human understanding and interaction, and these assumptions shape research design.
  • Epistemology concerns what counts as knowledge and how it is obtained; objectivist, constructionist and subjectivist positions differ on where meaning lies, and Crotty links epistemology to theoretical perspective, methodology and methods, with coherence across these layers strengthening research.
  • A paradigm is a set of beliefs about ontology, epistemology and methodology; the main social research paradigms include positivism, post-positivism, interpretivism, critical and transformative paradigms, pragmatism and critical realism, and the paradigm wars over incommensurability have largely given way to acceptance of mixed methods.
  • Positivism, named by Comte and developed by the logical positivists, assumes an objective, measurable reality, tests hypotheses through operationalised variables and seeks causal, generalisable, value-free findings, but is criticised for the unverifiable verification principle, theory-laden observation and neglect of meaning.
  • Post-positivism holds that an independent reality exists but knowledge of it is fallible and probabilistic; drawing on Popper, it tests theories by seeking refutation, uses triangulation and critical scrutiny to pursue objectivity, and underpins much modern quantitative research.
  • Interpretivism holds that social research must understand the meanings people attach to their actions, drawing on Weber's Verstehen, phenomenology and symbolic interactionism; it uses in-depth qualitative methods judged by credibility and related criteria, offering rich understanding but facing concerns about generalisability, bias and power.

Common mistakes in Philosophy of Research

  • Thinking ontology is irrelevant to empirical research. Every study rests on assumptions about what exists.
  • Equating qualitative methods with constructionism and quantitative with objectivism. Methods can serve different epistemologies.
  • Treating paradigms as labels to attach to a thesis. They should genuinely shape and justify the design.
  • Using "positivist" to describe any quantitative study. Quantitative methods can be used within post-positivist and other paradigms.
  • Thinking post-positivists claim to prove theories. They accept that knowledge is provisional and probabilistic.
  • Assuming interpretivist research lacks rigour. It has its own quality criteria, such as credibility and dependability.

Lessons in this school

  1. Ontology
    Ontology is the study of what exists and what reality is like.
  2. Epistemology
    Epistemology is the study of knowledge: what counts as knowledge and how we can know things.
  3. Research paradigms
    A research paradigm is a shared set of beliefs about how research should be done.
  4. Positivism
    Positivism holds that knowledge should be based on observable, measurable facts.
  5. Post-positivism
    Post-positivism keeps the goal of studying an objective reality but accepts that knowledge is fallible and imperfect.
  6. Interpretivism
    Interpretivism holds that social research must understand the meanings people give to their actions.
  7. Social constructionism
    Social constructionism holds that much of what we take as natural or obvious is created through social processes.
  8. Critical realism
    Critical realism combines a realist ontology with the recognition that knowledge is fallible and socially produced.
  9. Pragmatism
    Pragmatism judges ideas by their practical consequences.
  10. Critical and transformative paradigms
    Some researchers see research as a way to challenge injustice.
  11. Deduction, induction and abduction
    Researchers reason from theory to data, from data to theory, or to the best explanation.
  12. The role of theory in research
    Theory helps researchers explain, predict and interpret.
  13. Popper and falsification
    Karl Popper argued that science advances by trying to prove theories wrong.
  14. Kuhn and paradigm shifts
    Thomas Kuhn argued that science develops through periods of normal science interrupted by revolutions.
  15. Reflexivity and positionality
    Researchers bring their identities, values and experiences to their work.
