Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three ways to narrow a broad topic.

Limit the population, the place or the time period (also the theory or method). From “Choosing a research topic” →

In plain English: A good research topic is interesting, significant and feasible. Key idea: Topics come from interests, the literature, supervisors and funders; narrowing by population, place, time, theory or method turns an area into a researchable topic, which should then be tested for interest, significance, originality, feasibility and fit.

What is a methodological gap?

A question that has been studied only with certain methods, leaving room for a different approach. From “Finding the gap: gap-spotting and problematisation” →

In plain English: A research contribution responds to something missing or questionable in existing knowledge. Key idea: Research gaps may be empirical, methodological, theoretical, contradictory or practical; gap-spotting fills missing pieces, while problematisation challenges assumptions behind existing work, and either must be justified by a thorough search and a clear account of significance.

Why does grey literature help reduce publication bias?

It includes studies, often with null results, that were never published in journals. From “Grey literature” →

In plain English: Much valuable evidence is not published in academic journals. Key idea: Grey literature, from government reports to preprints and theses, broadens the evidence base, reduces publication bias and adds timely material, but because it is often not peer-reviewed it must be appraised carefully for authority, method and purpose.

What three things does critical appraisal assess?

Validity, results and relevance. From “Critical appraisal tools” →

In plain English: Critical appraisal tools provide structured ways to judge the quality and risk of bias in studies. Key idea: Critical appraisal assesses validity, results and relevance using tools matched to study design, such as CASP, JBI, RoB 2, ROBINS-I and AMSTAR 2, ideally by two reviewers, with results reported transparently and used to weight findings.

Which reporting guideline applies to scoping reviews?

PRISMA-ScR. From “Scoping reviews” →

In plain English: Scoping reviews map the breadth of research on a topic. Key idea: Scoping reviews map the extent and nature of research on broad or emerging topics, following the Arksey and O'Malley stages from question to charting and reporting, usually without quality appraisal, and are reported with PRISMA-ScR.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research Questions and the Literature Review

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the best source for spotting open questions in a field? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Recent reviews and "future research" sections. Applying the standard result: Reviews map what is unresolved.

  2. What does the C in PICO stand for? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Comparison. PICO compares the intervention with an alternative.

  3. What does the null hypothesis usually state? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    There is no effect or difference. Applying the standard result: H0 predicts no effect.

  4. A student proposes "education in the UK" as a PhD topic. What is the main problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is far too broad to investigate in depth. Applying the standard result: Narrowing is needed.

  5. "How do refugees experience applying to UK universities?" is what type of question? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Exploratory. Applying the standard result: It explores experiences.

  6. Which is a well-written objective? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To compare completion rates of full-time and part-time students between 2015 and 2020. Applying the standard result: It is specific and measurable.

  7. A topic is interesting and significant but needs archives the student cannot access. Which criterion fails? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Feasibility. Applying the standard result: Access is a feasibility issue.

  8. Which question best meets the FINER criteria for a one-year master's project? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A focused question with accessible participants and ethical approval. Applying the standard result: Feasibility and ethics matter.

  9. An exploratory interview study of artists' experiences has no hypotheses. Is this a weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No, exploratory qualitative studies often use questions instead. Applying the standard result: Hypotheses suit deductive research.

Key takeaways

  • Topics come from interests, the literature, supervisors and funders; narrowing by population, place, time, theory or method turns an area into a researchable topic, which should then be tested for interest, significance, originality, feasibility and fit.
  • Good research questions are clear, focused, answerable and significant; their type, descriptive, explanatory, exploratory or evaluative, shapes the design, and frameworks such as FINER and PICO help refine them.
  • The aim states the overall purpose, objectives set out specific achievable steps, and hypotheses make testable predictions with null and alternative forms; all should align with the research questions.
  • Research gaps may be empirical, methodological, theoretical, contradictory or practical; gap-spotting fills missing pieces, while problematisation challenges assumptions behind existing work, and either must be justified by a thorough search and a clear account of significance.
  • Use several databases suited to the discipline, multidisciplinary and subject-specific, supplemented by citation chasing and alerts; Google Scholar is useful for quick searches and citations but not as a principal source for systematic searching.
  • Systematic searches break questions into concepts, combine synonyms with OR and concepts with AND, use truncation, phrases, proximity and field searching, add subject headings such as MeSH, and record every search for reproducibility.

Common mistakes in Research Questions and the Literature Review

  • Choosing a topic so broad that it cannot be investigated in depth. Narrow first; you can situate the work in the wider area later.
  • Writing questions that can be answered with yes or no without investigation. Questions should require evidence and analysis to answer.
  • Writing objectives that are just methods ("to conduct interviews"). Objectives should state what will be found out or achieved.
  • Claiming a gap because you could not find studies after a quick search. Gaps must be established by systematic searching.
  • Relying on Google Scholar alone for a literature review. Its searches are not reproducible and cannot be filtered precisely.
  • Combining all terms with AND. Synonyms for the same concept must be joined with OR, or relevant records will be missed.

Research Questions and the Literature Review: questions students ask

Why is supervisory fit part of choosing a topic?

A supervisor needs relevant expertise to guide the work well.

What is an evaluative research question?

One asking how well something, such as an intervention or policy, works.

What is the difference between an aim and an objective?

The aim is the overall purpose; objectives are the specific steps to achieve it.

What is forward citation chasing?

Finding later papers that cite a key paper.
