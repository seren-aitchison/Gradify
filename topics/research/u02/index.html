Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check.

Name three ways to narrow a broad topic.

Limit the population, the place or the time period (also the theory or method). From “Choosing a research topic” →

What is a methodological gap?

A question that has been studied only with certain methods, leaving room for a different approach. From “Finding the gap: gap-spotting and problematisation” →

Why does grey literature help reduce publication bias?

It includes studies, often with null results, that were never published in journals. From “Grey literature” →

What three things does critical appraisal assess?

Validity, results and relevance. From “Critical appraisal tools” →

Which reporting guideline applies to scoping reviews?

PRISMA-ScR. From “Scoping reviews” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Choosing a research topic
    A good research topic is interesting, significant and feasible.
  2. Formulating research questions
    Research questions define what a study is trying to find out.
  3. Aims, objectives and hypotheses
    Aims, objectives and hypotheses translate research questions into a plan.
  4. Finding the gap: gap-spotting and problematisation
    A research contribution responds to something missing or questionable in existing knowledge.
  5. Searching databases
    Academic databases are the main route to published research.
  6. Search strategies: Boolean operators and subject headings
    A systematic search strategy finds relevant literature efficiently and reproducibly.
  7. Grey literature
    Much valuable evidence is not published in academic journals.
  8. Reference management
    Postgraduate research involves hundreds of sources, so references must be managed systematically.
  9. Critical reading
    Postgraduate reading means questioning, not just summarising.
  10. Evaluating sources
    Not all sources are equally trustworthy.
  11. Critical appraisal tools
    Critical appraisal tools provide structured ways to judge the quality and risk of bias in studies.
  12. Types of literature review
    "Literature review" covers many different approaches.
  13. Systematic reviews and PRISMA
    Systematic reviews follow explicit, reproducible methods to answer a focused question.
  14. Scoping reviews
    Scoping reviews map the breadth of research on a topic.
  15. Synthesising the literature
    Synthesis means bringing studies together to say something new, not listing them one by one.
  16. Structuring a literature review
    A literature review needs a clear structure that leads the reader to your research questions.
  17. Theoretical and conceptual frameworks
    Frameworks give a study its lens and logic.

Key terms

Research area
A broad field of interest
Research question
The question a study is designed to answer
Aim
A broad statement of what the research intends to achieve
Gap-spotting
Justifying research by identifying something not yet studied
Citation database
A database indexing articles and the citations between them
Boolean operators
AND, OR and NOT used to combine search terms
Grey literature
Material produced outside commercial and academic publishing
Reference manager
Software for storing sources and generating citations
Critical reading
Questioning a text's claims, evidence and assumptions
Peer review
Expert evaluation of research before publication
Critical appraisal
Systematic assessment of a study's validity, results and relevance
Systematic review
A review using explicit, reproducible methods to answer a focused question
Protocol
A pre-specified plan for a review's methods
Scoping review
A review mapping the extent and nature of research on a topic
