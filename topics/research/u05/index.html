Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What distinguishes ratio from interval measurement?

Ratio measurement has a true zero. From “Variables and levels of measurement” →

In plain English: Quantitative research measures variables, and the level of measurement determines which statistics are appropriate. Key idea: Variables can be independent, dependent, confounding, mediating or moderating, and their level of measurement, nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio, determines which descriptive and inferential statistics are appropriate.

Why is mean substitution not recommended?

It reduces variability and distorts relationships between variables. From “Missing data” →

In plain English: Almost every dataset has missing values, and how they are handled can bias results. Key idea: Missing data may be MCAR, MAR or MNAR, and modern methods such as multiple imputation and maximum likelihood are generally preferred over deletion or mean substitution, with transparent reporting and sensitivity analysis.

What is the conventional significance level?

0.05. From “Null hypothesis significance testing” →

In plain English: Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is the most widely used framework for statistical inference. Key idea: NHST tests data against a null hypothesis, and the p-value is the probability of data at least as extreme as observed if H0 is true; it is not the probability that H0 is true, and should be reported with effect sizes and intervals.

What does the F ratio compare?

Between-groups variance with within-groups variance. From “Analysis of variance (ANOVA)” →

In plain English: ANOVA compares means across three or more groups or conditions in a single test. Key idea: ANOVA compares several means by dividing variance into between- and within-groups parts and testing their ratio, F; one-way, factorial, repeated-measures and mixed designs are common, and post hoc tests, planned contrasts and interaction plots explain significant results.

What does an odds ratio of 1 indicate?

No association between the predictor and the outcome. From “Logistic regression” →

In plain English: Logistic regression models binary outcomes such as pass or fail, using odds and odds ratios. Key idea: Logistic regression models the log odds of a binary outcome, coefficients are exponentiated into odds ratios showing multiplicative changes in odds, and models are assessed with likelihood ratio tests, pseudo R squared, calibration and discrimination.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Quantitative Methods and Statistics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which level of measurement has ordered categories but unknown gaps between them? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Ordinal. Ordinal variables can be ranked, but the distances between ranks are not known to be equal.

  2. Which is an example of a leading question? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Don't you agree that the library should open longer?. The wording signals the expected answer, which pushes respondents towards agreement.

  3. Why are multi-item scales preferred for latent constructs? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They reduce the influence of any single item's error. Combining items averages out item-specific error and allows reliability to be estimated.

  4. A study links coffee drinking with heart disease, but smokers drink more coffee. What is smoking here? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A confounding variable. Smoking relates to both coffee and heart disease and may create a misleading association.

  5. Age categories are "18-25, 25-35, 35-45". What is the main problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The categories overlap. A 25-year-old fits two categories, so options must be mutually exclusive.

  6. An item loads 0.62 on one factor and 0.58 on another. What is this called? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cross-loading. An item relating strongly to more than one factor is cross-loading and may be ambiguous.

  7. Which summary is most appropriate for degree classification (First, 2:1, 2:2, Third)? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The median classification. Degree classes are ordered but not equally spaced, so the median suits this ordinal variable.

  8. A student plans to measure anxiety with ten new self-written items. What would most improve the design? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Using a validated anxiety scale. Validated scales bring evidence of reliability and validity and allow comparison with other studies.

  9. A student's EFA suggests four factors, but one factor has only two items. What is the most reasonable judgement? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The factor may be unstable and needs more items. Factors with very few items are often unreliable, so more items or a simpler structure may be needed.

Key takeaways

  • Variables can be independent, dependent, confounding, mediating or moderating, and their level of measurement, nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio, determines which descriptive and inferential statistics are appropriate.
  • Questionnaire design operationalises concepts into clear closed or open questions, avoiding double-barrelled, leading and loaded wording, and uses sensible order and pretesting, ideally building on validated instruments.
  • Latent constructs are measured with multi-item scales developed through construct definition, item generation, expert review, item analysis and factor analysis, with reliability and validity tested in new samples.
  • Data screening checks ranges, errors, outliers, missing data and assumptions before analysis, and cleaning decisions should be justified, scripted and documented, keeping the raw data unchanged.
  • Missing data may be MCAR, MAR or MNAR, and modern methods such as multiple imputation and maximum likelihood are generally preferred over deletion or mean substitution, with transparent reporting and sensitivity analysis.
  • Descriptive statistics summarise central tendency, dispersion and shape, and the choice between mean and median, SD and IQR depends on the level of measurement and the distribution.

Common mistakes in Quantitative Methods and Statistics

  • Calculating a mean of nominal codes, such as "average discipline = 1.8". Numbers used as labels for categories have no arithmetic meaning.
  • Writing new questions when validated scales exist. Validated instruments allow comparison with other studies and come with evidence of reliability and validity.
  • Running EFA and CFA on the same sample. Confirming a structure on the data that generated it overstates fit; use separate samples.
  • Deleting outliers simply because they are inconvenient. Outliers should be investigated, and genuine values removed only with clear justification and sensitivity analysis.
  • Filling gaps with the variable's mean. Mean substitution artificially reduces variability and distorts relationships between variables.
  • Reporting only the mean for heavily skewed data. The median and IQR give a more representative summary when distributions are skewed.

Lessons in this school

  1. Variables and levels of measurement
    Quantitative research measures variables, and the level of measurement determines which statistics are appropriate.
  2. Questionnaire design
    A good questionnaire turns concepts into clear, unbiased questions that respondents can answer accurately.
  3. Measurement scales and psychometrics
    Psychometrics is the science of measuring psychological and social constructs.
  4. Data screening and cleaning
    Before analysis, data must be checked for errors, outliers and violations of assumptions.
  5. Missing data
    Almost every dataset has missing values, and how they are handled can bias results.
  6. Descriptive statistics
    Descriptive statistics summarise the main features of a dataset.
  7. Distributions and the normal curve
    Probability distributions describe how values of a variable are spread.
  8. Visualising data
    Good graphs reveal patterns, outliers and uncertainty that tables can hide.
  9. Sampling distributions and confidence intervals
    A statistic calculated from a sample varies from sample to sample, and its sampling distribution describes that variation.
  10. Null hypothesis significance testing
    Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is the most widely used framework for statistical inference.
  11. Effect sizes
    Effect sizes quantify how large an effect or relationship is, independent of sample size.
  12. Statistical power and sample size
    Underpowered studies are common and can mislead even when results are significant.
  13. Type I and Type II errors
    Every significance test risks two kinds of wrong decision.
  14. t-tests
    The t-test compares means between two groups or conditions.
  15. Analysis of variance (ANOVA)
    ANOVA compares means across three or more groups or conditions in a single test.
  16. Chi-square tests
    Chi-square tests analyse categorical data by comparing observed and expected frequencies.
  17. Correlation
    Correlation measures the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables.
  18. Linear regression
    Simple linear regression predicts an outcome from one predictor using a straight line.
  19. Multiple regression
    Multiple regression predicts an outcome from several predictors at once.
  20. Logistic regression
    Logistic regression models binary outcomes such as pass or fail, using odds and odds ratios.
  21. Non-parametric tests
    Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about distributions, often by analysing ranks.
  22. Bayesian statistics
    Bayesian statistics updates beliefs about parameters in the light of data using Bayes' theorem.
  23. Statistical software: SPSS, R and Stata
    Postgraduate researchers can choose among several statistical packages.
  24. Reporting statistics
    Clear, complete statistical reporting allows readers to judge and reproduce findings.
