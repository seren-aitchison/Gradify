Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is the researcher called the research instrument in qualitative research?

Because data are collected and interpreted through the researcher's own engagement and judgement. From “What is qualitative research?” →

In plain English: Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and social processes, usually through words, images and observation. Key idea: Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and processes in context using rich, interpretively analysed data, flexible designs and reflexive researchers, and suits "how" and "what is it like" questions.

What is an elaboration probe?

A prompt asking the participant to say more, such as "Could you tell me more about that?" From “Interviewing skills” →

In plain English: Good interview data depend on the interviewer's skills in listening, questioning and managing the encounter. Key idea: Skilled interviewers build rapport, listen actively, use open probes and silence, avoid leading questions and reflect on their influence, while protecting participants through clear rights, distress protocols and signposting.

Which body publishes ethical guidelines for internet research?

The Association of Internet Researchers. From “Online and remote qualitative research” →

In plain English: Qualitative data are increasingly collected online, through video calls, messaging and digital platforms. Key idea: Remote and online qualitative research widens access and opens new data sources, but requires secure platforms, attention to rapport and privacy, and careful ethical handling of online posts, including traceability of quotations.

Why do Braun and Clarke object to saying themes "emerged"?

It implies themes exist in the data waiting to be found rather than being actively generated. From “Reflexive thematic analysis” →

In plain English: Braun and Clarke have developed their approach into reflexive thematic analysis, which emphasises researcher subjectivity and interpretation. Key idea: Reflexive thematic analysis treats themes as actively generated through the researcher's subjective, theoretically informed engagement with data, differs from coding reliability and codebook approaches, and is judged by reflexivity, coherence and interpretive depth rather than coder agreement.

Why do narrative researchers keep stories intact?

Because sequence and structure create meaning. From “Narrative analysis” →

In plain English: Narrative analysis studies the stories people tell and how they tell them. Key idea: Narrative analysis interprets storied accounts as a whole, through thematic, structural or dialogic approaches, to understand how people make sense of experience and construct identities in particular contexts.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Qualitative Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which aim is most typical of qualitative research? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Understanding how people make sense of experiences. Qualitative research focuses on meanings and interpretation in context.

  2. Which qualitative approach focuses on the stories people tell about their lives? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Narrative research. Narrative research collects and analyses individual stories and how they are told.

  3. What is the main basis for choosing participants in purposive sampling? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The information they can provide. Purposive sampling seeks information-rich cases relevant to the research question.

  4. Which research question is best suited to a qualitative approach? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How do carers experience hospital discharge planning?. A question about experience calls for rich accounts rather than measurement.

  5. A researcher wants to explain how nurses move from novice to expert. Which design fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Grounded theory. Explaining how a process unfolds and producing a theory of it is the aim of grounded theory.

  6. A study deliberately includes nurses of different ages, specialties and regions to capture a range of views. Which strategy is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Maximum variation sampling. Selecting diverse participants to capture range is maximum variation sampling.

  7. A critic says a 15-interview study "cannot be generalised, so it is worthless". What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It offers conceptual insight and transferable understanding. Qualitative studies generalise to concepts and theory, and thick description lets readers judge transfer.

  8. A thesis claims to use phenomenology but reports themes produced by coding, with no attention to lived experience. What is the best advice? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Align the analysis with phenomenology or reframe the design. The named design and the analysis must cohere; otherwise a generic label is more accurate.

  9. A methodology chapter states "participants were purposively sampled" and nothing more. What should be added? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The strategy, criteria and rationale for selection. Readers need to know which purposive strategy was used, with what criteria and why.

Key takeaways

  • Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and processes in context using rich, interpretively analysed data, flexible designs and reflexive researchers, and suits "how" and "what is it like" questions.
  • Qualitative traditions such as narrative research, phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography and case study differ in focus and analytic product, and the design should match the question's focus and be followed consistently.
  • Purposive sampling selects information-rich cases using strategies chosen in advance, such as homogeneous, maximum variation or critical case sampling, while theoretical sampling selects cases during analysis to develop emerging categories in grounded theory.
  • Semi-structured interviews combine a guide of open questions with flexibility to follow participants, and need a well-designed, piloted guide with probes and careful planning of mode, setting, consent and recording.
  • Skilled interviewers build rapport, listen actively, use open probes and silence, avoid leading questions and reflect on their influence, while protecting participants through clear rights, distress protocols and signposting.
  • Focus groups use facilitated group interaction to explore collective views and norms, need careful planning of composition and topic guide and skilled moderation, and their analysis should attend to interaction as well as content.

Common mistakes in Qualitative Methods

  • Judging qualitative studies by whether their samples are statistically representative. Qualitative research aims for depth and conceptual insight, not population estimates.
  • Naming a tradition such as grounded theory without following its procedures. Using a label without its methods weakens coherence; a generic qualitative design may be more honest.
  • Describing any qualitative sample as "theoretical". Theoretical sampling requires sampling decisions driven by ongoing analysis; a sample fixed in advance is purposive.
  • Asking the research question directly as an interview question. Participants answer better when research questions are translated into concrete, experience-based questions.
  • Filling every pause with the next question. Tolerating silence gives participants time to reflect and often produces the richest data.
  • Treating a focus group as a quick way to interview several people at once. The value lies in group interaction, which should shape the design and analysis.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is qualitative research?
    Qualitative research studies meanings, experiences and social processes, usually through words, images and observation.
  2. Qualitative research designs
    Qualitative research includes several established methodological traditions, each with its own aims and procedures.
  3. Purposive and theoretical sampling
    Qualitative studies select participants and settings deliberately for the information they can provide.
  4. Semi-structured interviews
    Semi-structured interviews are the most widely used qualitative data collection method.
  5. Interviewing skills
    Good interview data depend on the interviewer's skills in listening, questioning and managing the encounter.
  6. Focus groups
    Focus groups gather data through facilitated group discussion.
  7. Participant observation
    Participant observation collects data by taking part in and watching everyday activities in a setting.
  8. Documents and visual methods
    Documents and images are rich sources of qualitative data.
  9. Online and remote qualitative research
    Qualitative data are increasingly collected online, through video calls, messaging and digital platforms.
  10. Transcription
    Transcription turns recorded talk into text for analysis and involves many interpretive choices.
  11. Coding qualitative data
    Coding is the process of labelling segments of qualitative data to organise and interpret them.
  12. Thematic analysis: Braun and Clarke
    Thematic analysis is a widely used method for identifying and interpreting patterns of meaning across qualitative data.
  13. Reflexive thematic analysis
    Braun and Clarke have developed their approach into reflexive thematic analysis, which emphasises researcher subjectivity and interpretation.
  14. Grounded theory
    Grounded theory develops theory from data through iterative collection and analysis.
  15. Interpretative phenomenological analysis
    Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) explores how individuals make sense of significant life experiences.
  16. Discourse analysis
    Discourse analysis studies how language constructs social reality, identities and power.
  17. Narrative analysis
    Narrative analysis studies the stories people tell and how they tell them.
  18. Framework analysis
    Framework analysis is a structured, matrix-based method widely used in applied policy and health research.
  19. Qualitative data analysis software
    Computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software (CAQDAS) helps researchers organise, code and retrieve qualitative data.
  20. Rigour in qualitative research
    Qualitative research must show that it is rigorous, credible and ethically sound.
  21. Writing up qualitative findings
    Writing is part of qualitative analysis, and findings must be presented in a way that is persuasive and true to the data.

What is the analytic product of phenomenology?

A description of the essence of a lived experience.

When are theoretical sampling decisions made?

During analysis, in response to emerging categories.

What is a probe in an interview?

A follow-up question seeking more detail or clarification.

What is the distinctive feature of focus groups according to Kitzinger?

The explicit use of group interaction to generate data.
