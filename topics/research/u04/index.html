Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How does a research design differ from a research method?

The design is the overall logical structure of the study; a method is a technique for collecting or analysing data. From “What is research design?” →

In plain English: Research design is the overall plan that connects a research question to the evidence needed to answer it. Key idea: Research design is the logical structure of a study, specifying units, comparisons, timing and selection so that the evidence can answer the question, and it is distinct from the methods used to collect data.

What is the purpose of random allocation in an experiment?

To balance known and unknown differences between groups so effects can be attributed to the manipulation. From “Experimental designs” →

In plain English: Experiments manipulate a variable and use comparison to test causal claims. Key idea: True experiments manipulate an independent variable and randomly allocate participants to conditions, which balances other differences and strengthens internal validity against threats such as history, maturation, testing, attrition and selection.

What can a longitudinal design establish that a cross-sectional design cannot?

The temporal order of variables and change within cases. From “Cross-sectional and longitudinal designs” →

In plain English: Cross-sectional designs collect data at one point in time, while longitudinal designs follow cases over time. Key idea: Cross-sectional designs give a snapshot of associations at one time, while longitudinal designs such as panel, cohort and repeated cross-sectional studies track change and temporal order, at the cost of attrition, conditioning and expense.

What are the four stages of the action research cycle?

Plan, act, observe and reflect. From “Action research” →

In plain English: Action research combines inquiry with deliberate action to improve practice, usually with participants as co-researchers. Key idea: Action research combines practical change with systematic inquiry through repeated cycles of planning, acting, observing and reflecting, often with participants as co-researchers, and is judged by its usefulness and process as well as its rigour.

What does information power propose?

The more relevant information a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed. From “Sample size and saturation” →

In plain English: Deciding how many participants or cases to include depends on the design. Key idea: Quantitative sample sizes are justified by power analysis using effect size, significance level and desired power, while qualitative sample sizes are justified by saturation or information power, with transparent reasoning rather than a bare number.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is a research design rather than a research method? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A longitudinal panel. Longitudinal is a structure for timing data collection; the other options are data collection tools.

  2. In Crotty's scheme, what does "methodology" refer to? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The strategy or plan of action behind the methods. Crotty distinguishes methodology, the strategy, from methods, the techniques used within it.

  3. Which approach typically uses purposive samples and rich textual data? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Qualitative. Qualitative research seeks depth of meaning from purposively chosen participants rather than statistical coverage.

  4. A researcher wants to compare how two hospitals implemented the same policy. Which design family fits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Comparative. Two contrasting cases studied with similar methods is the defining feature of a comparative design.

  5. Which pairing shows the clearest misalignment? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An interpretivist study claiming national statistical generalisation. Interpretivist aims seek situated meaning, so statistical generalisation claims do not fit.

  6. Focus groups are run first to generate items for a later national questionnaire. Which design is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Exploratory sequential. The qualitative phase comes first and develops the instrument used in the quantitative phase.

  7. A student plans a study but cannot say what comparison their data will allow. What is the main weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The design cannot support the claims intended. Without a planned comparison, evidence cannot discriminate between rival answers to the question.

  8. An examiner writes "the methods are described but not justified". What would best address this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Explaining why each method fits the question and stance. Justification links methods back to the question and assumptions, which description alone does not do.

  9. A thesis reports survey and interview findings in separate chapters that never refer to each other. What is the main critique? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The strands are not integrated. Without integration, the study is two parallel studies rather than mixed methods research.

Key takeaways

  • Research design is the logical structure of a study, specifying units, comparisons, timing and selection so that the evidence can answer the question, and it is distinct from the methods used to collect data.
  • A coherent study aligns question, philosophy, methodology, methods and quality criteria, and the methodology chapter should make that chain of justification explicit.
  • Quantitative approaches use numerical data to measure and test, qualitative approaches use rich data to understand meaning and context, and mixed methods integrates both through convergent, explanatory or exploratory designs.
  • True experiments manipulate an independent variable and randomly allocate participants to conditions, which balances other differences and strengthens internal validity against threats such as history, maturation, testing, attrition and selection.
  • Quasi-experiments estimate intervention effects without random allocation, using designs such as non-equivalent groups, interrupted time series, regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences, and must address selection bias and rival explanations.
  • Survey research uses standardised questions with a sample to describe populations or relationships, and its quality depends on the sampling frame, mode and question design, judged against coverage, sampling, non-response and measurement error.

Common mistakes in Research Design

  • Treating "I will use interviews" as a research design. Interviews are a method; the design states which cases, comparisons and timing make the interview data able to answer the question.
  • Describing methods without justifying them. Examiners expect to see why the methods fit the question and the philosophical stance, not just what was done.
  • Calling any study with a survey and a few interviews "mixed methods". Genuine mixed methods requires a rationale and planned integration of the strands.
  • Confusing random allocation with random sampling. Random allocation assigns participants to groups and supports internal validity; random sampling selects participants from a population and supports generalisation.
  • Treating a before-and-after comparison in one group as strong evidence of effect. Without a comparison group or long time series, history and maturation remain plausible explanations.
  • Assuming a large number of responses removes bias. A large but self-selected sample can still suffer serious coverage and non-response error.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is research design?
    Research design is the overall plan that connects a research question to the evidence needed to answer it.
  2. Aligning question, philosophy and method
    A coherent study aligns its research question, philosophical assumptions, methodology and methods.
  3. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed approaches
    Research approaches are often grouped as quantitative, qualitative or mixed.
  4. Experimental designs
    Experiments manipulate a variable and use comparison to test causal claims.
  5. Quasi-experimental designs
    Quasi-experiments test interventions without random allocation, often in real-world settings.
  6. Survey research design
    Surveys collect standardised data from a sample to describe a population or examine relationships.
  7. Cross-sectional and longitudinal designs
    Cross-sectional designs collect data at one point in time, while longitudinal designs follow cases over time.
  8. Case study research
    Case study research examines one or a small number of cases in depth within their real-life context.
  9. Ethnography
    Ethnography studies people's everyday lives and cultures through sustained immersion in a setting.
  10. Action research
    Action research combines inquiry with deliberate action to improve practice, usually with participants as co-researchers.
  11. Evaluation research
    Evaluation research judges the design, implementation and effects of programmes, policies and interventions.
  12. Sampling strategies
    Sampling is how researchers select cases from a wider population or setting.
  13. Sample size and saturation
    Deciding how many participants or cases to include depends on the design.
  14. Validity and reliability
    Validity and reliability are central criteria for judging the quality of research, especially quantitative research.
  15. Trustworthiness in qualitative research
    Qualitative researchers often use trustworthiness criteria rather than validity and reliability.
  16. Pilot studies
    Pilot and feasibility studies test whether a study can be carried out as planned before the main study.

Research Design: questions students ask

What are Crotty's four elements of research?

Epistemology, theoretical perspective, methodology and methods.

What distinguishes an exploratory sequential design?

A qualitative phase comes first and is used to develop an instrument or hypotheses for a quantitative phase.

What is the central threat in a quasi-experiment?

Selection bias, because groups may differ before the intervention.

What is a sampling frame?

The list or source from which a sample is drawn.
