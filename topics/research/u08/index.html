Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three principles of the Belmont Report?

Respect for persons, beneficence and justice. From “Principles of research ethics” →

In plain English: Research ethics protects participants, researchers and the trust society places in research. Key idea: Research ethics rests on respect for persons, beneficence and non-maleficence, justice and integrity, applied through UK frameworks such as the ESRC framework and disciplinary codes, using judgement throughout a project.

When might a researcher need to break confidentiality?

When a participant reveals a serious risk of harm to themselves or others. From “Confidentiality and anonymity” →

In plain English: Participants share information trusting it will be protected. Key idea: Anonymity means data cannot be linked to individuals, while confidentiality means identities are known but protected; pseudonyms, careful reporting and secure storage protect identities, and limits of confidentiality must be explained.

What is a lay member of an ethics committee?

A member who is not a researcher or health professional. From “Research ethics committees” →

In plain English: Research ethics committees review proposed studies independently before they begin. Key idea: Research ethics committees provide independent, prospective review; UK university and NHS committees include lay members, use proportionate review and may approve, set conditions, request information or reject, with amendments needed for changes.

Who published the Concordat to Support Research Integrity?

Universities UK. From “The Concordat to Support Research Integrity” →

In plain English: The Concordat to Support Research Integrity sets out the UK's national framework for good research conduct. Key idea: The Concordat to Support Research Integrity commits signatories to rigour, ethical and legal compliance, a culture of integrity, fair misconduct processes and open review, promoting honesty, rigour, transparency, care and accountability.

What is ghost authorship?

Failing to list someone who made a substantial contribution. From “Authorship and plagiarism” →

In plain English: Authorship gives credit and responsibility for research. Key idea: Authorship requires substantial contribution, drafting or revising, final approval and accountability; CRediT roles describe contributions, others are acknowledged, and plagiarism, including self-plagiarism, is avoided by careful citation and paraphrasing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Research Ethics and Integrity

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which principle is mainly protected by informed consent? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Respect for persons. Consent respects people's autonomy to decide whether to take part.

  2. Which study withheld treatment from Black men with syphilis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Tuskegee study. Tuskegee ran from 1932 to 1972 without informed consent or treatment.

  3. Which is an element of valid informed consent? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Voluntary agreement without coercion. Consent must be freely given, without coercion or undue pressure.

  4. Which framework sets ethics principles for UKRI-funded social science research? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The ESRC Framework for Research Ethics. The ESRC framework sets expectations for ethical social science research.

  5. Which document did the Tuskegee scandal lead to in the United States? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Belmont Report. The Belmont Report set principles for human research protection.

  6. What is process consent? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Checking consent throughout a study. Process consent revisits agreement as the study unfolds.

  7. A study's benefits are high, but it carries some risk of distress. What is the best approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reduce the risk with safeguards and justify the balance. Researchers minimise risks and justify the balance of risk and benefit.

  8. What is the main lesson of these historical cases for researchers today? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Protections such as consent and independent review are essential safeguards. The cases show why external review and consent matter.

  9. A teacher plans to recruit her own pupils. What is the main ethical risk? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Pupils may feel pressured to take part. Power relationships can undermine voluntary consent.

Key takeaways

  • Research ethics rests on respect for persons, beneficence and non-maleficence, justice and integrity, applied through UK frameworks such as the ESRC framework and disciplinary codes, using judgement throughout a project.
  • Abuses such as Nazi experiments, Tuskegee and organ retention scandals led to the Nuremberg Code, the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report and the Human Tissue Act 2004, establishing consent and independent review.
  • Valid consent needs information, understanding, voluntariness and capacity, obtained through information sheets and consent forms, with ongoing consent, withdrawal rights and special care for capacity, children and power relationships.
  • Anonymity means data cannot be linked to individuals, while confidentiality means identities are known but protected; pseudonyms, careful reporting and secure storage protect identities, and limits of confidentiality must be explained.
  • Research can cause physical, psychological, social, economic and legal harms to participants, communities and researchers; assess likelihood and severity, weigh benefits and use distress protocols, signposting and safety procedures.
  • Vulnerability arises from age, capacity, dependence, institutional settings and difficult circumstances; safeguards include accessible consent, advocates and safeguarding, while inclusion rather than exclusion promotes justice.

Common mistakes in Research Ethics and Integrity

  • Treating ethics as a form to complete once. Ethical issues arise throughout research and need ongoing attention.
  • Assuming historical abuses are irrelevant today. Pressures that led to them, such as exploiting vulnerable groups, can still arise.
  • Treating a signed form as proof of consent. Consent depends on understanding and voluntariness, not just a signature.
  • Promising complete confidentiality. Researchers may have to break confidentiality to prevent serious harm.
  • Forgetting the researcher's own wellbeing and safety. Fieldwork and sensitive topics carry risks for researchers too.
  • Excluding vulnerable groups to make ethics approval easier. This can be unjust and leaves important voices unheard.

Research Ethics and Integrity mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Principles of research ethics
    Research ethics protects participants, researchers and the trust society places in research.
  2. The history of research ethics
    Modern research ethics grew out of serious abuses.
  3. Informed consent
    Informed consent means people agree to take part in research freely and with enough understanding.
  4. Confidentiality and anonymity
    Participants share information trusting it will be protected.
  5. Avoiding harm
    Researchers must minimise the risk of harm to participants, themselves and others.
  6. Vulnerable participants
    Some people face greater risks or barriers in research.
  7. Deception and covert research
    Some studies withhold information or observe people without their knowledge.
  8. Research ethics committees
    Research ethics committees review proposed studies independently before they begin.
  9. Applying for ethical approval
    A clear, well-prepared ethics application speeds approval and improves the research.
  10. Health research: HRA approval
    Health and social care research in the UK has its own approval system.
  11. Ethics in online research
    Online research raises new ethical questions about privacy, consent and identification.
  12. The Concordat to Support Research Integrity
    The Concordat to Support Research Integrity sets out the UK's national framework for good research conduct.
  13. The UKRIO Code of Practice for Research
    The UK Research Integrity Office provides practical guidance on good research practice.
  14. Research misconduct
    Research misconduct undermines trust in research and can harm people.
  15. Questionable research practices
    Questionable research practices fall short of misconduct but still distort the research record.
  16. Authorship and plagiarism
    Authorship gives credit and responsibility for research.
  17. Conflicts of interest
    Conflicts of interest can bias research or create the appearance of bias.
  18. UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018
    Research using personal data must comply with data protection law.
  19. Special category data and research
    Some personal data are especially sensitive and need extra protection.
