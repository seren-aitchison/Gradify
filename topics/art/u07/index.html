Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a descender?

The part of a letter, as in g or p, that drops below the baseline. From “The anatomy of type” →

In plain English: Letters have named parts and measurements that designers use to choose and set type precisely. Key idea: Letters are described by parts such as baseline, x-height, ascenders, descenders, counters and serifs, and their spacing is controlled by point size, leading, kerning and tracking.

Roughly how many characters per line does Bringhurst suggest for comfortable reading?

About 45 to 75. From “Hierarchy and layout” →

In plain English: Good layout tells readers what to look at first, second and third. Key idea: Visual hierarchy orders information using size, weight, colour, position and white space, supported by consistent alignment, sensible line length and an understanding of how readers scan.

Who designed the I love NY logo in 1977?

Milton Glaser. From “Logos and visual identity” →

In plain English: A logo is the most visible part of a wider visual identity that includes colour, type and imagery. Key idea: Logos include wordmarks, lettermarks, pictorial, abstract, combination and emblem marks, and the best are simple, memorable, versatile and appropriate within a consistent visual identity.

Who designed the 1933 London Underground diagram?

Harry Beck. From “Information design” →

In plain English: Information design makes complex information clear through diagrams, charts, maps and signs. Key idea: Information design, from Snow, Nightingale, Minard and Beck to modern infographics, makes data clear by choosing suitable forms, labelling honestly, removing clutter and simplifying where it helps understanding.

What does AIDA stand for?

Attention, interest, desire, action. From “Advertising and persuasion” →

In plain English: Advertising uses images, words and psychology to persuade audiences. Key idea: Adverts persuade through attention, interest, desire and action and through ethos, pathos and logos, as landmark campaigns show, and in the UK they are regulated by the ASA to be legal, decent, honest and truthful.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Graphic and Communication Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the counter of a letter? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The enclosed or partly enclosed space inside it. Counters are the spaces within letters such as o, e and a, and they strongly affect legibility.

  2. Which typeface is a geometric sans serif built from circles and straight lines? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Futura. Futura uses near-perfect circles and straight strokes, the key features of geometric sans serifs.

  3. Which alignment is usually easiest to read for long passages of body text? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Flush left, ragged right. A consistent left edge lets the eye return easily to the start of each new line.

  4. A designer wants to reduce the awkward gap between the letters T and o in a headline. Which adjustment should they use? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Kerning. Kerning changes the space between one particular pair of letters without affecting the rest of the word.

  5. Which feature best identifies a Didone typeface? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Extreme contrast between thick and thin strokes. Didone faces such as Bodoni have hairline thins against strong thick strokes and flat serifs.

  6. A leaflet's subheadings blend into the body text. Which change would most improve the hierarchy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Set the subheadings in a bolder weight with space above. Increasing weight and adding space creates contrast between levels, so subheadings stand out.

  7. Two typefaces are both set at 11 point, but one looks much larger on the page. What is the most likely reason? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It has a larger x-height. Point size measures the type body, so a typeface with a taller x-height appears bigger at the same size.

  8. A designer chooses a heavy slab serif for a children's hospital information leaflet's body text. What is the best criticism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A heavy display face is tiring to read at length; a clear text face would serve readers better. Display faces suit headings, but long information text needs a calm, highly readable typeface.

  9. A charity web page places its donate button at the bottom right, below long text. Based on scanning research, what is the main risk? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Many readers scan the top and left and may never reach the button. F-pattern scanning means content far down and to the right is often missed on text-heavy pages.

Key takeaways

  • Letters are described by parts such as baseline, x-height, ascenders, descenders, counters and serifs, and their spacing is controlled by point size, leading, kerning and tracking.
  • Typefaces are grouped as blackletter, old style, transitional, Didone and slab serifs, and grotesque, neo-grotesque, humanist and geometric sans serifs, and each carries its own history, mood and best uses.
  • Visual hierarchy orders information using size, weight, colour, position and white space, supported by consistent alignment, sensible line length and an understanding of how readers scan.
  • Grids organise layouts with margins, columns, gutters, rows and modules, and range from simple manuscript grids to modular and baseline grids, with deliberate grid-breaking as an expressive option.
  • Signs combine a signifier and a signified, can be icons, indexes or symbols, and work through denotation, connotation and anchoring text within shared cultural codes.
  • Logos include wordmarks, lettermarks, pictorial, abstract, combination and emblem marks, and the best are simple, memorable, versatile and appropriate within a consistent visual identity.

Common mistakes in Graphic and Communication Design

  • Confusing kerning with tracking. Kerning adjusts one pair of letters; tracking changes spacing evenly across a group of letters.
  • Assuming sans serif is always more modern or more readable. Readability depends on design, size and context; many humanist sans serifs and old style serifs read well in different situations.
  • Making everything bold or large to seem important. If every element shouts, nothing stands out; hierarchy needs contrast between levels.
  • Thinking a grid makes every page look the same. A good grid gives many possible layouts while keeping a consistent underlying order.
  • Assuming signs mean the same thing everywhere. Many signs, such as colours and gestures, are cultural symbols that can mean different things in different places.
  • Treating the logo as the whole brand identity. A logo works alongside consistent colours, type, imagery and rules set out in brand guidelines.

Lessons in this school

  1. The anatomy of type
    Letters have named parts and measurements that designers use to choose and set type precisely.
  2. Type classification
    Typefaces are grouped into families by their history and visual features.
  3. Hierarchy and layout
    Good layout tells readers what to look at first, second and third.
  4. Grids
    Grids are invisible structures that help designers place elements consistently.
  5. Visual communication and semiotics
    Semiotics is the study of signs and how they create meaning.
  6. Logos and visual identity
    A logo is the most visible part of a wider visual identity that includes colour, type and imagery.
  7. Poster design
    Posters must grab attention and communicate quickly, often from a distance.
  8. Illustration
    Illustration uses images to explain, decorate or tell stories alongside text or for a client's purpose.
  9. Information design
    Information design makes complex information clear through diagrams, charts, maps and signs.
  10. User interface design
    User interface design shapes how people interact with apps, websites and devices.
  11. Packaging design
    Packaging protects products, informs customers and helps sell goods on the shelf.
  12. Advertising and persuasion
    Advertising uses images, words and psychology to persuade audiences.
  13. The design process: from brief to final
    Designers move from a client's brief to a final outcome through a structured but flexible process.
  14. Sustainable design
    Designers influence how much material, energy and waste a product or campaign creates.

Graphic and Communication Design: questions students ask

Who designed the Transport typeface for British road signs?

Jock Kinneir and Margaret Calvert.

What is the problem with centred alignment for long text?

Each line starts at a different point, so it is hard for the eye to find the next line.

What is a gutter in a grid?

The space between columns.

What is anchorage?

Text that fixes the intended meaning of an image.
