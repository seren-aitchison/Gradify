Which church movement shaped Baroque religious art?

The Counter-Reformation. From “The Baroque” →

In plain English: Baroque art of the seventeenth century used drama, movement and strong light to move viewers emotionally. Key idea: Baroque art used drama, movement, tenebrist light, illusion and viewer involvement to serve the Counter-Reformation Church and powerful monarchs, in works by Caravaggio, Gentileschi, Bernini, Rubens and Velázquez.

Which Neoclassical sculptor carved Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss?

Antonio Canova. From “Neoclassicism” →

In plain English: Neoclassicism revived the art of ancient Greece and Rome to express seriousness, virtue and reason. Key idea: Neoclassicism, inspired by Pompeii, Herculaneum and Winckelmann, used clear line, balance, restraint and ancient subjects to express reason and civic virtue, in works by David, Canova, Kauffman and Ingres that often carried political meaning.

Which critic defended the Pre-Raphaelites in 1851?

John Ruskin. From “The Pre-Raphaelites” →

In plain English: The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood was a group of young British artists who rebelled against academic painting in 1848. Key idea: The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, founded in 1848 by Hunt, Millais and Rossetti, rejected academic formulas in favour of bright colour, minute detail and truth to nature, painting literary and religious subjects and influencing later design.

What does fauves mean?

Wild beasts. From “Fauvism and Expressionism” →

In plain English: In the early twentieth century, artists used bold, unnatural colour and distorted form to express feeling. Key idea: Fauvism, named in 1905, used pure, non-naturalistic colour, while Expressionist artists such as Munch, Die Brücke and Der Blaue Reiter distorted colour and form to show emotion, before the Nazis attacked modern art as degenerate.

Who wrote the Manifesto of Surrealism in 1924?

André Breton. From “Surrealism” →

In plain English: Surrealism sought to unlock the unconscious mind through dreams, chance and unexpected combinations. Key idea: Surrealism, launched by Breton's 1924 manifesto and shaped by Freud, explored the unconscious through automatic techniques such as frottage and through realistic dream images by Dalí, Magritte, Oppenheim and others.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which painter of The Calling of Saint Matthew is famous for tenebrism? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Caravaggio. Caravaggio's dramatic spotlighting against deep shadow became highly influential across Europe.

  2. Which painter created The Night Watch in 1642? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Rembrandt van Rijn. Rembrandt's large group portrait of a civic militia is famous for its movement and dramatic light.

  3. Which colours are typical of Rococo painting? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Soft pastels such as pink, blue and pale green. Rococo favoured light, delicate colours suited to elegant interiors.

  4. How does Bernini's David differ from Michelangelo's? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It shows the moment of action, twisting to throw the stone. Bernini chose peak movement, a typical Baroque choice that involves the viewer in the action.

  5. Why did Dutch painters produce so many landscapes, still lifes and genre scenes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Protestant churches commissioned little art, so artists sold to private buyers. An open market of middle-class buyers wanted affordable, varied paintings for their homes.

  6. Which artist is credited with creating the fête galante? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Antoine Watteau. Watteau's dreamy scenes of elegant couples in parks were accepted by the French Academy as a new category.

  7. Why did the Catholic Church favour dramatic, emotional images in the seventeenth century? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    To strengthen faith and respond to Protestant criticism. The Counter-Reformation used persuasive images to move believers and reaffirm Catholic teaching.

  8. A student says Girl with a Pearl Earring must show a famous wealthy woman. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is generally seen as a tronie, a character study, and the sitter is unknown. Dutch tronies depicted types or expressions, so the girl's identity is uncertain.

  9. Why did Enlightenment critics such as Diderot attack Rococo painting? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They saw it as frivolous, serving aristocratic pleasure rather than moral improvement. Diderot wanted art to teach virtue, and Boucher's sensual scenes seemed to promote luxury instead.

Key takeaways

  • Baroque art used drama, movement, tenebrist light, illusion and viewer involvement to serve the Counter-Reformation Church and powerful monarchs, in works by Caravaggio, Gentileschi, Bernini, Rubens and Velázquez.
  • The wealthy, Protestant Dutch Republic created an open art market in which painters such as Rembrandt, Vermeer, Hals, Leyster, Steen and Ruysch specialised in portraits, landscapes, still lifes and everyday scenes.
  • Rococo art, led by Watteau, Boucher, Fragonard and Tiepolo, used pastel colour, curves, asymmetry and light subjects for aristocratic interiors, before Enlightenment critics and Neoclassicism turned against it.
  • Neoclassicism, inspired by Pompeii, Herculaneum and Winckelmann, used clear line, balance, restraint and ancient subjects to express reason and civic virtue, in works by David, Canova, Kauffman and Ingres that often carried political meaning.
  • Romanticism valued emotion, imagination and the sublime power of nature, from Friedrich and Turner to Géricault, Delacroix and Goya, who also used dramatic images to respond to political events.
  • Ukiyo-e prints were collaboratively made woodblock images of Edo's floating world, landscapes and actors by artists such as Utamaro, Hokusai and Hiroshige, and their flat colour and bold design inspired Japonisme in Western art.

Common mistakes in Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism

  • Thinking the Baroque was only about decoration and excess. Its dramatic effects had a purpose: to persuade, move and involve viewers in religious and political messages.
  • Assuming Dutch genre scenes are simple snapshots of life. Many contain moral lessons, jokes or symbolism, and were carefully composed in the studio.
  • Treating Rococo as simply late Baroque. It shares some features, but Rococo is lighter, more intimate and less concerned with grand religious or political drama.
  • Thinking Neoclassical art was purely about copying old statues. It used ancient models to make new statements about virtue, patriotism and revolution.
  • Thinking Romantic means romantic love. In art history Romanticism refers to emotion, imagination, nature and the individual more broadly.
  • Thinking the designer made the whole print alone. Ukiyo-e depended on teams of publishers, carvers and printers as well as the designer.

Lessons in this school

  1. The Baroque
    Baroque art of the seventeenth century used drama, movement and strong light to move viewers emotionally.
  2. The Dutch Golden Age
    In the seventeenth century the Dutch Republic produced vast numbers of paintings for a new middle-class market.
  3. Rococo
    Rococo art of the eighteenth century was light, playful and decorative, made for aristocratic interiors.
  4. Neoclassicism
    Neoclassicism revived the art of ancient Greece and Rome to express seriousness, virtue and reason.
  5. Romanticism
    Romantic artists valued emotion, imagination and the power of nature over reason and order.
  6. Japanese woodblock prints
    Ukiyo-e woodblock prints from Edo-period Japan showed actors, beauties and landscapes and later transformed Western art.
  7. Realism
    In the mid-nineteenth century, Realist artists painted ordinary people and modern life truthfully, rejecting idealised history painting.
  8. The Pre-Raphaelites
    The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood was a group of young British artists who rebelled against academic painting in 1848.
  9. Impressionism
    The Impressionists painted modern life and the changing effects of light with quick, visible brushstrokes.
  10. Post-Impressionism
    Post-Impressionism covers artists who built on Impressionism but pushed it towards structure, symbolism or expression.
  11. Arts and Crafts and Art Nouveau
    In the late nineteenth century, designers reacted against poor-quality mass production and created new decorative styles.
  12. Fauvism and Expressionism
    In the early twentieth century, artists used bold, unnatural colour and distorted form to express feeling.
  13. Cubism
    Cubism, developed by Picasso and Braque from 1907, broke objects into planes and showed several viewpoints at once.
  14. Futurism and Constructivism
    Futurism in Italy celebrated speed and machines, while Russian Constructivism aimed to build a new society through art and design.
  15. Dada
    Dada was an anti-war, anti-art movement that began in Zurich in 1916 and spread to Berlin, Paris and New York.
  16. Surrealism
    Surrealism sought to unlock the unconscious mind through dreams, chance and unexpected combinations.
  17. The Bauhaus
    The Bauhaus was a German art and design school from 1919 to 1933 that shaped modern design worldwide.
  18. Abstract Expressionism
    Abstract Expressionism emerged in New York in the 1940s and 1950s and made the city a new centre of the art world.
  19. Pop Art
    Pop Art drew on advertising, comics, celebrities and consumer goods, blurring the line between high art and popular culture.
