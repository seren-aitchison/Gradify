Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Since when have the UK's national museums offered free entry to their permanent collections?

Since December 2001. From “Museums and galleries” →

In plain English: Museums and galleries collect, preserve and display art for the public. Key idea: Museums collect, conserve, research, display and educate; public museums differ from commercial galleries and artist-run spaces; UK national museums have been free since 2001, but debates continue about access, funding and their social role.

Why do art market participants follow anti-money laundering rules?

Because the market's privacy and high values have made it attractive for laundering money. From “The art market” →

In plain English: Art is bought and sold in a global market worth tens of billions of pounds. Key idea: The primary market sells new work through galleries, usually for about 50% commission, and the secondary market resells it through dealers and auctions; value depends on reputation, provenance and demand; the resale right and anti-money laundering rules regulate the market.

Name two UK fair dealing exceptions relevant to artists.

Criticism and review, quotation, research and private study, or caricature, parody and pastiche. From “Appropriation, influence and fair dealing” →

In plain English: Artists have always borrowed from others, but where does influence end and infringement begin? Key idea: Influence and styles are free, but copying a substantial part of a protected work can infringe; UK fair dealing covers criticism, quotation, study and parody or pastiche if fair; US cases such as Koons, Prince and Warhol v Goldsmith show debates about transformative use.

Why can titanium white reveal a forgery of a nineteenth-century painting?

It was not widely used as a pigment until about the 1920s. From “Forgery and authenticity” →

In plain English: Forgeries have fooled experts and collectors for centuries. Key idea: Forgers such as van Meegeren, Greenhalgh and Beltracchi fooled experts; authenticity is tested through provenance, connoisseurship, scientific analysis and catalogues raisonnés; authenticity affects value and art history, raising questions about why originals matter.

What is the purpose of the Foundation Diploma in Art and Design?

To let students explore a range of art and design disciplines and build a portfolio before specialising at degree level. From “Applying to art school” →

In plain English: There are several routes into art and design higher education. Key idea: Most applications go through UCAS with a portfolio, often after a Foundation Diploma that lets students explore disciplines; the personal statement, portfolio review and interview assess interest, ideas and development.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Art in Society and Professional Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When did Tate Modern open? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    2000. Tate Modern opened in a former power station in 2000.

  2. Who curated When Attitudes Become Form in 1969? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Harald Szeemann. Szeemann became a model for the independent curator.

  3. Which is an example of the secondary art market? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An auction of a painting by a previous owner. The secondary market involves resale by a previous owner, not a first sale.

  4. What is an artist-run space? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A venue organised by artists to show work, often outside the commercial market. Artist-run spaces support experimental and emerging work.

  5. Why do exhibitions limit light levels for works on paper? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Light can fade and damage sensitive materials over time. Conservation needs constrain display decisions.

  6. What is a buyer's premium? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A fee the buyer pays the auction house on top of the hammer price. Auction houses charge buyers a percentage above the hammer price.

  7. Which is the strongest argument for keeping national museums free? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Free entry widens access to shared cultural heritage, especially for those on low incomes. Removing charges lowers a key barrier to participation.

  8. How can the order of works in an exhibition shape meaning? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Sequence creates comparisons and a narrative that guides interpretation. Curators use sequence to build arguments.

  9. Which is the strongest criticism of the contemporary art market? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It concentrates attention and money on a few artists while most earn little. Market success is very unequally distributed.

Key takeaways

  • Museums collect, conserve, research, display and educate; public museums differ from commercial galleries and artist-run spaces; UK national museums have been free since 2001, but debates continue about access, funding and their social role.
  • Curators research, select and interpret art; exhibitions move from concept through loans, design and interpretation to evaluation; display choices such as the white cube, sequencing and labels shape how audiences understand art.
  • The primary market sells new work through galleries, usually for about 50% commission, and the secondary market resells it through dealers and auctions; value depends on reputation, provenance and demand; the resale right and anti-money laundering rules regulate the market.
  • Patronage has moved from the Church and rulers to public bodies such as Arts Council England and corporate sponsors; public art such as the Angel of the North and the Fourth Plinth reaches wide audiences; sponsorship raises ethical questions, as with BP and the Sacklers.
  • UK copyright arises automatically for original artistic works, protects expression rather than ideas, and lasts for the artist's life plus 70 years; moral rights protect attribution and integrity; selling a work does not transfer copyright, and artists can license use.
  • Influence and styles are free, but copying a substantial part of a protected work can infringe; UK fair dealing covers criticism, quotation, study and parody or pastiche if fair; US cases such as Koons, Prince and Warhol v Goldsmith show debates about transformative use.

Common mistakes in Art in Society and Professional Practice

  • Confusing commercial galleries with public museums. Commercial galleries sell art; public museums hold collections for the public.
  • Assuming exhibitions simply show art neutrally. Every choice of selection, order and text shapes meaning.
  • Assuming record auction prices reflect how most artists earn. Most artists earn modest and irregular incomes.
  • Assuming patronage is only historical. Public funding, sponsors and collectors are modern patrons.
  • Assuming that buying an artwork gives the right to reproduce it. Copyright stays with the artist unless it is assigned in writing.
  • Assuming changing a work slightly makes copying lawful. Using a substantial part without permission can still infringe, whatever the change.

Art in Society and Professional Practice: questions students ask

What is the main difference between a public museum and a commercial gallery?

A public museum holds a collection for public benefit; a commercial gallery represents artists and sells their work.

What does the word curator originally mean?

From the Latin curare, to take care of.

What is the primary art market?

The first sale of a work, usually through a commercial gallery representing the artist.

What is the Fourth Plinth?

A plinth in Trafalgar Square used since 1999 for a programme of contemporary art commissions.
