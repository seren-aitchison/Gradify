Key terms and formulas at a glance

Binder
The substance that holds pigment particles together and fixes them to the surface
Egg tempera
Paint made of pigment bound with egg yolk and water
Buon fresco
Painting with water-ground pigment into wet lime plaster, which binds it as it sets
Glaze
A thin, transparent layer of oil paint over a dry layer, modifying its colour
Wet-in-wet
Applying paint to wet paper or wet paint so colours blend softly
Acrylic emulsion
A dispersion of acrylic polymer particles in water, which forms a film as the water evaporates
Support
The physical surface on which a painting is made, such as panel, canvas or paper
Filbert
A flat brush with a rounded, oval tip, giving soft-edged strokes
Grisaille
Painting in shades of grey, used as an underpainting or as a finished monochrome
Alla prima
Painting wet-into-wet to completion in one session, without waiting for layers to dry
Impasto
Paint applied thickly so that it stands up from the surface and retains marks
Still life
A depiction of inanimate objects arranged by the artist

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the binder in egg tempera?

Egg yolk. From “What paint is: pigment and binder” →

In plain English: Every paint is a pigment held in a binder, often thinned by a solvent or diluent. Key idea: The binder defines the medium: it decides how paint handles, how fast it dries, whether it can be reworked and how the surface ages.

Who is often wrongly credited with inventing oil painting?

Jan van Eyck: drying oils were used earlier, but he perfected oil glazing. From “Oil paint” →

In plain English: Oil paint, pigment bound in drying oil, became the dominant European easel medium from the 15th century. Key idea: Oil's slow drying, transparency and depth allowed blended transitions, rich darks, translucent glazes and later thick impasto: the reasons it dominated European easel painting.

What does a toned ground do?

Replaces stark white with a mid-tone, unifying colour and making values easier to judge. From “Supports and grounds” →

In plain English: A painting's support is what it is painted on; the ground is the prepared layer between support and paint. Key idea: Size seals the support, the ground gives it a stable, even surface; together they decide how paint adheres, absorbs and ages.

What does "thick lights, thin darks" mean?

Paint lit areas thickly and keep shadows thin and transparent, so lights advance and shadows recede. From “Impasto and texture” →

In plain English: Impasto is paint applied thickly enough to stand up from the surface and keep the marks of brush or knife. Key idea: Impasto makes paint physical: thick, raised marks catch light, record gesture and give the surface a presence of its own.

What is an attribute in portraiture?

An object that identifies a sitter's role, profession or status, such as a book or instrument. From “Portraiture” →

In plain English: Portraiture represents particular people, but it has always been about more than likeness: status, identity, memory and power. Key idea: A portrait is a constructed image: likeness combines with pose, dress, setting and symbols to present identity and status, negotiated between artist and sitter.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Painting Media and Techniques

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the binder in watercolour? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Gum arabic. Watercolour and gouache use gum arabic; oil uses linseed, tempera egg yolk, acrylic a polymer emulsion.

  2. What lies beneath burnished gold leaf on a medieval panel? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Bole, a red clay. Gold leaf was laid on bole over gesso, then burnished.

  3. What is the name for a red-earth drawing on the rough plaster beneath a fresco? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sinopia. A sinopia is drawn on the arriccio; the cartoon is a full-size paper drawing transferred to the plaster.

  4. A painter wants to blend skin tones slowly over several hours. Which medium suits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Oil paint. Oil's slow curing allows extended blending; acrylic and tempera dry in minutes and fresco must be finished before the plaster sets.

  5. A conservator sees a greenish tone showing through worn faces in a 14th-century panel. What is the best explanation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A green earth underpainting used to model flesh. Italian tempera painters underpainted flesh in green earth, visible where upper layers have abraded.

  6. Why have the blue skies of some medieval murals flaked away while other colours remain? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The blues were often applied secco, with a binder on dry plaster. Costly blues such as azurite were often added secco, which is less durable than true fresco.

  7. Why must oil painters work "fat over lean"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Upper layers need more oil so they stay flexible and do not crack over faster-drying, less flexible layers beneath. Lean lower layers dry and stiffen first; flexible upper layers prevent cracking as the layers move and cure.

  8. Why might an art historian connect the gold backgrounds of Duccio's Maestà to both technique and meaning? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Gold leaf on bole was a costly workshop technique, and its shimmer signified a heavenly, timeless realm. Material, cost and devotional convention combine in the gold ground.

  9. Why did Leonardo's Last Supper deteriorate so quickly? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    He painted in tempera and oil on dry plaster rather than in buon fresco, so the paint did not bond into the wall. His experimental technique allowed slow, revised work but adhered poorly.

Key takeaways

  • The binder defines the medium: it decides how paint handles, how fast it dries, whether it can be reworked and how the surface ages.
  • Tempera dries almost instantly, so painters model form with countless small hatched strokes in thin layers over a smooth white gesso ground, giving a crisp, luminous surface.
  • In buon fresco, pigment in water is painted into wet lime plaster and bound as the lime sets, so the work is planned in daily patches (giornate) and must be painted fast and finally.
  • Oil's slow drying, transparency and depth allowed blended transitions, rich darks, translucent glazes and later thick impasto: the reasons it dominated European easel painting.
  • Watercolour is transparent: the paper is the light. Plan whites first, work light to dark, and use water to control edges and blends.
  • Acrylic dries fast to a flexible, water-resistant film; mediums make it behave like watercolour, oil or thick paste, so it suits flat colour, layering and mixed media.

Common mistakes in Painting Media and Techniques

  • Calling all paint "acrylic" or mixing media without knowing their chemistry. Incompatible layers fail: acrylic over oil, or thick oil over thin oil without more oil in the upper layer.
  • Expecting soft, blended transitions like oil. Tempera's character is linear and precise; smooth effects come from very fine hatching, not wet blending.
  • Assuming every mural is fresco. Leonardo's Last Supper (c.1495–98) was painted in tempera and oil on dry plaster, an experiment that began deteriorating within his lifetime.
  • Repeating that van Eyck "invented" oil paint. He perfected and popularised a technique; oil binders were already in use.
  • Overworking and adding more layers to fix mistakes. Each layer reduces transparency; muddy watercolour often comes from too many layers or too many pigments mixed.
  • Expecting long blending as in oil. Acrylic dries in minutes; use retarder, a stay-wet palette or work in smaller areas.

Lessons in this school

  1. What paint is: pigment and binder
    Every paint is a pigment held in a binder, often thinned by a solvent or diluent.
  2. Egg tempera
    Egg tempera, pigment bound with egg yolk, was the main panel painting medium in Europe from the Middle Ages to the late 15th century.
  3. Fresco
    Fresco is painting into wet lime plaster so the colour becomes part of the wall.
  4. Oil paint
    Oil paint, pigment bound in drying oil, became the dominant European easel medium from the 15th century.
  5. Watercolour and gouache
    Watercolour is transparent paint bound with gum arabic, relying on the white of the paper for light; gouache is its opaque cousin.
  6. Acrylic paint
    Acrylic paint, pigment in a polymer emulsion, was developed in the mid-20th century and is now the most common studio and school paint.
  7. Supports and grounds
    A painting's support is what it is painted on; the ground is the prepared layer between support and paint.
  8. Brushes, knives and tools
    Brush shape, fibre and size determine the marks a painter can make.
  9. Underpainting and glazing
    Indirect painting builds a picture in stages: an underpainting establishes drawing and values, then transparent glazes and opaque layers add colour.
  10. Alla prima
    Alla prima (Italian, "at the first attempt") means painting wet-into-wet in one session, without waiting for layers to dry.
  11. Impasto and texture
    Impasto is paint applied thickly enough to stand up from the surface and keep the marks of brush or knife.
  12. Still life
    Still life depicts arranged inanimate objects such as fruit, flowers, vessels and books.
  13. Landscape painting
    Landscape painting depicts natural and built scenery.
  14. Portraiture
    Portraiture represents particular people, but it has always been about more than likeness: status, identity, memory and power.
  15. Abstract painting
    Abstract painting does not depict recognisable objects, or reduces them until they are no longer the subject.
  16. Health and safety in the studio
    Art materials and tools can be hazardous if misused.
  17. Conservation and the life of a painting
    Paintings change over time: varnish yellows, paint cracks, pigments fade and supports move.

Painting Media and Techniques: questions students ask

How does oil paint dry?

It cures by oxidation: the drying oil reacts with oxygen and polymerises, over days to years.

What is gesso?

A ground of chalk or gypsum in animal glue, built up in layers and smoothed to prepare a panel.

What is a giornata?

The area of fresh plaster laid and painted in a single day in fresco.

How are whites made in transparent watercolour?

By leaving the paper unpainted (reserving) or using masking fluid, not by adding white paint.
