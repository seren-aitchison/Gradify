Key terms and formulas at a glance

Fine art
Art made chiefly to be looked at and contemplated rather than used, such as painting, sculpture and installation
Assessment objective
A statement of the skill or understanding an exam rewards; Art and Design has four (develop, refine, record, present)
Formal analysis
Analysis of how a work's visual elements and principles are organised, independent of its subject's meaning
Contour line
A line that follows the edges of a form, including internal edges, to describe its shape and volume
Organic shape
An irregular, often curving shape associated with natural forms
Tone
The relative lightness or darkness of a colour or surface; also called value
Hue
The name of a colour family, such as red, blue or yellow-green
Actual texture
Texture that can be felt, such as thick paint, carved surfaces or woven fibre
Picture plane
The flat surface of a picture, imagined as a window through which pictorial space is seen
Motif
The repeated unit in a pattern
Symmetrical balance
Balance in which elements on either side of a central axis mirror each other
Focal point
The area of a work that attracts the eye first

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a readymade?

An everyday manufactured object selected and presented as art, as in Duchamp's Fountain (1917). From “What is art and design?” →

In plain English: Art and design are the making of things that are looked at, used and thought about. Key idea: Art and design overlap: fine art is made mainly to be looked at and thought about, design to solve a problem for a user, and craft emphasises skilled making in a material.

What did Hogarth call his S-shaped curve?

The "line of beauty", in The Analysis of Beauty (1753). From “Line” →

In plain English: Line is the most basic visual element: a mark with length and direction, or an edge the eye perceives. Key idea: Line carries both information (edges, direction, structure) and feeling (through its weight, speed and character).

What is frottage?

A technique of rubbing a drawing material over paper placed on a textured surface, developed by Max Ernst from 1925. From “Texture” →

In plain English: Texture is the surface quality of a work, how it feels or looks as if it would feel. Key idea: Texture can be real or represented, and it carries meaning as well as surface: rough, smooth, soft or hard surfaces call on our memory of touch.

Name three things that increase an element's visual weight.

Any three: size, darkness or contrast, saturation, texture or detail, isolation, human interest, distance from the centre. From “Balance and symmetry” →

In plain English: Balance is the distribution of visual weight in a composition. Key idea: Balance depends on visual weight, not size alone: a small, bright, high-contrast or interesting element can balance a large quiet area.

What does "unity in variety" mean?

A work holds together as a whole while containing enough difference to stay interesting. From “Unity and variety” →

In plain English: Unity is the sense that all parts of a work belong together; variety is the difference that keeps it interesting. Key idea: Strong compositions combine unity (parts that clearly belong together) with variety (differences that hold attention): "unity in variety".

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Visual Language of Art and Design

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is best described as design rather than fine art? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A typeface commissioned for a city's road signs. A typeface answers a brief for users (drivers reading signs), which is the defining purpose of design; the others are made chiefly to be contemplated.

  2. Which assessment objective is concerned with recording observations and insights as work progresses? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    AO3. AO3 is record; AO1 develop through sources, AO2 refine through media, AO4 present a personal response.

  3. "The painting is 84 cm by 152 cm and shows four figures in a diner." Which stage is this? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Description. Size, medium and an inventory of what is shown are description: factual and without interpretation.

  4. Why is Duchamp's Fountain (1917) often used to discuss the definition of art? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It was presented as art although the artist did not make it by hand. The work's status rested on choice, signature and context, not handmade skill, which challenged skill-based and formalist definitions.

  5. A student photographs a derelict building, annotates the photos with notes on texture, and makes rubbings of its brickwork. Which objective does this most directly evidence? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    AO3, recording observations relevant to intentions. First-hand photographs, rubbings and annotation record observations; recording is not limited to drawing.

  6. Which sentence is the best example of formal analysis? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The diagonal counter leads the eye from the lower left into the lit interior. Formal analysis explains how visual elements are organised; the others are interpretation, context and judgement.

  7. A critic argues that a pile of bricks in a gallery is art only because the gallery shows it. Which theory is the critic using? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The institutional theory of art. The institutional theory (Dickie) defines art by its acceptance within the art world; formalism looks at visual form, mimesis at likeness, expression at feeling conveyed.

  8. A student's final piece is excellent but the portfolio shows no trials of media. What is the most accurate judgement? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    AO4 may be strong but AO2 is weak, which limits the overall mark. Marks are awarded across all four objectives, each 25%; missing experimentation weakens AO2 however good the outcome is.

  9. A student writes that a still life "symbolises death" because the artist died a year later. What is the main weakness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The claim relies on a coincidence of biography rather than visual or contextual evidence in the work. Context helps only when it connects to what is visible; a symbol of death (a skull, a snuffed candle) would be evidence, a later date of death is not.

Key takeaways

  • Art and design overlap: fine art is made mainly to be looked at and thought about, design to solve a problem for a user, and craft emphasises skilled making in a material.
  • The four objectives are equally weighted and assessed together across the whole submission: a strong final piece cannot carry a project with no visible development, experimentation or recording.
  • Every interpretive claim should point to something visible: "the figure seems isolated because she is placed at the far edge, in a cold blue field, separated by empty space."
  • Line carries both information (edges, direction, structure) and feeling (through its weight, speed and character).
  • Shape is two-dimensional; form is three-dimensional. Painting and drawing imply form with tone, overlap and perspective; sculpture has actual form that changes with viewpoint.
  • The eye reads form and space mostly from tonal relationships: if the tones are right, a drawing reads correctly even in a single colour.

Common mistakes in The Visual Language of Art and Design

  • Treating "art" as meaning only painting and sculpture. Exam specifications and galleries include photography, film, installation, performance, textiles, ceramics and digital work.
  • Thinking AO3 means only drawing. Recording includes photography, written annotation, digital capture, maquettes and notes, so long as they record observations and ideas relevant to your intentions.
  • Jumping straight to meaning ("it's about loneliness") without describing and analysing first. Examiners reward interpretations that are built on visual evidence.
  • Thinking line only means outline. Most expressive drawing uses varied, broken and internal contour lines; a single even outline flattens a form.
  • Using "form" and "shape" interchangeably in analysis. Say "the flat, organic shapes" of a Matisse cut-out but "the rounded form" of a Henry Moore sculpture.
  • Confusing tone with colour. A red and a green can have the same tone; squinting at a subject, or converting a photo to greyscale, helps you see tone separately from hue.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is art and design?
    Art and design are the making of things that are looked at, used and thought about.
  2. How art and design are assessed: the four assessment objectives
    GCSE and A level Art and Design are marked against the same four assessment objectives: develop, refine, record and present.
  3. Looking closely: an approach to visual analysis
    Visual analysis is the disciplined description and interpretation of what a work looks like.
  4. Line
    Line is the most basic visual element: a mark with length and direction, or an edge the eye perceives.
  5. Shape and form
    Shape is a flat, two-dimensional area; form is three-dimensional, with height, width and depth.
  6. Tone and value
    Tone (or value) is the lightness or darkness of a colour or surface.
  7. Colour as a visual element
    Colour is the visual element with the most immediate emotional impact.
  8. Texture
    Texture is the surface quality of a work, how it feels or looks as if it would feel.
  9. Space
    Space is the area around, between and within forms, real in sculpture and architecture and illusory in pictures.
  10. Pattern
    Pattern is the regular repetition of a motif across a surface.
  11. Balance and symmetry
    Balance is the distribution of visual weight in a composition.
  12. Contrast and emphasis
    Emphasis makes one part of a work dominant, and contrast is the main way to achieve it.
  13. Rhythm, repetition and movement
    Rhythm is created by repeating visual elements at intervals, and movement is the path the eye takes through a work or the illusion of motion within it.
  14. Proportion and scale
    Proportion is the size relationship of parts to each other and to the whole; scale is size relative to a standard, usually the human body.
  15. Unity and variety
    Unity is the sense that all parts of a work belong together; variety is the difference that keeps it interesting.
  16. Composition and the picture plane
    Composition is the arrangement of elements within the format of a work.
  17. The rule of thirds and the golden ratio
    The rule of thirds and the golden ratio are proportional systems often recommended for composition.
  18. Gestalt principles in art and design
    Gestalt psychology studies how we perceive wholes rather than separate parts.

The Visual Language of Art and Design: questions students ask

What does AO2 reward?

Refining work by exploring ideas and selecting and experimenting with appropriate media, materials, techniques and processes.

What are Feldman's four stages of art criticism?

Describe, analyse, interpret and judge.

What is an implied line?

A line the viewer perceives without it being drawn, such as the direction of a gaze or a row of objects.

What is the difference between shape and form?

Shape is a flat two-dimensional area; form is three-dimensional, with depth or the illusion of depth.
