Key terms and formulas at a glance

Observational drawing
Drawing made by looking directly at a subject, also called drawing from life
Graphite grade
The hardness of a pencil on the H to B scale, from hard and light (H) to soft and dark (B)
Mark-making
The range of lines, patterns and textures created with a tool, and the practice of exploring them
Sighting
Measuring proportions or angles by eye, typically with a pencil held at arm's length
Blind contour
A contour drawing made without looking at the paper
Negative space
The space around and between the subjects in a work
Sfumato
Soft, smoky gradation of tone without hard edges or lines, associated with Leonardo
Hatching
Building tone with closely spaced parallel lines
Horizon line
A horizontal line at the viewer's eye level, on which vanishing points lie
Two-point perspective
A system in which receding horizontal edges converge on two vanishing points on the horizon
Atmospheric perspective
The depiction of distance by making far objects paler, bluer, lower in contrast and less detailed; also called aerial perspective
Foreshortening
The apparent shortening of a form that extends towards or away from the viewer

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name four purposes of drawing.

Any four: observation, recording, thinking or invention, planning, communication, expression or finished work. From “Drawing as thinking and recording” →

In plain English: Drawing is the foundation of art and design practice: a way of seeing, thinking, planning and communicating, not only of making finished pictures. Key idea: Drawing is a way of thinking as much as a way of picturing: the same mark-making serves observation, invention, planning and communication.

Why must the arm stay fully extended when sighting?

So the distance from the eye to the pencil stays constant and every measurement uses the same scale. From “Measuring and sighting” →

In plain English: Sighting is the practice of measuring proportions and angles by eye with a pencil held at arm's length. Key idea: Measure relationships, not absolute sizes: one unit compared with others, with a locked arm and one eye closed, keeps proportions consistent.

What is subtractive drawing?

Starting from a toned ground and lifting out lights, for example with a putty rubber on charcoal. From “Tonal drawing and chiaroscuro” →

In plain English: Tonal drawing builds form with masses of light and dark rather than lines. Key idea: Tone describes form through light: block in a few large values first, then refine edges and save the extremes for last.

Name four changes atmospheric perspective produces with distance.

Any four: lighter tone, lower contrast, cooler and bluer colour, lower saturation, less detail, softer edges. From “Atmospheric perspective” →

In plain English: Atmospheric or aerial perspective shows distance through changes in colour, tone and detail caused by the air between viewer and object. Key idea: Distance adds air: distant forms become paler, bluer, greyer, lower in contrast and softer, while foreground forms are dark, warm, saturated, contrasted and detailed.

Which ankle bone is higher: inner or outer?

The inner (medial malleolus) is higher than the outer. From “Anatomy for artists” →

In plain English: Artistic anatomy is the study of the skeleton and muscles that shape the surface of the body. Key idea: Bony landmarks are where the skeleton shows at the surface; they give fixed points for measuring and constructing a figure, while muscles shape the forms between them.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Drawing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which purpose of drawing does a storyboard for an animation mainly serve? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Communication and planning of a sequence. A storyboard plans and communicates the sequence and framing of shots to others.

  2. Which pencil would give the lightest, finest line? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    2H. H pencils are harder and lighter; B pencils are softer and darker.

  3. Which of these is an expressive rather than a descriptive use of marks? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Violent scribbles conveying anger in a figure study. Descriptive marks represent form or texture; expressive marks convey feeling.

  4. A student has only highly finished drawings copied from photographs. What would best improve evidence of the creative process? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Add quick observational sketches, thumbnails and annotated studies showing decisions. Process is evidenced by exploratory drawing and annotation that show thinking and recording, not only finish.

  5. A student wants to draw a large figure quickly and make bold corrections. Which medium suits best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Willow charcoal on large paper. Willow charcoal covers large areas fast and lifts easily; a hard pencil or pen is slow and hard to correct.

  6. A drawing of a landscape looks uniform. What is the most effective improvement? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Use different marks for sky, foliage, water and ground. Differentiated marks separate surfaces and planes, as in Van Gogh's reed-pen drawings.

  7. Which is the most accurate evaluation of Betty Edwards's approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Her perceptual exercises are useful, though her right-brain explanation is not supported by neuroscience. The exercises (edges, spaces, relationships) help many learners; the hemispheric explanation is an outdated simplification.

  8. Why would a Renaissance draughtsman draw in black chalk with white highlights on blue paper? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The toned paper acts as the mid-tone, so dark and light can model form quickly. A mid-tone ground lets the artist add shadow and highlight, modelling form efficiently.

  9. Why does Frank Auerbach's repeated scraping and redrawing matter to the meaning of his charcoal heads? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The worked surface records time and the struggle of looking, so process becomes part of the content. The layered, erased marks show the history of making and looking, part of what the drawings express.

Key takeaways

  • Drawing is a way of thinking as much as a way of picturing: the same mark-making serves observation, invention, planning and communication.
  • Choose the medium for the job: hard graphite for precision, soft graphite and charcoal for tone and expression, ink for decisive line and wash, pastel for drawing in colour.
  • Every mark is a trace of an action; varying tool, pressure, speed and direction gives a drawing both descriptive information and expressive character.
  • Measure relationships, not absolute sizes: one unit compared with others, with a locked arm and one eye closed, keeps proportions consistent.
  • Contour exercises train the eye to lead the hand: the drawing becomes a record of looking, and line quality grows more varied and sensitive.
  • Drawing the spaces between and around things is often more accurate than drawing the things, because negative shapes carry no preconceptions.

Common mistakes in Drawing

  • Thinking drawing must be realistic to count. Diagrams, gestural sketches, maps, rubbings and annotated thumbnails are all drawing, and often evidence thinking better than a polished copy.
  • Using one pencil for everything. A single HB cannot give both fine construction lines and deep darks; a range of grades widens the tonal scale.
  • Using the same mark everywhere, for example even outlines filled with uniform shading. Differentiated marks separate materials, planes and distances.
  • Bending the elbow or moving closer between measurements. The unit changes size, so proportions drift; also, measuring with the pencil tilted towards you distorts lengths.
  • Judging blind contour drawings by likeness. Their purpose is to break the habit of drawing symbols and to slow looking down; distortion is expected.
  • Treating the background as leftover. Unconsidered negative space often produces awkward shapes and cramped compositions; plan it with the positive shapes.

Lessons in this school

  1. Drawing as thinking and recording
    Drawing is the foundation of art and design practice: a way of seeing, thinking, planning and communicating, not only of making finished pictures.
  2. Drawing materials: graphite, charcoal, ink and pastel
    Each drawing medium has its own range of marks, tones and handling.
  3. Mark-making
    Mark-making is the vocabulary of lines, dots, strokes and textures an artist produces with a tool.
  4. Measuring and sighting
    Sighting is the practice of measuring proportions and angles by eye with a pencil held at arm's length.
  5. Contour and blind contour drawing
    Contour drawing follows the edges of a form slowly and attentively; blind contour drawing does so without looking at the paper.
  6. Negative space
    Negative space is the space around and between objects.
  7. Tonal drawing and chiaroscuro
    Tonal drawing builds form with masses of light and dark rather than lines.
  8. Hatching and cross-hatching
    Hatching builds tone with parallel lines; cross-hatching layers sets of lines at angles.
  9. One-point perspective
    Linear perspective is a geometrical system for showing depth on a flat surface, in which parallel lines receding from the viewer converge on vanishing points.
  10. Two- and three-point perspective
    When a viewer sees an object corner-on, its receding edges converge on two vanishing points; looking steeply up or down adds a third.
  11. Atmospheric perspective
    Atmospheric or aerial perspective shows distance through changes in colour, tone and detail caused by the air between viewer and object.
  12. Foreshortening
    Foreshortening is the apparent shortening of a form that points towards or away from the viewer.
  13. Drawing the figure: proportion and gesture
    Figure drawing combines gesture, which captures movement and weight, with proportion, which captures the relative sizes of the body's parts.
  14. Anatomy for artists
    Artistic anatomy is the study of the skeleton and muscles that shape the surface of the body.
  15. Drawing the portrait
    Portrait drawing depends on accurate proportions of the head and on capturing individual character.
  16. Expressive and experimental drawing
    Expressive and experimental drawing moves beyond accurate depiction to explore materials, processes, scale, chance and the body.
  17. Drawing from imagination and memory
    Drawing from imagination and memory builds images that are not in front of the artist.

Drawing: questions students ask

Which assessment objective does drawing most directly evidence?

AO3, recording ideas, observations and insights.

What does the B in a pencil grade stand for?

Black: B pencils are softer and darker; higher numbers are softer still.

What is stippling?

Building tone or texture with many small dots: closer dots give darker tone.

What is a blind contour drawing?

A drawing that follows a subject's edges while looking only at the subject, never at the paper.
