Key terms and formulas at a glance

Matrix
The block, plate, stone or screen from which a print is made
Relief print
A print made from ink on the raised surface of a block, with cut-away areas printing white
Intaglio
A family of printmaking in which ink is held in lines or areas below the plate's surface
Lithography
A planographic printing method based on the repulsion of grease and water
Stencil
A material that blocks ink from passing through parts of the screen
Monotype
A unique print made by transferring an image painted or inked on a smooth, unworked surface
Subtractive process
Making a form by removing material, as in carving
Modelling
An additive process of building and shaping a form in a soft material such as clay or wax
Mould
A negative impression into which material is poured to make a cast
Construction
Making sculpture by joining separate parts and materials
Installation art
Art that transforms a space into an environment experienced by the viewer
Land art
Art made in and from the landscape, often large-scale or ephemeral, from the late 1960s

What does 7/40 on a print mean?

It is the seventh impression numbered in an edition of forty. From “What a print is: editions and proofs” →

In plain English: A fine art print is an original work made by transferring ink from a matrix (block, plate, stone or screen) to paper, usually in a numbered edition. Key idea: Printmaking families are defined by where the ink sits on the matrix: raised (relief), below the surface (intaglio), flat (planographic) or through a stencil (screen).

Who invented lithography?

Alois Senefelder, in Bavaria in the late 1790s. From “Lithography” →

In plain English: Lithography prints from a flat surface, using the fact that grease and water repel each other. Key idea: Lithography works because grease and water repel: a greasy drawing on a flat, dampened stone accepts ink while the wet areas reject it.

What is a pointing machine used for?

Transferring measurements from a model to a block so that a carving can reproduce it. From “Carving” →

In plain English: Carving is a subtractive process: the sculptor removes material from a block of stone, wood or other substance to reveal a form. Key idea: Carving is subtractive: form is found by removing material, so the sculptor must plan the whole from the start and work with the block's nature and limits.

Why was Picasso's Guitar (1912–14) significant?

It built a sculpture from flat planes and open space rather than carving or modelling a solid mass. From “Construction and assemblage” →

In plain English: Construction builds sculpture by joining parts, and assemblage combines found objects. Key idea: Construction builds sculpture from parts, opening it to space, line and industrial materials; assemblage adds found objects with their existing meanings.

What is leather-hard clay?

Clay dried enough to hold its shape and be joined, but still slightly damp. From “Ceramics” →

In plain English: Ceramics are objects made from clay and hardened by firing. Key idea: Ceramics are clay transformed by heat: forming (pinch, coil, slab, throw, cast), drying, a first bisque firing, glazing and a glaze firing, each stage chosen for clay body and purpose.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In which printmaking family does ink sit in grooves below the surface? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intaglio. Intaglio prints are made from ink held in incised or etched lines.

  2. In a linocut, which areas print white? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The areas cut away. Relief prints transfer ink from the raised surface; cut-away areas receive no ink.

  3. Which intaglio technique uses a needle to scratch the plate and throws up a burr? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Drypoint. Drypoint's burr holds ink and gives soft lines; engraving cuts clean grooves with a burin.

  4. A collector is offered a signed "print" that is a photographic reproduction of a painting. How should it be described? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A reproduction, not an original print. An original print is made from a matrix created by the artist; a signature does not make a reproduction original.

  5. A student's linocut prints the word "SEA" as "AES" reversed. What went wrong? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The lettering was not drawn in reverse on the block. Prints are mirror images, so text must be reversed on the block.

  6. An etcher wants some lines darker than others on the same plate. What should they do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stop out lighter lines and bite the darker ones for longer. Biting time controls depth; deeper lines hold more ink and print darker.

  7. Why might different impressions of a Rembrandt etching look significantly different? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They may come from different states of the plate, or differ in inking and wiping. States record changes to the plate; inking and wiping also vary between impressions.

  8. Why did German Expressionist artists favour the woodcut? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its raw, angular cuts and wood grain suited direct emotional expression and recalled earlier German prints. The medium's resistance and visible marks matched Expressionist aims and national tradition.

  9. Why did Goya use aquatint in Los Caprichos? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its areas of granular tone created dark, atmospheric settings for his satirical scenes. Aquatint gave tonal depth and mood beyond line etching.

Key takeaways

  • Printmaking families are defined by where the ink sits on the matrix: raised (relief), below the surface (intaglio), flat (planographic) or through a stencil (screen).
  • Relief prints come from the raised surface: whatever is cut away prints white, so the artist thinks in black and white masses and in reverse.
  • In intaglio, ink is held in grooves below the surface: cut with a burin (engraving), scratched (drypoint) or bitten by acid (etching, aquatint), then wiped clean and printed under heavy pressure.
  • Lithography works because grease and water repel: a greasy drawing on a flat, dampened stone accepts ink while the wet areas reject it.
  • Screen printing forces ink through open mesh with a squeegee; stencils, from cut paper to photo emulsion, block the rest, and each colour is a separate layer in register.
  • Monotypes are unique prints from an unworked surface; monoprints are unique variations from a partly fixed matrix. Both reward speed, experiment and chance.

Common mistakes in Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D

  • Confusing an original print with a reproduction. An original print is made by the artist (or under their supervision) from a matrix they created; a giclée or offset reproduction of a painting is a copy, even if signed.
  • Forgetting that the print is reversed. Lettering and asymmetrical compositions must be drawn backwards on the block.
  • Handling acid without proper safety measures. Etching uses corrosive mordants; in schools it is done only with supervision, ventilation, gloves and eye protection, often with safer ferric chloride or non-toxic alternatives.
  • Thinking lithography involves cutting the surface. It is planographic: the image is chemical, not physical, which is why it reproduces drawn marks so freely.
  • Letting ink dry in the screen. Water-based screen inks dry quickly and block the mesh; keep the screen flooded between pulls and wash it promptly.
  • Rolling the ink too thick. Heavy ink transfers everywhere and fills the drawing; a thin, tacky layer gives clear lines.

Lessons in this school

  1. What a print is: editions and proofs
    A fine art print is an original work made by transferring ink from a matrix (block, plate, stone or screen) to paper, usually in a numbered edition.
  2. Relief printing: woodcut and linocut
    In relief printing, the artist cuts away the areas that will print white and inks the raised surface.
  3. Intaglio: etching and engraving
    Intaglio prints are made from lines and tones cut or bitten into a metal plate, inked into the grooves and printed under heavy pressure.
  4. Lithography
    Lithography prints from a flat surface, using the fact that grease and water repel each other.
  5. Screen printing
    Screen printing pushes ink through a fine mesh where a stencil leaves it open.
  6. Monoprinting
    Monoprinting and monotype produce unique prints rather than editions.
  7. Carving
    Carving is a subtractive process: the sculptor removes material from a block of stone, wood or other substance to reveal a form.
  8. Modelling
    Modelling is an additive process in which a soft material such as clay or wax is built up and shaped.
  9. Casting
    Casting reproduces a form by pouring liquid material into a mould.
  10. Construction and assemblage
    Construction builds sculpture by joining parts, and assemblage combines found objects.
  11. Installation art
    Installation art creates an environment or arrangement that the viewer enters or moves around, often for a specific space and time.
  12. Site-specific and land art
    Site-specific art is made for and depends on a particular place; land art uses the landscape itself as material and setting.
  13. Ceramics
    Ceramics are objects made from clay and hardened by firing.
  14. Textiles and fibre art
    Textile art and design work with fibres, fabrics and threads through weaving, printing, dyeing, stitching and construction.
  15. Product and 3D design
    Product design creates objects that people use, balancing function, form, materials, manufacture and sustainability.
  16. Architecture as an art
    Architecture is the art and science of designing buildings and spaces.

Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D: questions students ask

What is a reduction linocut?

A multi-colour print made from one block, cut away further after each colour is printed.

What creates tone in aquatint?

Acid biting through a porous ground of melted resin dust, giving a granular tone.

What is a squeegee used for in screen printing?

Drawing ink across the screen to force it through the open mesh.

What is a ghost print?

A second, fainter impression taken from ink left on the plate after the first print.
