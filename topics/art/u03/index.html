Key terms and formulas at a glance

Visible spectrum
The range of wavelengths of light visible to the human eye, roughly 380 to 750 nanometres
Additive colour
Colour produced by combining light, with red, green and blue primaries mixing towards white
Chroma
The purity or intensity of a colour, from vivid to grey; similar to saturation
Secondary colour
A colour made by mixing two primaries on a given colour wheel
Munsell system
A colour system that describes colours by hue, value and chroma in perceptually even steps
Pigment
A finely ground coloured substance, insoluble in its binder, that gives paint its colour
Analogous colours
Hues adjacent on the colour wheel
Simultaneous contrast
The effect by which adjacent colours alter each other's appearance, each pushing its neighbour towards its complement
Contrast of extension
Contrast created by the relative areas of colours, used to balance their strengths
Colour temperature
The perceived warmth or coolness of a colour, judged relative to other colours
Push and pull
Hans Hofmann's term for the illusion of advancing and receding colour planes on a flat surface
Colour symbolism
The meanings a culture associates with particular colours

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why does a red apple look red?

Its surface absorbs most wavelengths and reflects mainly long (red) ones, which the eye's cones detect. From “Light and colour perception” →

In plain English: Colour is not a property of objects alone but an experience produced by light, surfaces and the visual system together. Key idea: Colour is made by light, surface and the visual system together: surfaces reflect some wavelengths, three cone types respond, and the brain compares signals and adjusts for context.

Who first arranged the colours of the spectrum in a circle?

Isaac Newton, in Opticks (1704). From “The colour wheel” →

In plain English: The colour wheel arranges hues in a circle to show their relationships. Key idea: The painter's wheel arranges twelve hues from three primaries; it shows complementary, analogous and warm-cool relationships at a glance.

What is a monochromatic scheme?

A scheme using tints, tones and shades of a single hue. From “Colour harmonies” →

In plain English: Colour harmonies are combinations of hues chosen by their positions on the colour wheel. Key idea: A harmony scheme chooses hues by their relationships on the wheel; it works when one colour dominates, others support, and an accent is used sparingly.

What does "warm light, cool shadow" mean?

In sunlight, lit areas look warm and shadows cool because they receive blue skylight. From “Warm and cool colour” →

In plain English: Colour temperature describes whether a colour feels warm (towards red-orange) or cool (towards blue-green). Key idea: Temperature is relative: every colour is warmer or cooler than its neighbours, and painters use these shifts to model light, form and space.

What is colour coding?

Using colours consistently to classify or signal information, as in an Underground map. From “Colour in design and branding” →

In plain English: Designers use colour to identify brands, organise information and guide action. Key idea: In design, give each colour a job (identity, background, text, action, status) and use it consistently; a small, disciplined palette communicates better than many colours.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Colour Theory

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How many types of cone does the typical human retina contain? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Three. Trichromatic vision depends on short, medium and long wavelength cones.

  2. What colour do red and green light make when combined? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Yellow. Additive mixing of red and green light produces yellow; pigments of those colours would mix to a dark brownish colour.

  3. Adding white to a colour produces a… (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Tint. A tint is a hue plus white; a shade adds black; a tone adds grey.

  4. A painter's colours matched perfectly in the studio but look different under gallery spotlights. What explains this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Metamerism: the pigments reflect differently under the new light. Colours that match under one illuminant can differ under another because their reflectance curves differ.

  5. A logo designed on screen in a vivid RGB orange prints dull. What is the most likely cause? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The colour lies outside the CMYK printing gamut. Highly saturated RGB colours often cannot be reproduced with CMYK inks.

  6. A painter wants a natural grey-green for distant trees. What is the best mix? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Green with a little of its complement, red, adjusted with white. A complement lowers chroma while keeping a lively grey; black can deaden and shift the hue.

  7. What does the disagreement over "the dress" photograph (2015) best show? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Perceived colour depends on the brain's assumptions about lighting, not only on the wavelengths reaching the eye. The ambiguous illumination led different visual systems to correct the same pixels differently.

  8. Why do Seurat's dots of pure colour not simply make dark mixtures as the same paints would on a palette? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dots side by side mix optically in the eye, averaging lightness, rather than subtracting light as mixed pigments do. Partitive mixing occurs in perception; physical mixing of pigments subtracts more wavelengths and darkens.

  9. Why does the sun in Monet's Impression, Sunrise seem to shimmer? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is close in value to the surrounding sky, so it differs in hue but not luminance. Livingstone showed the near-equal luminance confuses the brain's position system, causing instability.

Key takeaways

  • Colour is made by light, surface and the visual system together: surfaces reflect some wavelengths, three cone types respond, and the brain compares signals and adjusts for context.
  • Lights add (RGB, towards white); pigments and inks subtract (CMY, towards black). The two systems are complementary: each additive primary is the colour absorbed by one subtractive primary.
  • Hue, value and chroma vary independently. Painters change chroma with complements or grey, value with white or darker colours, and check value first because it carries form.
  • The painter's wheel arranges twelve hues from three primaries; it shows complementary, analogous and warm-cool relationships at a glance.
  • A colour system gives a precise address in three dimensions (hue, value, chroma) so that colours can be specified, compared and reproduced consistently.
  • Know your pigments: their bias, transparency, strength and permanence decide how they mix and how a painting will last.

Common mistakes in Colour Theory

  • Thinking an object "has" one true colour. A painter must observe the colour actually seen under the light and next to its neighbours, not the remembered local colour.
  • Telling students that the "real" primaries are red, yellow and blue in every context. RYB is a traditional painter's convention that works roughly with pigments; it is not the primary set for light (RGB) or for process printing (CMY).
  • Darkening every colour with black. Black can deaden colours and shift yellows towards green; darker neighbouring hues or complements often give richer shadows.
  • Expecting perfectly saturated secondaries from any red, yellow and blue. Real pigments are impure: a warm cadmium red and ultramarine make a dull violet; a cool quinacridone magenta and ultramarine make a clean one. Paint makers' "split primary" palettes (a warm and a cool of each primary) address this.
  • Assuming a hex code will look the same everywhere. Screens differ in calibration and colour space (sRGB, Display P3), and print conversions shift colours; specification needs a system and a check.
  • Over-mixing on the palette or mixing too many pigments. Three or more pigments with opposite biases quickly make mud; mix with as few pigments as possible.

Lessons in this school

  1. Light and colour perception
    Colour is not a property of objects alone but an experience produced by light, surfaces and the visual system together.
  2. Additive and subtractive colour
    Colour can be mixed by adding lights or by subtracting wavelengths with pigments, dyes and inks.
  3. Hue, value and saturation
    Any colour can be described by three properties: hue, value and saturation (chroma).
  4. The colour wheel
    The colour wheel arranges hues in a circle to show their relationships.
  5. Munsell and other colour systems
    Colour systems give every colour a precise address so it can be specified, matched and communicated.
  6. Pigments and colour mixing
    Paint colours come from pigments with their own chemistry, history and handling.
  7. Colour harmonies
    Colour harmonies are combinations of hues chosen by their positions on the colour wheel.
  8. Complementary colours and simultaneous contrast
    Colours change appearance according to their neighbours.
  9. Itten's seven colour contrasts
    Johannes Itten, who taught the preliminary course at the Bauhaus, classified seven kinds of colour contrast.
  10. Warm and cool colour
    Colour temperature describes whether a colour feels warm (towards red-orange) or cool (towards blue-green).
  11. Colour and space
    Colour can create, flatten or contradict pictorial space.
  12. Colour symbolism and culture
    Colours carry meanings, but those meanings come from culture, history, materials and context rather than from colour itself.
  13. Colour in design and branding
    Designers use colour to identify brands, organise information and guide action.
  14. Albers and the interaction of colour
    Josef Albers taught that colour is the most relative medium in art.
  15. Colour and accessibility
    About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour vision deficiency, and many more people read in poor light or on poor screens.

Colour Theory: questions students ask

What are the additive primaries?

Red, green and blue (RGB).

What is a tone, in colour mixing?

A colour mixed with grey, lowering its saturation.

What are the three dimensions of Munsell's system?

Hue, value and chroma.

What is lightfastness?

A pigment's resistance to fading when exposed to light.
