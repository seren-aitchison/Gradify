Why does a red apple look red?

Its surface absorbs most wavelengths and reflects mainly long (red) ones, which the eye's cones detect. From “Light and colour perception” →

Who first arranged the colours of the spectrum in a circle?

Isaac Newton, in Opticks (1704). From “The colour wheel” →

What is a monochromatic scheme?

A scheme using tints, tones and shades of a single hue. From “Colour harmonies” →

What does "warm light, cool shadow" mean?

In sunlight, lit areas look warm and shadows cool because they receive blue skylight. From “Warm and cool colour” →

What is colour coding?

Using colours consistently to classify or signal information, as in an Underground map. From “Colour in design and branding” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Light and colour perception
    Colour is not a property of objects alone but an experience produced by light, surfaces and the visual system together.
  2. Additive and subtractive colour
    Colour can be mixed by adding lights or by subtracting wavelengths with pigments, dyes and inks.
  3. Hue, value and saturation
    Any colour can be described by three properties: hue, value and saturation (chroma).
  4. The colour wheel
    The colour wheel arranges hues in a circle to show their relationships.
  5. Munsell and other colour systems
    Colour systems give every colour a precise address so it can be specified, matched and communicated.
  6. Pigments and colour mixing
    Paint colours come from pigments with their own chemistry, history and handling.
  7. Colour harmonies
    Colour harmonies are combinations of hues chosen by their positions on the colour wheel.
  8. Complementary colours and simultaneous contrast
    Colours change appearance according to their neighbours.
  9. Itten's seven colour contrasts
    Johannes Itten, who taught the preliminary course at the Bauhaus, classified seven kinds of colour contrast.
  10. Warm and cool colour
    Colour temperature describes whether a colour feels warm (towards red-orange) or cool (towards blue-green).
  11. Colour and space
    Colour can create, flatten or contradict pictorial space.
  12. Colour symbolism and culture
    Colours carry meanings, but those meanings come from culture, history, materials and context rather than from colour itself.
  13. Colour in design and branding
    Designers use colour to identify brands, organise information and guide action.
  14. Albers and the interaction of colour
    Josef Albers taught that colour is the most relative medium in art.
  15. Colour and accessibility
    About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour vision deficiency, and many more people read in poor light or on poor screens.

Key terms

Visible spectrum
The range of wavelengths of light visible to the human eye, roughly 380 to 750 nanometres
Additive colour
Colour produced by combining light, with red, green and blue primaries mixing towards white
Chroma
The purity or intensity of a colour, from vivid to grey; similar to saturation
Secondary colour
A colour made by mixing two primaries on a given colour wheel
Munsell system
A colour system that describes colours by hue, value and chroma in perceptually even steps
Pigment
A finely ground coloured substance, insoluble in its binder, that gives paint its colour
Analogous colours
Hues adjacent on the colour wheel
Simultaneous contrast
The effect by which adjacent colours alter each other's appearance, each pushing its neighbour towards its complement
Contrast of extension
Contrast created by the relative areas of colours, used to balance their strengths
Colour temperature
The perceived warmth or coolness of a colour, judged relative to other colours
Push and pull
Hans Hofmann's term for the illusion of advancing and receding colour planes on a flat surface
Colour symbolism
The meanings a culture associates with particular colours
Brand colour
A colour consistently used to identify an organisation or product
Relativity of colour
The principle that a colour's appearance depends on the colours around it
