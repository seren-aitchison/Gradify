Key terms and formulas at a glance

Population
All the individuals of one species in a given area at a given time, able to interbreed
Density
The number of individuals per unit area or volume
Dispersion
The spatial pattern of individuals within a population
Lincoln–Petersen estimate
N = (M × C) ÷ R, the basic mark–release–recapture estimate of population size
Per capita rate
A rate expressed per individual in the population
Exponential growth
Growth at a constant per capita rate, so the population increases by a constant proportion per unit time
Carrying capacity
The maximum population size an environment can sustain indefinitely, written K
Density-dependent factor
A factor whose effect on per capita birth or death rate changes with population density
Population cycle
A regular, repeated rise and fall in population size with a consistent period
Survivorship (lx)
The proportion of a cohort still alive at age x
Life-history strategy
The pattern of resource allocation to growth, survival and reproduction over an organism's life
Age structure
The proportion of individuals in each age class of a population

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between a population and a community?

A population is one species in an area; a community is all the interacting populations of different species there. From “What is a population?” →

In plain English: A population is a group of individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time and able to interbreed. Key idea: A population is one species in one place at one time; its boundaries are set by how much individuals move and by the purpose of the study.

Why must marks not make animals more visible to predators?

Marked animals would die faster, so fewer would be recaptured and N would be overestimated. From “Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture” →

In plain English: Mark–release–recapture estimates the size of a mobile animal population from the proportion of marked animals in a second sample. Key idea: Mark–release–recapture estimates N from the proportion marked in a second sample; it is only valid if the population is closed, marks persist and every animal is equally catchable.

Give two density-dependent factors.

Any two of: competition, disease and parasites, predation, waste accumulation. From “Density-dependent and density-independent factors” →

In plain English: Some factors that affect birth and death rates get stronger as a population becomes denser; others act regardless of density. Key idea: Density-dependent factors strengthen as density rises and can regulate a population; density-independent factors act regardless of density and cause fluctuations but not regulation.

What are the three strategies in Grime's CSR scheme?

Competitors, stress tolerators and ruderals. From “Life-history strategies: r and K selection” →

In plain English: A life-history strategy is how an organism allocates resources to growth, survival and reproduction over its life. Key idea: Life histories are shaped by trade-offs; the r–K continuum contrasts fast reproduction in unstable habitats with competitive, long-lived strategies in stable ones, but is a simplification.

In which stage of the demographic transition is population growth fastest?

Stage 2, when death rates have fallen but birth rates remain high. From “The demographic transition” →

In plain English: The demographic transition model describes how countries move from high birth and death rates to low ones as they develop. Key idea: The demographic transition moves a population from high to low birth and death rates; the gap while death rates fall first causes rapid growth.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Population Ecology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a population? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    All the red squirrels on the Isle of Wight. A population is a single species in a defined area; the others include several species.

  2. Which method suits estimating the density of a plant on a meadow? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Random quadrat sampling. Plants don't move, so quadrats placed at random give a direct estimate of density.

  3. Which pattern describes a herd of wildebeest? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Clumped. Social groups such as herds produce clumped dispersion.

  4. Salmon from one river return to breed there and rarely stray to other rivers. How should they be treated? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    As a separate population from salmon in other rivers. Fidelity to the home river limits gene flow, so each river's salmon form a distinct population.

  5. Five 0.25 m2 quadrats contain 3, 1, 2, 4 and 0 snails. What is the mean density per m2? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    8 per m2. The mean is 10 ÷ 5 = 2 snails per 0.25 m2, which is 2 × 4 = 8 per m2.

  6. Quadrat counts of a shrub have a mean of 4 and a variance of 1.2. What pattern is suggested? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Uniform. A variance-to-mean ratio of 0.3 is well below 1, which suggests regular spacing, as from competition for water.

  7. An ecologist counts a grass that spreads by underground runners. What is the main difficulty in measuring population size? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Deciding what counts as an individual, since many shoots may be one genetic clone. In clonal plants, many shoots (ramets) can belong to one genetic individual (genet), so population size depends on the unit chosen.

  8. Two woods have the same number of dormice, but in wood A only half the area has hazel scrub. What follows? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ecological density is higher in wood A, though crude density is the same. With the same number and total area, crude density is equal, but dormice in A are packed into half the habitat, doubling ecological density.

  9. Penguins are evenly spaced within each colony, yet a survey of the whole coastline shows strong clumping. What explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dispersion depends on scale: uniform within colonies, clumped between them. Pecking-distance spacing gives uniformity at small scale; colonies at suitable sites give clumping at large scale.

Key takeaways

  • A population is one species in one place at one time; its boundaries are set by how much individuals move and by the purpose of the study.
  • Density is size per unit area; sampling methods such as quadrats estimate it from a random subset, and the estimate is scaled to the whole area.
  • Dispersion is clumped, uniform or random; comparing the variance and mean of quadrat counts tests which, with a ratio above 1 indicating clumping.
  • Mark–release–recapture estimates N from the proportion marked in a second sample; it is only valid if the population is closed, marks persist and every animal is equally catchable.
  • Change in population size is births plus immigration minus deaths and emigration; per capita rates allow comparison and give the growth rate r = b − d.
  • Exponential growth means a constant per capita rate and an ever-increasing absolute rate; it produces a J-shaped curve and can only continue while resources are effectively unlimited.

Common mistakes in Population Ecology

  • Calling all the animals in a habitat a population. Several species together form a community; a population is always a single species.
  • Placing quadrats where the organisms look most common. Non-random placement biases the estimate; random coordinates or a systematic grid avoid this.
  • Forgetting scale. Gannets are uniformly spaced within a colony, but colonies are clumped along coasts. The pattern you find depends on quadrat size and study area.
  • Ignoring trap-happy or trap-shy animals. If marked animals return to bait (trap-happy), R is too high and N is underestimated; if they avoid traps (trap-shy), N is overestimated.
  • Treating a constant number of births as a constant birth rate. If the population grows and the per capita rate stays the same, the number of births rises each year.
  • Confusing a constant growth rate with a constant number added each year. Exponential growth adds a constant proportion, so the number added rises every year.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is a population?
    A population is a group of individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time and able to interbreed.
  2. Population size and density
    Population size is how many individuals there are; density is how many there are per unit area or volume.
  3. Dispersion patterns
    Dispersion is how individuals are spaced within a population's range.
  4. Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture
    Mark–release–recapture estimates the size of a mobile animal population from the proportion of marked animals in a second sample.
  5. Births, deaths, immigration and emigration
    Populations change through four processes: births and immigration add individuals; deaths and emigration remove them.
  6. Exponential growth
    With unlimited resources, a population grows at a constant per capita rate, so it increases faster and faster.
  7. Logistic growth and carrying capacity
    As a population grows, resources per individual fall and growth slows towards a ceiling, the carrying capacity.
  8. Density-dependent and density-independent factors
    Some factors that affect birth and death rates get stronger as a population becomes denser; others act regardless of density.
  9. Population cycles and fluctuations
    Some populations rise and fall in regular cycles; others fluctuate irregularly.
  10. Life tables and survivorship curves
    Life tables record how many individuals survive and die at each age.
  11. Life-history strategies: r and K selection
    A life-history strategy is how an organism allocates resources to growth, survival and reproduction over its life.
  12. Age structure
    Age structure is the proportion of individuals in each age class.
  13. Metapopulations
    A metapopulation is a set of local populations in separate habitat patches, linked by occasional dispersal.
  14. Human population growth
    The human population took until about 1800 to reach one billion and passed eight billion in 2022.
  15. The demographic transition
    The demographic transition model describes how countries move from high birth and death rates to low ones as they develop.
  16. Small populations and extinction risk
    Small populations face extra risks that can drive them to extinction even when the original threat has gone.
  17. Harvesting and maximum sustainable yield
    Harvesting fish, game or timber removes individuals from a population that can replace them.
  18. Population viability analysis
    Population viability analysis (PVA) uses models to estimate the chance that a population will survive for a given time.

Population Ecology: questions students ask

What is crude density?

The number of individuals per unit of total area, whether or not it is suitable habitat.

What causes uniform dispersion?

Interactions that push individuals apart, such as territoriality or competition for resources.

What does R stand for in the Lincoln–Petersen estimate?

The number of marked animals recaptured in the second sample.

What does a closed population lack?

Immigration and emigration: it changes only through births and deaths.
