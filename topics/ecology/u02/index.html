What is the difference between a population and a community?

A population is one species in an area; a community is all the interacting populations of different species there. From “What is a population?” →

Why must marks not make animals more visible to predators?

Marked animals would die faster, so fewer would be recaptured and N would be overestimated. From “Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture” →

Give two density-dependent factors.

Any two of: competition, disease and parasites, predation, waste accumulation. From “Density-dependent and density-independent factors” →

What are the three strategies in Grime's CSR scheme?

Competitors, stress tolerators and ruderals. From “Life-history strategies: r and K selection” →

In which stage of the demographic transition is population growth fastest?

Lessons in this school

  1. What is a population?
    A population is a group of individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time and able to interbreed.
  2. Population size and density
    Population size is how many individuals there are; density is how many there are per unit area or volume.
  3. Dispersion patterns
    Dispersion is how individuals are spaced within a population's range.
  4. Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture
    Mark–release–recapture estimates the size of a mobile animal population from the proportion of marked animals in a second sample.
  5. Births, deaths, immigration and emigration
    Populations change through four processes: births and immigration add individuals; deaths and emigration remove them.
  6. Exponential growth
    With unlimited resources, a population grows at a constant per capita rate, so it increases faster and faster.
  7. Logistic growth and carrying capacity
    As a population grows, resources per individual fall and growth slows towards a ceiling, the carrying capacity.
  8. Density-dependent and density-independent factors
    Some factors that affect birth and death rates get stronger as a population becomes denser; others act regardless of density.
  9. Population cycles and fluctuations
    Some populations rise and fall in regular cycles; others fluctuate irregularly.
  10. Life tables and survivorship curves
    Life tables record how many individuals survive and die at each age.
  11. Life-history strategies: r and K selection
    A life-history strategy is how an organism allocates resources to growth, survival and reproduction over its life.
  12. Age structure
    Age structure is the proportion of individuals in each age class.
  13. Metapopulations
    A metapopulation is a set of local populations in separate habitat patches, linked by occasional dispersal.
  14. Human population growth
    The human population took until about 1800 to reach one billion and passed eight billion in 2022.
  15. The demographic transition
    The demographic transition model describes how countries move from high birth and death rates to low ones as they develop.
  16. Small populations and extinction risk
    Small populations face extra risks that can drive them to extinction even when the original threat has gone.
  17. Harvesting and maximum sustainable yield
    Harvesting fish, game or timber removes individuals from a population that can replace them.
  18. Population viability analysis
    Population viability analysis (PVA) uses models to estimate the chance that a population will survive for a given time.

Key terms

Population
All the individuals of one species in a given area at a given time, able to interbreed
Density
The number of individuals per unit area or volume
Dispersion
The spatial pattern of individuals within a population
Lincoln–Petersen estimate
N = (M × C) ÷ R, the basic mark–release–recapture estimate of population size
Per capita rate
A rate expressed per individual in the population
Exponential growth
Growth at a constant per capita rate, so the population increases by a constant proportion per unit time
Carrying capacity
The maximum population size an environment can sustain indefinitely, written K
Density-dependent factor
A factor whose effect on per capita birth or death rate changes with population density
Population cycle
A regular, repeated rise and fall in population size with a consistent period
Survivorship (lx)
The proportion of a cohort still alive at age x
Life-history strategy
The pattern of resource allocation to growth, survival and reproduction over an organism's life
Age structure
The proportion of individuals in each age class of a population
Metapopulation
A set of local populations in habitat patches linked by dispersal
Total fertility rate
The average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime at current age-specific birth rates
