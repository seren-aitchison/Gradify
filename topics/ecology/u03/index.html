Key terms and formulas at a glance

Competitive exclusion principle

Fundamental niche
The full range of conditions and resources a species could use without competitors
Aposematism
Warning coloration signalling that prey is toxic or dangerous
Numerical response
The change in predator population size in response to changing prey density
Secondary metabolite
A plant chemical not needed for basic metabolism, often used in defence
Macroparasite
A large parasite that grows but usually doesn't multiply within its host
Mutualism
An interaction in which both species gain a net benefit
Commensalism
An interaction where one species benefits and the other is unaffected
Ecological community
All the populations of different species in an area that interact with each other
Keystone species
A species with a disproportionately large effect on its community relative to its abundance
Species richness
The number of different species in a community

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is interference competition?

Direct competition through fighting, territorial defence or chemical inhibition. From “Intraspecific and interspecific competition” →

In plain English: Competition occurs when organisms use the same limited resource and harm each other in the process. Key idea: Competition can be within or between species and can act through resource use (exploitation) or direct contact (interference); removal experiments show how it limits where species live.

Why does a type II functional response level off?

Because each prey item takes handling time, limiting how many a predator can eat. From “Predation” →

In plain English: Predation is the consumption of one organism by another. Key idea: Predators range from true predators to parasitoids; prey use crypsis, warning colours, mimicry and other defences; functional responses describe how consumption changes with prey density.

What do plants gain from mycorrhizal fungi?

Water and nutrients, especially phosphorus. From “Mutualism” →

In plain English: Mutualism is an interaction in which both species benefit. Key idea: Mutualisms benefit both partners through nutrition, protection or dispersal; many, such as mycorrhizae and pollination, underpin ecosystems, and they can break down under environmental stress.

What is a trophic cascade?

Effects of a predator spreading down the food chain through its prey to lower trophic levels. From “Keystone species and trophic cascades” →

In plain English: Some species have effects on their communities far greater than their abundance would suggest. Key idea: Keystone species have large effects relative to their abundance; predators can trigger trophic cascades that change plant communities, though evidence is strongest in simpler systems.

What is the role of marram grass in sand dune succession?

It traps and stabilises sand by growing up through it, building yellow dunes. From “Primary and secondary succession” →

In plain English: Succession can begin on bare ground with no soil or on land where soil remains after disturbance. Key idea: Primary succession starts without soil and is slow; secondary succession starts with soil, seeds and roots and is faster; sand dunes show a clear primary sequence from marram to heath.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Communities and Species Interactions

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an example of exploitation competition? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Two plant species taking up the same limited soil nitrogen. Exploitation is indirect, through using a shared resource; the others involve direct interference.

  2. Who formulated the phrase "complete competitors cannot coexist"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Garrett Hardin. Hardin's 1960 paper named and summarised the competitive exclusion principle.

  3. How did MacArthur's warblers reduce competition? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    By feeding in different parts of the same trees. They partitioned the tree spatially and behaviourally.

  4. Why is intraspecific competition often more intense than interspecific competition? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Individuals of the same species need exactly the same resources. Members of the same species have identical requirements, so overlap is complete.

  5. Why could P. caudatum and P. bursaria coexist in Gause's tubes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    P. bursaria fed in a different part of the tube, reducing overlap. Using different parts of the habitat is a form of niche differentiation.

  6. Two finch species have more different beak sizes where they live together than where each lives alone. What does this suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Character displacement caused by competition. Greater difference in sympatry is the signature of character displacement.

  7. Two species of beetle feed on the same leaves, but the leaves are always abundant. Are they competing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Not necessarily: competition needs the resource to be limiting. Overlap without a limiting resource doesn't reduce either species' fitness.

  8. A dominant competitor is kept rare by a specialist predator, allowing a weaker competitor to persist. What does this show? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Predation can prevent competitive exclusion and maintain diversity. Predators that target the dominant species can stop it excluding others.

  9. Two closely related species live in different habitats and never meet. A student says this proves competition has no role. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Their separation may reflect past competition or other adaptations, so absence of current competition doesn't rule out a role. The "ghost of competition past" may have shaped current distributions; evidence is needed either way.

Key takeaways

  • Competition can be within or between species and can act through resource use (exploitation) or direct contact (interference); removal experiments show how it limits where species live.
  • Species competing for exactly the same limiting resources can't coexist; coexistence depends on niche differences that make intraspecific competition stronger than interspecific competition.
  • Competitors coexist by partitioning resources in space, food or time; their realised niches are narrower than their fundamental niches, and competition can drive evolutionary character displacement.
  • Predators range from true predators to parasitoids; prey use crypsis, warning colours, mimicry and other defences; functional responses describe how consumption changes with prey density.
  • Predator and prey populations can oscillate, with predator peaks lagging prey peaks; refuges, habitat complexity and density dependence help both persist.
  • Herbivory reduces plant performance and shapes communities; plants respond with physical and chemical defences, constitutive or induced, indirect defences, tolerance and timing.

Common mistakes in Communities and Species Interactions

  • Assuming species that overlap in resources must be competing now. Competition only occurs if the resource is limiting; overlap alone isn't evidence.
  • Using the principle to claim no two similar species ever share a habitat. Many similar species coexist because they differ in resource use, respond to variation or are held below competitive levels by predators.
  • Assuming all differences between species are caused by present-day competition. Differences may reflect past competition ("the ghost of competition past") or unrelated adaptations.
  • Thinking Batesian mimics are protected regardless of their numbers. Protection depends on predators meeting the harmful model often enough to learn avoidance.
  • Assuming real predator–prey cycles prove the Lotka–Volterra model correct. Real cycles also involve food supply, disease and other factors; the model is a simplified starting point.
  • Assuming plant defences are free. Defences cost energy and nutrients that could be used for growth or reproduction, which is why induced defences are common.

Lessons in this school

  1. Intraspecific and interspecific competition
    Competition occurs when organisms use the same limited resource and harm each other in the process.
  2. The competitive exclusion principle
    The competitive exclusion principle states that two species competing for exactly the same limiting resources can't coexist indefinitely.
  3. Resource partitioning and niche differentiation
    Species that coexist often divide up resources between them.
  4. Predation
    Predation is the consumption of one organism by another.
  5. Predator–prey dynamics
    Predator and prey populations affect each other and can cycle together.
  6. Herbivory and plant defences
    Herbivores eat plants, and plants have evolved many defences in response.
  7. Parasitism and disease
    Parasites live on or in a host, taking resources and usually harming it without killing it quickly.
  8. Mutualism
    Mutualism is an interaction in which both species benefit.
  9. Commensalism and facilitation
    Not all interactions involve both costs and benefits.
  10. What is an ecological community?
    An ecological community is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
  11. Keystone species and trophic cascades
    Some species have effects on their communities far greater than their abundance would suggest.
  12. Species richness and evenness
    Biodiversity within a community has two parts: how many species there are and how evenly individuals are spread among them.
  13. Island biogeography
    The theory of island biogeography explains why larger, closer islands hold more species.
  14. Ecological succession
    Succession is the change in the species composition of a community over time.
  15. Primary and secondary succession
    Succession can begin on bare ground with no soil or on land where soil remains after disturbance.
  16. Climax communities and plagioclimax
    Succession tends towards a relatively stable climax community, but human activity often holds communities at earlier stages.
  17. Disturbance and the intermediate disturbance hypothesis
    Disturbances such as storms, fire and grazing disrupt communities and reset succession.
  18. Invasive non-native species
    Invasive non-native species are introduced outside their natural range and cause harm to ecosystems, the economy or health.

Communities and Species Interactions: questions students ask

What did Gause find when he grew P. aurelia and P. caudatum together?

P. aurelia drove P. caudatum to extinction.

What is the difference between the fundamental and realised niche?

The fundamental niche is what a species could use without competitors; the realised niche is what it actually uses with competitors.

What is Müllerian mimicry?

Two or more harmful species sharing a similar warning pattern.

What does the Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model predict?

Linked oscillations of predator and prey populations, with predator peaks lagging prey peaks.
