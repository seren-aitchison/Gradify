Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who coined the term ecosystem?

Arthur Tansley, in 1935. From “What is an ecosystem?” →

In plain English: An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with its physical environment, linked by flows of energy and cycles of matter. Key idea: An ecosystem is a community plus its physical environment; energy flows through it one way and is lost as heat, while matter cycles; boundaries are chosen for study and are always open.

What trophic level is a primary consumer?

Level 2. From “Trophic levels” →

In plain English: Trophic levels group organisms by their position in food chains. Key idea: Trophic levels rank organisms from producers upwards; energy losses of around 90% per step keep food chains short, and persistent pollutants biomagnify towards the top.

How is assimilation calculated?

Ingestion minus egestion. From “Secondary productivity” →

In plain English: Secondary productivity is the rate at which consumers build new biomass. Key idea: A consumer's ingested energy is partly lost in faeces and respiration, leaving production for growth and reproduction; endotherms lose most assimilated energy as heat, giving them low production efficiencies.

Why does peat form in waterlogged soils?

Low oxygen slows decomposition, so dead matter accumulates. From “Decomposition and detritus food webs” →

In plain English: Most of the energy fixed by plants is never eaten by herbivores but enters the detritus food web when organisms die. Key idea: Most NPP in many ecosystems enters detritus food webs; decomposition proceeds by leaching, fragmentation and microbial breakdown, controlled by temperature, moisture and litter quality.

Which nutrient limits productivity in high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll regions?

Iron. From “Ocean productivity” →

In plain English: The oceans produce about half of global NPP, but their productivity varies greatly. Key idea: Ocean productivity is limited by light in the photic zone and nutrients from below; upwelling, coasts and estuaries are highly productive, while open oceans are nutrient-poor; iron limits growth in HNLC regions.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Ecosystems and Energy Flow

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an abiotic component of an ecosystem? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Soil temperature. Abiotic factors are non-living, physical or chemical conditions.

  2. Which organism is a producer? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Seaweed on a rocky shore. Applying the standard result: Seaweed is a photosynthetic autotroph.

  3. In the chain grass → rabbit → fox, what does the arrow from grass to rabbit show? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Energy passing from grass to the rabbit. Arrows show the direction of energy transfer.

  4. What ultimately happens to the energy that enters an ecosystem as sunlight? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is lost as heat after passing through organisms. Energy flows one way; each transfer releases heat.

  5. What is the main difference between detritivores and decomposers? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Detritivores ingest detritus; decomposers digest it externally and absorb it. Decomposers secrete enzymes outside their bodies and absorb the products.

  6. What happened when Paine removed the starfish Pisaster from rocky shores? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Mussels dominated and species diversity fell. Without its predator, the mussel outcompeted other species for space.

  7. An ecologist studying a lake includes its whole catchment. Why might this be sensible? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Water and nutrients from the catchment strongly affect the lake. Boundaries are often drawn where flows are weakest, such as a watershed.

  8. Communities around deep-sea vents thrive with no sunlight. What supports them? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Chemoautotrophic bacteria using energy from chemical compounds. Bacteria oxidising compounds such as hydrogen sulfide are the producers.

  9. Sea otters are removed from a coastal area. What chain of effects is most likely? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Sea urchins increase and kelp forests decline. Fewer otters release urchins from predation, and urchins overgraze kelp.

Key takeaways

  • An ecosystem is a community plus its physical environment; energy flows through it one way and is lost as heat, while matter cycles; boundaries are chosen for study and are always open.
  • Producers make organic matter from inorganic sources; consumers feed on other organisms at different levels; detritivores and decomposers break down dead matter and return nutrients.
  • Food chains show single feeding sequences and food webs the network of links; removing keystone species can trigger trophic cascades that reshape whole communities.
  • Trophic levels rank organisms from producers upwards; energy losses of around 90% per step keep food chains short, and persistent pollutants biomagnify towards the top.
  • Photosynthesis captures light energy as chemical energy, but only a few per cent of sunlight at best; C4 and CAM pathways adapt plants to heat and drought.
  • GPP is the total rate of photosynthesis; NPP is what remains after producers' respiration and supports all other life; productivity is a rate, unlike biomass.

Common mistakes in Ecosystems and Energy Flow

  • Saying that energy is recycled in ecosystems. Energy flows one way and is lost as heat; only matter cycles.
  • Using "decomposer" for every organism that eats dead matter. Detritivores ingest detritus; decomposers digest it externally and absorb the products.
  • Drawing food chain arrows from predator to prey. Arrows point in the direction of energy flow, from the organism eaten to the organism eating it.
  • Assuming every organism fits neatly into one level. Omnivores and many predators feed at more than one level.
  • Assuming plants use most of the sunlight that falls on them. Most is reflected, transmitted, lost as heat or outside the usable wavelengths.
  • Confusing productivity with biomass. A forest has high biomass, but a young, fast-growing grassland may have similar or higher productivity per year.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is an ecosystem?
    An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with its physical environment, linked by flows of energy and cycles of matter.
  2. Producers, consumers and decomposers
    Organisms in an ecosystem can be grouped by how they obtain energy and nutrients.
  3. Food chains and food webs
    Food chains show who eats whom, and food webs show the network of feeding links.
  4. Trophic levels
    Trophic levels group organisms by their position in food chains.
  5. Photosynthesis and energy capture
    Almost all energy in ecosystems is captured by photosynthesis.
  6. Gross and net primary productivity
    Primary productivity measures the rate at which producers capture energy and build biomass.
  7. Secondary productivity
    Secondary productivity is the rate at which consumers build new biomass.
  8. Efficiency of energy transfer
    Only a fraction of energy at one trophic level reaches the next.
  9. Ecological pyramids
    Ecological pyramids show how numbers, biomass or energy change across trophic levels.
  10. Decomposition and detritus food webs
    Most of the energy fixed by plants is never eaten by herbivores but enters the detritus food web when organisms die.
  11. Soil ecosystems
    Soils are among the most diverse and important ecosystems on Earth.
  12. Productivity across biomes
    Net primary productivity varies enormously across the planet.
  13. Ocean productivity
    The oceans produce about half of global NPP, but their productivity varies greatly.
  14. Ecosystem stability and resilience
    Ecosystems vary in how well they withstand and recover from disturbance.
  15. Ecosystem services
    Ecosystems provide benefits that people depend on, from food and clean water to climate regulation and inspiration.
  16. Agricultural ecosystems
    Agricultural ecosystems, or agroecosystems, are designed by people to channel productivity into food.

Ecosystems and Energy Flow: questions students ask

What is a chemoautotroph?

An organism that makes organic compounds using energy from chemical reactions.

In which direction do food chain arrows point?

From the organism eaten to the organism that eats it, the direction of energy flow.

What is biomagnification?

The increase in concentration of a persistent substance at successive trophic levels.

What is the overall equation for photosynthesis?

6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, powered by light absorbed by chlorophyll.
