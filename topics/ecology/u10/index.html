Key terms and formulas at a glance

Primary energy
Energy in its natural form before conversion, such as coal or wind
Reserves
The part of a resource that can be extracted economically with current technology
Nuclear fission
The splitting of a heavy nucleus into smaller nuclei, releasing energy
Capacity factor
Actual energy output over a period as a fraction of the maximum possible
Coefficient of performance
Heat delivered by a heat pump divided by the electricity it uses
Soil erosion
Removal of soil particles by water, wind or tillage
Aquifer
A permeable rock layer that stores and transmits groundwater
Food security
When all people at all times have access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food
Maximum sustainable yield
The largest catch that can be taken indefinitely without depleting a stock
Deforestation
The permanent conversion of forest to other land uses
Ore grade
The concentration of a valuable mineral in an ore
Diffuse pollution
Pollution from many dispersed sources, such as runoff from fields and roads

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between primary and secondary energy?

Primary energy is found in nature; secondary energy, such as electricity, is converted from it. From “Energy resources” →

In plain English: Societies depend on energy for heat, transport, industry and electricity. Key idea: Energy resources are primary or secondary and renewable or non-renewable; fossil fuels dominate world energy, though UK electricity is now largely low-carbon; policy balances security, affordability and sustainability under the net zero target.

What is the rebound effect?

When efficiency gains lower costs, so people use more energy, offsetting some of the savings. From “Energy efficiency and storage” →

In plain English: Using less energy and storing it for when it is needed are as important as generating it cleanly. Key idea: Efficiency is useful output over input, and improving it is often the cheapest way to cut emissions; insulation and heat pumps reduce building energy use; storage such as pumped hydro, batteries and hydrogen, plus demand response, balances variable renewables.

At roughly what population size does a stock produce its maximum sustainable yield in the logistic model?

At about half its carrying capacity. From “Fisheries” →

In plain English: Fish are a vital source of food and livelihoods, but many stocks are overexploited. Key idea: MSY is the largest catch sustainable over time, reached near half carrying capacity (rK ÷ 4 in the logistic model); overfishing, bycatch and habitat damage threaten stocks, and quotas, gear rules, protected areas and certification support sustainable fisheries.

Which air pollutant comes mainly from road traffic, especially diesel vehicles?

Nitrogen dioxide. From “Air pollution” →

In plain English: Air pollution is the largest environmental risk to human health and also harms ecosystems. Key idea: PM2.5, NO2, ozone, SO2 and ammonia come from traffic, combustion and farming; air pollution causes heart and lung disease and premature deaths and harms ecosystems; clean air laws, emission standards and low emission zones improve air quality.

What does an increase of 10 dB represent?

A tenfold increase in sound intensity, heard as roughly twice as loud. From “Noise and light pollution” →

In plain English: Not all pollution is chemical. Key idea: Noise is measured in decibels on a logarithmic scale and harms sleep, health and animal communication; artificial light at night disrupts body clocks and wildlife; both can be reduced at source, along the path and at the receiver, and stop when the source is removed.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Resources and Pollution

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a secondary energy source? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Electricity. Electricity is generated by converting primary sources.

  2. From what does coal mainly form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Buried plant material from ancient swamps. Coal forms from compressed and heated plant remains.

  3. Which isotope is commonly used as fuel in nuclear fission? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Uranium-235. Uranium-235 nuclei split when they absorb neutrons.

  4. What did the closure of Ratcliffe-on-Soar in 2024 mark? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The end of coal-fired electricity generation in the UK. Ratcliffe-on-Soar was the UK's last operating coal power station.

  5. Why might an oil reserve shrink even though no oil has been extracted? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A fall in oil prices makes some oil uneconomic to extract. Reserves depend on economics as well as geology.

  6. Which type of radioactive waste has the largest volume? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Low-level waste. Low-level waste is bulky but least radioactive.

  7. Why does the 2022 gas price crisis illustrate the energy trilemma? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dependence on imported gas threatened both security and affordability. Supply shocks show how security and affordability are linked.

  8. What is a key limitation of carbon capture and storage? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is not yet proven at large scale and does not tackle all emissions. CCS captures some emissions from large sources but not others.

  9. What is the strongest argument for nuclear power in a net zero system? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It provides reliable low-carbon electricity regardless of weather. Firm low-carbon supply complements variable renewables.

Key takeaways

  • Energy resources are primary or secondary and renewable or non-renewable; fossil fuels dominate world energy, though UK electricity is now largely low-carbon; policy balances security, affordability and sustainability under the net zero target.
  • Coal forms from plants and oil and gas from marine plankton over millions of years; reserves are the economically extractable part of resources; fossil fuels emit CO2 and air pollutants and damage habitats, with CCS and phase-out as responses.
  • Fission of uranium-235 in a controlled chain reaction produces heat for steam turbines; nuclear gives reliable low-carbon baseload power but brings high costs, long-lived waste needing geological disposal and accident risks.
  • Wind, solar, hydro, tidal, biomass and geothermal harness natural flows; output depends on capacity factor; renewables are low-carbon but have local ecological impacts and variability that requires storage and flexibility.
  • Efficiency is useful output over input, and improving it is often the cheapest way to cut emissions; insulation and heat pumps reduce building energy use; storage such as pumped hydro, batteries and hydrogen, plus demand response, balances variable renewables.
  • Soil forms very slowly and provides vital services; erosion, compaction, organic matter loss, salinisation and contamination degrade it; cover crops, reduced tillage, contour ploughing, hedgerows and organic matter help conserve it.

Common mistakes in Resources and Pollution

  • Equating energy with electricity. Heating and transport make up much of energy use and still depend largely on fossil fuels.
  • Thinking reserves are a fixed quantity. Reserves rise and fall with prices and technology.
  • Assuming nuclear power stations emit large amounts of CO2. Their life-cycle emissions are among the lowest of any source.
  • Assuming all biomass is carbon neutral. It depends on sourcing, regrowth time and emissions in harvesting and transport.
  • Thinking a heat pump breaks the laws of physics with a COP above 1. It moves heat from outside rather than creating it.
  • Treating soil as a renewable resource on human timescales. Lost topsoil can take centuries to rebuild.

Lessons in this school

  1. Energy resources
    Societies depend on energy for heat, transport, industry and electricity.
  2. Fossil fuels
    Coal, oil and natural gas powered industrialisation but are the main cause of climate change.
  3. Nuclear power
    Nuclear power provides large amounts of low-carbon electricity, but raises concerns about safety, waste and cost.
  4. Renewable energy
    Renewable energy sources are expanding rapidly and now supply a large share of UK electricity.
  5. Energy efficiency and storage
    Using less energy and storing it for when it is needed are as important as generating it cleanly.
  6. Soil degradation and conservation
    Soil supports food production, stores carbon and hosts much of the world's biodiversity, yet it forms very slowly and is easily degraded.
  7. Water resources
    Fresh water is essential for people, farming, industry and ecosystems, yet it is unevenly distributed and under growing pressure.
  8. Agriculture and food security
    Feeding a growing world population without destroying the environment is one of the great challenges of this century.
  9. Fisheries
    Fish are a vital source of food and livelihoods, but many stocks are overexploited.
  10. Forestry
    Forests provide timber, fuel and many ecosystem services, but deforestation continues at a high rate.
  11. Mineral resources
    Modern technology depends on minerals, from iron and copper to lithium and rare earth elements.
  12. Principles of pollution
    Pollution is the addition of substances or energy to the environment at harmful levels.
  13. Air pollution
    Air pollution is the largest environmental risk to human health and also harms ecosystems.
  14. Water pollution
    Rivers, lakes and seas receive pollution from sewage, farming, industry and roads.
  15. Plastics and persistent pollutants
    Plastics and some chemicals persist in the environment for decades or longer.
  16. Pesticides and bioaccumulation
    Pesticides protect crops but can harm wildlife and people.
  17. Noise and light pollution
    Not all pollution is chemical.
  18. Waste management
    How we manage waste affects land, water, air and climate.
  19. Life-cycle assessment
    To compare the environmental impacts of products fairly, we need to consider their whole lives.
  20. The circular economy
    The circular economy aims to keep products and materials in use and design out waste.

Resources and Pollution: questions students ask

What is a cap rock?

An impermeable rock layer that traps oil and gas in the reservoir below.

What is the role of control rods in a nuclear reactor?

They absorb neutrons to control the rate of the chain reaction.

What is a capacity factor?

Actual energy output over a period as a fraction of the maximum possible output.

Why is soil considered effectively non-renewable?

It forms so slowly that lost topsoil cannot be replaced on human timescales.
