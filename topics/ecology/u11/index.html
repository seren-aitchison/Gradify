Key terms and formulas at a glance

Sustainable development
Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
Sustainable Development Goals
Seventeen global goals adopted by the UN in 2015 to achieve sustainable development by 2030
Ecological footprint
The area of biologically productive land and water needed to supply a population's resources and absorb its carbon emissions
Anthropocentrism
The view that only humans have moral standing
Externality
A cost or benefit of an activity that falls on a third party and is not reflected in the price
Total economic value
The sum of use and non-use values that people gain from an environmental resource
Pigouvian tax
A tax on an activity equal to the external cost it causes
Framework convention
A treaty that sets general goals and institutions, with detailed commitments added later in protocols
Duty of care
A legal duty on anyone handling waste to keep it safe and pass it only to authorised persons
Screening
The stage that decides whether a project needs a full EIA
Biodiversity unit
A measure of habitat value calculated from area, distinctiveness, condition and strategic significance
Precautionary principle
The principle that lack of full scientific certainty should not delay action to prevent serious or irreversible harm

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which report gave the most widely used definition of sustainable development?

The Brundtland Report, Our Common Future (1987). From “What is sustainability?” →

In plain English: Sustainability is about meeting human needs without undermining the natural systems that future generations will depend on. Key idea: Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations, and the main debate is whether natural capital can be substituted (weak sustainability) or must be kept intact where it is critical (strong sustainability).

What did Aldo Leopold call his ethical principle?

The land ethic. From “Environmental ethics” →

In plain English: Environmental ethics asks what we owe to nature and to future generations. Key idea: Environmental ethics ranges from anthropocentrism, which values nature for people, to biocentrism and ecocentrism, which give moral standing to organisms and ecosystems, and real decisions often involve weighing these positions.

What is a cap-and-trade scheme?

A scheme in which total emissions are capped and firms can buy and sell allowances. From “Policy instruments: regulation, taxes and markets” →

In plain English: Governments use a range of tools to correct environmental market failures. Key idea: Regulation sets clear standards, while taxes and tradable permits use prices to cut pollution at lower cost, and effective environmental policy usually combines several instruments.

Which US law of 1969 first required environmental impact assessment?

The National Environmental Policy Act. From “Environmental impact assessment” →

In plain English: Environmental impact assessment is the process of predicting and evaluating the environmental effects of a proposed development before a decision is made. Key idea: EIA predicts and evaluates the likely significant effects of projects through screening, scoping, baseline survey, impact prediction, mitigation and consultation, but its value depends on independent, thorough surveys and follow-up monitoring.

What rights does the Aarhus Convention give the public?

Access to environmental information, participation in decisions and access to justice. From “Environmental justice” →

In plain English: Environmental harms and benefits are not shared equally across society. Key idea: Environmental justice examines how environmental harms and benefits are distributed, who takes part in decisions and whose knowledge is recognised, and evidence shows that poorer and marginalised communities often bear the greatest burdens.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Sustainability and Environmental Policy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In which year was Our Common Future, the Brundtland Report, published? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1987. The World Commission on Environment and Development reported in 1987.

  2. Which SDG is titled "Life below water"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Goal 14. Goal 14 covers oceans, seas and marine resources.

  3. Which component makes up the largest share of humanity's ecological footprint? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Carbon. The carbon footprint is around 60% of the global total.

  4. What is the main criticism of the three pillars model? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It implies environmental losses can be offset by economic gains. Equal pillars suggest trade-offs are always acceptable.

  5. What is the main difference between the SDGs and the Millennium Development Goals? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The SDGs apply to all countries. The MDGs focused on lower-income countries; the SDGs are universal.

  6. A city's footprint is larger than its biocapacity. What does this mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It relies on resources and absorption capacity from elsewhere. Cities are in overshoot because they import resources and export wastes.

  7. A government argues that selling North Sea oil is sustainable because the revenue is invested in schools. Which view does this reflect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Weak sustainability. Converting natural capital into human capital assumes substitution is possible.

  8. A city installs rooftop solar panels that cut emissions and lower energy bills for poor households. How is this interaction best described? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A synergy between clean energy, climate and poverty goals. Progress on one goal supports progress on several others.

  9. Which is the strongest criticism of the ecological footprint as a measure of sustainability? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It omits pressures such as biodiversity loss and pollution. Important impacts that do not need land area are left out.

Key takeaways

  • Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations, and the main debate is whether natural capital can be substituted (weak sustainability) or must be kept intact where it is critical (strong sustainability).
  • The 17 SDGs, adopted in 2015, are a universal but non-binding framework for sustainable development to 2030, and their success depends on managing synergies and trade-offs between goals.
  • The ecological footprint compares the productive area a population needs with the biocapacity available, revealing overshoot, but it is dominated by carbon and leaves out several important environmental pressures.
  • Environmental ethics ranges from anthropocentrism, which values nature for people, to biocentrism and ecocentrism, which give moral standing to organisms and ecosystems, and real decisions often involve weighing these positions.
  • Environmental damage often results from market failure, because externalities are left out of prices and public goods and open-access resources are overused or underprovided, though communities can manage commons well with suitable rules.
  • Total economic value includes direct and indirect use values, option value and non-use values, estimated using market, revealed and stated preference methods, but valuation is incomplete and contested when applied to intrinsic and irreplaceable values.

Common mistakes in Sustainability and Environmental Policy

  • Treating sustainability as purely environmental. The Brundtland definition stresses human needs and fairness as well as environmental limits.
  • Thinking the SDGs apply only to developing countries. Unlike the MDGs, they are universal and apply to the UK.
  • Thinking the footprint measures all environmental impacts. It excludes many pressures, including biodiversity loss, water scarcity and toxic pollution.
  • Assuming anthropocentrism means not caring about nature. Enlightened anthropocentrism can strongly support conservation for the sake of present and future people.
  • Thinking the tragedy of the commons is inevitable. Ostrom showed that communities often manage shared resources sustainably; the tragedy applies mainly to unregulated open access.
  • Assuming a money value is the true worth of nature. Valuation estimates selected benefits to people and leaves out many values.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is sustainability?
    Sustainability is about meeting human needs without undermining the natural systems that future generations will depend on.
  2. Sustainable development and the SDGs
    The Sustainable Development Goals are the world's shared framework for development to 2030.
  3. Ecological footprints
    The ecological footprint estimates how much biologically productive land and water a population needs to supply its resources and absorb its carbon emissions.
  4. Environmental ethics
    Environmental ethics asks what we owe to nature and to future generations.
  5. Market failure and externalities
    Markets often fail to protect the environment because the costs of pollution and resource depletion fall on people who are not part of the transaction.
  6. Valuing nature
    Many benefits of nature have no market price, so they are often ignored in decisions.
  7. Policy instruments: regulation, taxes and markets
    Governments use a range of tools to correct environmental market failures.
  8. International environmental agreements
    Many environmental problems cross borders and need international cooperation.
  9. UK environmental law
    UK environmental protection rests on a body of Acts of Parliament, regulations and regulators.
  10. Environmental impact assessment
    Environmental impact assessment is the process of predicting and evaluating the environmental effects of a proposed development before a decision is made.
  11. Biodiversity net gain
    Biodiversity net gain requires most new developments in England to leave habitats in a measurably better state than before.
  12. The precautionary principle
    The precautionary principle guides decisions when an activity may cause serious harm but the science is uncertain.
  13. Environmental justice
    Environmental harms and benefits are not shared equally across society.
  14. Indigenous and local knowledge
    Indigenous peoples and local communities hold detailed knowledge of ecosystems built up over generations.
  15. Behaviour change and the environment
    Many environmental problems depend on everyday choices about energy, travel, food and waste.
  16. Monitoring progress: environmental indicators
    Indicators turn complex environmental change into measurable signals that show whether policies are working.

Sustainability and Environmental Policy: questions students ask

How many Sustainable Development Goals are there?

Seventeen, with 169 targets.

What is a global hectare?

A hectare of land or water with world-average biological productivity.

What is a negative externality?

A cost of an activity that falls on a third party and is not included in the price.

What is existence value?

The value people place on knowing that a species or place exists, even if they never use it.
