Lessons in this school

  1. What is sustainability?
    Sustainability is about meeting human needs without undermining the natural systems that future generations will depend on.
  2. Sustainable development and the SDGs
    The Sustainable Development Goals are the world's shared framework for development to 2030.
  3. Ecological footprints
    The ecological footprint estimates how much biologically productive land and water a population needs to supply its resources and absorb its carbon emissions.
  4. Environmental ethics
    Environmental ethics asks what we owe to nature and to future generations.
  5. Market failure and externalities
    Markets often fail to protect the environment because the costs of pollution and resource depletion fall on people who are not part of the transaction.
  6. Valuing nature
    Many benefits of nature have no market price, so they are often ignored in decisions.
  7. Policy instruments: regulation, taxes and markets
    Governments use a range of tools to correct environmental market failures.
  8. International environmental agreements
    Many environmental problems cross borders and need international cooperation.
  9. UK environmental law
    UK environmental protection rests on a body of Acts of Parliament, regulations and regulators.
  10. Environmental impact assessment
    Environmental impact assessment is the process of predicting and evaluating the environmental effects of a proposed development before a decision is made.
  11. Biodiversity net gain
    Biodiversity net gain requires most new developments in England to leave habitats in a measurably better state than before.
  12. The precautionary principle
    The precautionary principle guides decisions when an activity may cause serious harm but the science is uncertain.
  13. Environmental justice
    Environmental harms and benefits are not shared equally across society.
  14. Indigenous and local knowledge
    Indigenous peoples and local communities hold detailed knowledge of ecosystems built up over generations.
  15. Behaviour change and the environment
    Many environmental problems depend on everyday choices about energy, travel, food and waste.
  16. Monitoring progress: environmental indicators
    Indicators turn complex environmental change into measurable signals that show whether policies are working.

Key terms

Sustainable development
Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
Sustainable Development Goals
Seventeen global goals adopted by the UN in 2015 to achieve sustainable development by 2030
Ecological footprint
The area of biologically productive land and water needed to supply a population's resources and absorb its carbon emissions
Anthropocentrism
The view that only humans have moral standing
Externality
A cost or benefit of an activity that falls on a third party and is not reflected in the price
Total economic value
The sum of use and non-use values that people gain from an environmental resource
Pigouvian tax
A tax on an activity equal to the external cost it causes
Framework convention
A treaty that sets general goals and institutions, with detailed commitments added later in protocols
Duty of care
A legal duty on anyone handling waste to keep it safe and pass it only to authorised persons
Screening
The stage that decides whether a project needs a full EIA
Biodiversity unit
A measure of habitat value calculated from area, distinctiveness, condition and strategic significance
Precautionary principle
The principle that lack of full scientific certainty should not delay action to prevent serious or irreversible harm
Environmental justice
The fair distribution of environmental harms and benefits and fair participation in environmental decisions
Traditional ecological knowledge
Knowledge, practice and belief about relationships between living beings and their environment, passed down through generations
