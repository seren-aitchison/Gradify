A computer simulation of the climate system based on physical laws

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Over what period is climate usually defined?

30 years, using WMO climate normals. From “Weather and climate” →

In plain English: Weather is what happens in the atmosphere from day to day; climate is the long-term pattern of weather. Key idea: Weather is short-term atmospheric conditions; climate is the 30-year statistical pattern of weather, produced by interactions between atmosphere, oceans, ice, land and life.

What was the pre-industrial concentration of CO2?

About 280 parts per million. From “Greenhouse gases” →

In plain English: Different greenhouse gases come from different sources, last for different times and trap different amounts of heat. Key idea: CO2 dominates long-term warming because of its large emissions and long life; methane and nitrous oxide are more potent per tonne; water vapour amplifies warming as a feedback.

What did the IPCC AR6 conclude about human influence?

It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land. From “Attribution of climate change” →

In plain English: Attribution is the science of identifying the causes of observed climate change. Key idea: Detection and attribution studies, fingerprints such as stratospheric cooling and isotopic evidence, and comparisons with natural-only model runs show human influence is the dominant cause of recent warming.

Why will sea level keep rising after emissions stop?

The deep ocean continues to warm and ice sheets keep responding for centuries. From “Sea-level rise” →

In plain English: Global sea level is rising and will keep rising for centuries. Key idea: Sea level rises through thermal expansion and land ice loss; about 0.2 m since 1901, perhaps 0.3 to 1 m by 2100 depending on emissions, and more for centuries afterwards.

How much further are warm-water reefs projected to decline at 1.5 °C?

By about 70 to 90% (IPCC SR1.5). From “Coral reefs under climate change” →

In plain English: Coral reefs are among the most diverse and most threatened ecosystems on Earth. Key idea: Warm water breaks the coral-algae symbiosis, causing bleaching; mass bleaching events are becoming more frequent, and reefs face 70 to 90% decline at 1.5 °C and near-total loss at 2 °C.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Climate Change

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which describes climate rather than weather? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The average July temperature in Cardiff over 30 years. Climate is the long-term statistical pattern, not a single day's conditions.

  2. Which gas makes up most of the atmosphere? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nitrogen. Nitrogen makes up about 78% of dry air, with oxygen at about 21%.

  3. What is albedo? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The fraction of sunlight a surface reflects. Albedo measures reflectivity, from 0 (absorbs all) to 1 (reflects all).

  4. Which component of the climate system includes permafrost and sea ice? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The cryosphere. The cryosphere covers all frozen water on Earth, including permafrost.

  5. In which layer does almost all weather occur? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The troposphere. The troposphere contains most of the atmosphere's mass and water vapour.

  6. What would happen to Earth if absorbed sunlight exceeded emitted infrared? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It would warm until emission rose to restore balance. A positive imbalance adds heat, raising temperature until outgoing radiation balances incoming.

  7. A record cold day occurs in a region that is warming. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Natural variability still produces cold extremes, but less often. Warming shifts the distribution, making cold records rarer but not impossible.

  8. Why are many hot deserts found near 30° north and south? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Sinking air in the Hadley cell suppresses rainfall. Descending air warms and dries, giving high pressure and little rain.

  9. Why does losing Arctic sea ice amplify warming? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dark ocean has a much lower albedo than ice and absorbs more sunlight. Replacing bright ice with dark water reduces reflection and increases absorbed energy.

Key takeaways

  • Weather is short-term atmospheric conditions; climate is the 30-year statistical pattern of weather, produced by interactions between atmosphere, oceans, ice, land and life.
  • The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, but trace greenhouse gases control its temperature; the global circulation carries heat poleward and creates wet and dry climate belts.
  • Earth absorbs about 240 W/m2 of sunlight after reflecting about 30%, and must emit the same as infrared to be in balance; today it is absorbing more than it emits, so it is warming.
  • Greenhouse gases are transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb outgoing infrared; the natural effect keeps Earth about 33 °C warmer, and human emissions enhance it.
  • CO2 dominates long-term warming because of its large emissions and long life; methane and nitrous oxide are more potent per tonne; water vapour amplifies warming as a feedback.
  • Radiative forcing measures the change in energy balance in W/m2; greenhouse gases give positive forcing, aerosols negative; equilibrium climate sensitivity is likely 2.5 to 4 °C per doubling of CO2.

Common mistakes in Climate Change

  • Using a single weather event, hot or cold, as proof for or against climate change. Climate is judged on long-term trends and changing frequencies of extremes.
  • Assuming gases present in tiny amounts cannot matter. Carbon dioxide at around 0.04% strongly affects Earth's energy balance.
  • Thinking the Sun is causing current warming. Solar output has shown no upward trend since the 1980s while temperatures have risen.
  • Confusing the greenhouse effect with the ozone hole. Ozone depletion lets in more ultraviolet; it is a separate problem with a different cause.
  • Treating GWP values as exact. They depend on the time horizon chosen and are revised as science improves.
  • Assuming cleaning up aerosol pollution has no climate effect. Removing aerosols improves health but reduces their cooling, adding to warming.

Lessons in this school

  1. Weather and climate
    Weather is what happens in the atmosphere from day to day; climate is the long-term pattern of weather.
  2. The atmosphere
    Earth's atmosphere is a thin layer of gases that regulates temperature and redistributes heat.
  3. Earth's energy balance
    Earth's temperature depends on the balance between energy arriving from the Sun and energy leaving as infrared radiation.
  4. The greenhouse effect
    Greenhouse gases keep Earth habitable by trapping outgoing infrared radiation.
  5. Greenhouse gases
    Different greenhouse gases come from different sources, last for different times and trap different amounts of heat.
  6. Radiative forcing
    Radiative forcing measures how much a change, such as more CO2 or more aerosols, alters Earth's energy balance.
  7. The instrumental record
    Thermometers, tide gauges, satellites and ocean floats provide direct measurements of a changing climate.
  8. Past climates and proxies
    Natural archives such as ice cores, tree rings and sediments record climate long before thermometers.
  9. Attribution of climate change
    Attribution is the science of identifying the causes of observed climate change.
  10. Climate feedbacks
    Feedbacks amplify or dampen the warming caused by a forcing.
  11. Tipping points
    Some parts of the climate system may change abruptly and irreversibly once a threshold is crossed.
  12. Climate models and scenarios
    Climate models use physics to simulate the climate system and project how it will respond to future emissions.
  13. Sea-level rise
    Global sea level is rising and will keep rising for centuries.
  14. Ocean warming and acidification
    The oceans have absorbed most of the extra heat and a quarter or more of the carbon dioxide released by humans.
  15. Phenology and mismatch
    Phenology is the timing of seasonal events such as flowering, breeding and migration.
  16. Range shifts
    As climate warms, many species are moving towards the poles, uphill and into deeper water.
  17. Coral reefs under climate change
    Coral reefs are among the most diverse and most threatened ecosystems on Earth.
  18. Climate change and extinction risk
    Climate change is becoming a major driver of extinction alongside habitat loss, exploitation and invasive species.
  19. Mitigation
    Mitigation means reducing the causes of climate change by cutting greenhouse gas emissions and increasing removals.
  20. Adaptation
    Adaptation means adjusting to actual or expected climate change to reduce harm or exploit opportunities.
  21. Nature-based solutions
    Nature-based solutions use the protection, management and restoration of ecosystems to tackle societal challenges such as climate change.

Climate Change: questions students ask

What is the approximate concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere today?

Just over 420 parts per million, about 0.04%.

What is Earth's average albedo?

About 0.3: roughly 30% of sunlight is reflected.

Why is sunlight not absorbed by greenhouse gases on the way in?

Most incoming energy is shortwave radiation, which greenhouse gases absorb only weakly.

What is the IPCC best estimate of equilibrium climate sensitivity?

3 °C, with a likely range of 2.5 to 4 °C (AR6).
