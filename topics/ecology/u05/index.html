Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a flux in a biogeochemical cycle?

The rate at which material moves between reservoirs. From “Cycling of matter” →

In plain English: Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the Earth; it moves between living things, rocks, water and air. Key idea: Matter is recycled through biogeochemical cycles; cycles are described by reservoirs, fluxes and residence times, which show how quickly each part of a cycle responds to change.

What is the biological pump?

The transfer of carbon fixed by marine organisms into the deep ocean as they die and sink. From “The carbon cycle” →

In plain English: Carbon moves between the atmosphere, living things, oceans and rocks over timescales from days to millions of years. Key idea: Carbon cycles quickly through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and ocean exchange, and slowly through weathering, sedimentation and volcanism; burning fossil fuels moves slow-cycle carbon into the fast cycle far faster than it can be removed.

Which process returns nitrogen to the atmosphere as N2?

Denitrification. From “The nitrogen cycle” →

In plain English: Nitrogen is essential for proteins and DNA, but most of it is locked up as unreactive N2 gas. Key idea: The nitrogen cycle turns unreactive N2 into usable forms by fixation, moves it through organisms by assimilation and ammonification, converts ammonium to nitrate by nitrification, and returns it to the air by denitrification.

How does phosphorus mainly enter ecosystems?

By the weathering of rocks. From “The phosphorus cycle” →

In plain English: Phosphorus is essential for DNA, ATP and cell membranes, yet it cycles very slowly and has no significant gas phase. Key idea: Phosphorus cycles slowly through rocks, soils, organisms and sediments with no gas phase; it often limits freshwater productivity, and farming depends on finite mined phosphate.

What is the Redfield ratio?

The typical C:N:P ratio of about 106:16:1 in marine plankton. From “Nutrient limitation” →

In plain English: Productivity is often limited by the nutrient in shortest supply. Key idea: Growth is often limited by the scarcest nutrient; comparing nutrient ratios with the Redfield ratio of 106:16:1 predicts limitation, and experiments show co-limitation by nitrogen and phosphorus is widespread.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Biogeochemical Cycles

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is cycled rather than flowing one way through ecosystems? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Nitrogen atoms. Matter such as nitrogen is reused; energy is lost as heat.

  2. Which process moves water from leaves into the atmosphere? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Transpiration. Water evaporates from leaf cells and leaves through the stomata.

  3. Which process removes CO2 from the atmosphere in the fast carbon cycle? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Photosynthesis. Photosynthesis fixes atmospheric CO2 into organic matter.

  4. A reservoir gains more of an element each year than it loses. What is it called? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A sink. Applying the standard result: A sink accumulates material over time.

  5. Where is most of the Earth's fresh water stored? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    In ice sheets and glaciers. Ice holds the majority of fresh water; groundwater most of the rest.

  6. Why does atmospheric CO2 fall each northern summer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Northern forests photosynthesise more than they respire. Most land vegetation is in the Northern Hemisphere, so its growing season drives the cycle.

  7. Why will carbon dioxide from fossil fuels affect climate for centuries? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Parts of the carbon cycle that remove it have long residence times and slow fluxes. Removal into the deep ocean and rocks is slow, so excess persists.

  8. An upland catchment is heavily drained and compacted. What is the likely effect downstream? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Faster runoff and higher flood peaks. Compacted, drained soils absorb less water, so it reaches rivers faster.

  9. Why does burning fossil fuels raise atmospheric CO2 when the carbon was once in the atmosphere? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It returns carbon in decades that the slow cycle took millions of years to remove. The release rate far exceeds natural removal by weathering and burial.

Key takeaways

  • Matter is recycled through biogeochemical cycles; cycles are described by reservoirs, fluxes and residence times, which show how quickly each part of a cycle responds to change.
  • The water cycle moves water between oceans, ice, groundwater, atmosphere and life through evaporation, transpiration, precipitation and runoff; vegetation recycles rainfall and slows runoff, and human activity and warming are intensifying the cycle.
  • Carbon cycles quickly through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and ocean exchange, and slowly through weathering, sedimentation and volcanism; burning fossil fuels moves slow-cycle carbon into the fast cycle far faster than it can be removed.
  • Most carbon is in the ocean and rocks, with smaller but faster-cycling stores in soil, vegetation and the atmosphere; about half of human emissions accumulates in the air, and the ocean sink causes acidification.
  • Peatlands, forests and blue carbon habitats store large amounts of carbon because growth outpaces decomposition; disturbance turns them into sources, and restoration helps, but cannot substitute for cutting fossil emissions.
  • The nitrogen cycle turns unreactive N2 into usable forms by fixation, moves it through organisms by assimilation and ammonification, converts ammonium to nitrate by nitrification, and returns it to the air by denitrification.

Common mistakes in Biogeochemical Cycles

  • Confusing the size of a reservoir with the speed of its flux. A small reservoir with a large flux, such as atmospheric water, turns over very quickly.
  • Treating the water cycle as purely physical. Plants move vast amounts of water through transpiration and shape local rainfall and flooding.
  • Assuming plants and oceans will absorb all extra CO2. They take up roughly half of emissions; the rest accumulates in the atmosphere.
  • Assuming natural sinks will keep pace with emissions. Their uptake depends on climate, and warming may weaken land and ocean sinks.
  • Assuming tree planting can offset any emissions. Planting on peat can release more carbon than the trees store, and the area available is limited.
  • Mixing up nitrification and denitrification. Nitrification needs oxygen and makes nitrate; denitrification happens without oxygen and removes nitrate as N2.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cycling of matter
    Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the Earth; it moves between living things, rocks, water and air.
  2. The water cycle
    The water cycle moves water between oceans, atmosphere, land and living things, driven by solar energy.
  3. The carbon cycle
    Carbon moves between the atmosphere, living things, oceans and rocks over timescales from days to millions of years.
  4. Carbon stores and fluxes
    Quantifying where carbon is stored and how fast it moves lets scientists track human disturbance.
  5. Peatlands, forests and blue carbon
    Some ecosystems store very large amounts of carbon, and protecting them is part of climate policy.
  6. The nitrogen cycle
    Nitrogen is essential for proteins and DNA, but most of it is locked up as unreactive N2 gas.
  7. Nitrogen fixation
    Nitrogen fixation turns unreactive atmospheric N2 into ammonia that life can use.
  8. Human alteration of the nitrogen cycle
    Humans now add more reactive nitrogen to the Earth than all natural land processes combined.
  9. The phosphorus cycle
    Phosphorus is essential for DNA, ATP and cell membranes, yet it cycles very slowly and has no significant gas phase.
  10. Eutrophication
    Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, leading to algal blooms and oxygen loss.
  11. The sulfur cycle and acid deposition
    Sulfur cycles through rocks, oceans, organisms and the atmosphere.
  12. Nutrient limitation
    Productivity is often limited by the nutrient in shortest supply.
  13. Planetary boundaries
    The planetary boundaries framework identifies limits within which humanity can operate safely.
  14. Coupled cycles and global change
    Biogeochemical cycles do not operate in isolation: changes in one alter the others.

Biogeochemical Cycles: questions students ask

What is evapotranspiration?

The combined loss of water by evaporation and plant transpiration.

What is a petagram?

1015 grams, or one billion tonnes, used for global carbon stocks.

Why does peat accumulate?

Waterlogging slows decomposition, so plant material builds up faster than it breaks down.

Why can most organisms not use atmospheric N2?

Its triple bond makes it unreactive and unusable.
