Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why did Soulé call conservation biology a crisis discipline?

Because it must act before complete evidence is available, as delay risks irreversible loss. From “What is conservation biology?” →

In plain English: Conservation biology is the science of protecting biodiversity. Key idea: Conservation biology is an interdisciplinary, mission-driven science founded in the 1980s to protect biodiversity; as a crisis discipline it must act under uncertainty, combining biological research with social, economic and policy tools.

What is an Allee effect?

Reduced population growth at low density, for example from difficulty finding mates. From “Minimum viable populations” →

In plain English: How many individuals does a species need to survive? Key idea: Small populations face demographic, environmental, catastrophic and genetic risks that can create an extinction vortex; minimum viable populations and population viability analysis help set targets and compare conservation options.

Why is yellow rattle sown in grassland restoration?

It is a semi-parasite that weakens vigorous grasses, letting wildflowers establish. From “Habitat restoration” →

In plain English: Restoration aims to return degraded ecosystems towards a healthier state. Key idea: Ecological restoration helps degraded ecosystems recover, through techniques such as rewetting peat, spreading green hay, reconnecting floodplains and managed realignment, with success judged against reference sites over the long term.

Why does climate change make connectivity more important?

Species need to move to track suitable conditions, which isolated patches prevent. From “Wildlife corridors and landscape connectivity” →

In plain English: Fragmented habitats leave wildlife isolated. Key idea: Fragmentation shrinks and isolates populations and increases edge effects; corridors, stepping stones, wildlife-friendly land between patches and crossing structures restore connectivity, which helps species move, especially under climate change.

What does NEOLI stand for?

No-take, well enforced, old, large and isolated. From “Marine conservation” →

In plain English: The oceans face overfishing, pollution, habitat damage and climate change. Key idea: Marine biodiversity faces overfishing, habitat damage, pollution and climate change; well-enforced, large, long-established no-take MPAs give the strongest benefits, including spillover, while many existing MPAs offer weaker protection.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Conservation Biology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When did conservation biology emerge as a distinct field? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    In the 1980s. The Society for Conservation Biology was founded in 1985.

  2. Which describes intrinsic value? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Value in itself, independent of human use. Intrinsic value does not depend on usefulness.

  3. In conservation genetics, what does inbreeding depression describe? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Reduced fitness in offspring of related parents. Related parents expose harmful recessives.

  4. Why must conservation biology draw on the social sciences? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Most threats are driven by human behaviour and institutions. Applying the standard result: Human activity drives most threats.

  5. A community values a forest as part of its cultural identity. Which value is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Relational value. It arises from the human–nature relationship.

  6. Why does genetic drift matter more in small populations? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Random sampling of alleles has larger effects. Chance has more influence in small samples.

  7. A conservation team must decide whether to protect a site before research on a rare species is complete. What principle supports acting now? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Delay risks irreversible loss in a crisis discipline. Applying the standard result: Extinction cannot be reversed.

  8. What is a key criticism of relying only on ecosystem services arguments? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Species with no obvious use may be neglected. Usefulness-based arguments leave some species out.

  9. A rare plant population shows low seed set and many deformed seedlings. Which action could help most? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Introducing pollen or plants from another population. Genetic rescue can relieve inbreeding depression.

Key takeaways

  • Conservation biology is an interdisciplinary, mission-driven science founded in the 1980s to protect biodiversity; as a crisis discipline it must act under uncertainty, combining biological research with social, economic and policy tools.
  • Nature can be valued intrinsically, instrumentally through ecosystem services, and relationally through people's connections to it; conservation debates turn on which values to emphasise, and many practitioners now use plural values.
  • Small populations lose variation through drift and suffer inbreeding depression; effective population size, often much smaller than the census, measures this risk, and genetic rescue can restore variation if used carefully.
  • Small populations face demographic, environmental, catastrophic and genetic risks that can create an extinction vortex; minimum viable populations and population viability analysis help set targets and compare conservation options.
  • Protected areas range from strict reserves to lived-in landscapes; good design considers size, connectivity, shape and location, and their effectiveness depends on management, funding and the involvement of local people.
  • UK sites are protected through SSSIs under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and SACs and SPAs under the Habitats Regulations, with Ramsar sites for wetlands; but many sites are in poor condition, and the Lawton review called for more, bigger, better and joined sites.

Common mistakes in Conservation Biology

  • Seeing conservation as purely a biological science. Most threats are driven by human activity, so social science and policy are central.
  • Assuming putting a monetary value on nature means it can be sold. Valuation is a tool for decisions, not a market price.
  • Using the census count to judge genetic risk. The effective population size is often a fraction of the number of animals counted.
  • Treating an MVP figure as a precise number for every species. MVPs depend on the species, the time frame and the level of risk accepted.
  • Counting area designated as the measure of success. Paper parks without management may protect little.
  • Assuming designation means a site is in good condition. Many designated sites are in unfavourable condition.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is conservation biology?
    Conservation biology is the science of protecting biodiversity.
  2. Conservation values and ethics
    Why should we conserve nature?
  3. Conservation genetics
    Small populations lose genetic variation, which can threaten their survival.
  4. Minimum viable populations
    How many individuals does a species need to survive?
  5. Protected areas
    Protected areas are the cornerstone of conservation.
  6. UK protected sites: SSSIs, SACs and SPAs
    The UK protects its most important wildlife sites through national and international designations.
  7. Habitat management
    Many valuable habitats depend on continuing management to stop them changing.
  8. Habitat restoration
    Restoration aims to return degraded ecosystems towards a healthier state.
  9. Rewilding
    Rewilding aims to restore natural processes rather than manage for particular species.
  10. Species reintroductions
    Reintroductions return species to places where they were lost.
  11. Wildlife corridors and landscape connectivity
    Fragmented habitats leave wildlife isolated.
  12. Zoos and captive breeding
    Some species survive only because of captive breeding.
  13. Seed banks and botanic gardens
    Plants can be conserved as seeds, living collections and tissue cultures.
  14. Conservation on farmland
    Farmland covers about 70% of the UK's land, so how it is farmed shapes much of its wildlife.
  15. Marine conservation
    The oceans face overfishing, pollution, habitat damage and climate change.
  16. International conservation agreements
    Biodiversity crosses borders, so conservation needs international cooperation.
  17. Evidence-based conservation
    Conservation resources are limited, so actions should be chosen on the basis of evidence of what works.
  18. Community-based conservation
    Conservation succeeds or fails with the people who live alongside wildlife.

Conservation Biology: questions students ask

What is instrumental value?

Value that nature has because of the benefits it provides to people.

Why is Ne usually smaller than the census population?

Unequal sex ratios, variable breeding success and fluctuating numbers reduce it.

What is the extinction vortex?

A downward spiral in which small size, inbreeding and chance events reinforce each other.

What is a paper park?

A protected area that is designated but not effectively managed.
