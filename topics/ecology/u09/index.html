Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What makes a hypothesis falsifiable?

It makes predictions that could be shown to be wrong by evidence. From “The scientific method in ecology” →

In plain English: Ecologists answer questions about nature by observing, forming hypotheses, testing them and revising their ideas. Key idea: Ecologists move from observation to testable, falsifiable hypotheses and predictions, collect data, analyse it and draw provisional conclusions that lead to new questions.

What does "Check, Clean, Dry" aim to prevent?

The spread of invasive species and diseases between sites on equipment and clothing. From “Fieldwork ethics and safety” →

In plain English: Fieldwork must be safe for people and responsible towards wildlife, habitats and landowners. Key idea: Plan fieldwork with a risk assessment, obtain permissions and licences for protected species and sites, and minimise disturbance by following ethical good practice.

How does a Tullgren funnel work?

Heat and light from a lamp drive soil invertebrates downwards through a mesh into a collecting container. From “Sampling animals” →

In plain English: Mobile and hidden animals need special sampling methods. Key idea: Invertebrates are sampled with pitfall traps, sweep nets, beating trays, pooters and Tullgren funnels; mammals and birds with live traps, counts and cameras under licence where required; every method is biased, and welfare must be protected.

Which graph shows the relationship between two continuous variables?

A scatter graph. From “Descriptive statistics” →

In plain English: Descriptive statistics summarise a set of data. Key idea: The mean, median and mode summarise the centre of data, and the range, IQR and standard deviation its spread; choose measures and graphs to suit the data type and distribution.

How many degrees of freedom has an unpaired t-test with samples of 12 and 15?

12 + 15 − 2 = 25. From “The t-test” →

In plain English: The t-test compares the means of two groups of normally distributed data. Key idea: The unpaired t-test compares means of two independent, normally distributed samples: t = difference in means divided by the combined standard error, with n1 + n2 − 2 degrees of freedom.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Field Methods and Statistics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a testable hypothesis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Bluebells are more abundant where the woodland canopy is more open. A testable hypothesis makes a prediction that data can check.

  2. Which is a correctly written null hypothesis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    There is no correlation between soil moisture and moss cover. A null hypothesis states there is no relationship.

  3. Fencing plots to exclude deer and comparing them with unfenced plots is which kind of study? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A manipulative experiment. The researcher deliberately changes deer access.

  4. Why is peer review important in science? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Other experts check the methods and conclusions before publication. Peer review helps catch errors and weak reasoning.

  5. A test gives p = 0.12. What should be concluded at the 5% level? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Do not reject the null hypothesis. p is above 0.05, so the result is not significant.

  6. Why are treatments randomly allocated to plots? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    To spread unknown differences evenly between groups. Randomisation reduces systematic bias and confounding.

  7. Why are ecological conclusions often treated as provisional? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Complex interacting factors and limited data mean new evidence may change them. Complexity and incomplete data make revision likely.

  8. Why are Type II errors more likely with small samples? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small samples have less power to detect a real effect. With few data, real differences may not reach significance.

  9. Moss cover is higher in shaded quadrats, but shaded quadrats are also wetter. What is the problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Moisture is a confounding variable. Moisture could explain the pattern instead of shade.

Key takeaways

  • Ecologists move from observation to testable, falsifiable hypotheses and predictions, collect data, analyse it and draw provisional conclusions that lead to new questions.
  • Start with a null hypothesis of no effect; reject it when p is below 0.05; a Type I error rejects a true null, a Type II error misses a real effect.
  • Manipulative experiments with controls and randomisation give the strongest evidence of cause; observational studies reveal patterns but risk confounding; ecologists combine approaches.
  • Replicates must be independent experimental units; subsamples within one unit are not replicates; pseudoreplication inflates confidence and can give false conclusions.
  • Plan fieldwork with a risk assessment, obtain permissions and licences for protected species and sites, and minimise disturbance by following ethical good practice.
  • Random sampling avoids bias, systematic sampling tracks change along gradients, and stratified sampling ensures each habitat is represented; sample size should be large enough for a stable mean.

Common mistakes in Field Methods and Statistics

  • Calling a hypothesis "proved". Data can support or fail to support a hypothesis, but further evidence can always revise it.
  • Saying the null hypothesis is "accepted" or "proved". It is either rejected or not rejected on the evidence.
  • Concluding cause from a correlation. A third, confounding variable may explain both.
  • Counting quadrats within one site as independent replicates of a site-level treatment. That is pseudoreplication.
  • Assuming common animals can always be trapped without permission. Some, such as shrews in Great Britain, need a licence to trap.
  • Calling it random when quadrats are thrown over the shoulder. Throwing is not truly random and can be unsafe; use random coordinates.

Lessons in this school

  1. The scientific method in ecology
    Ecologists answer questions about nature by observing, forming hypotheses, testing them and revising their ideas.
  2. Hypotheses and null hypotheses
    Statistical tests in ecology start with a null hypothesis of no effect.
  3. Experimental and observational studies
    Ecologists can either manipulate the system or observe natural variation.
  4. Replication and pseudoreplication
    Replication lets ecologists distinguish real effects from natural variation.
  5. Fieldwork ethics and safety
    Fieldwork must be safe for people and responsible towards wildlife, habitats and landowners.
  6. Random, systematic and stratified sampling
    Ecologists rarely count every organism, so they take samples that should represent the whole area.
  7. Quadrats
    Quadrats are frames used to sample plants and slow-moving animals in a defined area.
  8. Transects
    Transects are lines along which samples are taken to show how organisms change across an environmental gradient.
  9. Measuring abundance: frequency, cover and ACFOR
    Ecologists use several measures of abundance, from precise counts to quick abundance scales.
  10. Sampling animals
    Mobile and hidden animals need special sampling methods.
  11. Freshwater sampling and biotic indices
    Freshwater invertebrates are sampled to assess water quality, because different groups tolerate pollution differently.
  12. Measuring abiotic factors
    Abiotic factors help explain where organisms live.
  13. Types of data and variables
    The type of data collected determines how it can be presented and analysed.
  14. Descriptive statistics
    Descriptive statistics summarise a set of data.
  15. Standard deviation and standard error
    Standard deviation describes how spread out data are, and standard error describes how precisely a sample mean estimates the population mean.
  16. Choosing a statistical test
    The right statistical test depends on the question, the type of data and its distribution.
  17. The chi-squared test
    The chi-squared test compares observed frequencies with those expected under a null hypothesis.
  18. Spearman's rank correlation
    Spearman's rank correlation tests whether two variables measured on the same samples tend to increase or decrease together.
  19. The t-test
    The t-test compares the means of two groups of normally distributed data.
  20. The Mann–Whitney U test
    The Mann–Whitney U test compares two independent samples without assuming normal distributions.
  21. Remote sensing and GIS
    Satellites, aircraft and drones collect data on ecosystems across whole landscapes, and geographic information systems (GIS) analyse them.
  22. Environmental DNA
    Organisms shed DNA into water, soil and air, and ecologists can detect species from these traces.
  23. Citizen science
    Members of the public collect large amounts of ecological data through citizen science projects.

Field Methods and Statistics: questions students ask

What does a p-value of 0.03 mean?

If the null hypothesis were true, there is a 3% probability of results at least as extreme as these.

What is a control in an ecological experiment?

A plot or group treated the same as the experimental one except for the factor being tested.

What is pseudoreplication?

Treating non-independent measurements, such as subsamples from one site, as independent replicates.

How are random coordinates generated for quadrat sampling?

Lay out tape measures as axes and use a random number generator or table to select coordinates.
