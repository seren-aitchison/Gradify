Key terms and formulas at a glance

Partial derivative
∂f/∂x: the rate of change of f as x varies with the other variables held fixed
Directional derivative
Duf = ∇f · u, the rate of change of f in the direction of the unit vector u
Gradient
∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂z)
Double integral
∬R f dA, the limit of Riemann sums over small rectangles in R
Type I region
A region a ≤ x ≤ b, g₁(x) ≤ y ≤ g₂(x), integrated dy then dx
Cylindrical coordinates
(r, θ, z) with dV = r dr dθ dz
Jacobian matrix
For (x, y) = T(u, v), the matrix [[∂x/∂u, ∂x/∂v], [∂y/∂u, ∂y/∂v]]
Change of variables formula
∬R f(x, y) dx dy = ∬S f(x(u, v), y(u, v)) |∂(x, y)/∂(u, v)| du dv
Vector field
A function assigning a vector to each point, such as F(x, y, z) = (P, Q, R)
Divergence
∇ · F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z for F = (P, Q, R)
Curl
∇ × F = (∂R/∂y − ∂Q/∂z, ∂P/∂z − ∂R/∂x, ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y)
Laplacian
∇2f = ∂2f/∂x2 + ∂2f/∂y2 + ∂2f/∂z2

Find ∂/∂y of x3y2.

2x3y. From “Multivariable Calculus” →

In plain English: Multivariable calculus extends derivatives and integrals to functions of several variables, like temperature across a room or profit depending on price and advertising. Key idea: Partial derivatives hold other variables fixed; mixed partials agree when continuous; the tangent plane gives Δf ≈ fxΔx + fyΔy.

What is ∫−∞∞ e−x^{2} dx?

√π. From “Double Integrals” →

In plain English: Double integrals over general regions need careful limits, and some become easy only after swapping the order of integration or switching to polar coordinates. Key idea: Sketch regions to set limits; swap the order when the inner integral is hard; polar coordinates use dA = r dr dθ; the Gaussian integral is √π.

What kind of object is ∇ · F?

A scalar field. From “Vector Calculus” →

In plain English: Vector calculus studies vector fields, such as wind velocity, electric fields and fluid flow, using three differential operators (gradient, divergence and curl) and integral theorems that link them. Key idea: Vector fields are acted on by grad, div, curl and the Laplacian; curl grad = 0 and div curl = 0; integral theorems relate a region to its boundary.

What is dS on a sphere of radius R?

R2 sin φ dφ dθ. From “Surface Integrals” →

In plain English: Surface integrals add up quantities over curved surfaces: the area of a dome, the mass of a shell, or the flux of a fluid or field through a membrane. Key idea: Parametrise the surface, find |ru × rv|, and integrate; flux uses F · n with a chosen orientation; constant fields through flat regions are easy.

What physical law is the divergence theorem applied to electric fields?

Gauss's law. From “Divergence Theorem” →

In plain English: The divergence theorem says that the total outward flux through a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence over the volume inside. Key idea: Outward flux = ∭ div F dV; use it to swap hard surface integrals for volume ones; it's the basis of Gauss's law and conservation equations.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Multivariable and Vector Calculus

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is ∂/∂x of x2y + y3? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    2xy. Treat y as a constant: the y3 term vanishes and x2y gives 2xy.

  2. ∇f = (3, 4) at a point. What is the rate of change in the direction (0, 1)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4. (0, 1) is already a unit vector, and (3, 4) · (0, 1) = 4.

  3. Which vector is normal to the sphere x2 + y2 + z2 = 9 at (1, 2, 2)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    (2, 4, 4). ∇(x2 + y2 + z2) = (2x, 2y, 2z) = (2, 4, 4), along the radius.

  4. Why does xy/(x2 + y2) have no limit at (0, 0)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Different paths give different values: 0 on the axes, ½ on y = x. A limit must be the same along every path of approach.

  5. ∇f = (6, 8) at P. What is the rate of change in the direction of (−8, 6)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0, since it's perpendicular to ∇f. (6, 8) · (−8, 6) = −48 + 48 = 0: that direction runs along the level curve.

  6. What does one step of gradient descent do? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Moves x a small step in the direction −∇f(x). Stepping against the gradient decreases f fastest locally.

  7. Why reverse the order in ∫₀¹∫y¹ ex^{2} dx dy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    ex^{2} has no elementary antiderivative in x, but after swapping the inner integral is in y. Swapping gives ∫₀¹ x ex^{2} dx = (e − 1)/2, which is elementary.

  8. Why must a gradient field be irrotational? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    curl(∇f) = 0 because mixed partial derivatives are equal. Each curl component is a difference of equal mixed partials, like fxy − fyx.

  9. Why can't a non-constant harmonic function have a maximum inside its domain? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its value equals the average over any small sphere, so it can't exceed all nearby values. The mean value property rules out strict interior maxima (the maximum principle).

Key takeaways

  • Partial derivatives hold other variables fixed; mixed partials agree when continuous; the tangent plane gives Δf ≈ fxΔx + fyΔy.
  • Duf = ∇f · u with u a unit vector; the maximum rate is |∇f| in the direction of ∇f; along level curves the rate is zero.
  • ∇f points uphill, perpendicular to level sets; it gives normals to curves and surfaces; conservative fields are gradients; ∇f = 0 at smooth extrema.
  • Compute multiple integrals by iterating; Fubini lets you choose the order; they give volumes, masses, averages and probabilities.
  • Sketch regions to set limits; swap the order when the inner integral is hard; polar coordinates use dA = r dr dθ; the Gaussian integral is √π.
  • Describe the solid with nested inequalities; choose coordinates to match its shape; include the Jacobian factors r and ρ2 sin φ.

Common mistakes in Multivariable and Vector Calculus

  • Concluding a limit exists after checking only one or two paths.
  • Forgetting to normalise the direction vector to length 1.
  • Using the gradient as a tangent direction instead of a normal to the level curve.
  • Using constant limits for the inner integral when the region isn't a rectangle.
  • Leaving out the factor r in the polar area element.
  • Letting φ run from 0 to 2π in spherical coordinates (it runs from 0 to π).

Lessons in this school

  1. Multivariable Calculus
    Multivariable calculus extends derivatives and integrals to functions of several variables, like temperature across a room or profit depending on price and advertising.
  2. Directional Derivatives
    A directional derivative measures how fast a function changes as you move in any chosen direction, not just along the axes.
  3. Gradient
    The gradient ∇f collects all the partial derivatives of a function into a vector.
  4. Multiple Integrals
    Multiple integrals add up quantities over areas and volumes: mass from density, probability from a joint density, average temperature over a region.
  5. Double Integrals
    Double integrals over general regions need careful limits, and some become easy only after swapping the order of integration or switching to polar coordinates.
  6. Triple Integrals
    Triple integrals add up quantities over solid regions: volume, mass and centre of mass.
  7. Jacobians
    The Jacobian matrix collects all first partial derivatives of a transformation, and its determinant measures how the transformation stretches area or volume.
  8. Change of Variables
    Changing variables in a multiple integral replaces awkward regions or integrands with simple ones, at the cost of a Jacobian factor.
  9. Vector Calculus
    Vector calculus studies vector fields, such as wind velocity, electric fields and fluid flow, using three differential operators (gradient, divergence and curl) and integral theorems that link them.
  10. Divergence
    The divergence of a vector field measures how much it spreads out from a point: positive at sources, negative at sinks, zero for an incompressible flow.
  11. Curl
    The curl of a vector field measures its local rotation: the axis and strength of the swirl a tiny paddle wheel would feel.
  12. Laplacian Operator
    The Laplacian ∇2f is the divergence of the gradient: it compares a function's value at a point with its average nearby.
  13. Line Integrals
    A line integral adds up a quantity along a curve: the mass of a wire, or the work done by a force along a path.
  14. Surface Integrals
    Surface integrals add up quantities over curved surfaces: the area of a dome, the mass of a shell, or the flux of a fluid or field through a membrane.
  15. Volume Integrals
    Volume integrals add a scalar or vector quantity over a three-dimensional region: total mass, charge, energy or the volume itself.
  16. Green's Theorem
    Green's theorem links a line integral around a closed plane curve with a double integral over the region inside.
  17. Stokes' Theorem
    Stokes' theorem says the circulation of a field around a closed curve equals the flux of its curl through any surface bounded by that curve.
  18. Divergence Theorem
    The divergence theorem says that the total outward flux through a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence over the volume inside.
  19. Matrix Calculus
    Matrix calculus differentiates functions of vectors and matrices with respect to those vectors and matrices.
  20. Tensor Calculus
    Tensor calculus extends vector calculus to curved spaces and to quantities with several indices, like stress or curvature.
  21. Calculus of Variations
    The calculus of variations finds functions that make an integral as small or large as possible: the shortest path, the fastest slide, the shape of a hanging chain.
  22. Fractional Calculus
    Fractional calculus defines derivatives and integrals of non-integer order, such as a half-derivative.

Multivariable and Vector Calculus: questions students ask

What does Clairaut's theorem say?

Mixed second partials are equal if they're continuous.

What is a directional derivative?

What is the gradient of f?

∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂z), the vector of partial derivatives.

How do you find the average of f over R?

Divide ∬R f dA by the area of R.
