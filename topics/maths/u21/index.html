Key terms and formulas at a glance

Time value of money
The principle that money now is worth more than the same amount later, because it can earn interest
Simple interest
I = Prt, for principal P, annual rate r and time t in years
Effective annual rate
AER = (1 + r/m)m − 1 for nominal rate r compounded m times a year
Discount factor
v = 1/(1 + i), the present value of 1 due in one period at interest rate i
Present value
PV = Σ Ct/(1 + r)ᵗ, the sum of discounted future cash flows
Future value
FV = PV(1 + r)ⁿ for a single sum after n periods
Annuity immediate
Payments at the end of each period; PV factor aₙ = (1 − vⁿ)/i
Amortisation schedule
A table showing, for each payment, the interest, the capital repaid and the remaining balance
Repayment mortgage
A mortgage whose monthly payments repay both interest and capital over the term
Terminal value
The value of all cash flows beyond the forecast horizon, often from a growing perpetuity
Net present value
NPV = Σ Ct/(1 + r)ᵗ, including the initial outlay as a negative C₀
Internal rate of return
The rate r* with Σ Ct/(1 + r*)ᵗ = 0

Five sample flashcards

What is arbitrage?

A riskless profit from mispricing. From “Financial Mathematics” →

In plain English: Financial mathematics applies mathematical tools to money: interest, investment, pricing and risk. Key idea: Financial mathematics covers interest, valuation, portfolios, derivatives and actuarial work; the time value of money and no arbitrage are core principles; probability models risk.

What does WACC combine?

The costs of debt and equity weighted by their shares of financing. From “Discounted Cash Flow” →

In plain English: Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis values an investment, project or company as the present value of its expected future cash flows. Key idea: DCF values an asset by discounting forecast cash flows plus a terminal value; WACC or CAPM sets the rate; terminal value and assumptions dominate, so sensitivity analysis is essential.

Under the risk-neutral measure, what do discounted prices form?

Martingales. From “Quantitative Finance” →

In plain English: Quantitative finance uses mathematical models, statistics and computation to price securities, manage risk and design trading strategies. Key idea: Quantitative finance prices derivatives with risk-neutral measures and manages risk with real-world models; it uses stochastic calculus, simulation and statistics; model risk and crises reveal its limits.

What feature makes rates return to a long-run level?

Mean reversion. From “Interest Rate Models” →

In plain English: Interest rate models describe how rates evolve over time and across maturities, the yield curve. Key idea: Yield curves link rates to maturities; forward rates come from spot rates; Vasicek, CIR and Hull–White model the short rate with mean reversion; models are calibrated to market curves.

Who bears investment risk in a DC scheme?

The member. From “Pension Mathematics” →

In plain English: Pension mathematics values the promises made by pension schemes and sets the contributions needed to fund them. Key idea: DB schemes promise salary-linked benefits with sponsor risk; DC schemes give members a pot; liabilities are PVs of projected benefits; discount rates and longevity strongly affect funding levels.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Financial Mathematics and Economics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which principle says two portfolios with identical future payoffs must have the same price? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    No arbitrage. If prices differed, buying the cheap one and selling the dear one would give a riskless profit.

  2. £1200 is invested at 4% simple interest for 6 months. How much interest is earned? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    £24. Simple interest is I = Prt = 1200 × 0.04 × 0.5 = £24, with the 6 months written as half a year.

  3. What is the effective annual rate of 12% compounded monthly? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 12.68%. (1 + 0.12/12)12 − 1 = 1.0112 − 1 ≈ 0.1268.

  4. Why must cash flows at different dates be discounted before comparing them? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Money at different times has different value because of interest. Converting to present values puts every payment on the same footing.

  5. Why does compound interest eventually exceed simple interest at the same rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It earns interest on previously added interest, giving exponential rather than linear growth. Each period's interest joins the principal for the next period.

  6. Why does continuous compounding give the largest amount for a given nominal rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    (1 + r/m)m increases with m and tends to er as m → ∞. More frequent compounding means interest starts earning interest sooner.

  7. Why is NPV preferred to the payback period? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It accounts for the time value of money and all cash flows, including those after payback. Payback ignores flows after the cut-off and doesn't discount.

  8. Why do mean–variance optimisers often give extreme, unstable weights? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small errors in expected return estimates are magnified, pushing weights towards assets that look best by chance. Optimisation acts as an "error maximiser" when inputs are noisy.

  9. Why does adding stochastic volatility make a market incomplete? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Volatility risk can't be hedged with the stock and bond alone, so many martingale measures fit. A new source of randomness without a matching traded asset leaves claims unreplicable.

Key takeaways

  • Financial mathematics covers interest, valuation, portfolios, derivatives and actuarial work; the time value of money and no arbitrage are core principles; probability models risk.
  • Simple interest I = Prt grows linearly; A = P(1 + rt); it's used for short terms and accrued interest; compound interest overtakes it after one period.
  • Compound interest grows exponentially; frequency raises the effective rate; continuous compounding gives Pert; the rule of 72 estimates doubling time.
  • Accumulate with (1 + i)ⁿ and discount with vⁿ; equations of value equate cash flows at one date; real rates adjust for inflation; this underlies all valuation.
  • PV discounts future cash flows to today; perpetuities have PV C/r and growing ones C/(r − g); higher discount rates lower PV, most strongly for distant flows.
  • Future value compounds lump sums and regular payments; FV = P((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)/r for regular savings; solve for payments or time to meet targets.

Common mistakes in Financial Mathematics and Economics

  • Comparing cash flows at different dates without discounting them to a common date.
  • Using months as t without converting to years.
  • Comparing nominal rates with different compounding frequencies without converting to AER.
  • Adding cash flows due at different dates without moving them to a common date.
  • Using a growing perpetuity formula when growth is at or above the discount rate.
  • Treating start-of-period payments as end-of-period, which understates future value.

Lessons in this school

  1. Financial Mathematics
    Financial mathematics applies mathematical tools to money: interest, investment, pricing and risk.
  2. Simple Interest
    Simple interest is paid only on the original principal, so it grows by the same amount every period.
  3. Compound Interest
    Compound interest adds each period's interest to the principal, so interest earns interest.
  4. Time Value of Money
    The time value of money says a pound today is worth more than a pound in the future, because it can be invested.
  5. Present Value
    Present value is today's worth of future cash flows, found by discounting each at the appropriate interest rate.
  6. Future Value
    Future value is what money or a series of payments will be worth at a later date after earning interest.
  7. Annuities
    An annuity is a series of equal payments at regular intervals, such as loan repayments, pensions or regular savings.
  8. Loan Amortisation
    Amortisation repays a loan with level payments that cover interest and gradually reduce the balance.
  9. Mortgage Mathematics
    A mortgage is a long-term loan secured on property, repaid by monthly amortising payments over 20 to 35 years.
  10. Discounted Cash Flow
    Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis values an investment, project or company as the present value of its expected future cash flows.
  11. Net Present Value (NPV)
    Net present value is the present value of an investment's cash inflows minus the present value of its outflows.
  12. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
    The internal rate of return is the discount rate that makes a project's NPV zero.
  13. Bond Pricing
    A bond pays fixed coupons and returns its face value at maturity.
  14. Equity Valuation
    Equity valuation estimates what a company's shares are worth.
  15. Portfolio Theory
    Portfolio theory studies how combining assets changes risk and return.
  16. Modern Portfolio Theory
    Modern portfolio theory, founded by Harry Markowitz in 1952, chooses portfolios that give the highest expected return for each level of risk.
  17. Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
    The Capital Asset Pricing Model says an asset's expected return depends only on its exposure to market risk, measured by beta.
  18. Arbitrage Pricing Theory
    Arbitrage pricing theory, proposed by Stephen Ross in 1976, explains expected returns by exposure to several systematic factors, such as inflation, growth and interest rates.
  19. Quantitative Finance
    Quantitative finance uses mathematical models, statistics and computation to price securities, manage risk and design trading strategies.
  20. Mathematical Finance
    Mathematical finance is the rigorous theory behind financial models: martingales, stochastic integrals and the fundamental theorems of asset pricing.
  21. Financial Modelling
    Financial modelling builds spreadsheet or code models of a company's finances to forecast results, value it and test decisions.
  22. Stochastic Calculus
    Stochastic calculus extends calculus to functions of random processes such as Brownian motion.
  23. Geometric Brownian Motion
    Geometric Brownian motion is the standard model for stock prices: returns are normally distributed and prices stay positive with log-normal distribution.
  24. Derivatives Pricing
    Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on another asset, such as forwards, futures, options and swaps.
  25. Options Pricing
    An option gives the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set strike price.
  26. Binomial Options Pricing
    The binomial model, introduced by Cox, Ross and Rubinstein in 1979, lets a stock move up or down each step.
  27. Black–Scholes Model
    The Black–Scholes model (1973) gives a closed-form price for European options assuming the stock follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility.
  28. Interest Rate Models
    Interest rate models describe how rates evolve over time and across maturities, the yield curve.
  29. Risk Theory
    Risk theory, or ruin theory, models an insurer's surplus as premiums come in and random claims go out.
  30. Market Risk Modelling
    Market risk is the risk of losses from movements in market prices: shares, interest rates, currencies and commodities.
  31. Credit Risk Modelling
    Credit risk is the risk that a borrower fails to repay.
  32. Value at Risk (VaR)
    Value at Risk is the loss level that won't be exceeded with a given confidence over a given period, such as "99% one-day VaR of £1m".
  33. Expected Shortfall
    Expected shortfall, also called conditional VaR, is the average loss in the worst (1 − α) of cases.
  34. Actuarial Mathematics
    Actuarial mathematics combines interest, probability and statistics to value future payments that depend on uncertain events such as death, illness or accidents.
  35. Insurance Mathematics
    Insurance mathematics prices general insurance, such as motor, home and liability cover, where claims vary in number and size.
  36. Survival Models
    Survival models describe the time until an event such as death, failure or relapse.
  37. Pension Mathematics
    Pension mathematics values the promises made by pension schemes and sets the contributions needed to fund them.
  38. Mathematical Economics
    Mathematical economics expresses economic theory precisely with optimisation, equilibrium and fixed-point theorems.
  39. Econometrics
    Econometrics applies statistics to economic data to estimate relationships, test theories and forecast.
  40. Microeconomic Modelling
    Microeconomic models describe individual decisions and markets: supply and demand, consumer choice, firm behaviour and market structure.
  41. Macroeconomic Modelling
    Macroeconomic models describe whole economies: output, inflation, unemployment and interest rates.
  42. Game-Theoretic Economics
    Game theory models strategic interaction, where each player's best choice depends on what others do.
  43. Cost-Benefit Analysis
    Cost–benefit analysis compares the total social costs and benefits of a policy or project, expressed in money and discounted over time.
  44. Forecasting
    Economic and business forecasting predicts future values such as sales, inflation or demand.
  45. Financial Time Series
    Financial time series, such as daily returns of shares or currencies, have distinctive features: little autocorrelation in returns, fat tails and volatility that clusters in calm and turbulent periods.

Financial Mathematics and Economics: questions students ask

What is the simple interest formula?

I = Prt, for principal P, annual rate r and time t in years.

What does the rule of 72 estimate?

Doubling time: 72 divided by the percentage rate.

What is the exact real rate for 5% nominal and 2% inflation?

1.05/1.02 − 1 ≈ 2.94%.

What is the present value formula for a stream of cash flows?

PV = Σ Ct/(1 + r)ᵗ, the sum of the discounted future cash flows.
