Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Write 4/√x in index form.

4x−1/2. From “Precalculus” →

In plain English: Precalculus is the toolkit you need before calculus: functions and their graphs, algebraic manipulation, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometry. Key idea: Calculus rests on algebra, indices, functions, logs and radian trigonometry; rewrite expressions as sums of powers first.

What is d2s/dt2 for motion?

Acceleration. From “Higher-Order Derivatives” →

In plain English: Differentiating a derivative gives the second derivative, and so on. Key idea: Differentiate repeatedly; f″ is the rate of change of the gradient: acceleration, or concavity.

Does L'Hôpital's rule use the quotient rule?

No: differentiate the top and bottom separately. From “L'Hôpital's Rule” →

In plain English: L'Hôpital's rule evaluates limits of the indeterminate forms 0/0 and ∞/∞ by differentiating the top and bottom separately. Key idea: Only for 0/0 or ∞/∞; differentiate numerator and denominator separately; repeat if needed; rewrite other forms first.

What is ∫ 5x4 dx?

x5 + c. From “Indefinite Integrals” →

In plain English: An indefinite integral is the family of all antiderivatives of a function. Key idea: Raise the power by one and divide by the new power; ∫ 1/x = ln|x|; always add c; check by differentiating.

Does the trapezium rule over- or underestimate ∫ x2 dx?

Overestimate: x2 is convex. From “Numerical Integration” →

In plain English: Many integrals can't be found exactly, and data often comes as a table rather than a formula. Key idea: Trapezium: (h/2)[ends + 2 × middles]; overestimates convex curves; Simpson's rule is more accurate.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Calculus

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is 5/x3 in index form? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    5x−3. Dividing by x3 is multiplying by x−3, and the 5 stays as a coefficient.

  2. Whose notation, dy/dx and ∫, do we use today? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Leibniz. Leibniz introduced dy/dx and the integral sign; Newton used dots and "fluxions".

  3. What is limx→∞ (5x + 2)/(2x − 1)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    5/2. Divide top and bottom by x: (5 + 2/x)/(2 − 1/x) → 5/2.

  4. Which is (x2 + 3)/√x in index form? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    x3/2 + 3x−1/2. x2/x1/2 = x3/2 and 3/x1/2 = 3x−1/2: divide each term separately.

  5. What made calculus rigorous in the 19th century? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The precise definition of a limit. Cauchy and Weierstrass defined limits precisely, replacing vague infinitesimals.

  6. For f(x) = |x| at x = 0, what do the secant gradients from the right and left approach? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    +1 from the right and −1 from the left, so the limit doesn't exist. The one-sided limits differ, so the difference quotient has no limit at 0.

  7. What is limx→0 (sin 3x)/x? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    3. (sin 3x)/x = 3 × (sin 3x)/(3x), and (sin u)/u → 1 as u → 0.

  8. What is d/dx (1/(2x + 3)2)? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    −4/(2x + 3)3. (2x + 3)−2 gives −2(2x + 3)−3 × 2 = −4(2x + 3)−3.

  9. Why can't the power rule differentiate xx? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The exponent isn't a constant. The power rule needs xn with n constant; here the exponent varies with x.

Key takeaways

  • Calculus rests on algebra, indices, functions, logs and radian trigonometry; rewrite expressions as sums of powers first.
  • Differentiation finds rates, integration finds accumulations, and they're inverses; limits made the infinitesimal ideas rigorous.
  • Substitute if possible; for 0/0 factorise, rationalise or use standard limits; compare left and right limits.
  • Continuous means the limit equals the value; a sign change of a continuous function guarantees a root.
  • Differentiable means a unique tangent slope; corners, jumps and vertical tangents fail; differentiable implies continuous, not conversely.
  • The derivative is the limit of chord gradients: the tangent's slope and the instantaneous rate of change.

Common mistakes in Calculus

  • Using degrees in calculus: d/dx (sin x) = cos x only when x is in radians.
  • Treating dy/dx as an ordinary fraction in every situation: it's a limit, though it often behaves like a fraction.
  • Concluding that a 0/0 limit is 0, 1 or undefined without further work.
  • Using a sign change to claim a root when the function has an asymptote in the interval.
  • Assuming that a continuous function must be differentiable.
  • Confusing the value of a function with its rate of change: y can be large while dy/dx is small.

Lessons in this school

  1. Precalculus
    Precalculus is the toolkit you need before calculus: functions and their graphs, algebraic manipulation, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometry.
  2. Infinitesimal Calculus
    Calculus began with the idea of infinitely small changes, the "infinitesimals" of Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century.
  3. Limits
    A limit describes the value a function approaches as its input approaches some value.
  4. Continuity
    A function is continuous at a point if its graph has no break there: you could draw it without lifting your pen.
  5. Differentiability
    A function is differentiable at a point if it has a well-defined tangent there: a single slope.
  6. Differential Calculus
    Differential calculus studies how quantities change.
  7. Derivatives
    A derivative is defined as the limit of the gradient of a chord, and computed in practice with rules.
  8. First Derivatives
    The first derivative f′(x) tells you the gradient of a curve everywhere: where it rises, falls and levels off.
  9. Higher-Order Derivatives
    Differentiating a derivative gives the second derivative, and so on.
  10. Chain Rule
    The chain rule differentiates composite functions, functions of functions like (3x + 1)5 or esin x.
  11. Product Rule
    The product rule differentiates a product of two functions.
  12. Quotient Rule
    The quotient rule differentiates one function divided by another.
  13. Implicit Differentiation
    Implicit differentiation finds dy/dx when y isn't the subject, as on the circle x2 + y2 = 25.
  14. Logarithmic Differentiation
    Logarithmic differentiation takes the log of both sides before differentiating.
  15. Parametric Differentiation
    When a curve is given parametrically, x = f(t) and y = g(t), its gradient comes from the chain rule: dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt).
  16. Rates of Change
    Derivatives measure how fast one quantity changes with another.
  17. Related Rates
    Related rates problems connect the rates of change of several linked quantities.
  18. L'Hôpital's Rule
    L'Hôpital's rule evaluates limits of the indeterminate forms 0/0 and ∞/∞ by differentiating the top and bottom separately.
  19. Stationary Points
    Stationary points are where a curve's gradient is zero: maxima, minima and stationary points of inflection.
  20. Critical Points
    Critical points are where a function's derivative is zero or doesn't exist.
  21. Local Maxima and Minima
    A local maximum is higher than all nearby points; a local minimum is lower.
  22. Global Optimisation
    Global optimisation finds the absolute largest or smallest value of a function, often under constraints.
  23. Concavity and Convexity
    Concavity describes how a graph bends: concave up (convex), like a cup, or concave down (concave), like a cap.
  24. Points of Inflection
    A point of inflection is where a curve changes concavity, from bending one way to bending the other.
  25. Curve Sketching
    Curve sketching uses calculus and algebra together to draw an accurate picture of a function: its intercepts, asymptotes, stationary points, inflections and end behaviour.
  26. Integral Calculus
    Integral calculus is the mathematics of accumulation: adding up infinitely many small pieces to find areas, volumes, distances and totals.
  27. Indefinite Integrals
    An indefinite integral is the family of all antiderivatives of a function.
  28. Riemann Sums
    A Riemann sum approximates the area under a curve by adding up the areas of thin rectangles.
  29. Definite Integrals
    A definite integral ∫ab f(x) dx gives a number: the net signed area between the curve and the x-axis from a to b.
  30. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
    The fundamental theorem of calculus links the two halves of calculus: differentiation and integration are inverse processes.
  31. Integration by Substitution
    Integration by substitution reverses the chain rule.
  32. Integration by Parts
    Integration by parts reverses the product rule.
  33. Partial Fraction Integration
    Rational functions are integrated by splitting them into partial fractions (School 3), each of which integrates to a logarithm or a power.
  34. Trigonometric Integration
    Integrating trigonometric functions uses standard results, identities to rewrite powers and products, and substitutions.
  35. Improper Integrals
    An improper integral has an infinite limit or an integrand that blows up somewhere in the interval.
  36. Numerical Integration
    Many integrals can't be found exactly, and data often comes as a table rather than a formula.
  37. Arc Length and Surface Area
    Integration measures more than areas: the length of a curve and the surface area of a solid of revolution come from adding up tiny straight pieces.
  38. Volumes of Revolution
    Rotating a region around an axis creates a solid of revolution.
  39. Sequences and Series
    A sequence is a list of numbers; a series is their sum.
  40. Infinite Series
    An infinite series adds infinitely many terms.
  41. Convergence and Divergence
    Deciding whether an infinite series converges is a central question in analysis.
  42. Power Series
    A power series is an infinite polynomial, Σ an(x − c)n.
  43. Taylor Series
    A Taylor series represents a function as a power series built from its derivatives at one point.
  44. Maclaurin Series
    A Maclaurin series is a Taylor series about x = 0.

Calculus: questions students ask

Why must trigonometric calculus use radians?

The derivative formulas (such as d/dx sin x = cos x) only hold in radians.

Who independently invented calculus?

Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz.

When does limx→a f(x) exist?

When the left and right limits exist and are equal.

Is f(x) = 1/(x − 2) continuous at x = 2?

No: it isn't defined there (an infinite discontinuity).
