Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does the parallel postulate say (Playfair's form)?

Through a point not on a line there is exactly one line parallel to it. From “Euclidean Geometry” →

In plain English: Euclidean geometry is the geometry of flat space, built by Euclid around 300 BCE from a handful of definitions and axioms. Key idea: Euclidean geometry deduces theorems from axioms; the parallel postulate distinguishes it from other geometries.

What is the circumference of a circle with radius 7 cm, in terms of π?

14π cm. From “Circles” →

In plain English: A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from a centre. Key idea: C = 2πr and A = πr2; include straight edges in perimeters of parts of circles; give exact answers in π when asked.

How many faces does an octahedron have?

8. From “Polyhedra” →

In plain English: A polyhedron is a solid with flat polygonal faces. Key idea: There are five Platonic solids; V − E + F = 2 for every convex polyhedron.

What is the eccentricity of a circle?

0. From “Conic Sections” →

In plain English: Conic sections are the curves formed by slicing a double cone with a plane: circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas. Key idea: Slicing a cone gives circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas; eccentricity and the signs of the squared terms classify them.

What is the densest packing of equal circles in the plane?

The hexagonal packing (about 90.7%). From “Discrete Geometry” →

In plain English: Discrete geometry studies finite or countable sets of geometric objects: points, lines, polygons and lattices, and how they're arranged. Key idea: Pick's theorem: area = I + B/2 − 1 for lattice polygons; packing problems ask for the densest arrangements.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Geometry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Around when did Euclid write the Elements? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 300 BCE. Euclid worked in Alexandria around 300 BCE.

  2. An isosceles triangle has an apex angle of 50°. What is each base angle? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    65°. The base angles share 180 − 50 = 130°, so each is 65°.

  3. What are points that lie on the same straight line called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Collinear. Collinear means on one line; coplanar means on one plane.

  4. Which result depends on the parallel postulate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The angles in a triangle add to 180°. The angle sum proof uses a parallel line and alternate angles, which rely on the parallel postulate.

  5. Which shape always has exactly one pair of parallel sides? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A trapezium. A trapezium has exactly one pair (in the UK definition); the others have two or none.

  6. Two distinct planes that aren't parallel meet in: (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A line. Two non-parallel planes always intersect along a straight line, like the pages of a book along the spine.

  7. Two similar cones have volumes 54 cm³ and 16 cm³. The larger has surface area 90 cm². What is the smaller's? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    40 cm². Volume ratio 16/54 = (2/3)3, so k = 2/3 and the area ratio is 4/9: 90 × 4/9 = 40.

  8. Which quadrilateral has diagonals that are equal and bisect each other, but not necessarily at right angles? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A rectangle. A rectangle's diagonals are equal and bisect each other; they're perpendicular only if it's a square.

  9. Can a regular polygon have interior angles of 145°? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No: the exterior angle 35° doesn't divide 360 exactly. n = 360/35 isn't a whole number, so no regular polygon has that angle.

Key takeaways

  • Euclidean geometry deduces theorems from axioms; the parallel postulate distinguishes it from other geometries.
  • Plane geometry studies 2D shapes; combine shape properties and angle facts, giving reasons.
  • Two points fix a line, three non-collinear points fix a plane; in 3D, non-parallel lines can be skew.
  • Straight line 180°, around a point 360°, vertically opposite angles equal; name angles by three letters with the vertex in the middle.
  • Alternate and corresponding angles are equal, co-interior angles add to 180°; parallel lines share a gradient and perpendicular gradients multiply to −1.
  • Angles add to 180°, the exterior angle equals the two opposite interior angles, and sides must satisfy the triangle inequality.

Common mistakes in Geometry

  • Treating a diagram as a proof: a diagram can mislead, while reasoning from axioms can't.
  • Assuming a shape has a property (equal sides, right angles) because it looks that way in a diagram.
  • Assuming that two lines that aren't parallel must meet: in 3D they may be skew.
  • Reading the wrong scale on a protractor.
  • Calling co-interior angles equal: they add to 180°.
  • Assuming a triangle is isosceles because two sides look equal.

Lessons in this school

  1. Euclidean Geometry
    Euclidean geometry is the geometry of flat space, built by Euclid around 300 BCE from a handful of definitions and axioms.
  2. Plane Geometry
    Plane geometry studies two-dimensional figures (points, lines, angles, polygons and circles) and their measurements.
  3. Points, Lines and Planes
    Points, lines and planes are the basic undefined objects of geometry.
  4. Angles
    An angle measures the amount of turn between two lines meeting at a point.
  5. Parallel and Perpendicular Lines
    Parallel lines never meet; perpendicular lines meet at right angles.
  6. Triangles
    Triangles are the simplest polygons and the building blocks of all others.
  7. Congruence and Similarity
    Congruent shapes are identical in size and shape; similar shapes have the same shape but may differ in size.
  8. Quadrilaterals
    Quadrilaterals are four-sided polygons: squares, rectangles, parallelograms, rhombuses, trapeziums and kites.
  9. Polygons
    A polygon is a closed shape with straight sides.
  10. Pythagorean Theorem
    Pythagoras' theorem says that in a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides.
  11. Circles
    A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from a centre.
  12. Chords and Tangents
    Chords and tangents connect lines and circles.
  13. Circle Theorems
    The circle theorems describe the angles formed by chords, tangents and radii in a circle.
  14. Constructions
    Geometric constructions use only a straight edge and compasses to draw accurate figures: perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, perpendiculars and triangles.
  15. Loci
    A locus is the set of all points satisfying a rule, such as "always 3 m from a tree".
  16. Bearings
    A bearing gives a direction as a three-figure angle measured clockwise from north.
  17. Perimeter
    The perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a shape.
  18. Area
    Area measures the amount of surface a 2D shape covers, in square units.
  19. Arc Length
    An arc is part of a circle's circumference.
  20. Solid Geometry
    Solid geometry studies three-dimensional shapes: prisms, pyramids, cylinders, cones and spheres.
  21. Polyhedra
    A polyhedron is a solid with flat polygonal faces.
  22. Surface Area
    The surface area of a solid is the total area of all its faces or curved surfaces.
  23. Volume
    Volume measures the space a 3D object occupies, in cubic units.
  24. Geometric Transformations
    Transformations move or change shapes: translations, reflections, rotations and enlargements.
  25. Transformations and Symmetry
    A shape has symmetry if a transformation maps it onto itself.
  26. Tessellations
    A tessellation is a pattern of shapes covering a plane with no gaps or overlaps.
  27. Coordinate Geometry
    Coordinate geometry describes points, lines and shapes with numbers, so geometric problems can be solved with algebra.
  28. Analytic Geometry
    Analytic geometry studies curves through their equations: lines, circles and the conics, in two and three dimensions.
  29. Vectors in Geometry
    Vectors have size and direction, and they make geometric proofs remarkably clean.
  30. 3D Coordinate Geometry
    3D coordinate geometry extends coordinates to (x, y, z).
  31. Conic Sections
    Conic sections are the curves formed by slicing a double cone with a plane: circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas.
  32. Parabolas
    A parabola is the set of points equidistant from a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix).
  33. Ellipses
    An ellipse is a stretched circle: the set of points whose distances to two foci add to a constant.
  34. Hyperbolas
    A hyperbola is the set of points whose distances to two foci differ by a constant.
  35. Non-Euclidean Geometry
    Non-Euclidean geometries keep Euclid's first four postulates but replace the parallel postulate.
  36. Spherical Geometry
    Spherical geometry is geometry on the surface of a sphere, where the "straight lines" are great circles.
  37. Hyperbolic Geometry
    Hyperbolic geometry has constant negative curvature: through a point not on a line there are infinitely many parallels, and triangles are "thin", with angle sums less than 180°.
  38. Affine Geometry
    Affine geometry studies the properties preserved by affine transformations: those that keep straight lines straight and parallel lines parallel, though not lengths or angles.
  39. Projective Geometry
    Projective geometry is the geometry of perspective: what stays the same when a scene is projected onto a canvas or a camera.
  40. Fractal Geometry
    Fractals are shapes with detail at every scale, often self-similar: zoom in and you see copies of the whole.
  41. Discrete Geometry
    Discrete geometry studies finite or countable sets of geometric objects: points, lines, polygons and lattices, and how they're arranged.
  42. Convex Geometry
    A convex set contains the whole segment between any two of its points: discs, triangles and filled polygons are convex; crescents aren't.
  43. Computational Geometry
    Computational geometry designs efficient algorithms for geometric problems: convex hulls, intersections, nearest neighbours, triangulations and Voronoi diagrams.
  44. Integral Geometry
    Integral geometry measures sets of geometric objects (lines, needles, planes) using integrals, often with probability.
  45. Differential Geometry
    Differential geometry uses calculus to study curves and surfaces: how they bend and twist.
  46. Riemannian Geometry
    Riemannian geometry studies curved spaces of any dimension using a metric that measures lengths and angles at every point.
  47. Symplectic Geometry
    Symplectic geometry is the geometry of classical mechanics' phase space, where positions and momenta live together.
  48. Algebraic Geometry
    Algebraic geometry studies the shapes defined by polynomial equations, called varieties.
  49. Topological Geometry
    Topological geometry studies the properties of shapes that survive stretching and bending without tearing or gluing.
  50. Knot Theory
    A mathematical knot is a closed loop in 3D space that can't pass through itself.

Geometry: questions students ask

What is a polygon?

A closed plane figure with straight sides.

How many points determine a plane?

Three, if they aren't collinear.

Why isn't AAA a congruence condition?

Equal angles only guarantee the same shape, not the same size.

Which quadrilaterals have diagonals that bisect at right angles?

Rhombuses and squares (and a kite's diagonals are perpendicular).
