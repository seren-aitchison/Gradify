Key terms and formulas at a glance

Time series
Observations y₁, y₂, …, yT recorded at successive times, usually equally spaced
Additive decomposition
yt = Tt + St + Rt, used when seasonal swings are roughly constant in size
Deterministic trend
A trend that is a fixed function of time, such as yt = a + bt + εt
Seasonal period
m, the number of observations per cycle, such as 12 for monthly data with yearly seasonality
Weak stationarity
Constant mean, constant variance and autocovariance depending only on the lag, not on time
First difference
Δyt = yt − yt−1 = (1 − B)yt, with backshift operator B
Autocorrelation function
ρ(k) = Cov(yt, yt−k)/Var(yt), the correlation at lag k
Partial autocorrelation
φkk, the coefficient of yt−k in a regression of yt on yt−1, …, yt−k
Centred moving average
An average of values symmetrically placed around time t, used to estimate the trend at t
Smoothing parameter
α in (0, 1); larger values weight recent observations more heavily
AR(p) model
yt = c + φ₁yt−1 + … + φpyt−p + εt
MA(q) model
yt = μ + εt + θ₁εt−1 + … + θqεt−q

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is seasonality?

A pattern that repeats with a fixed period. From “Time Series Analysis” →

In plain English: A time series is a sequence of observations ordered in time, such as monthly sales, daily temperatures or heartbeats. Key idea: Time series are ordered observations with trend, seasonality, cycles and noise; serial dependence changes inference; plot first, transform if needed, and respect time order when testing.

What should model residuals look like?

White noise. From “Autocorrelation” →

In plain English: Autocorrelation measures how strongly a series is correlated with its own past values. Key idea: The ACF measures correlation with past values; ±1.96/√T bounds test single lags; patterns reveal trend, seasonality and MA order; Ljung–Box tests residuals for white noise.

What does a constant with d = 1 produce in forecasts?

A linear trend. From “ARIMA Models” →

In plain English: ARIMA models extend ARMA to non-stationary series by differencing first: ARIMA(p, d, q) applies an ARMA(p, q) to the d-times differenced data. Key idea: ARIMA differences d times then fits ARMA; random walks, SES and Holt correspond to special cases; drift gives linear trends; intervals widen without bound for d ≥ 1.

What does the Viterbi algorithm find?

The most probable sequence of hidden states. From “Hidden Markov Models” →

In plain English: A hidden Markov model assumes the system switches between unobserved discrete states, each producing observations with its own distribution. Key idea: HMMs have hidden Markov states emitting observations; forward, Viterbi and Baum–Welch algorithms compute likelihoods, paths and parameters; regime-switching models apply them to economics and finance.

What does a flat spectrum indicate?

White noise. From “Spectral Analysis” →

In plain English: Spectral analysis decomposes a time series into oscillations of different frequencies, showing how much variance each frequency contributes. Key idea: Spectral analysis spreads variance across frequencies; the spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocovariance; periodograms estimate it but need smoothing; peaks reveal cycles.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Time Series and Forecasting

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which component describes a long-run upward movement in a series? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The trend. A trend is a persistent long-term change in level.

  2. Which decomposition suits a series whose seasonal peaks grow in proportion to its level? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Multiplicative. Multiplicative seasonal factors scale with the trend.

  3. A series follows yt = yt−1 + 0.5 + εt. What kind of trend does it have? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A stochastic trend (random walk with drift). Accumulated shocks plus drift give a unit-root process.

  4. Why can ordinary regression standard errors mislead with time series data? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Serially correlated errors make the effective sample size smaller than the number of observations. Positive autocorrelation means each observation adds less new information.

  5. Why are seasonally adjusted figures at the end of a series often revised? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Trend and seasonal estimates near the end use fewer future observations, so they change as new data arrive. Centred filters need data on both sides; at the end, forecasts fill in until actual values come.

  6. Why are Fourier terms preferred to dummies for weekly data with yearly seasonality? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A few sine–cosine pairs capture a smooth 52-week pattern with far fewer parameters than 51 dummies. Parsimony matters when the period is long.

  7. Why do regressions between unrelated random walks often look significant? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Both series wander persistently, so they share apparent trends by chance, inflating R2 and t-statistics. Non-stationary errors invalidate standard inference (spurious regression).

  8. Why are the ADF and KPSS tests often used together? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They have opposite null hypotheses, so agreement between them gives stronger evidence. ADF assumes a unit root and KPSS assumes stationarity; low power makes each alone inconclusive.

  9. Why might about 1 in 20 ACF spikes cross the 95% bounds even for white noise? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Each bound is a 5% test, so checking many lags produces some false alarms by chance. Multiple testing means joint tests like Ljung–Box are more reliable.

Key takeaways

  • Time series are ordered observations with trend, seasonality, cycles and noise; serial dependence changes inference; plot first, transform if needed, and respect time order when testing.
  • Decomposition separates trend, seasonal and remainder; additive suits constant swings and multiplicative proportional ones; X-13 and STL are standard; adjusted figures remove seasonality.
  • Trends are estimated by regression or smoothing; log-linear slopes give growth rates; deterministic and stochastic trends behave differently under shocks; unit root tests tell them apart; beware spurious regressions.
  • Seasonality repeats with period m; it shows in plots, ACF spikes and periodogram peaks; dummies, Fourier terms or seasonal differencing model it; multiple and moving seasonality need special methods.
  • Stationary series have constant mean, variance and lag-dependent autocovariance; ADF and KPSS tests check it; differencing, logs and seasonal differencing transform series towards stationarity.
  • Differencing removes trends and seasonality; d differences make an I(d) series stationary; log differences give growth rates; over-differencing adds unwanted structure.

Common mistakes in Time Series and Forecasting

  • Randomly splitting a time series into training and test sets, which leaks future information.
  • Using an additive decomposition when seasonal swings grow with the level.
  • Regressing two independent random walks on each other and reporting a strong relationship.
  • Including a dummy for every season as well as an intercept, causing perfect collinearity.
  • Fitting an ARMA model to a trending series without differencing.
  • Differencing more than needed, which inflates forecast variance.

Lessons in this school

  1. Time Series Analysis
    A time series is a sequence of observations ordered in time, such as monthly sales, daily temperatures or heartbeats.
  2. Time Series Decomposition
    Decomposition splits a series into trend–cycle, seasonal and remainder components.
  3. Trend Analysis
    Trend analysis estimates the long-run direction of a series and asks whether an apparent trend is real.
  4. Seasonality
    Seasonality is regular variation with a fixed period: higher energy use in winter, retail spikes at Christmas, rush-hour traffic.
  5. Stationarity
    A stationary series has statistical properties that don't change over time: constant mean, variance and autocorrelation structure.
  6. Differencing
    Differencing replaces a series by the changes between successive values.
  7. Autocorrelation
    Autocorrelation measures how strongly a series is correlated with its own past values.
  8. Partial Autocorrelation
    The partial autocorrelation at lag k measures the correlation between yt and yt−k after removing the effects of the lags in between.
  9. Moving Averages
    Moving averages smooth a series by averaging neighbouring values, revealing trends and removing noise or seasonality.
  10. Exponential Smoothing
    Exponential smoothing forecasts with weighted averages of past observations, with weights falling off exponentially for older data.
  11. AR Models
    An autoregressive model predicts a value from a linear combination of its own past values plus noise.
  12. MA Models
    A moving-average model expresses each value as a combination of current and past random shocks.
  13. ARMA Models
    ARMA models combine autoregressive and moving-average parts, describing stationary series parsimoniously.
  14. ARIMA Models
    ARIMA models extend ARMA to non-stationary series by differencing first: ARIMA(p, d, q) applies an ARMA(p, q) to the d-times differenced data.
  15. SARIMA Models
    Seasonal ARIMA models add seasonal AR, MA and differencing terms at multiples of the seasonal period.
  16. Multivariate Time Series
    Multivariate time series track several related variables over time, such as GDP, inflation and interest rates.
  17. Vector Autoregression (VAR)
    A vector autoregression models several series together, each regressed on lags of all of them.
  18. Vector Error Correction Models
    When non-stationary series share a long-run equilibrium, they're cointegrated, and a vector error correction model describes both their short-run changes and their pull back towards equilibrium.
  19. State-Space Models
    State-space models describe an observed series through an unobserved state that evolves over time.
  20. Kalman Filters
    The Kalman filter estimates the hidden state of a linear system from noisy measurements, updating its estimate each time a new observation arrives.
  21. Hidden Markov Models
    A hidden Markov model assumes the system switches between unobserved discrete states, each producing observations with its own distribution.
  22. Functional Time Series
    Functional time series are sequences of curves rather than numbers: a daily electricity demand curve, a yearly mortality-by-age curve, or the yield curve each day.
  23. ARCH Models
    ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity) models let a series' variance depend on recent squared shocks, capturing the clustering of volatility.
  24. GARCH Models
    GARCH models generalise ARCH by letting the conditional variance depend on its own past values too.
  25. Volatility Modelling
    Volatility, the size of typical price fluctuations, is central to risk management and option pricing.
  26. Change-Point Detection
    Change-point detection finds times when a series' statistical properties shift: its mean, variance or trend.
  27. Structural Break Analysis
    Structural breaks are lasting changes in the relationships a model describes, such as a policy change altering how inflation responds to interest rates.
  28. Spectral Analysis
    Spectral analysis decomposes a time series into oscillations of different frequencies, showing how much variance each frequency contributes.
  29. Frequency-Domain Analysis
    Frequency-domain methods analyse and filter series through their frequency components using the Fourier transform.
  30. Forecast Evaluation
    Forecast evaluation measures how good forecasts are and compares methods fairly.
  31. Prediction Intervals
    A prediction interval gives a range in which a future observation is expected to fall with stated probability.
  32. Probabilistic Forecasting
    Probabilistic forecasting provides a full predictive distribution for future values, not just a point or interval.
  33. Intermittent Demand Forecasting
    Intermittent demand has many periods of zero demand and occasional non-zero values, typical of spare parts and slow-moving stock.
  34. Causal Forecasting
    Causal (explanatory) forecasting predicts a variable from other variables believed to drive it, such as sales from price, advertising and weather.

Time Series and Forecasting: questions students ask

What does a unit root imply about shocks?

They have permanent effects.

What is the seasonal period m?

The number of observations per cycle, such as 12 for monthly data with yearly seasonality.

What is the null hypothesis of the ADF test?

A unit root (non-stationarity).
