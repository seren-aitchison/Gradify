What is a conjecture?

A statement believed true but not yet proved. From “Mathematical Reasoning” →

In plain English: Mathematical reasoning is the disciplined thinking that turns observations into conjectures and conjectures into proofs. Key idea: Mathematical reasoning cycles through exploring, conjecturing, testing and proving; specialising, generalising, invariants and counterexamples are key habits; intuition and rigour work together.

What is the payoff of proving a theorem for all groups?

It applies to every specific group at once. From “Abstract Reasoning” →

In plain English: Abstract reasoning strips away specific details to reveal common structure, so one argument applies to many situations. Key idea: Abstraction isolates shared structure; groups, vector spaces, graphs and metric spaces unify many examples; one proof covers all instances; balance theory with concrete examples.

What is the binomial approximation for (1 + x)ⁿ?

1 + nx for small x. From “Approximation” →

In plain English: Approximation replaces exact quantities or functions by simpler ones that are close enough for the purpose. Key idea: Approximations replace hard quantities with simpler ones; linear, Taylor, binomial and small-angle forms are standard; error bounds and valid ranges must be known.

What makes a hypothesis falsifiable?

Some possible outcome would show it false. From “Hypothesis Formulation” →

In plain English: A hypothesis is a precise, testable statement about what you expect to find. Key idea: Hypotheses must be specific, falsifiable and grounded; nulls state no effect; counterexamples falsify conjectures; pre-registration and separating exploration from confirmation prevent HARKing.

What's the difference between reproducibility and replicability?

Same data and code versus a new study with new data. From “Reproducible Research” →

In plain English: Reproducible research means others can regenerate your results from your data and code. Key idea: Reproducibility re-runs the same data and code; replicability repeats studies; version control, notebooks, containers and FAIR data sharing make work reproducible and trustworthy.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Mathematical Reasoning and Research Methods

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the best response to a conjecture that holds for the first ten cases? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Look for counterexamples and then try to prove it. Patterns can fail later, so the conjecture needs testing and proof.

  2. What is a corollary? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A result that follows readily from a theorem. Corollaries are quick consequences of a main result.

  3. Which argument form is valid? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Modus tollens is valid; the others are fallacies or unwarranted.

  4. Why do mathematicians specialise before generalising? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Particular cases reveal structure and suggest what the general statement and proof should be. Working examples builds the intuition that guides a general argument.

  5. Why should a proof be tested on a specific example? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Seeing the steps work on a concrete case can expose hidden errors or gaps. A step that fails on an example can't be valid in general.

  6. Why can a valid argument have a false conclusion? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Validity guarantees truth only when every premise is true; a false premise can lead to a false conclusion. Validity is about form, not the actual truth of premises.

  7. What does Guy's "strong law of small numbers" warn about? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Small numbers are too few for all the patterns asked of them, so coincidences pose as rules. Many early patterns are coincidences that break down later.

  8. What does Simpson's paradox show? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A trend in every subgroup can reverse when unequal groups are combined. Unequal group weights can flip the aggregate comparison.

  9. Why does the binomial approximation (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx get worse for large n even with small x? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The neglected term n(n − 1)x2/2 grows with n2, so it stops being small when nx isn't small. The first neglected term is n(n − 1)x2/2, roughly (nx)2/2, so the approximation needs nx to be small, not just x.

Key takeaways

  • Mathematical reasoning cycles through exploring, conjecturing, testing and proving; specialising, generalising, invariants and counterexamples are key habits; intuition and rigour work together.
  • Proofs state hypotheses, follow a strategy and justify each step; lemmas organise long proofs; critical reading checks every step, case and hypothesis.
  • Deduction guarantees conclusions from true premises; modus ponens and tollens are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are fallacies; soundness adds true premises.
  • Inductive reasoning generalises from cases to probable conclusions; it suggests conjectures; famous patterns fail later; mathematical induction is deductive; strength depends on evidence quality.
  • Abstraction isolates shared structure; groups, vector spaces, graphs and metric spaces unify many examples; one proof covers all instances; balance theory with concrete examples.
  • Quantitative reasoning uses proportions, rates, units and estimation; base rates, relative versus absolute risk and Simpson's paradox are classic traps; natural frequencies clarify reasoning.

Common mistakes in Mathematical Reasoning and Research Methods

  • Treating a pattern that fits several cases as proved.
  • Dividing by an expression without checking that it can't be zero.
  • Affirming the consequent: reasoning from Q back to P.
  • Confusing inductive generalisation with mathematical induction.
  • Learning abstract definitions without connecting them to concrete examples.
  • Reporting only relative risk, which can make tiny absolute changes sound dramatic.

Lessons in this school

  1. Mathematical Reasoning
    Mathematical reasoning is the disciplined thinking that turns observations into conjectures and conjectures into proofs.
  2. Mathematical Proofs
    A mathematical proof is a convincing argument that a statement follows from definitions, axioms and accepted results.
  3. Deductive Reasoning
    Deductive reasoning draws conclusions that must be true if the premises are true.
  4. Inductive Reasoning
    Inductive reasoning generalises from examples to broader claims.
  5. Abstract Reasoning
    Abstract reasoning strips away specific details to reveal common structure, so one argument applies to many situations.
  6. Quantitative Reasoning
    Quantitative reasoning uses numbers, units, proportions and data to understand and decide.
  7. Problem Solving
    Mathematical problem solving is the art of tackling unfamiliar problems without a ready-made method.
  8. Estimation
    Estimation finds approximate answers quickly using rounding, known reference values and sensible assumptions.
  9. Approximation
    Approximation replaces exact quantities or functions by simpler ones that are close enough for the purpose.
  10. Error Propagation
    Error propagation tracks how uncertainties in measured inputs carry through a calculation into uncertainty in the result.
  11. Research Methodology
    Research methodology is the systematic approach to asking and answering questions.
  12. Literature Review
    A literature review surveys what's already known about a topic, finding the key results, methods and open questions.
  13. Hypothesis Formulation
    A hypothesis is a precise, testable statement about what you expect to find.
  14. Model Validation
    Model validation checks whether a model represents the real system well enough for its intended use.
  15. Model Verification
    Model verification checks that a computational model correctly implements its intended mathematics: the code solves the equations right.
  16. Computational Experiments
    Computational experiments use computers to explore mathematical questions, test conjectures and simulate systems.
  17. Reproducible Research
    Reproducible research means others can regenerate your results from your data and code.
  18. Mathematical Visualisation
    Mathematical visualisation turns functions, data and abstract structures into pictures that reveal patterns, guide intuition and communicate results.
  19. Mathematical Communication
    Mathematical communication shares ideas so that others can understand, check and use them.
  20. Mathematical Writing
    Mathematical writing presents definitions, results and arguments in clear, precise prose with well-chosen notation.
  21. Scientific Reporting
    Scientific reporting presents research so that others can understand, evaluate and reproduce it.
