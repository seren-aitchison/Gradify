Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Is ℚ complete?

No: some bounded sets, like {q : q2 < 2}, have no rational supremum. From “Real Analysis” →

In plain English: Real analysis puts calculus on rigorous foundations: precise definitions of limits, continuity and convergence, built on the completeness of the real numbers. Key idea: Limits and continuity have ε definitions; completeness of ℝ drives monotone convergence and Bolzano–Weierstrass; uniform convergence preserves continuity.

What does Fejér's theorem say?

Cesàro means of the Fourier series of a continuous periodic function converge uniformly. From “Harmonic Analysis” →

In plain English: Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into basic waves and studies how well the pieces reconstruct the whole. Key idea: Parseval equates energy in the function and its coefficients; L2 series converge in mean and (Carleson) almost everywhere; harmonic analysis extends to groups and wavelets.

What is ∫ δ(x) cos x dx?

1. From “Distribution Theory” →

In plain English: Distributions, or generalised functions, give rigorous meaning to objects like the Dirac delta, which physicists used long before mathematicians could justify them. Key idea: Distributions act on test functions; δ samples at a point; derivatives are defined by moving them onto test functions; H′ = δ; Green's functions solve Lu = δ.

What does Cauchy–Schwarz state?

|⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖. From “Hilbert Spaces” →

In plain English: A Hilbert space is a complete space with an inner product, so it has lengths, angles and orthogonal projections, even in infinite dimensions. Key idea: Inner products give length, angle and projection; orthonormal bases expand every vector; Parseval and Riesz representation hold; L2 and ℓ2 are the models.

What is the spectrum of a function in C[0, 1]?

Its range. From “Banach Algebras” →

In plain English: A Banach algebra is a Banach space with a multiplication compatible with the norm. Key idea: Banach algebras combine completeness and multiplication; spectra are non-empty and compact; Neumann series invert 1 − x for ‖x‖ < 1; Gelfand theory turns commutative ones into function algebras.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Analysis

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which sequence converges? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    (1 + 1/n). 1 + 1/n → 1; the others oscillate or diverge.

  2. Which function is entire (holomorphic everywhere)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    ez. ez has a convergent power series everywhere; the others have poles or fail Cauchy–Riemann.

  3. What guarantees that Fourier series of square-integrable functions converge in mean square? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Completeness of the trigonometric system in L2. The sines and cosines form an orthonormal basis of L2.

  4. How does Liouville's theorem prove the fundamental theorem of algebra? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    If p had no roots, 1/p would be bounded and entire, hence constant. A non-constant polynomial p makes 1/p non-constant, so p must have a root.

  5. Why does multiplying by a fixed non-zero complex number w preserve angles between directions? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It rotates every direction by arg w and scales every length by |w|, so angles between directions are unchanged. A holomorphic map with non-zero derivative acts locally like multiplication by f′(z), which is why it's conformal.

  6. Why are wavelets useful where Fourier series struggle? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They localise in both time and frequency, capturing short events. Sines spread over the whole interval; wavelets capture transients and edges compactly.

  7. The functions fn(x) = xn on [0, 1] converge pointwise. Why isn't the convergence uniform? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The limit is discontinuous at 1, but uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous. The limit is 0 for x < 1 and 1 at x = 1, a jump that uniform convergence would forbid.

  8. The irrational numbers in [0, 1] have what Lebesgue measure? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    1. [0, 1] has measure 1 and the rationals have measure 0, so the irrationals have measure 1.

  9. Why doesn't ∫ fn → ∫ f for fn = n on (0, 1/n) and 0 elsewhere? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No integrable function dominates all the fn. fn → 0 pointwise but each has integral 1; the bound sup fn isn't integrable.

Key takeaways

  • Limits and continuity have ε definitions; completeness of ℝ drives monotone convergence and Bolzano–Weierstrass; uniform convergence preserves continuity.
  • Holomorphic ⇔ Cauchy–Riemann; holomorphic functions are analytic; ∮ f dz = 2πi Σ residues; Liouville and contour methods give powerful results.
  • Parseval equates energy in the function and its coefficients; L2 series converge in mean and (Carleson) almost everywhere; harmonic analysis extends to groups and wavelets.
  • Measures are countably additive set functions on σ-algebras; Lebesgue measure extends length; countable and some uncountable sets have measure 0; not every set is measurable.
  • Lebesgue integration slices the range; it integrates more functions; monotone and dominated convergence justify swapping limits and integrals.
  • Distributions act on test functions; δ samples at a point; derivatives are defined by moving them onto test functions; H′ = δ; Green's functions solve Lu = δ.

Common mistakes in Analysis

  • Choosing N that depends on n rather than only on ε.
  • Including poles outside the contour in the sum of residues.
  • Assuming every Fourier series converges at every point.
  • Assuming that a set with measure zero must be countable.
  • Swapping a limit and an integral without a dominating function: fn = n·1(0, 1/n) → 0 but ∫ fn = 1.
  • Treating δ as an ordinary function with a value at 0.

Lessons in this school

  1. Real Analysis
    Real analysis puts calculus on rigorous foundations: precise definitions of limits, continuity and convergence, built on the completeness of the real numbers.
  2. Complex Analysis
    Complex analysis studies differentiable functions of a complex variable.
  3. Harmonic Analysis
    Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into basic waves and studies how well the pieces reconstruct the whole.
  4. Measure Theory
    Measure theory generalises length, area and probability to assign sizes to very complicated sets.
  5. Lebesgue Integration
    Lebesgue integration slices the range of a function rather than its domain.
  6. Distribution Theory
    Distributions, or generalised functions, give rigorous meaning to objects like the Dirac delta, which physicists used long before mathematicians could justify them.
  7. Functional Analysis
    Functional analysis studies infinite-dimensional vector spaces of functions, equipped with norms or inner products, and the linear maps between them.
  8. Banach Spaces
    A Banach space is a complete normed vector space: Cauchy sequences always converge.
  9. Hilbert Spaces
    A Hilbert space is a complete space with an inner product, so it has lengths, angles and orthogonal projections, even in infinite dimensions.
  10. Operator Theory
    Operator theory studies linear maps between infinite-dimensional spaces: their norms, adjoints, inverses and special classes such as compact, self-adjoint and unitary operators.
  11. Spectral Theory
    Spectral theory generalises eigenvalues to operators on infinite-dimensional spaces.
  12. Banach Algebras
    A Banach algebra is a Banach space with a multiplication compatible with the norm.
  13. C*-Algebras
    A C-algebra is a Banach algebra with an adjoint operation whose norm obeys ‖xx‖ = ‖x‖2.
  14. Operator Algebras
    Operator algebras are algebras of operators on Hilbert space closed under adjoints and suitable limits: C*-algebras and von Neumann algebras.

Analysis: questions students ask

Is f(z) = conj(z) holomorphic?

No: it fails the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

What did Carleson prove?

Fourier series of L2 functions converge almost everywhere.

Why are measures defined on σ-algebras rather than all subsets?

Some sets can't be consistently measured.

What does the dominated convergence theorem need?

Pointwise convergence and a single integrable bound.
