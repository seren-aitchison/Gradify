Key terms and formulas at a glance

Measures of centre
Mean (sum ÷ count), median (middle value) and mode (most common value)
Pearson's correlation coefficient
r = Sxy/√(SxxSyy), between −1 and 1
Relative risk
The ratio of risks in two groups, such as 1.5 (a 50% increase)
Margin of error
The likely size of sampling error, often given as ±3 percentage points for a poll of about 1,000
Parameter
A fixed, usually unknown, number describing a population (such as μ or p)
Bias
E(θ̂) − θ: how far an estimator is from the truth on average
Sampling frame
The list from which a sample is drawn, ideally covering the whole population
Sampling distribution
The probability distribution of a statistic, such as x̄, across repeated samples
Selection bias
Systematic differences between those sampled and the population
Confidence interval
An interval, calculated from data, that would contain the parameter in a stated proportion (such as 95%) of repeated samples
Likelihood
L(θ) = Π f(xi; θ), the probability (or density) of the data as a function of θ
Method of moments
Set E(Xk) = (1/n)Σxik for k = 1, 2, … and solve for the parameters

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which measure of centre is resistant to outliers?

The median. From “Descriptive Statistics” →

In plain English: Descriptive statistics summarise data with a few numbers and pictures: where the centre is, how spread out the values are, and what shape they make. Key idea: Use median and IQR for skewed data or outliers, mean and SD for symmetric data; flag outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule; plot first.

What is a Type I error in a fire alarm?

The alarm sounds with no fire. From “Type I and Type II Errors” →

In plain English: Every test can go wrong in two ways: rejecting a true null hypothesis (a Type I error, a false alarm) or failing to reject a false one (a Type II error, a missed effect). Key idea: Type I: false rejection (α); Type II: missed effect (β); reducing one raises the other unless n increases; weigh their costs.

What are the degrees of freedom for one-way ANOVA with 4 groups and 20 observations?

(3, 16). From “Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)” →

In plain English: Analysis of variance tests whether several group means are equal by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within them. Key idea: Total SS = between + within; F = MSB/MSW with (k − 1, N − k) degrees of freedom; follow up with post hoc tests.

What does an ICC of 0 mean?

Groups explain none of the variance: observations within groups are no more alike than across groups. From “Mixed-Effects Models” →

In plain English: Mixed-effects (multilevel) models handle data with groups or repeated measurements: pupils in schools, patients in hospitals, measurements over time on the same person. Key idea: Random effects model grouping; ICC is the group share of variance; partial pooling shrinks small groups towards the mean; ignoring clustering understates uncertainty.

Why doesn't Metropolis–Hastings need the posterior's normalising constant?

It only uses ratios of the target density. From “Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)” →

In plain English: Markov chain Monte Carlo draws samples from complicated distributions, especially Bayesian posteriors, by running a random walk designed to spend time in each region in proportion to its probability. Key idea: MCMC builds a Markov chain with the target as its stationary distribution; Metropolis needs only density ratios; check burn-in, R̂ and effective sample size.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Statistics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which pair of summaries suits house prices best? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Median and interquartile range. House prices are right-skewed, so robust measures describe them better.

  2. A correlation of r = 0.8 means a straight line explains what share of the variation in y? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    64%. The proportion explained is r2 = 0.64.

  3. A bar chart's y-axis starts at 95 instead of 0. What's the risk? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Small differences look large. Truncating the axis exaggerates the visual difference between bars.

  4. Q1 = 20 and Q3 = 30. Which value would the 1.5 × IQR rule flag as an outlier? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    46. The upper fence is 30 + 15 = 45 and the lower is 5, so only 46 lies outside.

  5. Data lie exactly on y = x2 for x from −3 to 3. What is the Pearson correlation r, and why? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0, because r measures only linear association and the pattern is symmetric. Pearson's r detects straight-line trends; a symmetric parabola has none.

  6. Treatment cuts the risk of an illness from 4% to 3%. What is the relative risk reduction? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    25%. The fall of 1 point is a quarter of the original 4%.

  7. The points lie exactly on y = x2 for x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2. What is Pearson's r? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    0. The pattern is a perfect symmetric curve with no linear trend, so r = 0.

  8. A national poll and a city poll each use 1,000 random respondents. How do their margins of error compare? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    About the same. Precision depends on the sample size, not the population size, when the sample is a small fraction.

  9. The MLE of a normal σ2 is 9. What is the MLE of σ? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    3. By invariance, the MLE of √(σ2) is √9 = 3.

Key takeaways

  • Use median and IQR for skewed data or outliers, mean and SD for symmetric data; flag outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule; plot first.
  • r = Sxy/√(SxxSyy) measures linear association; r2 is variance explained; correlation isn't causation; plot the data.
  • Check axes, denominators and units; report absolute as well as relative risk; distinguish per cent from percentage points.
  • Official figures are estimates with uncertainty; polls of 1,000 have about ±3 points of sampling error; prefer medians for skewed quantities.
  • Samples estimate population parameters; report estimates with uncertainty; inference relies on random sampling and checked assumptions.
  • Judge estimators by bias, variance, MSE and consistency; s2 divides by n − 1 to remove bias; the frameworks differ in what probability means.

Common mistakes in Statistics

  • Reporting the mean alone for heavily skewed data.
  • Concluding that one variable causes the other from a correlation alone.
  • Reading a 50% relative increase as a 50% chance of harm.
  • Treating a poll lead smaller than the margin of error as decisive.
  • Confusing the sample statistic with the population parameter.
  • Assuming an unbiased estimator is always best.

Lessons in this school

  1. Descriptive Statistics
    Descriptive statistics summarise data with a few numbers and pictures: where the centre is, how spread out the values are, and what shape they make.
  2. Correlation
    Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.
  3. Data Interpretation
    Data interpretation is reading charts, tables and statistics critically: what they show, what they hide and how they might mislead.
  4. Statistics in Everyday Life
    Statistics shape everyday decisions: inflation figures set pay rises and benefits, polls guide elections, weather forecasts plan days out, and medical risks inform treatment.
  5. Inferential Statistics
    Inferential statistics draws conclusions about a population from a sample, with a measure of how uncertain those conclusions are.
  6. Statistical Inference
    Statistical inference has several frameworks (frequentist, Bayesian and likelihood-based) and a common toolkit for judging estimators: bias, variance, consistency and efficiency.
  7. Sampling Theory
    Sampling theory designs ways to choose samples so that they represent the population and give precise estimates for a given cost.
  8. Sampling Distributions
    A sampling distribution is the distribution of a statistic over all possible samples.
  9. Sampling Bias
    Sampling bias happens when the way a sample is chosen makes it unrepresentative.
  10. Confidence Intervals
    A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for a parameter, with a stated confidence level.
  11. Maximum Likelihood Estimation
    Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the parameter values that make the observed data most probable.
  12. Method of Moments
    The method of moments estimates parameters by matching the sample's moments (mean, variance, …) to the model's theoretical moments.
  13. Hypothesis Testing
    A hypothesis test asks whether data are consistent with a claim about a population.
  14. Null Hypothesis Significance Testing
    Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is the standard ritual of declaring results "significant" when p < 0.05.
  15. P-Values
    A p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting data at least as extreme as those observed.
  16. Type I and Type II Errors
    Every test can go wrong in two ways: rejecting a true null hypothesis (a Type I error, a false alarm) or failing to reject a false one (a Type II error, a missed effect).
  17. Statistical Power
    Statistical power is the probability that a test detects an effect that's really there.
  18. Effect Sizes
    An effect size measures how big a difference or relationship is, independently of sample size.
  19. Multiple Testing Correction
    When many hypotheses are tested, some will be "significant" by chance alone.
  20. False Discovery Rate
    The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected proportion of false positives among the results declared significant.
  21. Parametric Statistics
    Parametric methods assume the data come from a family of distributions described by a few parameters, usually the normal.
  22. Nonparametric Statistics
    Nonparametric methods make few assumptions about the shape of the population, often working with ranks or signs.
  23. Z-Tests
    A z-test compares a statistic with its hypothesised value using the standard normal distribution.
  24. T-Tests
    The t-test compares means when the population standard deviation is unknown and estimated from the data.
  25. Chi-Squared Tests
    Chi-squared tests compare observed counts with the counts expected under a hypothesis.
  26. Fisher's Exact Test
    Fisher's exact test analyses 2 × 2 tables using the exact hypergeometric probabilities, rather than a chi-squared approximation.
  27. Mann–Whitney U Test
    The Mann–Whitney U test (also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) compares two independent groups using ranks.
  28. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test
    The Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares paired data, or one sample with a hypothesised median, using the ranks of the absolute differences.
  29. Kruskal–Wallis Test
    The Kruskal–Wallis test compares three or more independent groups using ranks.
  30. Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test
    The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares whole distributions: a sample with a hypothesised distribution, or two samples with each other.
  31. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)
    Analysis of variance tests whether several group means are equal by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within them.
  32. Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA)
    Analysis of covariance combines ANOVA with regression: it compares group means after adjusting for a continuous covariate, such as a baseline score.
  33. Categorical Data Analysis
    Categorical data analysis deals with outcomes that fall into categories: yes or no, blood group, disease stage.
  34. Contingency Tables
    A contingency table cross-tabulates counts for two or more categorical variables.
  35. Regression Analysis
    Regression analysis models how a response variable depends on one or more predictors.
  36. Linear Regression
    Simple linear regression fits the straight line ŷ = a + bx that minimises the sum of squared residuals.
  37. Multiple Linear Regression
    Multiple linear regression models a response using several predictors at once.
  38. Logistic Regression
    Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome, such as disease or no disease, pass or fail, using predictors.
  39. Poisson Regression
    Poisson regression models counts, such as hospital admissions, accidents or species sightings, using a log link so predicted counts stay positive.
  40. Nonlinear Regression
    Nonlinear regression fits models whose parameters enter non-linearly, such as exponential growth, saturation curves and enzyme kinetics.
  41. Robust Regression
    Robust regression fits lines that aren't thrown off by outliers or heavy-tailed errors.
  42. Quantile Regression
    Quantile regression models how a chosen quantile of the response, such as the median or the 90th percentile, depends on predictors.
  43. Ridge Regression
    Ridge regression adds a penalty on the size of the coefficients to least squares.
  44. Lasso Regression
    The lasso penalises the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients.
  45. Generalised Linear Models (GLMs)
    Generalised linear models unify linear, logistic, Poisson and other regressions in one framework: a distribution for the response, a linear predictor and a link function joining them.
  46. Mixed-Effects Models
    Mixed-effects (multilevel) models handle data with groups or repeated measurements: pupils in schools, patients in hospitals, measurements over time on the same person.
  47. Generalised Additive Models
    Generalised additive models (GAMs) replace straight-line terms in a GLM with smooth curves learned from the data.
  48. Statistical Modelling
    Statistical modelling is the craft of building, fitting, checking and revising models that describe data and the process generating them.
  49. Multivariate Statistics
    Multivariate statistics studies several variables measured together, keeping track of how they relate.
  50. Multivariate Analysis
    Multivariate analysis covers techniques that find structure in many variables at once: principal components, canonical correlations, correspondence analysis and multidimensional scaling.
  51. Factor Analysis
    Factor analysis explains correlations among many observed variables by a few unobserved (latent) factors, such as general intelligence or anxiety.
  52. Discriminant Analysis
    Discriminant analysis classifies observations into known groups using their measurements, and finds the combinations of variables that best separate the groups.
  53. Cluster Analysis
    Cluster analysis groups observations so that those in a cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters, without any known labels.
  54. Frequentist Statistics
    Frequentist statistics defines probability as long-run frequency and judges methods by how they perform over repeated samples: how often intervals cover the truth, how often tests err.
  55. Bayesian Statistics
    Bayesian statistics treats unknown quantities as having probability distributions that express uncertainty.
  56. Bayesian Inference
    Bayesian inference is the practical process of fitting a Bayesian model: choosing a likelihood and prior, computing the posterior, summarising it and checking the model.
  57. Prior and Posterior Distributions
    The prior expresses what is believed about a parameter before the data; the posterior expresses what is believed after.
  58. Bootstrap Methods
    The bootstrap estimates the uncertainty of almost any statistic by resampling the data with replacement many times and recalculating the statistic.
  59. Permutation Tests
    A permutation test finds a p-value by shuffling group labels many times and seeing how often the shuffled data look as extreme as the real data.
  60. Monte Carlo Methods
    Monte Carlo methods use random simulation to compute quantities that are hard to find exactly: probabilities, integrals, the behaviour of complex systems.
  61. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
    Markov chain Monte Carlo draws samples from complicated distributions, especially Bayesian posteriors, by running a random walk designed to spend time in each region in proportion to its probability.
  62. Statistical Computing
    Statistical computing is the software, algorithms and numerical care that turn statistical ideas into reliable results.
  63. Experimental Design
    Experimental design plans how to collect data so that the comparisons of interest are fair and precise.
  64. Design of Experiments
    Design of experiments (DoE) studies many factors at once efficiently.
  65. Causal Inference
    Causal inference asks whether, and how much, one thing causes another, not just whether they're associated.
  66. Structural Equation Modelling
    Structural equation modelling (SEM) combines factor analysis with path analysis to test networks of relationships among observed and latent variables.
  67. Meta-Analysis
    Meta-analysis combines the results of several studies to estimate an overall effect more precisely and to explore why studies differ.
  68. Missing Data Analysis
    Almost every real data set has missing values.
  69. Imputation Methods
    Imputation fills in missing values so that complete-data methods can be used.
  70. Survival Analysis
    Survival analysis studies the time until an event (death, relapse, machine failure, customer churn), allowing for censoring, where some subjects haven't had the event by the end of follow-up.
  71. Spatial Statistics
    Spatial statistics analyses data where location matters: pollution readings, disease cases, mineral deposits, house prices.
  72. Reliability Analysis
    Reliability analysis studies how long products and systems work before failing, and how design choices such as redundancy change that.
  73. Extreme Value Theory
    Extreme value theory gives the statistical methods for estimating the probabilities of rare, extreme events from limited data: floods higher than any recorded, record winds, market crashes.
  74. Decision Theory
    Statistical decision theory chooses actions under uncertainty by weighing the consequences of each action against the probabilities of each state of the world.
  75. Statistical Learning
    Statistical learning builds models that learn patterns from data to make predictions or find structure, bridging statistics and machine learning.

Statistics: questions students ask

What does r = −0.9 indicate?

A strong negative linear relationship.

A rate rises from 4% to 6%. What is the rise in percentage points, and in per cent?

2 percentage points; a 50% relative rise.

Which organisation publishes UK inflation figures?

The Office for National Statistics (ONS).

Why divide by n − 1 in the sample variance?

To make it unbiased for σ2.
