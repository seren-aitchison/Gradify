Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the quotient and remainder when 47 is divided by 6?

q = 7, r = 5. From “Elementary Number Theory” →

In plain English: Number theory studies the integers: divisibility, primes and the patterns they hide. Key idea: a | b means b = ka; the division algorithm gives unique quotient and remainder; prove by general forms, cases by remainder and factorising.

What is the next perfect number after 28?

496. From “Perfect Numbers” →

In plain English: A perfect number equals the sum of its proper divisors, like 6 = 1 + 2 + 3. Key idea: Perfect means σ(n) = 2n; even perfect numbers are exactly 2p−1(2p − 1) with 2p − 1 prime; odd perfect numbers are unknown.

How many solutions does a CRT system with moduli 4, 9 and 25 have mod 900?

Exactly one. From “Chinese Remainder Theorem” →

In plain English: The Chinese remainder theorem says that a system of congruences with pairwise coprime moduli has exactly one solution modulo their product. Key idea: Coprime moduli give a unique solution mod their product; solve stepwise or with the Niyi formula; CRT speeds up RSA.

Which algorithm proved primality testing is in polynomial time?

AKS (Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena, 2002). From “Computational Number Theory” →

In plain English: Computational number theory designs algorithms for number-theoretic problems: testing primality, factorising, computing gcds and discrete logarithms. Key idea: Multiplying, gcds, powers and primality testing are fast; factorising and discrete logs aren't (classically); sieves find small primes efficiently.

Which systems rely on discrete logarithms?

Diffie–Hellman, ElGamal and DSA (and their elliptic curve versions). From “Discrete Logarithm Problem” →

In plain English: The discrete logarithm problem asks, given g and h = gx mod p, to find x. Key idea: Discrete logs invert modular powers; generic algorithms take about √p steps; avoid weak primes; quantum computers would break them.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Number Theory and Cryptography

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which form describes every odd integer? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    2k + 1 for some integer k. Odd integers leave remainder 1 on division by 2, so each is 2k + 1.

  2. What is 245 × 378 mod 10? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    0. Only last digits matter mod 10: 5 × 8 = 40 ≡ 0.

  3. Is 918,082 divisible by 11? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Yes: the alternating digit sum is 0. 2 − 8 + 0 − 8 + 1 − 9 = −22, a multiple of 11 (918,082 = 11 × 83,462).

  4. Why is the product of any three consecutive integers divisible by 3? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    One of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3. Remainders mod 3 cycle 0, 1, 2, so one of the three has remainder 0.

  5. Why can't you always cancel a factor in a congruence like 2x ≡ 2y (mod 4)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    2 shares a factor with 4, so x and y may differ by 2. x = 1 and y = 3 satisfy 2x ≡ 2y (mod 4) though x ≢ y (mod 4).

  6. Working mod 8 on the clock, what is 4 × 6? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0, since 24 is a multiple of 8. 24 = 3 × 8, so 4 × 6 ≡ 0 mod 8: two non-zero numbers multiplying to zero, which can't happen with a prime modulus.

  7. How many solutions does 6x ≡ 9 (mod 15) have modulo 15? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    3. gcd(6, 15) = 3 divides 9, so there are 3 solutions: x ≡ 4, 9, 14.

  8. Why can't 2p − 1 be prime when p is composite? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    If p = ab, then 2a − 1 divides 2p − 1. xb − 1 is divisible by x − 1; put x = 2a.

  9. A number n passes the Fermat test for base 2. What can you conclude? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    n is probably prime, but could be composite. Some composites (pseudoprimes such as 341) pass for base 2.

Key takeaways

  • a | b means b = ka; the division algorithm gives unique quotient and remainder; prove by general forms, cases by remainder and factorising.
  • Congruence means equal remainders; reduce before multiplying; powers cycle, so reduce exponents by the cycle length.
  • ax ≡ b (mod n) is solvable iff gcd(a, n) | b; multiply by the inverse when it exists; congruences prove divisibility tests.
  • Repeatedly replace (a, b) by (b, a mod b); the last non-zero remainder is the gcd; it's fast, with steps proportional to the number of digits.
  • Back-substitute through the Euclidean steps to write the gcd as ax + by; when the gcd is 1, x is the inverse of a mod b.
  • ax + by = c is solvable iff gcd(a, b) | c; find one solution and add multiples of (b/d, −a/d); non-linear equations range from easy to undecidable.

Common mistakes in Number Theory and Cryptography

  • Checking a few values of n and calling it a proof.
  • Dividing both sides of a congruence by a number that isn't coprime to the modulus.
  • Assuming every linear congruence has exactly one solution.
  • Stopping at the zero remainder and reporting 0 instead of the previous remainder.
  • Losing track of signs during back-substitution: check the final equation numerically.
  • Giving one solution when the question asks for all of them.

Lessons in this school

  1. Elementary Number Theory
    Number theory studies the integers: divisibility, primes and the patterns they hide.
  2. Modular Arithmetic
    Modular arithmetic is "clock arithmetic": numbers wrap around after reaching a modulus.
  3. Congruences
    A congruence equation such as 3x ≡ 4 (mod 7) asks which integers satisfy a relationship modulo n.
  4. Euclidean Algorithm
    The Euclidean algorithm finds the greatest common divisor of two integers by repeated division.
  5. Extended Euclidean Algorithm
    The extended Euclidean algorithm finds integers x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b).
  6. Diophantine Equations
    Diophantine equations are equations whose solutions must be integers, named after Diophantus of Alexandria.
  7. Divisor Functions
    Divisor functions count or add the divisors of a number.
  8. Perfect Numbers
    A perfect number equals the sum of its proper divisors, like 6 = 1 + 2 + 3.
  9. Fibonacci Numbers
    The Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … each add the two before.
  10. Continued Fractions
    A continued fraction writes a number as an integer plus 1 over (an integer plus 1 over …).
  11. Prime Number Theory
    Prime number theory studies how primes are distributed.
  12. Fermat's Little Theorem
    Fermat's little theorem says that for a prime p and any a not divisible by p, ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p).
  13. Euler's Totient Function
    Euler's totient function φ(n) counts the numbers from 1 to n that are coprime to n.
  14. Euler's Theorem
    Euler's theorem generalises Fermat's little theorem to any modulus: if gcd(a, n) = 1, then aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n).
  15. Chinese Remainder Theorem
    The Chinese remainder theorem says that a system of congruences with pairwise coprime moduli has exactly one solution modulo their product.
  16. Quadratic Residues
    A quadratic residue modulo p is a number that's a perfect square mod p.
  17. Primitive Roots
    A primitive root modulo p is a number whose powers run through every non-zero residue.
  18. Pell's Equation
    Pell's equation x2 − Dy2 = 1, for a positive non-square integer D, always has infinitely many integer solutions: every positive solution is a power of the smallest (fundamental) one, and sign changes give the rest.
  19. Diophantine Approximation
    Diophantine approximation asks how closely real numbers can be approximated by fractions with small denominators.
  20. Analytic Number Theory
    Analytic number theory uses calculus and complex analysis to study integers, especially primes.
  21. Algebraic Number Theory
    Algebraic number theory studies number systems such as the Gaussian integers and ℤ[√−5].
  22. Computational Number Theory
    Computational number theory designs algorithms for number-theoretic problems: testing primality, factorising, computing gcds and discrete logarithms.
  23. Elliptic Curves
    An elliptic curve is a curve y2 = x3 + ax + b whose points can be added together, forming a group.
  24. Arithmetic Geometry
    Arithmetic geometry studies the integer and rational solutions of polynomial equations using geometry.
  25. Cryptography
    Cryptography protects information: keeping it secret, proving it hasn't been altered and confirming who sent it.
  26. Public-Key Cryptography
    Public-key cryptography lets strangers communicate securely without sharing a secret in advance.
  27. Modular Exponentiation
    Modular exponentiation computes ae mod n.
  28. RSA Cryptography
    RSA, published by Rivest, Shamir and Adleman in 1977, was the first practical public-key system for encryption and signatures.
  29. Discrete Logarithm Problem
    The discrete logarithm problem asks, given g and h = gx mod p, to find x.
  30. Elliptic Curve Cryptography
    Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) uses the group of points on an elliptic curve over a finite field.
  31. Cryptographic Hash Functions
    A cryptographic hash function turns any input into a short fixed-length digest that acts like a fingerprint.
  32. Primality Testing
    Primality tests decide whether a number is prime without factorising it.
  33. Integer Factorisation
    Integer factorisation is splitting a number into primes.
  34. Prime Factorisation Algorithms
    Beyond trial division, mathematicians have built clever factorisation algorithms that exploit hidden structure: Pollard's rho and p − 1 methods, Lenstra's elliptic curve method, and the quadratic and number field sieves.
  35. Lattice-Based Cryptography
    Lattice-based cryptography builds security on hard geometric problems in high-dimensional lattices, such as finding short vectors.
  36. Post-Quantum Cryptography
    Post-quantum cryptography is public-key cryptography designed to resist attacks by large quantum computers.

Number Theory and Cryptography: questions students ask

What does it mean for a to divide b?

b = ka for some integer k.

What day of the week is 100 days after a Monday?

Wednesday (100 ≡ 2 mod 7).

Does 4x ≡ 6 (mod 10) have solutions?

Yes: gcd(4, 10) = 2 divides 6, giving two solutions mod 10 (x ≡ 4 or 9).

What step does the Euclidean algorithm repeat, and what does it return?

Replace (a, b) by (b, a mod b) until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd.
