What is the sum of degrees in a graph with 10 edges?

20. From “Graph Theory” →

In plain English: Graph theory studies networks of points (vertices) joined by lines (edges). Key idea: Graphs are vertices plus edges; Σdeg = 2|E|; odd-degree vertices come in even numbers; graphs model any pairwise connections.

What do random forests in machine learning combine?

Many decision trees. From “Trees and Forests” →

In plain English: A forest is a graph with no cycles: a collection of trees. Key idea: A forest is acyclic with n − c edges; edges between trees merge them; union–find tracks components efficiently.

What is the coefficient of kn−1 in P(G, k)?

−|E|. From “Chromatic Polynomials” →

In plain English: The chromatic polynomial P(G, k) counts the proper colourings of a graph using k colours. Key idea: P(G, k) counts k-colourings; trees give k(k − 1)n−1, cycles (k − 1)ⁿ + (−1)ⁿ(k − 1), Kₙ the falling factorial; deletion–contraction computes it.

What does node strength generalise?

Degree. From “Weighted Networks” →

In plain English: Real networks have ties of different strengths: how often people call, how many passengers fly a route, how much two countries trade. Key idea: Strength sums edge weights; decide whether weights are similarities or distances; thresholding simplifies but loses information.

At what average degree does a giant component emerge in G(n, p)?

Just above 1. From “Random Graphs” →

In plain English: Random graph models generate networks by chance, to compare with real ones and to prove that graphs with certain properties exist. Key idea: G(n, p) includes each edge independently; mean degree np; a giant component appears when np > 1 and connectivity when p > ln n/n; real networks differ in clustering and degree tails.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Graph Theory and Networks

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does the degree of a vertex count? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The edges meeting it. The degree counts the edges meeting a vertex, with a loop counted twice.

  2. Which invariant shows two graphs aren't isomorphic? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Different degree sequences. Isomorphisms preserve degrees; labels and drawings don't matter.

  3. Which describes a strongly connected digraph? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Every vertex can reach every other along directed paths. Strong connectivity respects the arc directions.

  4. Why must the number of odd-degree vertices be even? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The total degree 2|E| is even, so odd degrees must pair up. A sum with an odd number of odd terms would be odd.

  5. A graph on 7 vertices has 12 edges. How many does its complement have? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    9. K₇ has C(7, 2) = 21 edges, and the complement takes the missing ones: 21 − 12 = 9.

  6. Why does every tournament contain a Hamiltonian path? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It can be built by inserting vertices one at a time into a growing path. Rédei's theorem: each new vertex fits somewhere in the existing directed path.

  7. Why isn't (3, 3, 1, 1) graphical? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Two degree-3 vertices on 4 vertices must each join all others, giving the others degree ≥ 2. Each degree-3 vertex is adjacent to every other vertex, contradicting degree 1.

  8. Why is union–find almost constant time per operation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Union by rank and path compression keep trees extremely shallow. Their combined cost grows like the inverse Ackermann function, below 5 for practical sizes.

  9. Why must every simple planar graph have a vertex of degree at most 5? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    If all degrees were ≥ 6, then 2E ≥ 6V, contradicting E ≤ 3V − 6. Summing degrees gives 2E ≥ 6V > 2(3V − 6).

Key takeaways

  • Graphs are vertices plus edges; Σdeg = 2|E|; odd-degree vertices come in even numbers; graphs model any pairwise connections.
  • Undirected simple graphs have symmetric adjacency matrices; Kₙ has n(n − 1)/2 edges; complements fill the missing pairs; isomorphism means identical structure.
  • Arcs have direction; in-degrees and out-degrees each sum to the number of arcs; strong vs weak connectivity; Aᵏ counts directed walks.
  • Weights quantify edges; path weight is the sum; interpret them as distance, cost or capacity; watch for negative weights.
  • Degree sums must be even and degrees ≤ n − 1; Havel–Hakimi tests graphicality; many graphs share one sequence.
  • Walks may repeat, trails don't repeat edges, paths don't repeat vertices; distance is shortest path length; diameter is the largest distance; odd cycles rule out bipartiteness.

Common mistakes in Graph Theory and Networks

  • Assuming two different drawings show different graphs.
  • Concluding two graphs are isomorphic because they have the same number of vertices and edges.
  • Treating a directed graph's adjacency matrix as symmetric.
  • Comparing paths by number of edges when the question is about total weight.
  • Assuming an even sum is enough for a sequence to be graphical.
  • Calling any walk between two vertices a path.

Lessons in this school

  1. Graph Theory
    Graph theory studies networks of points (vertices) joined by lines (edges).
  2. Undirected Graphs
    In an undirected graph, edges have no direction: if A is joined to B, then B is joined to A.
  3. Directed Graphs
    In a directed graph (digraph), edges have directions, like one-way streets, web links or "follows" on social media.
  4. Weighted Graphs
    A weighted graph attaches a number to each edge: a distance, cost, time, capacity or strength.
  5. Degree Sequences
    A graph's degree sequence lists its vertex degrees.
  6. Paths and Cycles
    Paths and cycles are the routes through a graph.
  7. Graph Connectivity
    Connectivity measures how well a graph holds together: whether every vertex can reach every other, and how many vertices or edges must fail before it falls apart.
  8. Trees
    A tree is a connected graph with no cycles: the simplest connected structure.
  9. Trees and Forests
    A forest is a graph with no cycles: a collection of trees.
  10. Spanning Trees
    A spanning tree is a tree that includes every vertex of a graph, using some of its edges.
  11. Directed Acyclic Graphs
    A directed acyclic graph (DAG) has directed edges and no directed cycles.
  12. Bipartite Graphs
    A bipartite graph splits its vertices into two groups with edges only between groups: workers and jobs, students and courses, users and products.
  13. Planar Graphs
    A planar graph can be drawn in the plane with no edges crossing.
  14. Planarity
    Planarity testing decides whether a graph can be drawn without crossings, and if not, why not.
  15. Eulerian Paths
    An Eulerian path uses every edge of a graph exactly once.
  16. Hamiltonian Paths
    A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once.
  17. Graph Colouring
    Graph colouring assigns colours to vertices so that adjacent vertices differ.
  18. Chromatic Polynomials
    The chromatic polynomial P(G, k) counts the proper colourings of a graph using k colours.
  19. Matching Theory
    A matching pairs up vertices along edges with no vertex used twice: workers with jobs, students with places, donors with recipients.
  20. Matching and Vertex Covers
    A vertex cover is a set of vertices touching every edge, such as security cameras covering every corridor.
  21. Shortest Path Algorithms
    Shortest path algorithms find the cheapest routes through weighted graphs.
  22. Shortest Path Problems
    Shortest path problems come in many forms: one source or all pairs, fastest or cheapest, with constraints or alternatives.
  23. Minimum Spanning Trees
    A minimum spanning tree connects all vertices of a weighted graph at the least total cost.
  24. Network Flows
    A flow network moves something (water, traffic, data, goods) from a source to a sink through edges with capacities.
  25. Maximum Flow
    The maximum flow problem asks for the largest possible flow from source to sink.
  26. Minimum Cut
    A cut splits a network into a side containing the source and a side containing the sink; its capacity is the total capacity of edges crossing from source side to sink side.
  27. Weighted Networks
    Real networks have ties of different strengths: how often people call, how many passengers fly a route, how much two countries trade.
  28. Network Centrality
    Centrality measures identify the most important nodes in a network, but "important" depends on the question: the most connected person, the best-placed broker, or the one closest to everyone.
  29. Betweenness Centrality
    Betweenness centrality measures how often a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes.
  30. Closeness Centrality
    Closeness centrality measures how near a node is to all others: the inverse of its average distance.
  31. Eigenvector Centrality
    Eigenvector centrality scores a node highly if it's linked to other high-scoring nodes: influence by association.
  32. PageRank Mathematics
    PageRank ranks web pages by imagining a random surfer who follows links and occasionally jumps to a random page.
  33. Community Detection
    Community detection finds groups of nodes more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network: friend circles, research fields, functional modules in cells.
  34. Network Clustering
    Clustering in networks measures how often a node's neighbours are also connected to each other: the tendency of friends to be friends.
  35. Spectral Graph Theory
    Spectral graph theory studies graphs through the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of their matrices, especially the Laplacian.
  36. Random Graphs
    Random graph models generate networks by chance, to compare with real ones and to prove that graphs with certain properties exist.
  37. Small-World Networks
    In small-world networks, any two nodes are linked by a short chain, even though most links are local.
  38. Scale-Free Networks
    In a scale-free network, the degree distribution follows a power law: most nodes have few links but a few hubs have enormous numbers.
  39. Network Science
    Network science studies complex networks across physics, biology, sociology and computing using shared mathematical tools.
  40. Percolation Theory
    Percolation theory studies what happens when parts of a network are randomly removed or kept: at a critical threshold, a spanning cluster suddenly appears or disappears.
  41. Social Network Analysis
    Social network analysis studies relationships among people and organisations: who knows whom, who advises whom, who trades with whom.
  42. Biological Network Analysis
    Biological networks map interactions among genes, proteins, metabolites, neurons and species.
  43. Network Epidemiology
    Network epidemiology models how infections spread along contacts rather than through a perfectly mixed population.
  44. Graph Neural Network Mathematics
    Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn from data on graphs by repeatedly passing messages between neighbouring nodes.
