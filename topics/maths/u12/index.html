Lessons in this school

  1. Probability Theory
    Probability theory is the mathematics of uncertainty.
  2. Foundations of Probability
    Modern probability rests on three axioms set out by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933.
  3. Independent Events
    Two events are independent when knowing that one has happened doesn't change the probability of the other.
  4. Conditional Probability
    Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another has happened.
  5. Joint Probability
    A joint probability is the probability that two or more events, or random variables, take particular values together.
  6. Bayes' Theorem
    Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probabilities: from P(evidence ∣ cause) it gives P(cause ∣ evidence).
  7. Random Variables
    A random variable assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment: the number of heads, the waiting time, the profit.
  8. Discrete Random Variables
    A discrete random variable takes a countable set of values, each with a positive probability.
  9. Continuous Random Variables
    A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval: heights, times, temperatures.
  10. Probability Distributions
    A probability distribution describes how probability is spread over the values of a random variable.
  11. Probability Mass Functions
    A probability mass function (pmf) gives the probability of each value of a discrete random variable.
  12. Probability Density Functions
    A probability density function (pdf) describes a continuous random variable: probabilities are areas under it.
  13. Cumulative Distribution Functions
    The cumulative distribution function (CDF) F(x) = P(X ≤ x) works for every random variable, discrete or continuous.
  14. Expected Value
    The expected value of a random variable is its long-run average: the probability-weighted mean of its values.
  15. Variance
    Variance measures how far a random variable typically strays from its mean: the expected squared deviation.
  16. Standard Deviation
    The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, which puts spread back into the original units.
  17. Covariance
    Covariance measures how two random variables vary together: positive when they rise together, negative when one rises as the other falls.
  18. Moments and Moment-Generating Functions
    Moments summarise a distribution's shape: the first gives the mean, the second the spread, the third the skewness, the fourth the tail weight.
  19. Law of Large Numbers
    The law of large numbers says that the average of many independent repetitions settles down to the expected value.
  20. Central Limit Theorem
    The central limit theorem says that sums and averages of many independent random variables are approximately normally distributed, whatever the original distribution.
  21. Bernoulli Distribution
    The Bernoulli distribution describes a single trial with two outcomes: success with probability p, failure with probability 1 − p.
  22. Binomial Distribution
    The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials with the same probability of success.
  23. Geometric Distribution
    The geometric distribution models the number of trials until the first success.
  24. Negative Binomial Distribution
    The negative binomial distribution counts the number of trials needed to get r successes.
  25. Hypergeometric Distribution
    The hypergeometric distribution counts successes when sampling without replacement from a finite population.
  26. Poisson Distribution
    The Poisson distribution counts events that happen randomly and independently at a constant average rate: calls per minute, decays per second, accidents per year.
  27. Multinomial Distribution
    The multinomial distribution generalises the binomial to more than two outcomes.
  28. Beta-Binomial Distribution
    The beta-binomial distribution is a binomial whose success probability is itself random, drawn from a beta distribution.
  29. Uniform Distribution
    The uniform distribution spreads probability evenly: every value in an interval is equally likely.
  30. Normal Distribution
    The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell-shaped curve defined by its mean and standard deviation.
  31. Standard Normal Distribution
    The standard normal distribution Z ~ N(0, 1) is the normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  32. Exponential Distribution
    The exponential distribution models the waiting time until the next event in a Poisson process: the next customer, decay or failure.
  33. Gamma Distribution
    The gamma distribution models the waiting time until the kth event in a Poisson process, and more generally any positive, right-skewed quantity.
  34. Beta Distribution
    The beta distribution lives on [0, 1], so it naturally models proportions and probabilities.
  35. Chi-Squared Distribution
    The chi-squared distribution is the distribution of a sum of squared independent standard normal variables.
  36. Student's T-Distribution
    Student's t-distribution is like the standard normal but with heavier tails.
  37. F-Distribution
    The F-distribution is the ratio of two independent chi-squared variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom.
  38. Weibull Distribution
    The Weibull distribution models lifetimes and strengths.
  39. Log-Normal Distribution
    A variable is log-normal when its logarithm is normally distributed.
  40. Pareto Distribution
    The Pareto distribution is a power law: the probability of exceeding x falls like x−α.
  41. Cauchy Distribution
    The Cauchy distribution looks like a bell curve but has such heavy tails that it has no mean or variance.
  42. Multivariate Normal Distribution
    The multivariate normal extends the bell curve to several correlated variables, described by a mean vector and a covariance matrix.
  43. Student's T Multivariate Distribution
    The multivariate t-distribution is a heavy-tailed version of the multivariate normal.
  44. Dirichlet Distribution
    The Dirichlet distribution is a distribution over probability vectors: sets of non-negative numbers adding to 1.
  45. Wishart Distribution
    The Wishart distribution is the distribution of a sample covariance (scatter) matrix from multivariate normal data.
  46. Mixture Distributions
    A mixture distribution combines several distributions, each chosen with some probability.
  47. Empirical Distributions
    The empirical distribution puts equal probability on each observed data point.
  48. Heavy-Tailed Distributions
    Heavy-tailed distributions give extreme values far more probability than the normal does.
  49. Stable Distributions
    Stable distributions keep their shape when independent copies are added: the sum is the same type, just shifted and rescaled.
  50. Extreme Value Distributions
    Extreme value distributions describe the largest (or smallest) values in a sample: the highest flood, the strongest gust, the biggest daily loss.

Key terms

Sample space
The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment, often written Ω or S
Kolmogorov's axioms
P(A) ≥ 0; P(Ω) = 1; for mutually exclusive A₁, A₂, …, P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ …) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …
Independent events
Events with P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), equivalently P(A ∣ B) = P(A)
Conditional probability
P(A ∣ B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), defined when P(B) > 0
Joint distribution
A table or function giving P(X = x, Y = y) for every pair of values
Bayes' theorem
P(A ∣ B) = P(B ∣ A)P(A)/P(B)
Random variable
A function X from the sample space to the real numbers
Probability distribution (discrete)
The list of values x with their probabilities P(X = x), adding to 1
Continuous random variable
A variable with P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx for a density f
Parameter
A constant that picks one member of a family, such as n and p for the binomial
Probability mass function
p(x) = P(X = x) for a discrete random variable X
Probability density function
A function f ≥ 0 with ∫ f(x) dx = 1, giving P(a < X < b) = ∫ab f(x) dx
Cumulative distribution function
F(x) = P(X ≤ x)
Expected value
E(X) = Σ x P(X = x) (discrete) or ∫ x f(x) dx (continuous)
