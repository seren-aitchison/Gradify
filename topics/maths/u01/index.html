Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is 7 × 23 = 7 × 20 + 7 × 3?

Multiplication distributes over addition: 7 × (20 + 3) = 7 × 20 + 7 × 3. From “Arithmetic” →

In plain English: Arithmetic is calculating with numbers: adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing. Key idea: Use written methods that keep place value, and check every answer with an inverse operation or an estimate.

Which fractions in lowest terms give terminating decimals?

Those whose denominators have only 2 and 5 as prime factors. From “Rational Numbers” →

In plain English: Rational numbers are numbers that can be written as a fraction of two integers, p/q with q not zero. Key idea: Rationals are fractions of integers; their decimals terminate or recur, and recurring decimals convert by multiplying and subtracting.

How do you prove 87 is composite?

Show a factor pair: 87 = 3 × 29. From “Composite Numbers” →

In plain English: A composite number is a natural number greater than 1 that isn't prime: it has at least one factor other than 1 and itself. Key idea: Composites have proper factors; one factor pair is enough to prove a number composite.

What is 3/8 as a percentage?

37.5%. From “Percentages” →

In plain English: A percentage is a fraction out of 100. Key idea: Percent means per hundred: multiply by the decimal multiplier, or divide and multiply by 100 to compare.

What is 7.2649 to 2 decimal places?

7.26. From “Rounding and Estimation” →

In plain English: Rounding simplifies numbers to a sensible accuracy; estimation uses rounded numbers to predict an answer. Key idea: Round by looking at the next digit; estimate by rounding everything to one significant figure first.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Number and Arithmetic

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which operation is commutative? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Multiplication. 6 × 4 = 4 × 6, but 6 − 4 ≠ 4 − 6 and 6 ÷ 4 ≠ 4 ÷ 6.

  2. What is the value of the digit 4 in 64,215? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4,000. The 4 is in the thousands place, so it's worth 4 × 1,000 = 4,000.

  3. Which of these is a natural number in every convention? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    7. 7 is a counting number; 0 depends on the convention.

  4. Which check best shows that 52 × 19 = 988 is plausible? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    52 × 19 ≈ 50 × 20 = 1,000. Estimating by rounding each number shows the answer should be close to 1,000.

  5. What is 2 × 32? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    18. Indices first: 32 = 9, then 2 × 9 = 18. (2 × 3)2 would be 36.

  6. Why are written calculations hard with Roman numerals? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They aren't positional and have no zero. Positional notation lets column methods work; Roman numerals don't line up by place value.

  7. A student types 10 + 6 ÷ 2 into a calculator wanting (10 + 6) ÷ 2. What do they get? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    13. The calculator divides first: 6 ÷ 2 = 3, then adds 10. Brackets are needed to get 8.

  8. What is 0.\dot{1}\dot{8} as a fraction in its simplest form? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    2/11. Let x = 0.1818…; then 100x − x = 18, so x = 18/99, which simplifies to 2/11.

  9. Which product is rational? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    √8 × √2. √8 × √2 = √16 = 4, a whole number, so it's rational; the others are irrational.

Key takeaways

  • Use written methods that keep place value, and check every answer with an inverse operation or an estimate.
  • Brackets, indices, then multiplication and division left to right, then addition and subtraction left to right.
  • In a positional system, place gives value and zero holds empty places; the base sets the multiplier between places.
  • The natural numbers are the counting numbers; they're closed under addition and multiplication but not subtraction or division.
  • Adding a negative subtracts, subtracting a negative adds, and same signs multiply to a positive.
  • Rationals are fractions of integers; their decimals terminate or recur, and recurring decimals convert by multiplying and subtracting.

Common mistakes in Number and Arithmetic

  • Treating subtraction as commutative: 3 − 7 isn't the same as 7 − 3.
  • Doing all multiplication before any division: they have equal priority and go left to right.
  • Forgetting the zero placeholder, writing 2,046 as 246.
  • Assuming everyone agrees whether 0 is a natural number.
  • Thinking −7 is greater than −2 because 7 is greater than 2.
  • Thinking 0.999… is less than 1: by the same method, it equals 1.

Lessons in this school

  1. Arithmetic
    Arithmetic is calculating with numbers: adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing.
  2. Order of Operations
    When a calculation has several operations, the order you do them in changes the answer.
  3. Number Systems
    A number system is a way of representing numbers with symbols.
  4. Natural Numbers
    The natural numbers are the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, … (and, in many university courses, 0 as well).
  5. Integers
    The integers extend the natural numbers to include zero and the negatives: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ….
  6. Rational Numbers
    Rational numbers are numbers that can be written as a fraction of two integers, p/q with q not zero.
  7. Irrational Numbers
    An irrational number can't be written as a fraction of two integers.
  8. Real Numbers
    The real numbers are all the rational and irrational numbers together: every point on the number line.
  9. Factors and Multiples
    A factor of a number divides it exactly; a multiple is the result of multiplying it by a whole number.
  10. Divisibility
    Divisibility tests tell you whether one number divides another without doing the division.
  11. Prime Numbers
    A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.
  12. Composite Numbers
    A composite number is a natural number greater than 1 that isn't prime: it has at least one factor other than 1 and itself.
  13. Prime Factorisation
    Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from order.
  14. Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)
    The greatest common divisor (GCD), called the highest common factor (HCF) at GCSE, is the largest number that divides two numbers exactly.
  15. Least Common Multiple (LCM)
    The least common multiple (LCM), called the lowest common multiple at GCSE, is the smallest number that is a multiple of two numbers.
  16. Fractions
    A fraction represents part of a whole, or a division: 3/4 means 3 ÷ 4.
  17. Decimals
    Decimals extend place value to the right of the units: tenths, hundredths, thousandths.
  18. Percentages
    A percentage is a fraction out of 100.
  19. Percentage Change and Reverse Percentages
    Percentage change measures an increase or decrease relative to the original amount.
  20. Ratios and Proportions
    A ratio compares the sizes of quantities; a proportion says two ratios are equal.
  21. Exponents and Powers
    A power (exponent, index) tells you how many times to multiply a number by itself.
  22. Roots and Radicals
    A root undoes a power: the square root of 49 is 7 because 72 = 49.
  23. Scientific Notation
    Scientific notation (standard form) writes numbers as A × 10n, with 1 ≤ A < 10.
  24. Rounding and Estimation
    Rounding simplifies numbers to a sensible accuracy; estimation uses rounded numbers to predict an answer.
  25. Significant Figures
    Significant figures count the digits that carry meaning about a number's size and precision.
  26. Sequences of Numbers
    A sequence is an ordered list of numbers following a rule.
  27. Number Patterns
    Number patterns (square, triangular, cube and Fibonacci numbers, and patterns in tables) reveal structure in mathematics.
  28. Number Bases
    A number base is the number of digits a positional system uses.
  29. Binary Arithmetic
    Binary (base 2) uses only the digits 0 and 1, matching the off and on states of electronic circuits.

Number and Arithmetic: questions students ask

What is 12 ÷ 4 × 3?

9: divide and multiply from left to right.

Why is zero important in a positional system?

It holds empty places, so 3,507 and 357 can be told apart.

Is ℕ closed under subtraction?

No: for example 3 − 5 = −2 isn't natural.

What is 6 − (−4)?

10: subtracting a negative is adding.
