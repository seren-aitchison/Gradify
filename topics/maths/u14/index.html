Key terms and formulas at a glance

Discrete structure
A set of separate objects with relations between them, such as a graph or a set of strings
Bijective proof
Proving two sets have the same size by pairing their elements exactly
Product rule
If a task has stages with m and n options, there are mn ways overall
Factorial
n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 1, with 0! = 1
Binomial coefficient
C(n, r) = n!/(r!(n − r)!), the number of r-element subsets of an n-element set
Binomial theorem
(a + b)ⁿ = Σk=0n C(n, k) an−kbk
Pigeonhole principle
If N objects are placed in k boxes and N > k, some box contains at least two
Inclusion–exclusion
|A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|
Ordinary generating function
A(x) = Σ aₙxⁿ for a sequence a₀, a₁, a₂, …
Integer partition
A way to write n as a sum of positive integers, order ignored; p(n) counts them
Mantel's theorem
A triangle-free graph on n vertices has at most ⌊n2/4⌋ edges
Ramsey number
R(s, t), the smallest n such that every red/blue colouring of the edges of Kn has a red Ks or a blue Kt

How many binary strings of length 8 are there?

256. From “Discrete Mathematics” →

In plain English: Discrete mathematics studies countable, separate objects: integers, graphs, logical statements, finite sets and algorithms. Key idea: Discrete maths studies separate objects; key tools are counting, induction, recurrences and logic; it underlies computing.

How many subsets does a 6-element set have?

64. From “Combinations” →

In plain English: A combination is a selection where order doesn't matter: a committee, a lottery ticket, a hand of cards. Key idea: C(n, r) counts unordered selections; use symmetry, Pascal's rule and complements; stars and bars handles repetition.

What is the Bell number B₃?

5. From “Enumerative Combinatorics” →

In plain English: Enumerative combinatorics counts families of structures: partitions of numbers and sets, permutations with given properties, trees and lattice paths. Key idea: p(n) counts number partitions, S(n, k) set partitions into k blocks, Bell numbers all set partitions; recurrences and generating functions organise them.

What are the parameters of the Fano plane?

(7, 7, 3, 3, 1). From “Block Designs” →

In plain English: A balanced incomplete block design (BIBD) arranges v items into b blocks of size k so that every pair of items appears together in exactly λ blocks. Key idea: BIBDs satisfy bk = vr and λ(v − 1) = r(k − 1); Fisher's inequality b ≥ v; the conditions are necessary, not sufficient.

What was the first problem proved NP-complete?

Boolean satisfiability (SAT). From “Computational Complexity Theory” →

In plain English: Complexity theory classifies problems by the resources needed to solve them. Key idea: P = solvable fast, NP = checkable fast; NP-complete problems are the hardest in NP; reductions transfer hardness; P vs NP is open and crucial.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Discrete Mathematics and Combinatorics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which area belongs to discrete rather than continuous mathematics? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Graph theory. Graphs consist of separate vertices and edges.

  2. How many shortest lattice paths go from (0, 0) to (4, 3)? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    35. Choose the 3 up-steps among 7: C(7, 3) = 35.

  3. Number plates have 2 letters then 3 digits. How many are possible? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    676,000. Multiply the choices for each position: 26 × 26 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 676,000.

  4. Why is discrete mathematics central to computer science? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Computers store and process separate symbols, bits and structures. Data, programs and networks are discrete objects.

  5. What does a bijective proof show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Two sets have the same size, by pairing their elements exactly. A one-to-one correspondence proves equal cardinality.

  6. How many ways can 7 people sit around a round table, if only relative positions matter? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    720. Fix one person to remove rotations: (7 − 1)! = 720.

  7. How many 3-letter strings from A–Z contain at least one vowel (A, E, I, O, U)? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    8,315. Total 17,576 minus 213 = 9,261 with no vowels.

  8. How many ways can 3 of 8 people be chosen if two particular people refuse to serve together? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    50. All C(8, 3) = 56 minus those with both: 6 (choose the third from 6).

  9. Four letters go into four addressed envelopes at random. What is the probability none is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    3/8. There are D₄ = 9 derangements among 4! = 24 arrangements, so the probability is 9/24 = 3/8.

Key takeaways

  • Discrete maths studies separate objects; key tools are counting, induction, recurrences and logic; it underlies computing.
  • Combinatorics counts, proves existence and constructs; lattice paths are C(m + n, n); Catalan numbers count nested structures; bijections explain equal counts.
  • Add for disjoint alternatives, multiply for successive stages, subtract complements, divide out overcounting; decide whether order and repetition matter.
  • n! arrangements; P(n, r) = n!/(n − r)!; divide by repeats' factorials; circular is (n − 1)!; factorials grow extremely fast.
  • C(n, r) counts unordered selections; use symmetry, Pascal's rule and complements; stars and bars handles repetition.
  • (a + b)ⁿ = Σ C(n, k)an−kbk; coefficients come from Pascal's triangle; the general series extends to any power for |x| < 1.

Common mistakes in Discrete Mathematics and Combinatorics

  • Proving only the base case and the step for one specific k rather than general k.
  • Counting lattice paths as m × n instead of choosing step positions.
  • Adding counts of overlapping cases.
  • Forgetting to divide by the factorials of repeated letters.
  • Using permutations when order doesn't matter, overcounting by r!.
  • Forgetting to raise the constant term to its power (writing C(5, 3) × 2 instead of × 22).

Lessons in this school

  1. Discrete Mathematics
    Discrete mathematics studies countable, separate objects: integers, graphs, logical statements, finite sets and algorithms.
  2. Combinatorics
    Combinatorics is the mathematics of counting, arranging and selecting.
  3. Counting Principles
    The basic counting principles (add for "or", multiply for "and", subtract the complement, divide out overcounting) solve a huge range of problems.
  4. Permutations
    A permutation is an ordered arrangement.
  5. Combinations
    A combination is a selection where order doesn't matter: a committee, a lottery ticket, a hand of cards.
  6. Binomial Theorem
    The binomial theorem expands (a + b)ⁿ using binomial coefficients, connecting algebra to counting.
  7. Pigeonhole Principle
    If more than n pigeons go into n holes, some hole has at least two.
  8. Inclusion–Exclusion Principle
    Inclusion–exclusion counts the union of overlapping sets by adding sizes, subtracting pairwise overlaps, adding back triple overlaps and so on.
  9. Generating Functions
    A generating function packs a whole sequence into the coefficients of a power series.
  10. Enumerative Combinatorics
    Enumerative combinatorics counts families of structures: partitions of numbers and sets, permutations with given properties, trees and lattice paths.
  11. Extremal Combinatorics
    Extremal combinatorics asks how large or small a structure can be while avoiding (or forcing) some property: the most edges in a graph with no triangle, the largest family of sets where none contains another.
  12. Ramsey Theory
    Ramsey theory shows that complete disorder is impossible: any large enough structure contains an orderly substructure.
  13. Design Theory
    Design theory constructs arrangements with perfect balance: Latin squares, Steiner systems and block designs in which every pair of items appears together equally often.
  14. Block Designs
    A balanced incomplete block design (BIBD) arranges v items into b blocks of size k so that every pair of items appears together in exactly λ blocks.
  15. Invariants
    An invariant is a quantity that doesn't change when allowed moves are made.
  16. Coding Theory
    Coding theory designs ways to send information reliably over noisy channels by adding structured redundancy.
  17. Boolean Functions
    A Boolean function takes true/false inputs and returns true or false.
  18. Algorithms and Complexity
    An algorithm is a precise step-by-step procedure; complexity measures how its running time or memory grows with input size.
  19. Computational Complexity Theory
    Complexity theory classifies problems by the resources needed to solve them.
  20. Automata Theory
    Automata are abstract machines that read input and change state.
  21. Formal Languages
    A formal language is a set of strings over an alphabet, defined by rules.
  22. Cellular Automata
    A cellular automaton is a grid of cells, each updating its state by a simple rule depending on its neighbours.
  23. Game Theory
    Game theory studies strategic decisions where each player's outcome depends on others' choices.

Discrete Mathematics and Combinatorics: questions students ask

What are the two parts of an induction proof?

The base case and the inductive step.

What three kinds of question does combinatorics ask?

Enumeration (how many?), existence (is there one?) and construction or optimisation (build the best one).

What does the sum rule of counting say?

Disjoint alternatives add: for example, a dessert or a cheese from 4 and 3 options gives 4 + 3.

What is a permutation?

An ordered arrangement of objects.
