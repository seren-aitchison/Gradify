Key terms and formulas at a glance

Topological invariant
A property preserved by continuous deformations with continuous inverses, such as the number of components
Closure
The smallest closed set containing A: A together with its limit points
Topology
A collection τ of subsets of X containing ∅ and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections
Metric
d(x, y) ≥ 0 with equality iff x = y, d(x, y) = d(y, x), and d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z)
Continuous map
f: X → Y with f−1(U) open in X for every open U in Y
Homeomorphism
A bijection f with f and f−1 both continuous
Compact
Every collection of open sets covering the space contains a finite subcollection that still covers it
Connected space
A space that isn't the union of two disjoint non-empty open sets
Functorial invariant
An assignment of algebraic objects to spaces and homomorphisms to maps, respecting composition
Fundamental group
π₁(X, x₀), the group of loops at x₀ up to homotopy, with composition by joining loops
Homotopy
A continuous family of maps H(x, t) deforming f = H(·, 0) into g = H(·, 1)
Betti number
bₙ, the rank of the homology group Hₙ: the number of independent n-dimensional holes

Is every map from a discrete space continuous?

Yes. From “Continuity in Topology” →

In plain English: In topology, a function is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open. Key idea: Continuous means open preimages; it matches ε–δ; continuity preserves compactness and connectedness but not openness of images.

What does the Brouwer fixed-point theorem guarantee?

A fixed point for any continuous map of a closed disc to itself. From “Algebraic Topology” →

In plain English: Algebraic topology attaches algebraic objects, such as groups, to spaces so that topological questions become algebra. Key idea: Algebraic topology turns spaces into groups and numbers; different invariants prove spaces differ; it proves fixed-point, hairy ball and Borsuk–Ulam theorems.

How many critical points does the height function on an upright torus have?

4. From “Differential Topology” →

In plain English: Differential topology studies smooth manifolds and smooth maps, with tools such as transversality and Morse theory. Key idea: Morse theory relates critical points to topology (χ = Σ(−1)index); Sard and transversality give genericity; exotic spheres show smooth structure is subtle.

Is the tangent bundle of S2 trivial?

Quick quiz: test yourself on Topology and Advanced Pure Mathematics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which pair of letters are topologically the same as curves? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    O and D. O and D are each a single closed loop, so one can be deformed into the other; B has two loops and C, A have different shapes.

  2. Which subset of ℝ is neither open nor closed? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    [0, 1). It contains 0 but not its limit point 1, and no neighbourhood of 0 stays inside.

  3. Which collection is a topology on {a, b, c}? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    {∅, {a}, {a, b}, {a, b, c}}. It contains ∅ and X and is closed under unions and intersections; the others fail one rule.

  4. Why isn't a sphere the same shape as a torus topologically? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their Euler characteristics differ (2 and 0). χ is a topological invariant, so different values mean different shapes.

  5. In the discrete topology, which subsets are closed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Every subset. Every subset is open, so every complement is open too.

  6. Why does d(x, y) = (x − y)2 fail to be a metric on ℝ? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It breaks the triangle inequality: d(0, 2) = 4 > d(0, 1) + d(1, 2) = 2. Squaring distances breaks the triangle inequality.

  7. Why do sequences in a Hausdorff space have at most one limit? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Two limits would have disjoint neighbourhoods, and the sequence can't eventually lie in both. Disjoint open sets separate the candidate limits.

  8. Why is t ↦ (cos t, sin t) from [0, 2π) to the circle not a homeomorphism? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its inverse jumps near (1, 0), sending nearby points to t near 0 and near 2π. The inverse is discontinuous at the point (1, 0).

  9. Why is the topologist's sine curve connected but not path-connected? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its wiggling part accumulates on the segment, but no path can reach the segment through infinitely fast oscillation. The closure joins the pieces, yet any path would have to oscillate infinitely.

Key takeaways

  • Topology studies properties unchanged by continuous deformation; invariants such as components, holes and χ classify shapes.
  • Interior, closure and boundary describe how a set sits in a space; subspace, product and quotient topologies build new spaces; Hausdorff spaces separate points.
  • A topology contains ∅ and X and is closed under unions and finite intersections; examples include standard, discrete, indiscrete and cofinite topologies.
  • Metrics satisfy positivity, symmetry and the triangle inequality; open balls define topologies; completeness means Cauchy sequences converge.
  • Continuous means open preimages; it matches ε–δ; continuity preserves compactness and connectedness but not openness of images.
  • Homeomorphisms are continuous bijections with continuous inverses; invariants like cut points distinguish spaces; continuous bijections can fail to be homeomorphisms.

Common mistakes in Topology and Advanced Pure Mathematics

  • Treating geometric properties like length or curvature as topological.
  • Thinking every set is either open or closed (many are neither, like [0, 1)).
  • Requiring arbitrary intersections of open sets to be open.
  • Forgetting to check the triangle inequality when proposing a metric.
  • Testing continuity with images of open sets instead of preimages.
  • Assuming every continuous bijection is a homeomorphism.

Lessons in this school

  1. General Topology
    Topology studies the properties of shapes that survive stretching and bending without tearing or gluing.
  2. Point-Set Topology
    Point-set topology gives the precise language of open and closed sets, closure, interior and boundary.
  3. Topological Spaces
    A topological space is a set with a chosen collection of "open" subsets satisfying three simple rules.
  4. Metric Spaces
    A metric space measures distance between points with a function satisfying a few natural rules.
  5. Continuity in Topology
    In topology, a function is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open.
  6. Homeomorphisms
    A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse: the topological notion of "the same shape".
  7. Compactness
    A space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover.
  8. Connectedness
    A space is connected if it can't be split into two separate open pieces.
  9. Algebraic Topology
    Algebraic topology attaches algebraic objects, such as groups, to spaces so that topological questions become algebra.
  10. Fundamental Groups
    The fundamental group records the loops in a space up to continuous deformation.
  11. Homotopy Theory
    Homotopy theory studies spaces and maps up to continuous deformation.
  12. Homology
    Homology counts holes of each dimension by algebra: H₀ counts components, H₁ loops, H₂ enclosed voids.
  13. Cohomology
    Cohomology is a dual version of homology that assigns values to holes rather than counting them, and it comes with a multiplication (the cup product).
  14. Differential Topology
    Differential topology studies smooth manifolds and smooth maps, with tools such as transversality and Morse theory.
  15. Geometric Topology
    Geometric topology studies low-dimensional manifolds and the ways spaces sit inside one another, including knots.
  16. Manifold Theory
    A manifold is a space that looks like ordinary Euclidean space near every point: the Earth's surface looks flat locally, though it's a sphere globally.
  17. Differential Forms
    Differential forms are the objects integrated over curves, surfaces and higher-dimensional regions.
  18. Fibre Bundles
    A fibre bundle is a space that locally looks like a product of a base and a fibre, but may twist globally.
  19. Complex Manifolds
    A complex manifold looks locally like ℂⁿ, with holomorphic changes of coordinates.
  20. Riemann Surfaces
    A Riemann surface is a one-dimensional complex manifold: a surface on which complex analysis works.
  21. Scheme Theory
    Scheme theory, developed by Grothendieck in the 1960s, rebuilds algebraic geometry on commutative rings.
  22. Category Theory
    Category theory studies mathematical structures through the maps between them.

Topology and Advanced Pure Mathematics: questions students ask

Are a coffee mug and a doughnut topologically the same?

Yes: each has one hole.

What topology turns an interval into a circle?

A quotient topology gluing its endpoints.

What shape is the unit ball of d₁ in ℝ2?

A diamond (a square rotated 45°).

Is ℚ connected?

No: it splits at any irrational, such as √2.
