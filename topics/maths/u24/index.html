Key terms and formulas at a glance

Empirical risk
(1/n) Σ L(f(xᵢ), yᵢ), the average loss of model f on the training data
VC dimension
The largest number of points a model class can label in every possible way (shatter)
Logistic regression
P(y = 1 ∣ x) = σ(wᵀx + b) with sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z), fitted by maximising likelihood
Momentum
v ← βv + ∇L, w ← w − ηv: accumulating past gradients to speed progress along consistent directions
Mini-batch gradient
(1/B) Σi∈batch ∇Lᵢ(w), an unbiased estimate of the full gradient from B random examples
Backpropagation
Computing ∂L/∂w for all weights by propagating error signals δ backwards layer by layer
Likelihood
L(θ) = Π p(xᵢ ∣ θ), the probability of the observed data as a function of the parameters
Posterior
p(θ ∣ D) ∝ p(D ∣ θ)p(θ): the prior updated by the likelihood of the data
Directed acyclic graph
A graph with directed edges and no cycles, used to show which variables influence which
Markov random field
An undirected graphical model where the joint distribution factorises over cliques: p(x) = (1/Z) Π ψC(xC)
Stationary distribution
A distribution π with πP = π, unchanged by one step of the chain
Gaussian process
f ~ GP(m, k): any finite collection f(x₁), …, f(xₙ) is jointly Gaussian with mean m and covariance kᵢⱼ = k(xᵢ, xⱼ)

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is empirical risk?

The average loss on the training data. From “Mathematics for Machine Learning” →

In plain English: Machine learning builds models that improve from data, and it rests on four mathematical pillars: linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimisation. Key idea: ML combines linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimisation; learning minimises empirical risk with regularisation; generalisation to new data is the real goal.

What is the posterior for a Beta(a, b) prior after k successes in n trials?

Beta(a + k, b + n − k). From “Bayesian Learning” →

In plain English: Bayesian learning treats model parameters as uncertain, updating a prior distribution with data to get a posterior. Key idea: Bayesian learning updates priors to posteriors; conjugate priors give closed forms; MAP links priors to regularisation; MCMC and variational inference approximate posteriors.

What does the representer theorem say?

Regularised solutions are combinations of kernels at the training points. From “Kernel Methods” →

In plain English: Kernel methods let linear algorithms learn non-linear patterns by implicitly mapping data into a high-dimensional feature space. Key idea: Kernels compute feature-space inner products implicitly; Mercer kernels are positive semidefinite; the representer theorem gives solutions as kernel expansions; costs scale with n2 to n3.

What do the principal components of data correspond to?

Eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. From “Dimensionality Reduction” →

In plain English: Dimensionality reduction represents high-dimensional data with fewer variables while keeping its important structure. Key idea: PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximal variance via eigen- or singular value decomposition; explained variance guides how many to keep; ICA, random projections and autoencoders offer alternatives.

What two terms make up the ELBO?

Expected reconstruction log-likelihood minus KL to the prior. From “Information-Theoretic Learning” →

In plain English: Information theory gives machine learning its common losses and many of its explanations. Key idea: ML losses are information-theoretic: cross-entropy, KL and mutual information; VAEs maximise the ELBO; contrastive losses bound MI; compression links to generalisation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Mathematics for Machine Learning and AI

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which branch of mathematics provides gradients for training models? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Calculus. Gradients come from differentiation via the chain rule.

  2. Which change usually reduces a model's variance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Adding regularisation. Regularisation restricts flexibility, reducing sensitivity to the training sample.

  3. A logistic coefficient is w = ln 3. What happens to the odds when x rises by 1? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    They triple. In logistic regression the odds ratio for a one-unit rise in x is ew = eln 3 = 3, so the odds triple.

  4. Why is training error usually lower than test error? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The model's parameters were chosen to fit the training data, including some of its noise. Optimising on a sample tailors the model to that sample.

  5. Why does ridge regression help when features are highly correlated? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Adding λI makes XᵀX + λI well conditioned, stabilising coefficient estimates. Near-singular XᵀX makes ordinary least squares unstable.

  6. What does ordinary least squares minimise? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The sum of squared vertical residuals. OLS chooses coefficients minimising Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)2.

  7. Why can't any set of 4 points in the plane be shattered by straight lines? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Some labelling, such as the XOR pattern, always needs a non-linear boundary. Radon's theorem guarantees a partition of 4 points that no line separates.

  8. Why can SGD's gradient noise help training of neural networks? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It perturbs the parameters out of saddle points and sharp minima, favouring flatter solutions. Exact gradients can stall at saddles that stochastic steps escape.

  9. Why does observing a common effect make its two independent causes dependent? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Learning one cause occurred lowers the need for the other to explain the effect. In a collider, conditioning on the child opens the path between the parents.

Key takeaways

  • ML combines linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimisation; learning minimises empirical risk with regularisation; generalisation to new data is the real goal.
  • Learning theory bounds generalisation by model complexity; bias–variance explains over- and under-fitting; VC dimension and PAC bounds formalise sample needs; deep learning raises open questions.
  • Linear regression minimises squared error via the normal equations; ridge and lasso regularise; logistic regression models probabilities through the sigmoid; coefficients are interpretable.
  • ML training uses gradient-based optimisers; momentum accelerates; Adam adapts step sizes; learning-rate schedules matter greatly; second-order methods help in some settings.
  • SGD uses unbiased mini-batch gradients; noise falls with batch size; batch size and learning rate interact; SGD scales to huge datasets and its noise can aid generalisation.
  • Backpropagation applies the chain rule backwards to get all weight gradients in one pass; it's reverse-mode AD; deep networks suffer vanishing or exploding gradients, eased by ReLU, residuals, normalisation and initialisation.

Common mistakes in Mathematics for Machine Learning and AI

  • Judging a model by its training error alone.
  • Assuming more parameters always means worse generalisation.
  • Using linear regression for a binary outcome, which can predict probabilities outside [0, 1].
  • Keeping a fixed large learning rate throughout training, which prevents fine convergence.
  • Forgetting to shuffle data between epochs, which can bias the gradient sequence.
  • Using sigmoid activations in very deep networks without residual connections or normalisation.

Lessons in this school

  1. Mathematics for Machine Learning
    Machine learning builds models that improve from data, and it rests on four mathematical pillars: linear algebra, calculus, probability and optimisation.
  2. Statistical Learning Theory
    Statistical learning theory asks when and why learning from finite data works.
  3. Linear Models Interactive lesson
    Linear models predict outputs as weighted sums of inputs.
  4. Optimisation Algorithms Interactive lesson
    Training a machine learning model means minimising a loss function, often with millions of parameters.
  5. Stochastic Gradient Descent Interactive lesson
    Stochastic gradient descent updates a model using the gradient on a small random batch of data rather than the whole dataset.
  6. Backpropagation Mathematics
    Backpropagation computes the gradient of a neural network's loss with respect to every weight by applying the chain rule backwards through the network.
  7. Probability Models
    Probability models describe data as generated by random processes with parameters to learn.
  8. Bayesian Learning
    Bayesian learning treats model parameters as uncertain, updating a prior distribution with data to get a posterior.
  9. Bayesian Networks
    A Bayesian network represents a joint probability distribution with a directed acyclic graph, where each variable depends only on its parents.
  10. Probabilistic Graphical Models
    Probabilistic graphical models use graphs to represent dependencies among many random variables.
  11. Markov Models
    Markov models assume the future depends on the present but not the past beyond it.
  12. Gaussian Processes
    A Gaussian process is a probability distribution over functions, where any finite set of function values has a joint Gaussian distribution.
  13. Uncertainty Estimation
    Machine learning models should know what they don't know.
  14. Conformal Prediction
    Conformal prediction wraps any machine learning model to produce prediction sets or intervals with a guaranteed coverage rate, such as 90%, assuming only that data are exchangeable.
  15. Kernel Methods
    Kernel methods let linear algorithms learn non-linear patterns by implicitly mapping data into a high-dimensional feature space.
  16. Support Vector Machines
    Support vector machines find the boundary that separates two classes with the widest possible margin.
  17. Decision Trees
    Decision trees predict by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about features, splitting the data into regions.
  18. Ensemble Learning Mathematics
    Ensemble methods combine many models to get better predictions than any one alone.
  19. Random Forest Theory
    Random forests average many decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample and choosing splits from a random subset of features.
  20. Boosting Theory
    Boosting builds a strong model by adding weak learners one at a time, each correcting the errors of the current ensemble.
  21. Clustering Mathematics
    Clustering groups data points so that points in a cluster are more similar to each other than to those in other clusters.
  22. Dimensionality Reduction
    Dimensionality reduction represents high-dimensional data with fewer variables while keeping its important structure.
  23. Manifold Learning
    Manifold learning assumes high-dimensional data lie near a lower-dimensional curved surface and tries to unroll it.
  24. Neural Network Theory
    Neural network theory explains what networks can represent and why they learn.
  25. Deep Learning Mathematics
    Deep learning mathematics covers the building blocks of modern networks: convolutions, activation functions, normalisation, initialisation and regularisation.
  26. Representation Learning
    Representation learning discovers useful features of data automatically rather than designing them by hand.
  27. Attention Mechanisms
    Attention lets a model weigh different parts of its input when producing each output, focusing on what's relevant.
  28. Transformer Mathematics
    Transformers stack attention and feed-forward layers with residual connections and normalisation.
  29. Information-Theoretic Learning
    Information theory gives machine learning its common losses and many of its explanations.
  30. Reinforcement Learning
    Reinforcement learning trains agents to make sequences of decisions by trial and error, maximising cumulative reward.
  31. Bellman Equations
    The Bellman equations express the value of a state as immediate reward plus the discounted value of what comes next.
  32. Policy Optimisation
    Policy optimisation improves a parameterised policy directly by following the gradient of expected return.
  33. Multi-Armed Bandits
    A multi-armed bandit chooses repeatedly among options with unknown rewards, balancing exploring uncertain arms with exploiting the best so far.
  34. Causal Machine Learning
    Causal machine learning combines causal inference with flexible models to estimate what would happen under interventions, such as a treatment's effect, rather than mere associations.
  35. Explainable AI Mathematics
    Explainable AI develops mathematical tools to show why a model made a prediction.

Mathematics for Machine Learning and AI: questions students ask

What three parts make up expected squared error?

Bias squared, variance and irreducible noise.

Which penalty produces sparse coefficients?

The L1 (lasso) penalty.

What does Adam combine?

Momentum and adaptive per-parameter step sizes.

Is the mini-batch gradient an unbiased estimate of the full gradient?

Yes, for uniformly sampled batches.
