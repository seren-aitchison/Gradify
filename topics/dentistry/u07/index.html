Five sample flashcards

What does the slope of the elastic region of a stress–strain curve represent?

The elastic modulus, or stiffness. From “Properties of dental materials” →

In plain English: Every restorative choice depends on how a material behaves under load, heat, moisture and time in the mouth. Key idea: Stress, strain, modulus, strength and toughness describe how a material handles load, while thermal conductivity, expansion, hardness, sorption and optical properties decide how it behaves in the mouth, and together they guide the choice of restoration for each site.

Which phase is largely eliminated in high-copper amalgam?

The gamma-2 (tin–mercury) phase. From “Dental amalgam” →

In plain English: Dental amalgam has been used for over 150 years and remains a durable posterior material, though its use is now restricted. Key idea: Amalgam is a mercury–silver–tin–copper alloy whose high-copper forms resist corrosion and creep, it needs a retentive cavity and careful condensation, and UK law restricts its use in children under 15 and pregnant or breastfeeding women as amalgam is phased down.

Which monomer is usually added to a resin-modified glass ionomer?

HEMA. From “Resin-modified glass ionomers and compomers” →

In plain English: Hybrid materials try to combine the benefits of glass ionomers and composites. Key idea: Resin-modified glass ionomers add HEMA and a photoinitiator to glass ionomer, giving a light-cured initial set plus a continuing acid–base reaction, while compomers are resin composites with acid-functional groups that need bonding and release less fluoride.

What is the chemical name of set dental gypsum?

Calcium sulphate dihydrate. From “Gypsum products” →

In plain English: Gypsum products are used to make models, casts and dies from impressions. Key idea: Gypsum products are calcium sulphate hemihydrates that set by dissolving and reprecipitating as interlocking dihydrate crystals with slight expansion, ranging from weak model plaster to strong die stone, and their strength depends mainly on a low water to powder ratio.

What is the initiator in PMMA denture base powder?

Benzoyl peroxide. From “Denture base polymers” →

In plain English: Most complete and partial dentures have a base made of acrylic resin. Key idea: Denture bases are mostly PMMA, made from a powder of polymer beads with benzoyl peroxide and a methyl methacrylate monomer liquid that polymerise by free-radical addition, with heat-cured resin giving the best properties and porosity, residual monomer and fracture the main faults.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Dental Materials

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which term describes the energy absorbed by a material before it fractures? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Toughness. Toughness is the area under the stress–strain curve up to fracture.

  2. Which ISO standard covers preclinical biocompatibility testing specific to dental materials? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    ISO 7405. ISO 7405 deals with dental biocompatibility evaluation.

  3. Which acid is usually used to etch enamel before bonding? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Phosphoric acid. Phosphoric acid at about 30 to 40% is the standard enamel etchant.

  4. Why can a mismatch in thermal expansion cause leakage at a restoration margin? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The gap opens and closes with temperature. Repeated expansion and contraction pumps fluid in and out of the margin, called percolation.

  5. In what order are dental materials usually tested for biocompatibility? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Cell culture, then animal usage, then clinical. Staged testing begins in the laboratory and ends with patients.

  6. What happens if etched dentine is dried with air until it looks chalky? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The collagen network collapses. Collapsed collagen prevents resin penetration and weakens the bond.

  7. A clinician wants a posterior crown material that resists crack growth in thin sections. Which property matters most? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Fracture toughness. Thin ceramics fail by crack propagation, so fracture toughness is the key property.

  8. A patient develops a white, lace-like patch on the buccal mucosa touching an old amalgam. What is the most likely explanation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    An oral lichenoid reaction. Lichenoid reactions occur in direct contact with amalgam; persistent or atypical lesions should be referred for assessment.

  9. Why is selective enamel etching often recommended with universal adhesives? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It improves enamel bonds without over-etching dentine. Phosphoric acid helps enamel, while self-etch mode suits dentine.

Key takeaways

  • Stress, strain, modulus, strength and toughness describe how a material handles load, while thermal conductivity, expansion, hardness, sorption and optical properties decide how it behaves in the mouth, and together they guide the choice of restoration for each site.
  • Biocompatibility means an appropriate host response for a specific use, is assessed by staged ISO tests and regulated as a medical device by the MHRA, and the main allergic risks in dentistry are methacrylates, nickel and latex.
  • Phosphoric acid etching gives durable micromechanical bonds to enamel, dentine bonding relies on a resin-infiltrated collagen hybrid layer, and adhesives are etch-and-rinse, self-etch or universal, each used to suit the tissue.
  • Amalgam is a mercury–silver–tin–copper alloy whose high-copper forms resist corrosion and creep, it needs a retentive cavity and careful condensation, and UK law restricts its use in children under 15 and pregnant or breastfeeding women as amalgam is phased down.
  • Composites combine a methacrylate resin, silane-coated filler and a camphorquinone photoinitiator, are classified by filler size and viscosity, and shrink 2 to 3% on curing, so incremental layering, good curing and moisture control reduce shrinkage stress.
  • Glass ionomers set by an acid–base reaction between fluoroaluminosilicate glass and polyalkenoic acid, bond chemically to tooth and release fluoride, and are used in primary teeth, root caries, ART and as bases, though they are weaker and less wear-resistant than composite.

Common mistakes in Dental Materials

  • Equating strength with stiffness. A material can be stiff but brittle, like ceramic, or flexible but tough; modulus and strength are different properties.
  • Believing gloves fully protect against resin monomers. Methacrylates penetrate most gloves quickly, so a no-touch technique is needed.
  • Drying etched dentine until it is chalky. Over-drying collapses the collagen network; leave dentine slightly moist with etch-and-rinse systems.
  • Advising patients to have sound amalgams replaced for health reasons. There is no evidence of benefit, and removal itself releases mercury vapour and removes tooth tissue.
  • Holding the curing light far from the tooth or at an angle. Light intensity falls with distance, so hold the tip close and perpendicular for the full time.
  • Leaving a fresh glass ionomer unprotected. Early water contamination and drying both weaken it, so apply a protective coat.

Lessons in this school

  1. Properties of dental materials
    Every restorative choice depends on how a material behaves under load, heat, moisture and time in the mouth.
  2. Biocompatibility of dental materials
    Dental materials sit in contact with pulp, mucosa and bone for many years.
  3. Adhesion to enamel and dentine
    Modern minimally invasive dentistry depends on bonding restorations to tooth tissue.
  4. Dental amalgam
    Dental amalgam has been used for over 150 years and remains a durable posterior material, though its use is now restricted.
  5. Resin composites
    Resin composites are now the most widely used direct restorative material.
  6. Glass ionomer cements
    Glass ionomer cements bond chemically to tooth and release fluoride, which makes them valuable in children, root caries and high-risk patients.
  7. Resin-modified glass ionomers and compomers
    Hybrid materials try to combine the benefits of glass ionomers and composites.
  8. Dental cements and liners
    Cements hold indirect restorations in place, and liners and bases protect the pulp under restorations.
  9. Impression materials
    Accurate impressions are the basis of crowns, bridges, dentures and orthodontic appliances.
  10. Gypsum products
    Gypsum products are used to make models, casts and dies from impressions.
  11. Dental ceramics
    Ceramics give the most natural-looking and durable indirect restorations.
  12. Dental alloys
    Metal alloys are used for crowns, bridges, partial denture frameworks and orthodontic wires.
  13. Denture base polymers
    Most complete and partial dentures have a base made of acrylic resin.
  14. Endodontic materials
    Root canal treatment uses materials to disinfect, fill and seal the canal system and to protect the pulp.
  15. Dental implant materials
    Dental implants replace missing teeth by anchoring into bone.

Dental Materials: questions students ask

Which UK body regulates dental materials as medical devices?

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA).

Who first described acid etching of enamel?

Michael Buonocore, in 1955.

What does the silane coupling agent do?

Bonds the filler particles to the resin matrix.

Who introduced glass ionomer cement?

Wilson and Kent, in 1972.
