Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What proportion of electron energy in an X-ray tube becomes X-rays?

About 1%; the rest is heat. From “Production of X-rays” →

In plain English: Dental radiographs depend on X-rays generated in a small tube head. Key idea: X-rays are produced when electrons from a heated tungsten filament are accelerated by a high voltage into a tungsten target, giving mainly continuous bremsstrahlung plus characteristic radiation, with kV setting beam quality, mA and time the quantity, and filtration and collimation reducing patient dose.

What is the unit of absorbed dose?

The gray (Gy). From “Radiation doses in dentistry” →

In plain English: Comparing doses helps dentists explain risk and choose the right examination. Key idea: Absorbed dose is in grays, equivalent and effective doses are in sieverts, and effective dose uses tissue weighting factors to compare risk; intraoral films give a few microsieverts, panoramics a few to about 20, and CBCT much more, against UK background of about 2.7 mSv a year.

When should a thyroid collar be used?

Only when the thyroid is in or near the primary beam. From “Radiation protection in the dental surgery” →

In plain English: Good radiation protection keeps staff and public doses tiny and patient doses as low as reasonably practicable. Key idea: Staff are protected by time, distance and shielding and by staying outside the 1.5 m controlled area and primary beam; patients are protected by 60 to 70 kV sets, rectangular collimation and holders, while lead aprons are not routinely needed and thyroid collars are used only when the thyroid is in the beam.

Which occlusal view is used to look for a submandibular duct stone?

The lower true occlusal. From “Occlusal radiographs” →

In plain English: Occlusal radiographs show larger areas of the jaws than periapicals. Key idea: The upper standard occlusal shows the anterior maxilla for canines and supernumeraries, the lower true occlusal shows the floor of the mouth for duct stones, and parallax with the SLOB rule localises objects: same direction as the tube means lingual, opposite means buccal.

Who now publishes the UK selection criteria for dental radiography?

The College of General Dentistry (formerly FGDP UK). From “Selection criteria for dental radiographs” →

In plain English: Selection criteria help dentists decide which radiograph to take and when. Key idea: Selection criteria, mainly the FGDP guidance, support justification by linking each radiograph to the clinical problem; bitewing intervals depend on caries risk, about 6 months for high, 12 months for moderate and 24 months for low-risk adults, and routine screening panoramics are not justified.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which process releases electrons from the filament of an X-ray tube? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Thermionic emission. Heating the tungsten filament releases electrons.

  2. Which tissue would appear most radiopaque on a dental radiograph? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Enamel. Enamel is the most mineralised natural tissue, attenuating most.

  3. Which effect of radiation has no threshold dose? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cancer induction. Cancer is a stochastic effect with probability rising with dose.

  4. Which exposure factor mainly controls the energy of the X-ray photons? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Kilovoltage. Higher kV accelerates electrons more, giving more energetic photons.

  5. Which interaction is most responsible for contrast between enamel and soft tissue? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Photoelectric absorption. Photoelectric absorption rises steeply with atomic number.

  6. Which describes a deterministic effect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Severity increases with dose above a threshold. Tissue reactions need a threshold and worsen with dose.

  7. Why is a copper block used to hold the tungsten target? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It conducts heat away from the target. Most electron energy becomes heat, which copper removes.

  8. Why is lead effective as a shielding material in X-ray rooms? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its high atomic number absorbs photons. Lead's high Z gives strong photoelectric absorption.

  9. Which patient group has the highest risk per unit of dose from a dental radiograph? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Children. Risk per unit dose is highest in the young and falls with age.

Key takeaways

  • X-rays are produced when electrons from a heated tungsten filament are accelerated by a high voltage into a tungsten target, giving mainly continuous bremsstrahlung plus characteristic radiation, with kV setting beam quality, mA and time the quantity, and filtration and collimation reducing patient dose.
  • Attenuation depends on thickness, density, atomic number and photon energy; the photoelectric effect absorbs photons, depends on atomic number and creates contrast and patient dose, while Compton scatter depends on density, reduces contrast and is the main dose to staff.
  • X-rays damage DNA mainly indirectly through free radicals; deterministic effects have thresholds far above dental doses, while stochastic effects such as cancer have no threshold, so under the linear no-threshold model every exposure must be justified and kept as low as reasonably practicable.
  • Absorbed dose is in grays, equivalent and effective doses are in sieverts, and effective dose uses tissue weighting factors to compare risk; intraoral films give a few microsieverts, panoramics a few to about 20, and CBCT much more, against UK background of about 2.7 mSv a year.
  • IRR17 protects staff and the public through HSE registration, risk assessment, an RPA and RPS, local rules and dose limits, while IR(ME)R 2017 protects patients through the roles of employer, referrer, practitioner and operator, written procedures, justification and recorded evaluation.
  • Justification means every radiograph must give a net benefit after history and examination and be likely to change management, and optimisation keeps dose as low as reasonably practicable with rectangular collimation, fast receptors, holders and good technique, with justification and evaluation recorded.

Common mistakes in Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection

  • Thinking that higher mA makes X-rays more penetrating. Tube current changes the number of photons; kilovoltage changes their energy.
  • Assuming radiolucent areas are where the beam was absorbed. Dark areas are where many photons passed through; white areas are where they were absorbed.
  • Saying dental X-rays carry no risk. The stochastic risk is very small but not zero, so exposures must be justified and optimised.
  • Treating effective dose as the exact dose to one patient. It is a risk-weighted estimate for a reference person, useful for comparison only.
  • Mixing up the two sets of regulations. IRR17 is about workers and the public; IR(ME)R is about patients.
  • Taking a full set of radiographs for every new patient "to be safe". Radiographs must be justified individually after examination, in line with selection criteria.

Dental Radiography and Radiation Protection: questions students ask

What does radiolucent mean?

An area that lets many X-ray photons through and appears dark.

Why does indirect DNA damage predominate with X-rays?

Cells are mostly water, which radiation splits into free radicals.

When should a radiograph be taken?

Only after history and examination, when it is likely to change management.

What is the controlled area for a typical intraoral X-ray set?

Within 1.5 m of the tube and patient, and in the primary beam until attenuated.
