Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the first phase of a restorative treatment plan?

The emergency phase: relief of pain and infection. From “History, examination and treatment planning” →

In plain English: Restorative care starts with a thorough assessment and a logical, phased plan. Key idea: A restorative assessment combines a full history with a systematic extraoral and intraoral examination and justified special tests, and a phased plan moves from urgent care to stabilisation, reassessment, definitive treatment and risk-based maintenance.

Why is floss tied to a rubber dam clamp?

So the clamp can be retrieved if it breaks or slips. From “Moisture control and rubber dam” →

In plain English: Good isolation protects the patient and makes adhesive and endodontic work reliable. Key idea: Moisture control combines aspiration, absorbents, retraction, cord and rubber dam; rubber dam gives the best isolation and airway protection, is mandatory for root canal treatment and strongly recommended for adhesive restorations.

What does BEWE score 3 mean?

Hard tissue loss over 50% of the surface area. From “Managing tooth wear” →

In plain English: Tooth wear is increasingly common as people keep their teeth longer. Key idea: Tooth wear includes erosion, attrition, abrasion and abfraction and is usually multifactorial; the BEWE scores each sextant from 0 to 3, and management starts with identifying the cause, prevention and monitoring, with additive restorations and the Dahl concept when treatment is needed.

When is a canal ready to obturate?

When it is shaped, disinfected, dry and free of persistent exudate. From “Root canal obturation” →

In plain English: Obturation fills the cleaned canal to prevent reinfection and seal off remaining bacteria. Key idea: Obturation fills a shaped, disinfected, dry canal in three dimensions with gutta-percha and sealer, by lateral, warm vertical, single-cone or carrier techniques, and a good root filling ends 0.5 to 2 mm from the apex, is dense and tapered, and is followed by a sound coronal seal.

What is the main failure of resin-bonded bridges?

Debonding. From “Fixed bridges” →

In plain English: Fixed bridges replace missing teeth using adjacent teeth for support. Key idea: Bridges have retainers on abutments, pontics and connectors in fixed–fixed, cantilever or fixed–movable designs; resin-bonded bridges are minimally invasive and suit unrestored teeth, often as single-retainer cantilevers, while conventional bridges suit heavily restored abutments, and abutment health guides the plan.

  1. Which special test assesses the response of pulpal nerves? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sensibility testing. Cold and electric tests assess pulp sensibility.

  2. Which option must always be discussed when obtaining consent? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    No treatment. Patients must understand the consequences of not having treatment.

  3. What is the position of maximum intercuspation called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Intercuspal position. Applying the standard result: ICP is where the teeth meet most fully.

  4. Which phase should come before definitive crown and bridge work? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Stabilisation and reassessment. Disease must be controlled before definitive work.

  5. Under Montgomery, which risks must be disclosed? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Risks a reasonable patient would consider significant. The test is what matters to a reasonable patient, or this patient.

  6. Which approach keeps the patient's existing occlusion when placing a crown? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Conformative. Conforming fits the restoration to the existing ICP and guidance.

  7. A patient with uncontrolled periodontitis asks for a bridge to replace a missing incisor. What is the best initial plan? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Treat the periodontitis and reassess first. Abutment teeth need healthy support, so stabilise disease first.

  8. A patient with moderate dementia needs an extraction but cannot weigh the options. What is the right approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Act in her best interests after consulting carers. When capacity is lacking, decisions follow the best-interests process.

  9. A patient needs ten crowns and has an unstable ICP with few posterior stops. What is the most suitable approach? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Plan a reorganised occlusion with articulated models. When ICP is unsuitable and many teeth are restored, reorganising is planned.

Key takeaways

  • A restorative assessment combines a full history with a systematic extraoral and intraoral examination and justified special tests, and a phased plan moves from urgent care to stabilisation, reassessment, definitive treatment and risk-based maintenance.
  • Valid consent needs an informed, capacitous patient choosing voluntarily; after Montgomery, dentists must disclose material risks and reasonable alternatives, including no treatment, and shared decision-making records options, risks, costs and the patient's choice.
  • The intercuspal position is maximum fit, the retruded contact position is the first contact on the hinge axis, and guidance is canine or group function; occlusion is examined with articulating paper and shim stock, and most restorations follow a conformative approach, with reorganisation reserved for complex cases.
  • Black's principles and classification still guide cavity description, but modern preparation is minimally invasive, shaped by caries and material, and caries removal is selective, to hard dentine at the periphery and leaving soft dentine over the pulp in deep lesions, with a good seal.
  • Moisture control combines aspiration, absorbents, retraction, cord and rubber dam; rubber dam gives the best isolation and airway protection, is mandatory for root canal treatment and strongly recommended for adhesive restorations.
  • Composite is the usual direct posterior material when isolation is good, with amalgam and glass ionomer for specific cases; sectional matrices, rings and wedges give tight contacts, and secondary caries and fracture are the main causes of failure, so repair is preferred where possible.

Common mistakes in Restorative Dentistry: Operative, Endodontics and Prosthodontics

  • Planning crowns and bridges before disease is controlled. Stabilise caries and periodontal disease and reassess first, or definitive work may fail.
  • Treating a signed form as consent. Consent is an ongoing conversation; the signature only records it.
  • Adjusting a new restoration only with articulating paper marks. Marks can mislead; check that shim stock holds on neighbouring teeth as it did before.
  • Removing all stained or soft dentine over the pulp in a deep lesion. This risks exposure; selective removal with a sealed restoration has better outcomes.
  • Skipping rubber dam for a "quick" root canal procedure. It is mandatory to protect the airway and keep the canal free from saliva.
  • Replacing a whole restoration for a small marginal defect. Repair conserves tooth tissue and is often as successful.

Restorative Dentistry: Operative, Endodontics and Prosthodontics mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. History, examination and treatment planning
    Restorative care starts with a thorough assessment and a logical, phased plan.
  2. Consent and shared decision-making in treatment
    Valid consent is a legal and ethical requirement for every dental treatment.
  3. Occlusion in restorative dentistry
    Occlusion is the way the teeth meet, and every restoration changes it a little.
  4. Principles of cavity preparation
    Cavity design has moved from extension for prevention to minimal, adhesive preparation.
  5. Moisture control and rubber dam
    Good isolation protects the patient and makes adhesive and endodontic work reliable.
  6. Direct posterior restorations
    Most restorations in posterior teeth are placed directly in one visit.
  7. Direct anterior restorations
    Anterior composites restore appearance and function with little tooth removal.
  8. Managing deep caries and the pulp
    Deep carious lesions threaten the pulp, and the choice of treatment decides whether it survives.
  9. Managing tooth wear
    Tooth wear is increasingly common as people keep their teeth longer.
  10. Pulp and periapical diagnosis
    Accurate diagnosis of the pulp and periapical tissues decides whether a tooth needs restoration, root canal treatment or extraction.
  11. Access and canal preparation
    Successful root canal treatment depends on good access and careful shaping of the canals.
  12. Irrigation and disinfection
    Bacteria in the canal system are the cause of apical periodontitis, and instruments alone cannot remove them.
  13. Root canal obturation
    Obturation fills the cleaned canal to prevent reinfection and seal off remaining bacteria.
  14. Endodontic outcomes and retreatment
    Most root canal treatments heal, but some fail and need further care.
  15. Dental trauma in adults
    Dental injuries in adults range from enamel chips to avulsion and are often linked to sport, falls and assaults.
  16. Crowns and onlays
    Indirect restorations protect and restore heavily damaged teeth.
  17. Fixed bridges
    Fixed bridges replace missing teeth using adjacent teeth for support.
  18. Complete dentures
    Complete dentures restore function and appearance for edentulous patients.
  19. Removable partial dentures
    Removable partial dentures replace some missing teeth while relying on remaining teeth and tissues for support.
  20. Dental implants
    Implants are a valuable but complex way to replace missing teeth.
  21. Restoring the endodontically treated tooth
    Root-filled teeth are prone to fracture and leakage, so the final restoration is as important as the root filling.
