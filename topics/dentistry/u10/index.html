Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the three steps of tell-show-do?

Explain the procedure, demonstrate it, then perform it. From “Child development and behaviour management” →

In plain English: Successful dental care for children depends on understanding how they develop and communicate. Key idea: Behaviour management matches communication to the child's stage of development, uses tell-show-do, reinforcement, distraction and desensitisation as first-line approaches, and reserves inhalation sedation and general anaesthesia for children who cannot otherwise cope, delivered by trained teams.

Should an avulsed primary incisor be replanted?

No, because it may damage the permanent successor. From “Dental trauma in children” →

In plain English: Dental injuries are common in children and may affect both primary teeth and their developing successors. Key idea: Primary tooth trauma is managed simply to protect the successor, with no replantation of avulsed primary teeth and spontaneous re-eruption of most intrusions, while immature permanent teeth are managed to preserve pulp vitality and root growth, and inconsistent histories or unusual injuries must prompt safeguarding action.

Why are dental infections a particular concern in some children with medical conditions?

They can cause serious systemic illness in children with some heart conditions or immunosuppression. From “Children with additional needs” →

In plain English: Children with medical, physical, learning or sensory needs often have higher oral disease risk and need adapted care. Key idea: Children with additional needs often have higher oral disease risk and medical factors that complicate treatment, so prevention, medical liaison and adapted communication and environments are central, with reasonable adjustments required by the Equality Act and referral to specialist services when needed.

What does MOCDO stand for?

Missing teeth, overjet, crossbite, displacement of contacts, overbite. From “The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need” →

In plain English: The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need ranks malocclusions by their effect on dental health and appearance. Key idea: IOTN grades the worst occlusal feature in the Dental Health Component from 1 to 5 using MOCDO, and the Aesthetic Component from 1 to 10 using photographs; NHS treatment in under-18s is usually for DHC 4 and 5, or DHC 3 with AC 6 or more.

Which archwire is used first for alignment?

Flexible nickel–titanium. From “Fixed appliances and tooth movement” →

In plain English: Fixed appliances give precise, three-dimensional control of tooth movement. Key idea: Teeth move as the PDL and bone remodel, with osteoclastic resorption on the pressure side and osteoblastic deposition on the tension side, best with light continuous forces; fixed appliances use brackets, archwires and auxiliaries for precise movement, with risks of demineralisation, root resorption, gingival problems and relapse.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which scale is used to record a child's cooperation? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Frankl scale. The Frankl scale rates behaviour from definitely negative to definitely positive.

  2. Which technique cements a preformed metal crown over a carious primary molar without caries removal? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Hall technique. The Hall technique seals caries under a crown.

  3. Which treatment removes the coronal pulp of a vital primary molar? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pulpotomy. Pulpotomy keeps the radicular pulp vital.

  4. Which technique gradually exposes a child to feared steps? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Systematic desensitisation. Desensitisation reduces fear step by step.

  5. Which drink advice best reduces caries risk for a toddler? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Only milk or water between meals. Frequent sugars between meals and at night drive caries.

  6. What does uncontrollable bleeding after coronal pulp removal suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The radicular pulp is inflamed. Persistent bleeding means inflammation extends into the roots.

  7. A nine-year-old needs several fillings but is too anxious to accept local anaesthetic despite repeated tell-show-do. What is the best next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Consider inhalation sedation. Inhalation sedation can help anxious children who cannot cope otherwise.

  8. An anxious three-year-old has asymptomatic caries in several primary molars. Which approach is most suitable first? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Prevention with Hall crowns where possible. Minimally invasive options suit young, anxious children.

  9. A primary molar with a sinus has extensive root resorption and is close to exfoliation. What is the best management? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Extraction. Pulp therapy is not worthwhile for a resorbed tooth near exfoliation.

Key takeaways

  • Behaviour management matches communication to the child's stage of development, uses tell-show-do, reinforcement, distraction and desensitisation as first-line approaches, and reserves inhalation sedation and general anaesthesia for children who cannot otherwise cope, delivered by trained teams.
  • Caries in children is driven by frequent sugar, poor brushing and deprivation; prevention combines fluoride toothpaste, supervised brushing, diet advice, fluoride varnish and sealants by risk, and carious primary teeth can be managed by cavity control, the Hall technique, restoration, pulp therapy or extraction.
  • Pulp diagnosis in primary teeth relies on history, clinical signs and radiographs; vital exposed molars can be treated by pulpotomy with a calcium silicate and a preformed crown, non-vital molars by pulpectomy with resorbable paste, and extraction is preferred for unrestorable, resorbed or infected teeth or children who cannot cope.
  • Primary tooth trauma is managed simply to protect the successor, with no replantation of avulsed primary teeth and spontaneous re-eruption of most intrusions, while immature permanent teeth are managed to preserve pulp vitality and root growth, and inconsistent histories or unusual injuries must prompt safeguarding action.
  • MIH causes demarcated, porous opacities in first permanent molars and incisors that break down, are sensitive and decay quickly; it is distinguished from fluorosis and amelogenesis imperfecta, and managed with prevention, desensitising, interim and definitive restorations, and sometimes planned molar extraction with orthodontic advice.
  • Abuse is physical, emotional, sexual or neglect, and dental neglect is persistent failure to meet a child's oral health needs; dental teams should recognise injuries and patterns of concern, record carefully, discuss with the safeguarding lead and refer through local procedures, calling 999 for immediate danger.

Common mistakes in Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics

  • Using vague threats or the word "hurt" to gain cooperation. Frightening words increase anxiety; use positive, simple language.
  • Assuming carious primary teeth do not matter because they will fall out. Untreated caries causes pain, infection and GA admissions, and primary molars are needed for years.
  • Relying on cold tests in primary teeth. They are unreliable; use history, clinical signs and radiographs.
  • Replanting an avulsed primary incisor. This can damage the developing permanent tooth; primary teeth are not replanted.
  • Confusing MIH with fluorosis. MIH opacities are demarcated and asymmetrical; fluorosis is diffuse and symmetrical.
  • Waiting for certainty before raising a concern. Professionals should act on reasonable concern; investigation is for social care and police.

Paediatric Dentistry and Orthodontics mind map

Lessons in this school

  1. Child development and behaviour management
    Successful dental care for children depends on understanding how they develop and communicate.
  2. Caries in children
    Caries remains the most common chronic disease of childhood in the UK.
  3. Pulp therapy in primary teeth
    Primary teeth with pulp involvement can often be kept until they exfoliate.
  4. Dental trauma in children
    Dental injuries are common in children and may affect both primary teeth and their developing successors.
  5. Molar–incisor hypomineralisation
    Molar–incisor hypomineralisation is a common developmental enamel defect that causes sensitivity, breakdown and caries.
  6. Safeguarding children in dentistry
    Dental professionals see children regularly and are well placed to recognise abuse and neglect.
  7. Children with additional needs
    Children with medical, physical, learning or sensory needs often have higher oral disease risk and need adapted care.
  8. Growth of the face and jaws
    Facial growth shapes the occlusion and affects the timing of orthodontic treatment.
  9. Development of occlusion
    The occlusion develops from the gum pads of infancy to the permanent dentition.
  10. Classification of malocclusion
    Malocclusions are described with standard classifications so clinicians can communicate and plan care.
  11. The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need
    The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need ranks malocclusions by their effect on dental health and appearance.
  12. Orthodontic assessment
    A structured orthodontic assessment gathers the information needed for diagnosis and treatment planning.
  13. Removable and functional appliances
    Removable appliances tip teeth and hold space, while functional appliances use muscle forces to correct jaw relationships.
  14. Fixed appliances and tooth movement
    Fixed appliances give precise, three-dimensional control of tooth movement.
  15. Retention and stability
    Teeth tend to move back after orthodontic treatment, so retention is part of every plan.
  16. Interceptive orthodontics
    Some developing malocclusions can be prevented or reduced by simple, timely treatment.
  17. Cleft lip and palate
    Cleft lip and palate is the most common craniofacial birth anomaly and needs coordinated care from birth to adulthood.
