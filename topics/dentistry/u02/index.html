Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the FDI number of the upper left permanent first molar?

26. From “Dental notation systems” →

In plain English: Every tooth must be named unambiguously in records, referrals and laboratory prescriptions. Key idea: In FDI, 1 to 4 are permanent quadrants and 5 to 8 primary; the second digit counts out from the midline. Universal 3 is the upper right first molar, which is FDI 16.

How many roots does the upper first premolar usually have?

Two: buccal and palatal. From “Permanent premolars” →

In plain English: The premolars bridge the incisors and molars, with two cusps (sometimes three) that both tear and grind. Key idea: Upper premolars have buccal and palatal cusps of similar width; the upper first usually has two roots. Lower premolars are tilted lingually; the lower first's lingual cusp is tiny.

What does the dental follicle form?

Cementum, periodontal ligament and alveolar bone proper. From “Odontogenesis: from bud to bell” →

In plain English: Teeth develop from an interaction between oral epithelium and neural crest-derived mesenchyme. Key idea: Enamel organ → enamel (via ameloblasts). Dental papilla → dentine and pulp (via odontoblasts). Dental follicle → cementum, periodontal ligament and alveolar bone.

What do pulp sensibility tests actually test?

Nerve responsiveness, not blood supply (vitality). From “The dental pulp” →

In plain English: The pulp is the soft connective tissue in the centre of the tooth, enclosed in rigid dentine. Key idea: Sharp, brief pain on a stimulus suggests A-delta activity in vital dentine; lingering, throbbing, poorly localised pain suggests C-fibre activity from pulpal inflammation.

Which tissue is essential for tooth eruption?

The dental follicle. From “Eruption and the chronology of the dentition” →

In plain English: Teeth move from their crypts into the mouth over months to years, in a predictable order. Key idea: The dental follicle drives eruption by orchestrating bone resorption and formation; removing the follicle stops eruption in experimental models.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Teeth: Morphology, Development and Oral Histology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In FDI notation, which tooth is 47? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Lower right second permanent molar. Quadrant 4 is lower right permanent; tooth 7 is the second molar. Primary lower right teeth are 81 to 85.

  2. Which tooth class is present in the permanent but not the primary dentition? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Premolars. The primary dentition has incisors, canines and molars only; premolars erupt in place of the primary molars.

  3. Which tooth is usually the longest in the permanent dentition? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Upper canine. The upper canine has the longest root and greatest overall length; the lower canine is shorter.

  4. A referral asks for extraction of Universal tooth 30. What is it in FDI? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    46. Universal 17 to 32 runs from the lower left third molar to the lower right third molar; 30 is the lower right first molar, FDI 46.

  5. A cavity involves the surface of a lower right first premolar that faces the canine, and the biting surface. How is it recorded? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Mesio-occlusal (MO). The surface facing the canine is towards the midline along the arch, so mesial; premolars have occlusal, not incisal, surfaces.

  6. Which feature identifies the mesial side of an upper central incisor crown? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The sharper, more square incisal angle. The mesio-incisal angle is nearly a right angle; the disto-incisal angle is rounded.

  7. A record reads "extract 11" with no system stated, and a mirrored radiograph is attached. What should the dentist do before treatment? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Confirm which tooth is meant from the referrer, the clinical findings and correctly oriented radiographs. "11" is the upper right central incisor in FDI but the upper left canine in Universal, and mirrored images reverse sides; the tooth must be confirmed before an irreversible procedure.

  8. A 9-year-old has erupted permanent incisors and first molars and retained primary canines and molars. How should this dentition be described, and what is expected next? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A mixed dentition; the canines and premolars are expected to erupt over the next few years. At 9 the mixed dentition is normal; primary canines and molars are shed as canines and premolars erupt, typically between about 10 and 12 years.

  9. A root-filled lower central incisor develops a periapical lesion two years later; the radiograph shows the root filling centred in a broad, flattened root. What is the most likely cause? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A missed lingual canal. Lower incisor roots are flattened and often contain labial and lingual canals; a single centred root filling suggests the lingual canal was missed, leaving infected tissue.

Key takeaways

  • In FDI, 1 to 4 are permanent quadrants and 5 to 8 primary; the second digit counts out from the midline. Universal 3 is the upper right first molar, which is FDI 16.
  • Mesial and distal follow the curve of the arch, not left and right: the mesial surface of a central incisor faces the other central incisor; the mesial surface of a molar faces the premolar in front of it.
  • The upper canine's long root and its position at the corner of the arch make it the keystone of the anterior dentition: it guides lateral movements and is usually the last tooth to be lost.
  • Upper premolars have buccal and palatal cusps of similar width; the upper first usually has two roots. Lower premolars are tilted lingually; the lower first's lingual cusp is tiny.
  • Upper molars have three roots and the largest cusp is mesiopalatal; lower molars have two roots, and the first has five cusps, the second four.
  • The primary second molars look like the first permanent molars that erupt behind them: check the number of teeth and the root development on a radiograph before extracting a "molar" in a child.

Common mistakes in Teeth: Morphology, Development and Oral Histology

  • Treating "11" in FDI as the eleventh tooth. In FDI, 11 is the upper right central incisor; in Universal, 11 is the upper left canine. Always state which system a record uses, because the same number means different teeth.
  • Calling the inner surface of an upper tooth "lingual" in a record where the local convention is "palatal". Both are understood, but use the convention consistently; mixing them causes confusion in charting.
  • Assuming every lower incisor has one canal. A second, lingual canal is common; missing it is a frequent cause of root canal treatment failure in lower incisors.
  • Overlooking the mesial concavity of the upper first premolar. A straight matrix band leaves a gap or overhang at the cervical margin there; wedges and contoured matrices are needed.
  • Thinking furcations are only an endodontic issue. Bone loss into the furcation of a molar makes plaque control very difficult and worsens the periodontal prognosis.
  • Mistaking a lower primary second molar for a lower first permanent molar during extraction. The first permanent molar has no successor and must not be removed in error; the primary molar has divergent roots around a developing premolar.

Lessons in this school

  1. Dental notation systems
    Every tooth must be named unambiguously in records, referrals and laboratory prescriptions.
  2. The primary and permanent dentitions
    Humans have two dentitions: 20 primary teeth and 32 permanent teeth, with a mixed dentition in between.
  3. Permanent incisors and canines
    The anterior teeth cut and tear food and shape the smile.
  4. Permanent premolars
    The premolars bridge the incisors and molars, with two cusps (sometimes three) that both tear and grind.
  5. Permanent molars
    The molars are the main grinding teeth, with broad occlusal tables and two or three roots.
  6. Primary teeth
    Primary teeth are not small permanent teeth.
  7. Odontogenesis: from bud to bell
    Teeth develop from an interaction between oral epithelium and neural crest-derived mesenchyme.
  8. Amelogenesis and enamel structure
    Enamel is the hardest tissue in the body and the only one made by epithelial cells that are lost once the tooth erupts, so it cannot repair itself biologically.
  9. Dentinogenesis and dentine
    Dentine forms the bulk of the tooth.
  10. The dental pulp
    The pulp is the soft connective tissue in the centre of the tooth, enclosed in rigid dentine.
  11. Dentine sensitivity and the hydrodynamic theory
    Dentine hypersensitivity is short, sharp pain from exposed dentine in response to cold, air, touch or sweet.
  12. Root formation and cementum
    Once the crown is complete, Hertwig's epithelial root sheath maps out the root, and the dental follicle forms the cementum that anchors the periodontal ligament fibres.
  13. Eruption and the chronology of the dentition
    Teeth move from their crypts into the mouth over months to years, in a predictable order.
  14. The periodontal ligament and alveolar bone
    The periodontal ligament (PDL) suspends each tooth in its socket, absorbs chewing forces and senses pressure, while the alveolar bone that holds it remodels constantly.
  15. The oral mucosa
    The oral mucosa lines the mouth and varies with the job each region does: tough and keratinised where food is chewed, flexible where the cheeks and floor must stretch, and specialised for taste on the tongue.
  16. Developmental anomalies of the teeth
    Teeth can vary in number, size, shape and structure because of genetic and environmental influences during development.
