How do local anaesthetics block pain?

By blocking voltage-gated sodium channels so nerve action potentials cannot be conducted. From “Local anaesthetics: pharmacology” →

In plain English: Local anaesthetics make most dental treatment painless. Key idea: Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels; amides such as lidocaine and articaine are standard in UK dentistry, with adrenaline or felypressin as vasoconstrictors; maximum doses are calculated by weight to avoid toxicity.

What MDAS score suggests high dental anxiety?

A score of 19 or more out of 25. From “Dental anxiety” →

In plain English: Dental anxiety is common and can stop people getting the care they need. Key idea: Dental anxiety is common and leads to avoidance and worse oral health; the MDAS identifies high anxiety (19 or more); management uses behavioural approaches and CBT for long-term change, with sedation for treatment when needed.

Which drugs in the medical history are particularly important before extraction?

Anticoagulants, antiplatelets and antiresorptive drugs such as bisphosphonates. From “Principles of exodontia” →

In plain English: Tooth extraction is one of the most common oral surgery procedures. Key idea: Extractions are indicated for unrestorable teeth, periodontal disease, orthodontic reasons and pathology; assessment includes medical history, radiographs and consent; forceps luxate or rotate and elevators use lever, wedge and wheel principles.

When does dry socket usually start?

Two to four days after the extraction. From “Complications of extractions” →

In plain English: Most extractions heal without problems, but some complications need prompt recognition and management. Key idea: Bleeding is managed with pressure and local measures, and dry socket with irrigation, dressing and analgesia; oroantral communication and MRONJ need recognition; patients on warfarin can usually proceed if INR is below 4, with drug-specific advice for DOACs.

What is the maximum daily dose of paracetamol for a healthy adult?

4 g in 24 hours. From “Analgesia for dental pain” →

In plain English: Effective pain relief is essential after dental treatment and while patients wait for care. Key idea: Paracetamol and ibuprofen are the main analgesics for dental pain, and combining them is more effective than either alone; NSAIDs need caution in ulcers, kidney disease, asthma and anticoagulation; analgesics do not replace definitive treatment.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Oral Surgery, Pain and Anxiety Control

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which local anaesthetic is most widely used in UK dental cartridges? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Lidocaine 2% with 1:80,000 adrenaline. Lidocaine with adrenaline is the standard UK dental cartridge.

  2. Which technique is usually used for mandibular molars? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An inferior alveolar nerve block. Thick cortical bone limits infiltration in the posterior mandible.

  3. What should be done before injecting during a nerve block? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Aspirate to check the needle is not in a blood vessel. Aspiration reduces the risk of intravascular injection.

  4. Why is a vasoconstrictor added to local anaesthetic? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It prolongs anaesthesia, reduces bleeding and lowers systemic toxicity. Reduced blood flow keeps the drug at the site longer.

  5. Which nerve block is added for buccal gingiva of lower molars? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The long buccal nerve block. The long buccal nerve supplies buccal soft tissues of lower molars.

  6. What causes temporary facial nerve palsy after an IANB? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Anaesthetic deposited into the parotid gland reaching the facial nerve. The facial nerve runs through the parotid gland.

  7. Which agent is most associated with methaemoglobinaemia at high doses? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Prilocaine. A metabolite of prilocaine can oxidise haemoglobin.

  8. An IANB has numbed the lip but the molar is still painful. What is a good next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Give a supplementary buccal articaine infiltration or intraligamentary injection. Pulpal anaesthesia can lag behind soft-tissue numbness.

  9. A patient feels faint and pale after an injection. What is the first management? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Lay them flat with legs raised. Raising the legs improves blood flow to the brain.

Key takeaways

  • Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels; amides such as lidocaine and articaine are standard in UK dentistry, with adrenaline or felypressin as vasoconstrictors; maximum doses are calculated by weight to avoid toxicity.
  • Infiltration suits the maxilla and often anterior mandible; the IANB anaesthetises mandibular teeth, lip and, with the lingual nerve, the tongue; when anaesthesia fails, articaine infiltration and intraligamentary injection can help.
  • Local complications include failure, haematoma, trismus, facial nerve palsy, nerve injury and lip biting; systemic ones include toxicity, fainting and rare allergy; prevention relies on history, dose calculation, aspiration and slow injection.
  • Dental anxiety is common and leads to avoidance and worse oral health; the MDAS identifies high anxiety (19 or more); management uses behavioural approaches and CBT for long-term change, with sedation for treatment when needed.
  • Conscious sedation depresses the nervous system while maintaining verbal contact; inhalation sedation uses nitrous oxide and IV sedation uses titrated midazolam (reversible with flumazenil); IACSD standards require assessment, monitoring, escorts and trained teams.
  • Dental GA is used when other approaches are not possible, and since 2001 is given only in hospitals with critical care; tooth extraction for decay is a leading cause of child hospital admissions in England, and prevention is essential.

Common mistakes in Oral Surgery, Pain and Anxiety Control

  • Forgetting to calculate the maximum dose in small children. Toxicity can occur with only a few cartridges.
  • Assuming a numb lip means the tooth is anaesthetised. Lip numbness shows the nerve has been reached, but pulpal anaesthesia may still be incomplete.
  • Labelling a patient allergic to all local anaesthetics after a faint. Fainting and adrenaline effects are often mistaken for allergy.
  • Relying only on sedation for very anxious patients. Sedation helps with treatment but does not usually reduce anxiety for the future.
  • Treating sedation as a light general anaesthetic. In conscious sedation the patient must remain able to respond to verbal contact.
  • Seeing GA as the end of treatment. Without prevention, many children need further GA later.

Lessons in this school

  1. Local anaesthetics: pharmacology
    Local anaesthetics make most dental treatment painless.
  2. Local anaesthetic techniques
    Different techniques are used to anaesthetise teeth in the upper and lower jaws.
  3. Complications of local anaesthesia
    Local anaesthesia is very safe but can cause local and systemic complications.
  4. Dental anxiety
    Dental anxiety is common and can stop people getting the care they need.
  5. Conscious sedation
    Conscious sedation helps anxious patients receive dental treatment while remaining able to respond.
  6. General anaesthesia in dentistry
    General anaesthesia is sometimes needed for dental treatment, but it carries risks and must be used carefully.
  7. Principles of exodontia
    Tooth extraction is one of the most common oral surgery procedures.
  8. Surgical removal of teeth
    Some teeth and roots cannot be removed with forceps alone and need a surgical approach.
  9. Third molar surgery
    Wisdom teeth often become impacted.
  10. Complications of extractions
    Most extractions heal without problems, but some complications need prompt recognition and management.
  11. Odontogenic infections and their management
    Dental infections are common and usually localised, but can spread and become life-threatening.
  12. Antibiotic prescribing in dentistry
    Dentists prescribe a significant share of antibiotics, and inappropriate use drives antimicrobial resistance.
  13. Analgesia for dental pain
    Effective pain relief is essential after dental treatment and while patients wait for care.
  14. Maxillofacial trauma: an introduction
    Injuries to the face and jaws may involve teeth, bones and the airway.
  15. Referral pathways and urgent dental care
    Dentists must know when and how to refer patients, and patients need to know where to get urgent care.
