Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the supracrestal tissue attachment?

The junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment above the alveolar crest, about 2 mm on average. From “The periodontium in health” →

In plain English: The periodontium is the set of tissues that invest and support the teeth: gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone. Key idea: Periodontal health is not the absence of bacteria: a biofilm is always present, and health is a balanced state in which the host's defences keep it in check without destructive inflammation.

What is the most important modifiable risk factor for periodontitis?

Smoking. From “Risk factors for periodontitis” →

In plain English: Not everyone with plaque develops periodontitis. Key idea: In the 2017 grading system, heavy smoking (10 or more cigarettes a day) and poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c of 7.0% or more, 53 mmol/mol) shift a patient towards grade C, rapid progression.

Which two 1999 categories were replaced in 2017?

Chronic periodontitis and aggressive periodontitis. From “The 2017 classification of periodontal diseases” →

In plain English: The 2017 World Workshop, run by the American Academy of Periodontology and the European Federation of Periodontology, produced a new classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases. Key idea: A 2017 diagnosis of periodontitis has three parts: stage (I to IV), grade (A to C) and extent; in the UK implementation the current status (stable, in remission or unstable) is added.

What does a disclosing agent do?

Stains plaque so the patient can see where it remains. From “Oral hygiene instruction and behaviour change” →

In plain English: The patient's own daily plaque removal decides the long-term success of periodontal care. Key idea: Instruction is personalised: show the patient their own plaque (disclosing), demonstrate in their mouth, let them practise, and choose aids that suit their mouth, dexterity and motivation.

Does the EFP S3 guideline recommend routine systemic antibiotics for periodontitis?

No; they may be considered only in specific groups because of resistance and side effects. From “Adjuncts to periodontal therapy” →

In plain English: Adjuncts are treatments added to mechanical therapy in the hope of better results: antiseptics, local and systemic antimicrobials and host-modulating agents. Key idea: Mechanical biofilm disruption and patient self-care are the treatment; adjuncts give at most a small additional benefit, so systemic antibiotics are reserved for selected cases because of resistance and side effects.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Periodontology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is part of the periodontium? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Cementum. The periodontium is gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone; enamel and pulp belong to the tooth itself.

  2. What distinguishes gingivitis from periodontitis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Gingivitis has no loss of connective tissue attachment. Periodontitis involves attachment and bone loss; gingivitis is confined to the gingiva and reversible.

  3. Which species is described as a keystone pathogen in periodontitis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Porphyromonas gingivalis. P. gingivalis subverts host defences at low abundance; S. mutans and lactobacilli are linked to caries, and Candida to fungal infections.

  4. A treated periodontitis patient has residual attachment loss but no bleeding and probing depths of 4 mm or less. How is the periodontium described? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Healthy on a reduced periodontium. Past attachment loss means the periodontium is reduced, but without inflammation it can be classed as health on a reduced periodontium.

  5. How long did gingivitis take to develop when oral hygiene stopped in the experimental gingivitis study? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    About 10 to 21 days. Löe and colleagues found gingivitis developed within 10 to 21 days of plaque accumulation and resolved when cleaning resumed.

  6. Which enzymes are mainly responsible for collagen breakdown in periodontitis? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Matrix metalloproteinases. MMPs from fibroblasts and inflammatory cells degrade collagen; amylase digests starch and glucosyltransferases make plaque polysaccharides.

  7. Why does a crown margin placed deep below the gingiva often lead to persistent inflammation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It invades the supracrestal tissue attachment, so the tissues remain inflamed and bone may be lost to re-establish it. Violating the zone of junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment provokes chronic inflammation; bone may resorb until the attachment is re-formed.

  8. A heavy smoker has visible calculus and 5 mm pockets but almost no bleeding on probing. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Smoking is masking inflammation, so the absence of bleeding does not mean health. Nicotine-induced vasoconstriction reduces bleeding; disease is judged by probing depths, attachment loss and radiographs as well.

  9. Which observation most strongly supports the idea that the host response determines the severity of periodontitis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    People with neutrophil defects or poorly controlled diabetes develop severe disease with similar plaque levels. Severe disease in hosts with altered immunity despite similar plaque shows that susceptibility, not just bacteria, drives destruction.

Key takeaways

  • Periodontal health is not the absence of bacteria: a biofilm is always present, and health is a balanced state in which the host's defences keep it in check without destructive inflammation.
  • Gingivitis is reversible; it is a necessary precursor of periodontitis, but most gingivitis does not progress, and whether it does depends on host susceptibility.
  • Inflammation and dysbiosis reinforce each other: the dysbiotic biofilm drives inflammation, and inflammation supplies the nutrients (crevicular fluid, haem) that favour the pathogenic community.
  • In the 2017 grading system, heavy smoking (10 or more cigarettes a day) and poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c of 7.0% or more, 53 mmol/mol) shift a patient towards grade C, rapid progression.
  • An association, even a consistent one, does not prove that treating periodontitis will prevent another disease; this is established for diabetes control (modestly), but not for heart attacks, strokes or preterm birth.
  • Following British Society of Periodontology guidance: codes 0 to 2 lead to prevention and plaque removal; code 3 to treatment and a detailed chart of that sextant after initial therapy; code 4 (or an asterisk) in any sextant to a full six-point pocket chart, radiographs and consideration of referral.

Common mistakes in Periodontology

  • Equating a 1–3 mm probing depth with the histological sulcus depth. The probe tip penetrates the junctional epithelium, so clinical probing depth is greater than the true sulcus, especially when tissues are inflamed.
  • Interpreting little bleeding in a smoker as health. Nicotine causes vasoconstriction, so smokers bleed less despite disease; probing depths and attachment levels must be checked.
  • Thinking periodontitis is simply an infection to be cured with antibiotics. Bacteria are necessary, but most destruction is host-mediated, and disruption of the biofilm with control of risk factors is the basis of treatment.
  • Telling smokers their gums look healthy because they bleed little. Smoking masks the key sign of inflammation; disease must be assessed by probing and radiographs.
  • Overstating the systemic benefits of periodontal treatment to motivate patients. It is misleading and inconsistent with the GDC's requirement to give patients honest information; the oral benefits are reason enough.
  • Using the BPE to diagnose periodontitis. It is a screening tool: it cannot measure attachment loss, distinguish true from false pockets or monitor treatment of deep sites, for which full charting is needed.

Lessons in this school

  1. The periodontium in health
    The periodontium is the set of tissues that invest and support the teeth: gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone.
  2. Gingivitis
    Gingivitis is inflammation of the gingiva without loss of attachment.
  3. Pathogenesis of periodontitis
    Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease in which a dysbiotic biofilm triggers a host response that destroys the tooth's attachment and bone.
  4. Risk factors for periodontitis
    Not everyone with plaque develops periodontitis.
  5. Periodontitis and systemic health
    Periodontitis is associated with several systemic diseases, notably diabetes, cardiovascular disease and adverse pregnancy outcomes.
  6. Periodontal examination: BPE and charting
    The Basic Periodontal Examination (BPE) is the screening tool used in UK practice to decide who needs further periodontal assessment.
  7. The 2017 classification of periodontal diseases
    The 2017 World Workshop, run by the American Academy of Periodontology and the European Federation of Periodontology, produced a new classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases.
  8. Staging and grading periodontitis
    Staging describes how severe and complex a patient's periodontitis is; grading describes how fast it has progressed and how much risk the patient carries.
  9. Necrotising periodontal diseases and periodontal abscess
    Some periodontal conditions present acutely, with pain and sometimes systemic upset.
  10. Oral hygiene instruction and behaviour change
    The patient's own daily plaque removal decides the long-term success of periodontal care.
  11. Professional mechanical plaque removal
    Professional mechanical plaque removal (PMPR) is the removal of supragingival and gingival plaque and calculus by the dental team, together with correction of plaque-retentive factors.
  12. Non-surgical periodontal therapy
    Non-surgical periodontal therapy is the core treatment for periodontitis.
  13. Adjuncts to periodontal therapy
    Adjuncts are treatments added to mechanical therapy in the hope of better results: antiseptics, local and systemic antimicrobials and host-modulating agents.
  14. Periodontal surgery: an overview
    When pockets persist after good non-surgical therapy, or when tissues need reshaping or regenerating, periodontal surgery may help.
  15. Supportive periodontal care
    Periodontitis is a chronic disease: once treated, it needs lifelong supportive care to keep it stable.
  16. Peri-implant health and disease
    Dental implants can develop inflammatory diseases similar to gingivitis and periodontitis: peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis.

Periodontology: questions students ask

Which balance controls periodontal bone resorption?

The balance of RANKL to osteoprotegerin (OPG).

Which systemic link with periodontitis is best established?

The bidirectional relationship with diabetes.

What does BPE code 3 indicate?

A shallow pocket of 3.5 to 5.5 mm: the black band is partly visible.

How is grade estimated in the BSP implementation?

Percentage bone loss at the worst site divided by age: under 0.5 grade A, 0.5–1.0 grade B, over 1.0 grade C.
