Key terms and formulas at a glance

Dental caries
A biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, dynamic disease causing phasic loss and gain of mineral in teeth
Ecological plaque hypothesis
The theory that environmental changes such as frequent sugar shift the biofilm towards a cariogenic community
Critical pH
The pH below which saliva and plaque fluid are undersaturated with respect to tooth mineral, about 5.5 for enamel
Free sugars
Sugars added to foods and drinks plus those in honey, syrups and fruit juices; not those in intact fruit or milk
ICDAS
International Caries Detection and Assessment System, grading coronal lesions from 0 (sound) to 6 (extensive cavity)
Caries risk assessment
A structured judgement of a person's likelihood of developing new caries lesions
Root caries
Caries of the exposed root surface of a tooth, usually after gingival recession
Fluorapatite
Apatite in which fluoride replaces hydroxyl, less soluble than hydroxyapatite
ppm F

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the four interacting factors in caries?

Biofilm, fermentable carbohydrates (free sugars), a susceptible host and time. From “What dental caries is” →

In plain English: Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, dynamic disease in which the dental hard tissues lose and regain mineral many times a day. Key idea: The lesion is the sign; the disease is the imbalance between demineralisation and remineralisation in the biofilm. Filling a cavity removes a sign but does not treat the disease.

What did the Vipeholm study show?

Sugar between meals, especially sticky sugar, increased caries far more than sugar at meals. From “Diet, sugars and caries” →

In plain English: Free sugars are the essential dietary cause of caries. Key idea: Dietary advice targets both amount and frequency: reduce free sugars overall and confine them to mealtimes; water and plain milk are the safe drinks between meals.

What is the main way fluoride prevents caries?

Topically: inhibiting demineralisation and enhancing remineralisation at the tooth surface. From “How fluoride works” →

In plain English: Fluoride is the cornerstone of caries prevention. Key idea: Fluoride works chiefly topically, after eruption, by being present in the oral fluids frequently and at low concentration: twice-daily brushing with fluoride toothpaste, spitting not rinsing, keeps it there.

When should toothbrushing start, according to DBOH?

As soon as the first tooth erupts. From “Delivering Better Oral Health” →

In plain English: Delivering Better Oral Health (DBOH) is the evidence-based toolkit for prevention used by dental teams in England. Key idea: DBOH separates advice for everyone from extra measures for those at increased risk, so prevention is universal but proportionate.

What is the social gradient in oral health?

The stepwise worsening of oral health at each lower level of social position, across the whole population. From “Oral health inequalities” →

In plain English: Oral disease follows a social gradient: at every step down the social ladder, oral health gets worse. Key idea: Inequalities arise mainly from the conditions in which people live; health education alone tends to benefit the better-off most and can widen inequalities, whereas measures that change environments reach everyone.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best defines dental caries? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven disease of mineral loss and gain in the teeth. Caries is a dynamic process of demineralisation and remineralisation driven by biofilm acids from sugars; erosion is acid wear without bacteria, and gingivitis is a gum disease.

  2. Who proposed the ecological plaque hypothesis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Marsh. Philip Marsh proposed it in 1994; Miller's chemo-parasitic theory and Stephan's pH curve came earlier.

  3. What does the Stephan curve show? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The fall and recovery of plaque pH after a sugar challenge. Stephan measured plaque pH falling after sugar and recovering over 30 to 60 minutes.

  4. A white spot lesion on a dried smooth surface is chalky, rough and covered by plaque. How should it be classified? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Active, non-cavitated lesion. Chalky, rough enamel under plaque indicates an active initial lesion; arrested lesions are shiny and hard.

  5. According to the ecological plaque hypothesis, what is the main driver of the shift to a cariogenic biofilm? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Frequent sugar intake producing repeated low pH. Repeated acid conditions from frequent sugar give acid-tolerant bacteria a competitive advantage, changing the community's composition.

  6. Two people eat the same amount of sugar a day. Who is at higher caries risk? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The one who sips a sugary drink throughout the day. Frequent exposures keep plaque pH below the critical pH for longer, leaving little time for remineralisation.

  7. A dentist restores every cavity in a young adult but gives no dietary or fluoride advice; two years later several new lesions have appeared. What best explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Restorations treat lesions, not the disease process, which continued unchanged. Without changing the biofilm, sugar exposure and fluoride, the imbalance driving caries persists, so new lesions form; restoration alone doesn't control the disease.

  8. Why does the specific plaque hypothesis struggle to explain caries in some patients? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Caries occurs in people with few mutans streptococci, and many people carry them without caries. Associations are real but imperfect; other acidogenic, aciduric organisms can drive caries, which the ecological hypothesis accommodates.

  9. Why does an early enamel lesion keep an apparently intact surface even though the body beneath has lost much mineral? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Ions released from the body reprecipitate at the surface, aided by fluoride and saliva. Dissolution products diffusing outward redeposit at the less acidic surface, especially with fluoride, so loss is greatest subsurface.

Key takeaways

  • The lesion is the sign; the disease is the imbalance between demineralisation and remineralisation in the biofilm. Filling a cavity removes a sign but does not treat the disease.
  • Under the ecological hypothesis disease results from an environmental shift that changes the whole community, so prevention targets the drivers (sugar frequency, low pH, low saliva) rather than trying to eliminate a single species.
  • Frequency of sugar intake matters as much as amount: each intake starts a new acid challenge, and constant sipping or grazing never lets the pH recover.
  • Dietary advice targets both amount and frequency: reduce free sugars overall and confine them to mealtimes; water and plain milk are the safe drinks between meals.
  • Detection asks "is there a lesion?", assessment asks "how deep and is it active?", and diagnosis integrates these with the patient's history and risk to decide management.
  • Past caries experience is the best single predictor of future caries, because it reflects the combined effect of all the other factors over time.

Common mistakes in Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health

  • Equating "caries" with "a hole". Most of the disease process happens before cavitation, when it is reversible or arrestable by prevention alone.
  • Aiming to "kill the caries bacteria" with long courses of antimicrobials. The resident microbiota recolonises quickly; without changing sugar exposure and pH, the cariogenic community returns.
  • Using a sharp probe to "stick" in fissures to diagnose caries. It can cavitate the fragile surface zone of an early lesion and transfer bacteria; caries is assessed visually on clean, dry teeth with radiographs as needed.
  • Recommending fruit juice and smoothies as healthy between-meal drinks for teeth. They contain free sugars and are acidic; they are best limited and taken with meals.
  • Drilling every radiolucency. Many radiographically visible lesions, especially in enamel and the outer third of dentine on approximal surfaces, are not cavitated and can be arrested.
  • Fixing a risk category for life. Risk changes with medication, diet, life events and age, so it is reassessed at every examination.

Lessons in this school

  1. What dental caries is
    Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, sugar-driven, dynamic disease in which the dental hard tissues lose and regain mineral many times a day.
  2. The ecological plaque hypothesis
    Which bacteria cause caries?
  3. Demineralisation and remineralisation
    Every time sugar is eaten, the pH of plaque falls and the tooth surface loses mineral; between intakes, saliva helps it regain mineral.
  4. Diet, sugars and caries
    Free sugars are the essential dietary cause of caries.
  5. Caries detection and diagnosis
    Detecting a caries lesion, judging its extent and deciding whether it is active are three separate steps, and all three guide treatment.
  6. Caries risk assessment
    Caries risk assessment predicts who is likely to develop new lesions, so that prevention, recall intervals and radiographs can be matched to need.
  7. Root caries
    Root caries affects exposed root surfaces, usually in older adults with gingival recession, and is becoming more common as people keep their teeth for longer.
  8. How fluoride works
    Fluoride is the cornerstone of caries prevention.
  9. Fluoride toothpaste, varnish and other vehicles
    Fluoride reaches teeth through toothpaste, varnish, mouthrinses, water, milk and salt.
  10. Fissure sealants
    Pits and fissures on molars trap plaque and are where most caries occurs in children's permanent teeth.
  11. Delivering Better Oral Health
    Delivering Better Oral Health (DBOH) is the evidence-based toolkit for prevention used by dental teams in England.
  12. Minimal intervention dentistry
    Minimal intervention dentistry (MID) manages caries by detecting it early, controlling its causes, remineralising early lesions and, when restoration is needed, removing as little tooth as possible.
  13. Epidemiology of oral disease
    Epidemiology describes how common oral diseases are, who has them and why, and it underpins planning of services and prevention.
  14. Measuring oral disease: indices
    An index turns oral disease into a number that can be compared between people, places and times.
  15. Oral health inequalities
    Oral disease follows a social gradient: at every step down the social ladder, oral health gets worse.
  16. Community water fluoridation
    Community water fluoridation adjusts the fluoride in public water supplies to a level that helps prevent caries.
  17. Oral health promotion
    Oral health promotion enables people to increase control over their oral health.
  18. NHS dental services
    Most dental care in the UK is delivered through the NHS by general dental practitioners under contract, alongside community and hospital services and private care.

Dental Caries, Prevention and Dental Public Health: questions students ask

Which bacteria are classically associated with caries?

Mutans streptococci and lactobacilli.

What is the approximate critical pH of enamel?

About 5.5 (higher for root dentine, about 6.2 to 6.7).

Are the sugars in a whole apple free sugars?

No. Sugars within the structure of intact fruit are not free sugars; fruit juice sugars are.

What does ICDAS code 1 describe?

The first visual change in enamel, seen only after prolonged drying.
