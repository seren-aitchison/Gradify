Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Which nerve passes through foramen rotundum?

The maxillary nerve (V2), into the pterygopalatine fossa. From “The skull and facial skeleton” →

In plain English: The skull protects the brain and carries the teeth, the airway and the special senses. Key idea: The branches of the trigeminal nerve leave the skull through three openings, in order front to back: V1 the superior orbital fissure, V2 foramen rotundum, V3 foramen ovale ("Standing Room Only").

Which muscle inserts into the TMJ disc?

The superior head of the lateral pterygoid. From “The temporomandibular joint” →

In plain English: The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a synovial joint that both hinges and slides, with an articular disc dividing it into two compartments. Key idea: Rotation happens in the lower compartment, translation in the upper: the disc moves with the condyle, so a displaced disc disturbs the smooth sequence.

Which duct pierces buccinator?

The parotid duct, opposite the upper second molar. From “Muscles of facial expression” →

In plain English: The muscles of facial expression lie in the superficial fascia and move the skin of the face. Key idea: All the muscles of facial expression, including buccinator and platysma, are supplied by the facial nerve (VII); they derive from second-arch mesoderm. Buccinator's sensory supply is different: the buccal nerve (V3).

Which nerve supplies the buccal gingiva of the lower molars?

The buccal (long buccal) nerve. From “Nerve supply of the teeth and gingivae” →

In plain English: Each tooth and each area of gingiva has its own named nerve, and the choice of local anaesthetic technique follows directly from this map. Key idea: An inferior alveolar nerve block anaesthetises the lower teeth on that side, the lip and (usually with the lingual nerve) the tongue, but not the buccal gingiva of the molars: a separate buccal infiltration is needed for extractions.

What are Fordyce spots?

Ectopic sebaceous glands, seen as yellowish spots in the buccal mucosa and lips; a normal variant. From “The oral cavity: anatomy and landmarks” →

In plain English: A dentist examines the whole mouth at every visit, so the normal landmarks and the common normal variants must be known well enough that anything unusual stands out. Key idea: Knowing the normal variants prevents unnecessary alarm and biopsy, but a lesion that does not fit a normal pattern, or persists, must be recorded and followed up.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these bones is part of the viscerocranium rather than the neurocranium? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Palatine bone. The palatine bones form the posterior hard palate and are facial bones; the sphenoid, ethmoid and parietal bones contribute to the braincase.

  2. What transmits the nasopalatine nerves to the anterior palate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The incisive foramen. The nasopalatine nerves emerge from the incisive foramen behind the central incisors; the greater palatine foramen lies in the molar region.

  3. Where does the mental nerve emerge from the mandible? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The mental foramen, usually near the apex of the second premolar. The mental nerve leaves the canal at the mental foramen on the lateral surface; the mandibular foramen is where the inferior alveolar nerve enters.

  4. A patient has numbness of the lower lip and chin after a lesion at the skull base. Through which opening does the affected nerve trunk leave the skull? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Foramen ovale. The mental nerve is a branch of the inferior alveolar nerve from V3, which leaves through foramen ovale; rotundum carries V2 and the stylomastoid foramen carries the motor facial nerve.

  5. Why is buccal infiltration usually effective for upper molars in adults? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The buccal cortical bone of the maxilla is thin and porous, so solution reaches the apical nerves. Thin, porous maxillary bone allows diffusion to the superior alveolar nerves; the thick mandibular cortex is why lower molars usually need a block.

  6. An abscess from a lower right second molar perforates the lingual plate. Which space is it most likely to enter first? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The submandibular space. The second molar apices lie below the mylohyoid line, so lingual spread goes below mylohyoid into the submandibular space; premolar and anterior teeth drain to the sublingual space.

  7. After a fall onto the cheek, a patient has a flattened cheekbone and numbness of the upper lip and side of the nose. What best explains the numbness? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The fracture runs through the infraorbital foramen and injures the infraorbital nerve. Zygomatic complex fractures commonly cross the orbital floor and infraorbital foramen, so the infraorbital branch of V2, which supplies the upper lip, cheek and side of nose, is injured. The facial nerve is motor to the face, not sensory.

  8. A patient has dull aching of the upper right premolars and molars for a week after a cold; all the teeth are slightly tender to percussion and pain worsens on bending forward. What is the most likely explanation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Maxillary sinusitis irritating the superior alveolar nerves. Several adjacent upper posterior teeth aching together after a cold, worse on bending, suggests sinusitis: the superior alveolar nerves run in the sinus walls. Pulpitis of many teeth at once is unlikely, and trigeminal neuralgia causes brief electric pains.

  9. A man in his eighties who has worn a complete lower denture for decades reports burning pain and tingling of the lower lip when he bites. What anatomical change best explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Alveolar resorption has brought the mental foramen close to the crest, so the denture compresses the mental nerve. After long-term tooth loss the alveolar ridge resorbs down towards the canal and mental foramen; pressure from the denture flange then irritates the mental nerve. The lingual nerve supplies the tongue, not the lip.

Key takeaways

  • The branches of the trigeminal nerve leave the skull through three openings, in order front to back: V1 the superior orbital fissure, V2 foramen rotundum, V3 foramen ovale ("Standing Room Only").
  • The palatal mucoperiosteum is bound tightly to bone, so injections there are uncomfortable and only small volumes can be given; the greater palatine vessels run forward from the molar region, so palatal incisions are planned to avoid them.
  • The thick buccal cortical plate of the adult mandible, especially in the molar region, is why lower molars usually need an inferior alveolar nerve block rather than buccal infiltration alone.
  • Rotation happens in the lower compartment, translation in the upper: the disc moves with the condyle, so a displaced disc disturbs the smooth sequence.
  • Lateral pterygoid is the only muscle of mastication that helps open the mouth; the other three close it. Depression is completed by the suprahyoid muscles (digastric, mylohyoid, geniohyoid) and gravity.
  • Rest vertical dimension minus occlusal vertical dimension equals freeway space. Losing it (for example by making dentures too tall) makes the teeth meet constantly, causing soreness.

Common mistakes in Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy

  • Thinking the facial skeleton is uniformly strong. It is built of strong vertical buttresses (nasomaxillary, zygomaticomaxillary, pterygomaxillary) with thin bone between, including the walls of the maxillary sinus, which fracture easily.
  • Assuming upper molar pain is always dental. Maxillary sinusitis can cause aching of several upper posterior teeth, because the superior alveolar nerves run in the sinus walls; tenderness of several adjacent teeth, worse on bending forward, points to the sinus.
  • Placing the needle for an inferior alveolar block too low. The mandibular foramen lies roughly level with the occlusal plane in adults (higher than in children), so a low injection misses the nerve before it enters the canal.
  • Treating every click as disease. Joint sounds are common in people without pain; management is guided by pain and function, not the sound alone.
  • Thinking the lateral pterygoid closes the mouth like the others. It pulls the condyle forward onto the eminence: it protrudes and opens.
  • Confusing the working side with the side being restored. The working side is the side the mandible moves towards, whichever tooth is being checked.

Lessons in this school

  1. The skull and facial skeleton
    The skull protects the brain and carries the teeth, the airway and the special senses.
  2. The maxilla and palate
    The paired maxillae carry the upper teeth, form most of the hard palate and enclose the largest paranasal sinus.
  3. The mandible
    The mandible is the largest and strongest bone of the face and the only one that moves.
  4. The temporomandibular joint
    The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a synovial joint that both hinges and slides, with an articular disc dividing it into two compartments.
  5. Muscles of mastication
    Four paired muscles move the mandible in chewing: masseter, temporalis and the medial and lateral pterygoids.
  6. Mandibular movements
    The mandible opens, closes, protrudes, retrudes and moves from side to side, and the teeth guide it as they meet.
  7. Muscles of facial expression
    The muscles of facial expression lie in the superficial fascia and move the skin of the face.
  8. The tongue and floor of the mouth
    The tongue is a muscular organ of taste, speech, chewing and swallowing, with a complex nerve supply that reflects its development.
  9. The trigeminal nerve
    The trigeminal nerve (V) is the great sensory nerve of the face, mouth and teeth and the motor nerve of mastication.
  10. Nerve supply of the teeth and gingivae
    Each tooth and each area of gingiva has its own named nerve, and the choice of local anaesthetic technique follows directly from this map.
  11. The facial nerve and other cranial nerves in dentistry
    Beyond the trigeminal, several cranial nerves matter in the dental chair: the facial nerve for expression, taste and saliva, the glossopharyngeal and vagus for the gag reflex and swallowing, and the h
  12. Blood supply and lymphatic drainage of the head and neck
    The external carotid artery and its maxillary branch supply the jaws and teeth, the pterygoid venous plexus links the face to the cavernous sinus, and lymph from the mouth drains through predictable node groups.
  13. The oral cavity: anatomy and landmarks
    A dentist examines the whole mouth at every visit, so the normal landmarks and the common normal variants must be known well enough that anything unusual stands out.
  14. The salivary glands
    Three paired major salivary glands and hundreds of minor glands produce saliva.
  15. The pharynx and larynx
    The pharynx is shared by the airway and the food passage, and the larynx guards the lower airway.
  16. Fascial spaces of the head and neck
    Fascial layers divide the soft tissues of the head and neck into potential spaces, and pus from a dental infection spreads along them.

Oral and Head and Neck Anatomy: questions students ask

Which bones form the hard palate?

The palatine processes of the maxillae (anterior two-thirds) and the horizontal plates of the palatine bones (posterior third).

Which ligament attaches to the lingula?

The sphenomandibular ligament.

What is the nerve supply of all the muscles of mastication?

The mandibular nerve (V3).

What is freeway space?

The gap between the teeth with the mandible at rest: rest vertical dimension minus occlusal vertical dimension, typically 2–4 mm.
