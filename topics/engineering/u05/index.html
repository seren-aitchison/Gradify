Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why are metals ductile?

Their non-directional metallic bonds let atoms slide past each other without breaking the material apart. From “Atomic bonding in materials” →

In plain English: The way atoms bond largely determines a material's properties. Key idea: Ionic, covalent and metallic bonds give materials their strength and stiffness, while weaker secondary bonds between polymer chains explain the low stiffness of many plastics; bond type links directly to melting point, conductivity and ductility.

What does the lever rule calculate?

The mass fractions of two phases in a two-phase region. From “Phase diagrams” →

In plain English: Phase diagrams map which phases exist in an alloy at each temperature and composition. Key idea: Binary phase diagrams show the phases present at each temperature and composition; tie lines give phase compositions and the lever rule gives their fractions; isomorphous, eutectic and eutectoid systems are the key types.

Which type of crystal structure commonly shows a ductile-to-brittle transition?

Body-centred cubic, as in ferritic steels. From “Hardness and impact testing” →

In plain English: Hardness and impact tests are quick, practical measures of a material's resistance to indentation and to sudden loading. Key idea: Hardness tests measure resistance to indentation and correlate with strength; the Charpy test measures impact toughness, revealing a ductile-to-brittle transition in many steels that must be considered for low-temperature service.

What are the four parts of a corrosion cell?

An anode, a cathode, an electrolyte and an electrical connection. From “Corrosion and degradation” →

In plain English: Corrosion and degradation cost economies trillions each year. Key idea: Corrosion is electrochemical, faster for more anodic metals and in dissimilar-metal contact; it takes uniform, galvanic, pitting and stress-corrosion forms, and is controlled by coatings, galvanising, cathodic protection, alloy choice and design.

How is toughened glass made?

By heating it and cooling the surface rapidly so the surface is in compression. From “Ceramics and glasses” →

In plain English: Ceramics and glasses are hard, heat-resistant and stiff, but brittle. Key idea: Ceramics and glasses are hard, stiff and heat-resistant but brittle, with flaw-controlled, variable strength; tempering, lamination, transformation toughening and composites improve their reliability.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Materials Science

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which bond type involves a "sea" of shared electrons? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Metallic bonding. Metals share valence electrons among all ion cores.

  2. Which crystal structure does aluminium have? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Face-centred cubic. Aluminium is FCC, which helps explain its ductility.

  3. What type of defect is a vacancy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A point defect. Applying the standard result: A vacancy is a single missing atom.

  4. Why are most ceramics electrical insulators? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Their ionic and covalent bonds hold electrons in place. Without free electrons, charge cannot flow easily.

  5. What is the atomic packing factor of a BCC structure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.68. Applying the standard result: BCC packs less densely than FCC or HCP.

  6. Why does cold rolling make a metal stronger? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It multiplies dislocations, which tangle and obstruct each other. Work hardening raises dislocation density.

  7. A designer needs a material with high stiffness and a very high melting point for a furnace lining. Which bond type suggests a suitable class? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Strong ionic and covalent bonding, as in ceramics. Ceramics combine stiffness with high melting points.

  8. Why is magnesium generally less ductile than aluminium at room temperature? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    HCP magnesium has fewer easily activated slip systems than FCC aluminium. Ductility depends on available slip systems, not only packing.

  9. Why do real metals yield at far lower stresses than a perfect crystal would? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dislocations let slip occur by breaking bonds one row at a time. Dislocation motion needs much less stress than shearing whole planes at once.

Key takeaways

  • Ionic, covalent and metallic bonds give materials their strength and stiffness, while weaker secondary bonds between polymer chains explain the low stiffness of many plastics; bond type links directly to melting point, conductivity and ductility.
  • Metals mostly crystallise as BCC, FCC or HCP; FCC and HCP pack most densely, FCC has many slip systems and is ductile, and theoretical density follows from atoms per cell and cell size.
  • Crystals contain point, line, planar and volume defects; dislocations make plastic flow possible at low stresses, and metals are strengthened by hindering dislocations through work hardening, grain refinement, solid solutions and precipitates.
  • Binary phase diagrams show the phases present at each temperature and composition; tie lines give phase compositions and the lever rule gives their fractions; isomorphous, eutectic and eutectoid systems are the key types.
  • Steel's phases are ferrite, austenite and cementite; at 727 °C and 0.76% C austenite forms pearlite; hypoeutectoid steels contain proeutectoid ferrite plus pearlite, and the lever rule gives their fractions.
  • The tensile test gives Young's modulus, yield or proof stress, UTS, elongation and reduction of area; the engineering curve falls after necking because it uses the original area.

Common mistakes in Materials Science

  • Thinking polymers are weak because their chains are weak. The carbon–carbon backbone is strong; it is the secondary bonds between chains that are weak.
  • Assuming close-packed means ductile. HCP is as densely packed as FCC but has fewer active slip systems, so it is often less ductile.
  • Thinking defects only weaken materials. Controlled defects, such as fine grains and precipitates, are the main way metals are made stronger.
  • Reading phase fractions from the tie-line end nearest the alloy. In the lever rule, each phase's fraction is the length of the opposite arm divided by the total.
  • Treating the equilibrium diagram as showing quenched microstructures. Rapid cooling forms martensite and bainite, which do not appear on the equilibrium diagram.
  • Designing to the ultimate tensile strength. Most structural design uses the yield or proof stress, with safety factors, to avoid permanent deformation.

Lessons in this school

  1. Atomic bonding in materials
    The way atoms bond largely determines a material's properties.
  2. Crystal structures
    Most metals and many ceramics are crystalline, with atoms in regular repeating patterns.
  3. Defects and dislocations
    Real crystals contain defects that control their strength and ductility.
  4. Phase diagrams
    Phase diagrams map which phases exist in an alloy at each temperature and composition.
  5. The iron–carbon system
    Steel is the most important engineering alloy, and its properties depend on the iron–carbon phase diagram.
  6. The tensile test
    The tensile test is the most widely used mechanical test of materials.
  7. Hardness and impact testing
    Hardness and impact tests are quick, practical measures of a material's resistance to indentation and to sudden loading.
  8. Fracture and fracture toughness
    Cracks concentrate stress, and fracture mechanics predicts when they will grow.
  9. Fatigue
    Most mechanical failures in service are caused by fatigue: cracks that grow under repeated loading at stresses below yield.
  10. Creep
    At high temperatures, materials deform slowly under constant load.
  11. Corrosion and degradation
    Corrosion and degradation cost economies trillions each year.
  12. Metals and alloys
    Metals are the backbone of engineering because they combine strength, toughness and formability.
  13. Polymers
    Polymers are long-chain molecules with low density and versatile properties.
  14. Ceramics and glasses
    Ceramics and glasses are hard, heat-resistant and stiff, but brittle.
  15. Composites
    Composites combine two or more materials to achieve properties neither has alone.
  16. Heat treatment of steels
    Heat treatment transforms the properties of steel without changing its composition.
  17. Materials selection and Ashby charts
    Choosing the right material means matching properties to the demands of a design.

Materials Science: questions students ask

What is a dislocation?

A line defect in a crystal whose movement allows plastic deformation.

What is pearlite?

A layered mixture of ferrite and cementite formed by the eutectoid reaction.

What is the 0.2% proof stress?

The stress at which 0.2% plastic strain occurs, used where there is no clear yield point.

What does the Charpy test measure?

The energy absorbed in breaking a notched specimen under impact.
