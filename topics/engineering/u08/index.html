Key terms and formulas at a glance

Current
The rate of flow of electric charge, measured in amperes
Resistance
Opposition to current, the ratio of voltage to current, in ohms
Node
A point in a circuit where two or more elements are connected
Reference node
The node chosen as zero volts, against which other node voltages are measured
Thévenin equivalent
A voltage source in series with a resistance that reproduces a network's terminal behaviour
Maximum power transfer
The condition that a load receives the most power when its resistance equals the source resistance
Capacitance
Charge stored per volt, measured in farads
Time constant
The time for a first-order response to cover 63.2% of the change from initial to final value
RMS value
The equivalent DC value that produces the same power in a resistor
Impedance
The complex ratio of voltage phasor to current phasor, R + jX, in ohms
Real power
The average power converted to useful work or heat, in watts
Phase sequence
The order in which the three phase voltages reach their maxima

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is one volt?

One joule of energy transferred per coulomb of charge. From “Charge, current, voltage and power” →

In plain English: Every circuit calculation rests on four quantities: charge, current, voltage and power. Key idea: Current is flow of charge, voltage is energy per charge, and power is their product; energy is power multiplied by time.

What is a supermesh?

Two meshes sharing a current source, treated together with KVL round their combined boundary. From “Nodal and mesh analysis” →

In plain English: Nodal and mesh analysis turn Kirchhoff's laws into a systematic method that always gives the right number of equations. Key idea: Nodal analysis writes KCL in terms of node voltages; mesh analysis writes KVL in terms of mesh currents; both give the same answers, so choose the one with fewer equations.

What percentage of the final value does a charging capacitor reach after one time constant?

About 63.2%. From “RC and RL transients” →

In plain English: When a circuit containing a capacitor or inductor is switched, voltages and currents change exponentially before settling. Key idea: First-order circuits respond exponentially with time constant τ = RC or L/R; use initial value, final value and τ to write any step response.

Why does a low power factor matter?

More current is needed for the same real power, increasing losses, voltage drop and equipment size. From “AC power and power factor” →

In plain English: In AC circuits with reactance, not all the current does useful work. Key idea: Real power P = VI cos φ does work, reactive power Q = VI sin φ circulates, and apparent power S = VI sets the current; parallel capacitors improve a lagging power factor.

What is the gain of a voltage follower?

1, with very high input impedance and low output impedance. From “Operational amplifiers” →

In plain English: The operational amplifier is a versatile integrated circuit used for amplification, filtering, signal conditioning and comparison. Key idea: With negative feedback, an ideal op-amp holds its inputs at equal voltage and draws no input current; inverting gain is −Rf/Rin, non-inverting gain is 1 + Rf/R1.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Electrical Circuits and Electronics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the SI unit of electric charge? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The coulomb. Charge is measured in coulombs; the ampere is one coulomb per second.

  2. Three 10 Ω resistors are connected in series. What is the total resistance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    30 Ω. Series resistances add: 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 Ω.

  3. Kirchhoff's current law is a consequence of what? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Conservation of charge. Charge cannot accumulate at a node, so currents in equal currents out.

  4. A current of 2 A flows for 30 s. How much charge passes? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    60 C. Charge is current multiplied by time: Q = It = 2 × 30 = 60 C.

  5. A 12 V supply feeds 2 kΩ and 1 kΩ in series. What is the voltage across the 1 kΩ? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    4 V. By the voltage divider rule, V = 12 × 1 ÷ (2 + 1) = 4 V.

  6. A 24 V source drives a loop containing 4 Ω and 8 Ω. What is the drop across the 8 Ω? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    16 V. I = 24 ÷ 12 = 2 A, so the drop is 2 × 8 = 16 V; KVL: 24 − 8 − 16 = 0.

  7. Current of 2 A leaves the positive terminal of a 12 V element. What is happening? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It delivers 24 W. Current leaving the positive terminal means the element is a source delivering P = VI = 24 W.

  8. A copper wire is replaced by one twice as long with half the cross-sectional area. How does its resistance change? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It becomes four times larger. R = ρL/A: doubling L doubles R, halving A doubles it again.

  9. A circuit has 5 branches and 3 nodes. How many independent KVL equations are there? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    3. Independent loop equations number b − n + 1 = 5 − 3 + 1 = 3.

Key takeaways

  • Current is flow of charge, voltage is energy per charge, and power is their product; energy is power multiplied by time.
  • Series resistances add; parallel conductances add; the divider rules give voltages and currents without solving the whole circuit.
  • KCL (charge conservation) and KVL (energy conservation) apply to every circuit; a negative answer only means the true direction is opposite to the one assumed.
  • Nodal analysis writes KCL in terms of node voltages; mesh analysis writes KVL in terms of mesh currents; both give the same answers, so choose the one with fewer equations.
  • A linear two-terminal network is equivalent to VTh in series with RTh, or IN in parallel with the same resistance; VTh is the open-circuit voltage and IN the short-circuit current.
  • Maximum power reaches the load when RL = RTh, giving Pmax = VTh2/4RTh at only 50% efficiency; matching suits signal circuits, not power supply.

Common mistakes in Electrical Circuits and Electronics

  • Confusing power (W) with energy (J or kWh). Electricity bills charge for energy, in kilowatt-hours, not for power.
  • Using the voltage divider rule when a load is connected across the output. The load is in parallel with R2 and changes the result.
  • Mixing sign conventions within one loop equation. Choose a direction round the loop and apply rises and drops consistently.
  • Forgetting that a shared resistor carries the difference of two mesh currents. Write the voltage across it as R(I1 − I2) in mesh 1 and R(I2 − I1) in mesh 2.
  • Opening a voltage source when finding RTh. An ideal voltage source is replaced by a short circuit; a current source by an open circuit.
  • Thinking maximum power transfer means maximum efficiency. At the matched point, half the power is lost inside the source.

Lessons in this school

  1. Charge, current, voltage and power
    Every circuit calculation rests on four quantities: charge, current, voltage and power.
  2. Ohm's law and resistor networks
    Ohm's law links voltage, current and resistance, and lets complicated networks of resistors be reduced to a single equivalent.
  3. Kirchhoff's laws
    Kirchhoff's two laws express conservation of charge and of energy in a circuit and are the basis of all circuit analysis.
  4. Nodal and mesh analysis
    Nodal and mesh analysis turn Kirchhoff's laws into a systematic method that always gives the right number of equations.
  5. Thévenin and Norton equivalents
    Any linear two-terminal network, however complex, behaves like a single source with a single resistance.
  6. Maximum power transfer
    A source delivers the most power to a load when the load is matched to the source's internal resistance.
  7. Capacitors and inductors
    Capacitors store energy in an electric field and inductors store it in a magnetic field.
  8. RC and RL transients
    When a circuit containing a capacitor or inductor is switched, voltages and currents change exponentially before settling.
  9. AC fundamentals
    Mains electricity and most signals are alternating.
  10. Impedance and AC circuit analysis
    In AC circuits, resistors, inductors and capacitors are represented by complex impedances, so the DC methods of circuit analysis carry over.
  11. AC power and power factor
    In AC circuits with reactance, not all the current does useful work.
  12. Three-phase systems
    Electricity is generated, transmitted and used in large amounts as three-phase AC.
  13. Diodes and rectifiers
    A diode lets current flow easily in one direction only.
  14. Transistors as switches and amplifiers
    Transistors are the building blocks of modern electronics, used as switches in digital and power circuits and as amplifiers in analogue circuits.
  15. Operational amplifiers
    The operational amplifier is a versatile integrated circuit used for amplification, filtering, signal conditioning and comparison.
  16. Digital logic
    Digital systems represent information with two states and process it with logic gates.
  17. Transformers
    Transformers change AC voltages efficiently and make long-distance electricity transmission possible.
  18. Electric motors and generators
    Electric machines convert between electrical and mechanical energy and account for a large share of electricity use.

Electrical Circuits and Electronics: questions students ask

In a current divider, which resistor carries more current?

The smaller resistance.

Which conservation law underlies KVL?

Conservation of energy.

How many equations does nodal analysis need for n nodes?

n − 1, one for each non-reference node.

How is VTh found?

As the open-circuit voltage at the network's terminals.
