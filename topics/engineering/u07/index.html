Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What happens to the viscosity of a liquid as it warms?

It decreases. From “Fluid properties” →

In plain English: A fluid deforms continuously under shear. Key idea: Fluids are characterised by density, specific weight, specific gravity, compressibility and surface tension; viscosity links shear stress to velocity gradient through τ = μ du/dy, with kinematic viscosity ν = μ ÷ ρ.

What gives the vertical force on a curved submerged surface?

The weight of fluid above it. From “Hydrostatic forces on surfaces” →

In plain English: Dams, tanks and gates must resist the force of the fluid they hold. Key idea: The resultant hydrostatic force on a plane surface is ρghcA, acting perpendicular to the surface at the centre of pressure, which lies below the centroid; curved surfaces are handled by horizontal and vertical components.

What is the velocity head?

V2 ÷ 2g, the kinetic energy per unit weight of fluid. From “The Bernoulli equation” →

In plain English: The Bernoulli equation relates pressure, velocity and height along a streamline. Key idea: The Bernoulli equation states that pressure, velocity and elevation heads sum to a constant along a streamline in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow; it explains Torricelli's result and the pressure drop where flow speeds up.

What does the Reynolds number represent?

The ratio of inertial to viscous forces. From “Viscous flow and the Reynolds number” →

In plain English: Flow can be smooth and layered or chaotic and mixing. Key idea: The Reynolds number, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, predicts whether pipe flow is laminar (below about 2300), transitional or turbulent (above about 4000), which controls velocity profile, friction and mixing.

What determines a pump's operating point?

The intersection of the pump characteristic curve with the system curve. From “Pumps and system curves” →

In plain English: Pumps supply the energy to move fluid through systems. Key idea: A pump's operating point is where its falling head–flow curve meets the rising system curve; shaft power is ρgQH ÷ η, the affinity laws relate speed to flow, head and power, and NPSH available must exceed NPSH required to avoid cavitation.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Fluid Mechanics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the specific gravity of mercury? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    About 13.6. Applying the standard result: Mercury is 13.6 times denser than water.

  2. What does hydrostatic pressure depend on? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Depth and fluid density. Since p = ρgh, only depth and density matter, not shape.

  3. Where is the pressure taken when calculating the resultant force on a plane surface? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    At the centroid of the surface. Applying the standard result: F = pressure at centroid × area.

  4. Which fluid is non-Newtonian? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Toothpaste. Applying the standard result: Its viscosity depends on shear rate.

  5. A gauge reads 150 kPa where atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa. What is the absolute pressure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    251 kPa. Applying the standard result: Absolute = gauge + atmospheric.

  6. For a vertical rectangle with its top at the surface, where is the centre of pressure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Two-thirds of the depth down. Applying the standard result: It is at 2d ÷ 3.

  7. The gap in an oil film halves while the plate speed is unchanged. What happens to the shear stress? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It doubles. Applying the standard result: du/dy doubles, so τ doubles.

  8. Why is mercury used in manometers for large pressure differences? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its high density gives a manageable column height. Applying the standard result: Dense fluids give shorter columns.

  9. The water level behind a vertical gate doubles in depth, with the gate fully submerged from the surface. How does the force change? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It increases about fourfold. Applying the standard result: Both hc and A double.

Key takeaways

  • Fluids are characterised by density, specific weight, specific gravity, compressibility and surface tension; viscosity links shear stress to velocity gradient through τ = μ du/dy, with kinematic viscosity ν = μ ÷ ρ.
  • Static pressure increases with depth by ρgh, gauge pressure is measured from atmospheric, and manometers measure pressures and differences by balancing columns of fluid at a common level.
  • The resultant hydrostatic force on a plane surface is ρghcA, acting perpendicular to the surface at the centre of pressure, which lies below the centroid; curved surfaces are handled by horizontal and vertical components.
  • Buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced; floating bodies sink until they displace their own weight, and stability depends on a positive metacentric height, GM = BM − BG.
  • Volume flow rate is area times mean velocity; for steady incompressible flow, AV is constant along a pipe and flows balance at junctions, so narrowing a pipe raises the velocity.
  • The Bernoulli equation states that pressure, velocity and elevation heads sum to a constant along a streamline in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow; it explains Torricelli's result and the pressure drop where flow speeds up.

Common mistakes in Fluid Mechanics

  • Confusing dynamic and kinematic viscosity. Check the units: Pa s for μ, m2/s for ν.
  • Mixing absolute and gauge pressure in one calculation. Decide which you are using and convert consistently.
  • Placing the resultant force at the centroid. It acts lower, at the centre of pressure.
  • Assuming a floating body is unstable whenever G is above B. Floating bodies can be stable if the metacentre lies above G.
  • Scaling velocity with diameter rather than area. Velocity changes with the square of the diameter ratio.
  • Applying Bernoulli across a pump or through long pipes without losses. Add pump head and friction losses to the energy equation.

Lessons in this school

  1. Fluid properties
    A fluid deforms continuously under shear.
  2. Pressure and manometry
    Pressure in a fluid at rest increases with depth.
  3. Hydrostatic forces on surfaces
    Dams, tanks and gates must resist the force of the fluid they hold.
  4. Buoyancy and stability
    Ships, buoys and submarines rely on buoyancy and must remain stable.
  5. Conservation of mass and continuity
    Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a flow.
  6. The Bernoulli equation
    The Bernoulli equation relates pressure, velocity and height along a streamline.
  7. The momentum equation and jet forces
    Changing the momentum of a flowing fluid requires a force.
  8. Flow measurement
    Engineers need to measure flow in pipes, channels and air streams.
  9. Viscous flow and the Reynolds number
    Flow can be smooth and layered or chaotic and mixing.
  10. Pipe flow and friction losses
    Friction in pipes causes a loss of head that pumps must overcome.
  11. Minor losses and pipe systems
    Bends, valves, entrances and exits add further losses to a pipe system.
  12. Pumps and system curves
    Pumps supply the energy to move fluid through systems.
  13. Boundary layers, drag and lift
    Bodies moving through fluids experience drag and, if shaped suitably, lift.
  14. Dimensional analysis and similitude
    Dimensional analysis reduces the number of variables in a problem and allows small-scale models to predict full-scale behaviour.

Fluid Mechanics: questions students ask

What defines a fluid?

A substance that deforms continuously under shear stress, however small.

Why does a dam become thicker towards its base?

Water pressure increases with depth.

Why is the centre of pressure below the centroid?

Pressure increases with depth, so more force acts on the lower part.

Why does a steel ship float?

Its hull displaces a volume of water whose weight equals the ship's weight.
