Key terms and formulas at a glance

Resultant
The single force with the same effect as a system of forces acting at a point
Moment
The turning effect of a force, equal to force times perpendicular distance from the point
Equivalent system
A system with the same resultant force and resultant moment about every point
Free-body diagram
A sketch of a body isolated from its surroundings showing all external forces and moments on it
Particle
A body whose size is negligible for the analysis, so all forces act through one point
Simply supported beam
A beam with a pin at one end and a roller at the other
Ball-and-socket joint
A support preventing translation in all three directions but allowing rotation, with three force reactions
Statically determinate
Able to be fully analysed using equilibrium equations alone
Truss
A structure of straight members joined at their ends to form triangles
Method of sections
Finding member forces by cutting a truss and applying equilibrium to one part
Frame
A stationary structure with at least one multi-force member
Distributed load
A load spread over a length or area, described by its intensity

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the components of a force F at angle θ to the x-axis?

Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ. From “Forces as vectors” →

In plain English: A force has size and direction, so it is a vector. Key idea: Resolve every force into components, add the components, and rebuild the resultant; in 3D write forces as Cartesian vectors using unit vectors along their lines of action.

How many reactions does a pin support provide in 2D?

Two force components; no moment. From “Free-body diagrams” →

In plain English: A free-body diagram isolates a body and shows every external force and moment acting on it. Key idea: A free-body diagram shows the isolated body with every external force and moment, each support replaced by exactly the reactions it can provide.

How many reactions does a ball-and-socket joint provide?

Three force components and no moments. From “Equilibrium in three dimensions” →

In plain English: In three dimensions a rigid body has six equilibrium equations: three for forces and three for moments. Key idea: A 3D rigid body needs six equations; taking moments about axes that pass through several unknown forces isolates one unknown at a time.

Which truss members mainly carry the shear force?

The web members (diagonals and verticals). From “Plane trusses: the method of sections” →

In plain English: The method of sections finds the forces in particular truss members directly, by cutting the truss and applying equilibrium to one part. Key idea: Cut through at most three unknown members and take moments where two of them meet; chord forces equal the bending moment divided by the truss depth.

How is a hole treated in a composite centroid calculation?

As a negative area. From “Centroids and centres of gravity” →

In plain English: The centre of gravity is where a body's weight acts; the centroid is the geometric centre of a shape. Key idea: The centroid of a composite area is the area-weighted average of its parts' centroids; holes count as negative areas, and symmetry fixes one coordinate for free.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Statics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A 100 N force acts at 90° to the x-axis. What is its x-component? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    0 N. cos 90° = 0, so a force along the y-axis has no x-component.

  2. What unit is used for moments in SI? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    N m. Moment is force times distance, so newton-metres.

  3. Two systems are equivalent if they have the same resultant force and the same what? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Resultant moment about every point. External effects on a rigid body depend only on resultant force and resultant moment.

  4. Forces of 6 kN along +x and 8 kN along −y act at a point. What is the resultant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    10 kN at 53.1° below the x-axis. R = √(62 + 82) = 10 kN; tan-1(8/6) = 53.1°, below the axis because the y-component is negative.

  5. Two 50 N forces form a couple, 0.4 m apart. What is the moment of the couple about a point 2 m away? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    20 N m. A couple's moment is Fd = 50 × 0.4 = 20 N m about any point.

  6. Downward loads of 3 kN at x = 1 m and 5 kN at x = 4 m act on a beam. Where does the resultant act? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    x = 2.875 m. Taking moments about x = 0: x̄ = (3 × 1 + 5 × 4)/8 = 23/8 = 2.875 m.

  7. A cable runs from A(0, 0, 0) to B(2, 3, 6) m and carries 700 N. What is the force on A? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    200i + 300j + 600k N. Length AB = √(4 + 9 + 36) = 7 m; unit vector (2/7, 3/7, 6/7); F = 700 × that = (200, 300, 600) N.

  8. A force F = 30i + 40j N acts at r = 2i + 1j m from O. What is the moment about O? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    50 N m anticlockwise. M = xFy − yFx = 2 × 40 − 1 × 30 = 80 − 30 = 50 N m, positive so anticlockwise.

  9. A system reduces to FR = 10 kN upward at O and MRO = 25 kN m anticlockwise. Where does the single equivalent force act? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    2.5 m to the right of O. An upward force at x to the right of O has moment +10x about O (anticlockwise). Setting 10x = 25 gives x = 2.5 m to the right; to the left it would turn the other way.

Key takeaways

  • Resolve every force into components, add the components, and rebuild the resultant; in 3D write forces as Cartesian vectors using unit vectors along their lines of action.
  • Moment = force × perpendicular distance; Varignon's theorem lets you sum the moments of components; a couple is a pure moment, the same about any point.
  • A force can be moved to any point by adding a couple equal to its moment about that point; any 2D system reduces to one force or one couple.
  • A free-body diagram shows the isolated body with every external force and moment, each support replaced by exactly the reactions it can provide.
  • For a particle in equilibrium the components of all forces sum to zero; shallow cables need large tensions to support a vertical load.
  • In 2D, ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0 give three equations; taking moments about a point on two unknown lines of action makes the solution quick.

Common mistakes in Statics

  • Using the wrong trigonometric function after measuring the angle from the y-axis. Always check which axis θ is measured from: sin and cos swap if it is measured from the vertical.
  • Multiplying force by the distance to the point of application rather than the perpendicular distance to the line of action. Only the perpendicular distance gives the moment.
  • Forgetting the couple when moving a force. Sliding a force along its own line of action changes nothing, but moving it sideways changes its moment, which must be added back as a couple.
  • Including internal forces or forces the body exerts on something else. A free-body diagram shows only forces acting on the body from outside.
  • Assuming each of two cables carries half the weight. That is only true if both are vertical; inclined cables carry more, and the tension grows rapidly as they flatten.
  • Forgetting the moment of a load when taking moments about a point not on its line of action, or adding forces and moments together in one equation. Each equation must contain only forces or only moments.

Lessons in this school

  1. Forces as vectors
    A force has size and direction, so it is a vector.
  2. Moments and couples
    A moment is the turning effect of a force about a point or axis.
  3. Equivalent force systems
    Any system of forces and couples on a rigid body can be replaced by a simpler equivalent system.
  4. Free-body diagrams
    A free-body diagram isolates a body and shows every external force and moment acting on it.
  5. Equilibrium of a particle
    A particle is in equilibrium when the resultant force on it is zero.
  6. Equilibrium of a rigid body in two dimensions
    A rigid body is in equilibrium when both the resultant force and the resultant moment are zero.
  7. Equilibrium in three dimensions
    In three dimensions a rigid body has six equilibrium equations: three for forces and three for moments.
  8. Statical determinacy and stability
    A structure is statically determinate if equilibrium alone can find all its reactions and internal forces.
  9. Plane trusses: the method of joints
    A truss is a framework of straight members joined at their ends.
  10. Plane trusses: the method of sections
    The method of sections finds the forces in particular truss members directly, by cutting the truss and applying equilibrium to one part.
  11. Frames and machines
    Frames and machines are structures with at least one multi-force member, so their members carry bending as well as axial force.
  12. Distributed loads
    Many loads are spread along a beam rather than acting at a point: self-weight, floor loads, snow, water pressure.
  13. Centroids and centres of gravity
    The centre of gravity is where a body's weight acts; the centroid is the geometric centre of a shape.
  14. Second moment of area
    The second moment of area measures how a cross-section's area is distributed about an axis, and so how well it resists bending.
  15. Dry friction
    Dry friction resists sliding between surfaces in contact.
  16. Shear force and bending moment diagrams
    Shear force and bending moment diagrams show how internal forces vary along a beam, and so where it is most highly stressed.

Statics: questions students ask

What is a couple?

Two equal and opposite parallel forces, not collinear, producing a pure moment.

What must be added when a force is moved parallel to itself to a new point?

A couple equal to the moment of the force about the new point.

Why does a nearly horizontal cable carry a large tension?

Only its small vertical component supports the load, so the tension must be large.

Why take moments about a support with two unknown reactions?

Both unknowns pass through that point, so they drop out of the moment equation.
