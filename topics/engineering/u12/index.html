Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a work breakdown structure?

A hierarchy that splits a project deliverable into manageable work packages. From “The project lifecycle and planning” →

In plain English: Engineering work is usually delivered as projects with a defined goal, budget and timescale. Key idea: Projects follow a lifecycle with gate reviews, are planned with SMART objectives and a work breakdown structure, and are controlled by balancing scope, time, cost and quality through change control.

What does a positive NPV indicate?

The project adds value at the chosen discount rate. From “Engineering economics” →

In plain English: Engineers must show that projects are worth their cost. Key idea: Money has a time value, so future cash flows are discounted with P = F / (1 + i)n; NPV sums discounted cash flows and is the most robust measure, while payback is simpler but ignores timing and later cash flows.

Which section interprets the results?

The discussion. From “Technical report writing” →

In plain English: Technical reports communicate engineering work so that others can understand, check and act on it. Key idea: A technical report follows a clear structure from summary to appendices, presents numbered and captioned figures and tables with SI units and consistent references, and uses concise, objective prose.

What is the standard uncertainty of the mean of n readings?

The standard deviation divided by the square root of n. From “Laboratory practice and experimental design” →

In plain English: Laboratory work tests ideas and generates data that others rely on. Key idea: Safe laboratory work needs risk and COSHH assessments, calibrated instruments and a contemporaneous notebook; fair experiments control variables, replicate and randomise, and results are reported with uncertainty following the GUM.

What are the four stages of Kolb's learning cycle?

Concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation. From “Continuing professional development” →

In plain English: Engineering knowledge changes quickly, so registered engineers must keep learning throughout their careers. Key idea: CPD is lifelong, planned learning required of registered engineers; it is planned against needs, recorded and reflected on, and reflective cycles such as Kolb's turn experience into learning.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Projects and Professional Practice

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these best describes a project? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A temporary undertaking to deliver a unique result. Projects are temporary and unique, unlike ongoing operations.

  2. What does a Gantt chart show? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Activities as bars against a time axis. Gantt charts display each activity's start, duration and finish against time.

  3. What does discounting do? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Converts future cash flows into present values. Discounting finds what future money is worth today.

  4. A client adds three features without extending the deadline or budget. What is the most likely result? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pressure on quality, or overruns in time or cost. Scope, time and cost are linked, so extra scope must be paid for somewhere.

  5. A non-critical activity with 4 days of float is delayed by 2 days. What happens to the project end date? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It is unchanged. The delay is within the float, so the critical path and end date are not affected.

  6. What is the main weakness of the simple payback period? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It ignores cash flows after payback and their timing. Payback counts only the years to recover cost and does not discount.

  7. Which is the best example of a SMART objective for a design project? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reduce bracket mass by 15% by the end of March. It is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

  8. A manager adds staff to shorten a non-critical activity by 3 days. What is the effect on project duration? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    No reduction, because the critical path is unchanged. Only shortening critical activities can shorten the project.

  9. Raising the discount rate from 3.5% to 10% for a wind farm with long-lived savings will most likely (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    lower its NPV. A higher rate shrinks the present value of distant benefits, reducing NPV.

Key takeaways

  • Projects follow a lifecycle with gate reviews, are planned with SMART objectives and a work breakdown structure, and are controlled by balancing scope, time, cost and quality through change control.
  • Gantt charts show activities against time; the critical path method finds the longest path through the network, which sets the project duration, and float shows how far non-critical activities can slip.
  • Money has a time value, so future cash flows are discounted with P = F / (1 + i)^n; NPV sums discounted cash flows and is the most robust measure, while payback is simpler but ignores timing and later cash flows.
  • Project risks are uncertain events that could affect objectives, both threats and opportunities; they are identified, analysed with probability, impact and EMV, recorded in a risk register with owners, and treated by avoid, reduce, transfer or accept.
  • Teams develop through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning; balanced Belbin roles, clear responsibilities in a RACI matrix and psychological safety help teams perform.
  • A technical report follows a clear structure from summary to appendices, presents numbered and captioned figures and tables with SI units and consistent references, and uses concise, objective prose.

Common mistakes in Projects and Professional Practice

  • Starting work before scope is agreed. Unclear scope leads to scope creep, rework and overruns; define and baseline scope first.
  • Thinking the critical path is the path with the most activities. It is the path with the longest total duration.
  • Adding future savings without discounting. Money received in later years is worth less today and must be discounted to its present value.
  • Confusing risks with issues. A risk may happen; an issue has already happened and needs action now.
  • Assuming conflict means a team is failing. Storming is a normal stage, and well-managed disagreement often improves the design.
  • Putting interpretation in the results section. Results should present the data; explanation and comparison with theory belong in the discussion.

Lessons in this school

  1. The project lifecycle and planning
    Engineering work is usually delivered as projects with a defined goal, budget and timescale.
  2. Scheduling: Gantt charts and the critical path
    A schedule shows when each activity happens and which activities control the finish date.
  3. Engineering economics
    Engineers must show that projects are worth their cost.
  4. Risk management in projects
    Every project faces uncertain events that could harm or help its objectives.
  5. Teamwork and roles
    Engineering is almost always a team activity.
  6. Technical report writing
    Technical reports communicate engineering work so that others can understand, check and act on it.
  7. Presentations and technical communication
    Engineers must explain their work to clients, colleagues and the public.
  8. Searching literature and standards
    Engineers need reliable information from journals, books, standards and data.
  9. Laboratory practice and experimental design
    Laboratory work tests ideas and generates data that others rely on.
  10. Professional registration: EngTech, IEng and CEng
    Professional registration shows that an engineer has met recognised standards of competence and commitment.
  11. The UK-SPEC competences
    UK-SPEC describes the competence and commitment expected of registered engineers and technicians.
  12. Continuing professional development
    Engineering knowledge changes quickly, so registered engineers must keep learning throughout their careers.
  13. Intellectual property and contracts
    Engineers create valuable ideas and work under legal agreements.
  14. The individual project
    Most engineering degrees end with an individual project in which students investigate a problem independently.
