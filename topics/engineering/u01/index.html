Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Express the pascal in SI base units.

kg m-1 s-2 (one newton per square metre). From “SI units and dimensional analysis” →

In plain English: Every engineering number carries a unit, and every valid equation balances its dimensions. Key idea: A dimensionally wrong equation is certainly wrong; a dimensionally right one may still be wrong, because dimensionless constants and missing variables are invisible to the check.

What straight-line plot reveals the exponent n in y = axn?

log y against log x: the gradient is n. From “Functions and graphs in engineering” →

In plain English: Engineers describe how one quantity depends on another with functions and read behaviour off graphs. Key idea: Ask what the gradient and the area of a graph mean physically: that is usually where the engineering answer is.

How is the moment of a force about a point written as a vector?

M = r × F, with r from the point to the line of action of F. From “Vectors and their products” →

In plain English: Forces, velocities, moments and fields all have direction as well as size. Key idea: Dot product: how much of one vector lies along another (a scalar). Cross product: the turning effect, perpendicular to both (a vector).

What is ∂(x2y3)/∂y?

3x2y2. From “Partial differentiation and error propagation” →

In plain English: Most engineering quantities depend on several variables. Key idea: The exponent of a variable is its error multiplier: identify the variable with the largest exponent times relative error, and control that one.

What is the critical damping coefficient of a mass–spring system?

cc = 2√(km). From “Second-order differential equations” →

In plain English: Systems with two energy stores, such as a mass and a spring or an inductor and a capacitor, obey second-order differential equations. Key idea: Natural frequency sets how fast a second-order system responds; damping ratio sets whether it overshoots and rings (ζ < 1) or creeps (ζ > 1).

Quick quiz: test yourself on Engineering Mathematics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is the pascal written in SI base units? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    kg m-1 s-2. A pascal is a newton (kg m s-2) per square metre, giving kg m-1 s-2. The distractors are the newton, joule and watt.

  2. A micrometer reads 0.02 mm when its jaws are fully closed. What kind of error does this cause? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A systematic (zero) error. A zero offset shifts every reading by the same amount, so it is systematic; repeating and averaging would reproduce it every time.

  3. Which activity is validation of a model? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Comparing its predictions with measurements from a physical test. Validation compares the model with reality. Mesh refinement and checking the solver are verification; unit checks are part of verifying inputs.

  4. Which of these equations of motion is dimensionally consistent (s distance, u and v speeds, a acceleration, t time)? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    v2 = u2 + 2as. Each term of v2 = u2 + 2as has dimension L2 T-2. In s = ut + ½at3 the last term is L T, and in v = u + as the last term is L2 T-2, so neither balances.

  5. Ten repeated force readings have a standard deviation of 0.50 N. What is the standard uncertainty of their mean? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    0.16 N. u = s/√n = 0.50/√10 = 0.16 N. Dividing by n (0.05 N) understates it; multiplying by √10 (1.58 N) inverts the formula.

  6. A motor must raise a 500 kg load at a steady 2.0 m s-1. Which assumption most affects whether the calculated power of 9.8 kW is enough in service? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Neglecting the extra power needed to accelerate the load from rest. The steady-speed model ignores acceleration, which needs extra force and power at start-up; the particle idealisation and the value of g change little, and air mass is negligible.

  7. A student derives a formula for the deflection of a beam and finds it is dimensionally homogeneous. What can she conclude? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Only that it may be correct: its numerical constant and any missing dimensionless group are untested. Dimensional homogeneity is necessary, not sufficient. When more variables are involved than dimensions, exponents are not fully fixed either, and a homogeneous equation holds in any consistent unit system.

  8. A calculation gives a resistance of 6.0247 Ω with a standard uncertainty of 0.078 Ω. How should it be reported? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    6.02 ± 0.08 Ω. Round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures (0.08 Ω), then round the value to the same decimal place (6.02 Ω). Keeping all digits implies false precision; dropping to 6 discards real information.

  9. A finite element model of a bracket gives a peak stress well below yield, but the bracket cracks in service. Which is the most likely shortcoming of the model? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It omitted a mechanism such as fatigue under cyclic loading or a stress concentration the mesh did not resolve. Cracking below yield in service usually means a missing mechanism (fatigue, a sharp corner, a load case not modelled). Blaming the solver skips the more common failure: correct maths on the wrong model.

Key takeaways

  • A dimensionally wrong equation is certainly wrong; a dimensionally right one may still be wrong, because dimensionless constants and missing variables are invisible to the check.
  • Averaging reduces random error but never systematic error; only calibration, a different method or a correction removes a bias.
  • A model is only as good as its assumptions: state them, check them against the real system, and know which ones the answer is sensitive to.
  • Ask what the gradient and the area of a graph mean physically: that is usually where the engineering answer is.
  • An exponential model applies when the rate of change is proportional to the current value; its time constant summarises the whole response.
  • Sinusoids of the same frequency always add to another sinusoid of that frequency, which is why AC circuits can be analysed with phasors.

Common mistakes in Engineering Mathematics

  • Mixing unit systems. In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter after ground software reported thruster impulse in pound-force seconds where newton seconds were expected; the investigation board identified the units mismatch as the root cause.
  • Reporting every digit from the calculator. A result of 6.0247 Ω with an uncertainty of 0.078 Ω should be written 6.02 ± 0.08 Ω: the extra digits imply precision that does not exist.
  • Trusting a numerical model because it produces detailed, colourful output. A finite element result with the wrong boundary conditions is precisely wrong; check reactions balance the loads and compare with a hand calculation.
  • Fitting a straight line to curved data because a spreadsheet will draw one. Plot the residuals: a systematic curve in them means the model is wrong, however high R2 is.
  • Using 20 log for power ratios. Power ratios use 10 log10; 20 log10 is for voltage or current ratios, because power is proportional to their square.
  • Calculators in degree mode. Evaluating sin(ωt) with ωt in radians while the calculator is in degrees gives wrong answers with no warning.

Lessons in this school

  1. SI units and dimensional analysis
    Every engineering number carries a unit, and every valid equation balances its dimensions.
  2. Uncertainty, significant figures and estimation
    No measurement is exact, and an engineering result is only useful with an honest statement of how uncertain it is.
  3. Engineering models and assumptions
    Engineers predict how things will behave by building models: simplified representations that can be calculated.
  4. Functions and graphs in engineering
    Engineers describe how one quantity depends on another with functions and read behaviour off graphs.
  5. Exponentials and logarithms in engineering
    Whenever a rate of change is proportional to the amount present, the result is exponential growth or decay.
  6. Trigonometry and sinusoids
    Trigonometry resolves forces into components and describes everything that oscillates: alternating current, vibration and rotating machinery.
  7. Complex numbers and phasors
    Complex numbers make AC circuits, vibrations and control systems solvable with algebra instead of trigonometry.
  8. Vectors and their products
    Forces, velocities, moments and fields all have direction as well as size.
  9. Matrices and simultaneous equations
    Engineering analysis often produces many linear equations at once: forces at the joints of a truss, currents in a circuit, displacements in a finite element model.
  10. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors
    An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches, and its eigenvalue is the stretch factor.
  11. Differentiation and rates of change
    The derivative measures how fast one quantity changes with another.
  12. Partial differentiation and error propagation
    Most engineering quantities depend on several variables.
  13. Integration in engineering
    Integration adds up a quantity that varies continuously: work from a varying force, displacement from velocity, charge from current, and the centroids and second moments that describe a cross-section.
  14. Taylor series and linearisation
    Near any point, a smooth function looks like a polynomial.
  15. First-order differential equations
    A first-order differential equation relates a quantity to its own rate of change.
  16. Second-order differential equations
    Systems with two energy stores, such as a mass and a spring or an inductor and a capacitor, obey second-order differential equations.
  17. Numerical methods
    Most real engineering equations have no neat closed-form answer, so they are solved numerically.
  18. Engineering statistics
    Materials, measurements and manufactured parts all vary.
  19. Probability and reliability
    Reliability engineering asks how likely a component or system is to keep working.

Engineering Mathematics: questions students ask

What can a dimensional check never confirm?

The value of dimensionless constants, or that no variable is missing: a homogeneous equation may still be wrong.

What is the difference between accuracy and precision?

Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is how closely repeated readings agree with each other.

What is the difference between verification and validation?

Verification checks the equations are solved correctly; validation checks they are the right equations by comparison with reality.

What does the area under a force–extension graph represent?

The work done (energy stored, if elastic) in producing the extension.
