Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is a steam turbine analysed as an open system?

Steam flows continuously in and out across its boundary. From “Systems, properties and states” →

In plain English: Thermodynamics begins by drawing a boundary around the thing being studied. Key idea: Choosing the system boundary carefully is the first step in every thermodynamic analysis; two independent intensive properties then fix the state of a simple substance, and processes and cycles describe how that state changes.

Why is cp larger than cv?

At constant pressure the gas also does expansion work as it is heated. From “Ideal gases” →

In plain English: Air and combustion gases behave very nearly as ideal gases at the pressures used in engines. Key idea: Ideal gases obey pV = mRT with absolute pressure and temperature; their internal energy and enthalpy depend only on temperature, with cp − cv = R and γ about 1.4 for air.

What property is constant across a throttling valve?

Specific enthalpy. From “Nozzles, turbines, compressors and heat exchangers” →

In plain English: The steady-flow energy equation simplifies neatly for each common device. Key idea: The SFEE reduces to a simple form for each device: enthalpy drop gives turbine power or nozzle velocity, enthalpy rise gives compressor power, enthalpy is constant across a throttle, and heat exchangers balance hot and cold streams.

What does the area under a reversible line on a T–s diagram represent?

The heat transferred per kilogram. From “Entropy” →

In plain English: Entropy turns the second law into numbers. Key idea: Entropy is a property with dS = δQrev ÷ T; the entropy of an isolated system never decreases, and entropy generation measures how far a real process falls short of the reversible ideal.

Why is component efficiency critical in gas turbines?

The compressor absorbs a large fraction of the turbine work. From “Gas power cycles: Otto, Diesel and Brayton” →

In plain English: Petrol engines, diesel engines and gas turbines are modelled with air-standard cycles. Key idea: The Otto, Diesel and Brayton cycles model spark-ignition engines, compression-ignition engines and gas turbines; efficiency rises with compression or pressure ratio, and the gas turbine's large compressor work makes component efficiency critical.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Thermodynamics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is an intensive property? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Temperature. Temperature does not depend on the amount of matter.

  2. What drives heat transfer across a boundary? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A temperature difference. Heat flows because of a temperature difference.

  3. What does a dryness fraction of 0.8 mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    80% of the mass is vapour. Applying the standard result: Quality is the vapour mass fraction.

  4. A gauge reads 300 kPa where atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa. Which pressure goes into thermodynamic equations? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    400 kPa absolute. Absolute pressure is gauge plus atmospheric pressure.

  5. In this school's sign convention, which is positive? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Heat added to the system and work done by it. Applying the standard result: Q in and W out are both positive.

  6. Water at 1 bar is at 150 °C. What state is it in? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Superheated vapour. 150 °C is above the 99.6 °C saturation temperature.

  7. Why can a quasi-equilibrium process be drawn as a line on a p–V diagram? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Each point along it is an equilibrium state with defined properties. Non-equilibrium states have no single values of p and V.

  8. Two processes link the same two states, one with more area under its p–V curve. What follows? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It involves more boundary work. Work is a path function equal to the area.

  9. Why are pressure and temperature not enough to fix the state of wet steam? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are linked during phase change and not independent. In the two-phase region a second independent property such as x is needed.

Key takeaways

  • Choosing the system boundary carefully is the first step in every thermodynamic analysis; two independent intensive properties then fix the state of a simple substance, and processes and cycles describe how that state changes.
  • Energy crosses a boundary as heat, driven by temperature difference, or as work; both depend on the path, and boundary work equals the area under the p–V curve of the process.
  • For a pure substance, pressure and temperature are linked during boiling, so wet steam needs the dryness fraction to fix its state; steam tables give saturated and superheated properties, read directly or by linear interpolation.
  • Ideal gases obey pV = mRT with absolute pressure and temperature; their internal energy and enthalpy depend only on temperature, with cp − cv = R and γ about 1.4 for air.
  • For a closed system Q − W = ΔU; constant-volume, constant-pressure, isothermal and polytropic processes are special cases, and around any cycle the net heat in equals the net work out.
  • In steady flow the mass flow in equals the mass flow out, and the steady-flow energy equation balances heat and shaft work against changes in enthalpy, kinetic and potential energy, with enthalpy including the flow work.

Common mistakes in Thermodynamics

  • Treating absolute and gauge pressure as the same. Thermodynamic equations use absolute pressure: absolute = gauge + atmospheric (about 101.3 kPa at sea level).
  • Saying a hot object "has a lot of heat". It has high internal energy; heat is only the energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
  • Using temperature and pressure to fix the state of wet steam. In the two-phase region they are not independent; you need x or another property.
  • Using degrees Celsius in pV = mRT. Always convert to kelvin by adding 273.15.
  • Forgetting the sign of work. Work done on the gas during compression is negative in this convention, so it adds to ΔU.
  • Mixing units in the SFEE. Velocities give J/kg, while steam-table enthalpies are in kJ/kg.

Lessons in this school

  1. Systems, properties and states
    Thermodynamics begins by drawing a boundary around the thing being studied.
  2. Energy, work and heat
    Energy crosses a system boundary in only two ways: as work or as heat.
  3. Properties of pure substances and steam tables
    Water is the working fluid of most power stations, and it changes phase.
  4. Ideal gases
    Air and combustion gases behave very nearly as ideal gases at the pressures used in engines.
  5. The first law for closed systems
    Energy is conserved. This lesson applies the first law to closed systems, Q − W = ΔU, through constant-volume, constant-pressure, isothermal and polytropic processes.
  6. The first law for steady-flow systems
    Turbines, pumps and boilers run with fluid flowing steadily through them.
  7. Nozzles, turbines, compressors and heat exchangers
    The steady-flow energy equation simplifies neatly for each common device.
  8. The second law and heat engines
    The first law says energy is conserved; the second says which way processes go and limits how much heat can become work.
  9. The Carnot cycle
    The Carnot cycle sets the maximum efficiency any heat engine can reach between two temperatures.
  10. Entropy
    Entropy turns the second law into numbers.
  11. Isentropic efficiency
    Real turbines and compressors fall short of the ideal reversible process.
  12. The Rankine cycle
    Most of the world's electricity from coal, nuclear, biomass and concentrated solar plants comes from the Rankine steam cycle.
  13. Gas power cycles: Otto, Diesel and Brayton
    Petrol engines, diesel engines and gas turbines are modelled with air-standard cycles.
  14. Refrigeration and heat pumps
    Refrigerators and heat pumps use work to move heat from cold to hot.
  15. Heat transfer by conduction
    Thermodynamics says how much heat flows; heat transfer says how fast.
  16. Convection and radiation
    Heat also leaves surfaces by convection to fluids and by radiation.

Thermodynamics: questions students ask

Why is heat not a property of a system?

Its amount depends on the process path, not only on the end states.

What is the dryness fraction of saturated vapour?

1, because all the mass is vapour.

Why is ΔU zero for an ideal gas in an isothermal process?

Its internal energy depends only on temperature, which stays constant.

Why is enthalpy used for flowing fluids?

It combines internal energy with the flow work needed to push fluid across the boundary.
