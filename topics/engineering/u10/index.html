Key terms and formulas at a glance

Embodiment design
Developing a concept into a definitive layout with materials and dimensions
Product design specification
A document listing all requirements a design must meet
Morphological chart
A table of alternative solutions for each sub-function, used to combine concepts
First-angle projection
A projection method in which the plan view is drawn below the front view
Tolerance
The permitted variation between the upper and lower limits of a dimension
Draft angle
A slight taper on moulded or cast features to allow removal from the mould
Factor of safety
The ratio of a component's capacity to the demand placed on it
Preload
Tension applied to a bolt during tightening that clamps the joint
Basic rating life L10
The life in millions of revolutions that 90% of identical bearings reach
Gear ratio
The ratio of driven to driver teeth, or input to output speed
Investment casting
Casting using a wax pattern coated in ceramic, which is melted out to leave a mould
Cutting speed
The relative surface speed between tool and workpiece, in m/min

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the difference between verification and validation?

Verification checks the design meets its specification; validation checks it meets the user's real need. From “The engineering design process” →

In plain English: Engineering design turns a need into a product or system that can be made and used safely. Key idea: Design moves from problem definition to concept, embodiment and detail design, then testing; it is iterative and controlled by reviews, and early decisions commit most of the life-cycle cost.

In first-angle projection, where is the plan view drawn?

Below the front view. From “Engineering drawing and CAD” →

In plain English: Engineering drawings and CAD models communicate exactly what is to be made. Key idea: Drawings use orthographic views in first- or third-angle projection, with standard line types, sections and dimensions to BS 8888; 3D parametric CAD captures design intent and links to analysis and manufacture.

What is the difference between ultimate and serviceability limit states?

Ultimate limit states involve collapse or rupture; serviceability limit states involve problems such as excessive deflection that affect use. From “Factors of safety and design codes” →

In plain English: Engineers cannot know loads and strengths exactly, so they design with margins. Key idea: A factor of safety is capacity divided by demand and covers uncertainty; limit state design applies partial factors to loads and strengths at ultimate and serviceability limits; codes capture experience and support legal duties but do not replace judgement.

Why do helical gears run more quietly than spur gears?

Their angled teeth engage gradually rather than all at once. From “Gears and power transmission” →

In plain English: Gears, belts and chains transmit power between shafts while changing speed and torque. Key idea: Spur, helical, bevel, worm and epicyclic gears suit different shaft layouts; ratio equals driven over driver teeth, reducing speed and increasing torque less losses; belts are cheap and quiet, chains positive, and gears compact and efficient.

Why are printed parts often weaker in the build direction?

Bonding between layers is weaker than within layers, causing anisotropy. From “Additive manufacturing” →

In plain English: Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer from digital models. Key idea: Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer in categories such as material extrusion, vat photopolymerisation and powder bed fusion; it enables complex, consolidated designs but brings anisotropy, porosity and post-processing, and is most economical at low volumes or high complexity.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Design and Manufacturing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which stage produces full drawings with tolerances? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Detail design. Detail design produces the information needed to manufacture each part.

  2. Which is the best-written requirement? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The handle shall withstand a 500 N pull without permanent deformation. It is measurable and testable without prescribing a material.

  3. Which method breaks a product's overall function into sub-functions? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Functional decomposition. Decomposition makes it easier to find solutions for each part.

  4. A prototype fails a test and the team changes the concept. This illustrates (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Iteration in design. Design loops back to earlier stages when problems are found.

  5. What does the house of quality matrix show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    How customer needs relate to engineering characteristics. QFD translates the voice of the customer into technical targets.

  6. Why should no concept that fails a demand be selected, whatever its score? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Demands must be met, so a failure rules the concept out. A high score cannot compensate for missing an essential requirement.

  7. Why should engineers spend effort on early design decisions? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Early decisions commit most of the life-cycle cost, and changes later are expensive. Concept and material choices lock in most of the cost.

  8. Why should every requirement be traceable to a test? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    So the team can verify the design meets each requirement. Traceability ensures nothing is missed in verification.

  9. Two concepts score 3.9 and 3.8 in a weighted matrix. What is the best next step? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Test the sensitivity of the result and gather more evidence. Close scores are within the uncertainty of the method.

Key takeaways

  • Design moves from problem definition to concept, embodiment and detail design, then testing; it is iterative and controlled by reviews, and early decisions commit most of the life-cycle cost.
  • Requirements turn vague needs into measurable, solution-neutral statements; a PDS gathers them under headings such as performance, cost and safety, demands are distinguished from wishes, and QFD and traceability link needs to design and tests.
  • Concepts come from functional decomposition, morphological charts and creative methods; Pugh's datum method and weighted matrices help select between them, but results depend on subjective scores and need sensitivity checks and judgement.
  • Drawings use orthographic views in first- or third-angle projection, with standard line types, sections and dimensions to BS 8888; 3D parametric CAD captures design intent and links to analysis and manufacture.
  • Tolerances define acceptable variation and cost more as they tighten; ISO 286 hole-basis fits give clearance, transition or interference, calculated from the limits; GD&T controls form, orientation and position relative to datums.
  • Design for manufacture fits features to the process and volume; design for assembly reduces part count and makes parts easy to handle, orient and fit; DFMA cuts cost and improves quality but must balance repairability and tooling cost.

Common mistakes in Design and Manufacturing

  • Treating design as a straight line from idea to product. Real design loops back as testing reveals problems.
  • Writing a solution into the specification, such as "use an aluminium frame". This rules out better alternatives before concepts are explored.
  • Treating the highest weighted score as an objective answer. Weights and scores are judgements, and close results need further testing.
  • Misreading the projection method. Always check the projection symbol before interpreting views.
  • Specifying tight tolerances everywhere "to be safe". This raises cost without improving function.
  • Assuming fewer parts is always better. Combining parts can make products harder to repair or recycle and tooling more expensive.

Lessons in this school

  1. The engineering design process
    Engineering design turns a need into a product or system that can be made and used safely.
  2. Requirements and specifications
    A good specification defines what a design must achieve without dictating how.
  3. Concept generation and selection
    Good designs usually come from exploring many concepts before choosing one.
  4. Engineering drawing and CAD
    Engineering drawings and CAD models communicate exactly what is to be made.
  5. Tolerances and fits
    No part can be made to an exact size, so engineers specify how much variation is acceptable.
  6. Design for manufacture and assembly
    The way a part is designed largely determines how easily and cheaply it can be made and assembled.
  7. Factors of safety and design codes
    Engineers cannot know loads and strengths exactly, so they design with margins.
  8. Bolted and welded joints
    Most machines and structures are held together by bolts or welds.
  9. Shafts and bearings
    Shafts transmit power by rotating, and bearings support them.
  10. Gears and power transmission
    Gears, belts and chains transmit power between shafts while changing speed and torque.
  11. Casting and forming
    Many metal parts are shaped by casting molten metal or by deforming solid metal.
  12. Machining processes
    Machining removes material to produce accurate shapes and fine finishes.
  13. Additive manufacturing
    Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer from digital models.
  14. Quality, metrology and inspection
    Quality means meeting requirements consistently.
  15. Lean manufacturing and production systems
    Lean manufacturing aims to deliver value to customers with minimum waste.

Design and Manufacturing: questions students ask

What makes a requirement solution-neutral?

It states what must be achieved without saying how to achieve it.

What is a morphological chart?

A table listing alternative ways to achieve each sub-function, combined to create concepts.

What is tolerance stack-up?

The accumulation of individual part tolerances in an assembly.

What three questions identify parts that could be combined in DFA?

Does it move relative to other parts, must it be a different material, and must it be separate for assembly or service?
