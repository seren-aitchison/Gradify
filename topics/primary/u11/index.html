Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does living memory mean?

The time that people who are still alive can remember. From “Changes within living memory” →

In plain English: Some changes have happened so recently that people alive today remember them. Key idea: Living memory is the time people alive today can remember; we can learn about changes in toys, technology, shopping, school and travel by asking older people and looking at photographs and objects.

Who was Boudica?

Queen of the Iceni tribe, who led a rebellion against the Romans in AD 60–61. From “Roman Britain” →

In plain English: The Romans ruled much of Britain for nearly 400 years. Key idea: The Romans invaded in AD 43, faced resistance such as Boudica's revolt, built towns, roads, villas and Hadrian's Wall, and left evidence in buildings, objects and writing; Roman rule ended around AD 410.

Give two sources for a local history study.

Old maps, census records, photographs, buildings, war memorials or people's memories. From “A local history study” →

In plain English: History happened in your own area too. Key idea: A local history study starts with a question and uses old maps, census records, photographs, buildings, memorials and memories; comparing sources builds an account that links the area to national history.

What does the word democracy mean?

Rule by the people. From “Ancient Greece” →

In plain English: The ancient Greeks developed ideas that still shape our world, from democracy to the Olympic Games. Key idea: Ancient Greece was made of city-states such as democratic Athens and military Sparta; Greeks worshipped many gods, began the Olympic Games and developed philosophy, maths and theatre, influencing democracy, language and architecture today.

Who was known as "the Lady with the Lamp"?

Florence Nightingale. From “Significant people” →

In plain English: Some people's actions changed the world. Key idea: Significant people such as Florence Nightingale, Mary Seacole, Neil Armstrong, Rosa Parks and Emmeline Pankhurst changed things for many people; we compare their lives and think about who is remembered and why.

Quick quiz: test yourself on History

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a change within living memory? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Smartphones replacing house telephones. People alive today remember using telephones before smartphones.

  2. Which came first? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Stone Age. The Stone Age was the earliest of these periods.

  3. Which emperor began building a wall across northern England? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Hadrian. Emperor Hadrian ordered the wall to be built in AD 122.

  4. What is the best question to ask a grandparent about the past? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    What games did you play when you were my age?. Questions about their own childhood use their memories.

  5. What important change happened in the New Stone Age? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    People began farming and building permanent homes. Farming let people stay in one place and build permanent homes.

  6. Which change did the Romans bring to Britain? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Straight roads connecting towns. Roman roads linked their towns and forts.

  7. Why are photographs useful for learning about living memory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They show what people, places and objects looked like at the time. Photos are evidence of how people, places and things looked in the recent past.

  8. How do we know about life in the Iron Age? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Archaeologists study objects, buildings and burials. Without writing, archaeology is our main evidence.

  9. Why must we be careful when using Tacitus's writing about Britain? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    He wrote from a Roman point of view, so it may be one-sided. Every source has a point of view, and Tacitus was a Roman writer.

Key takeaways

  • Living memory is the time people alive today can remember; we can learn about changes in toys, technology, shopping, school and travel by asking older people and looking at photographs and objects.
  • Britain's prehistory runs from Stone Age hunter-gatherers to Neolithic farmers, then the Bronze Age and Iron Age, when metal tools, roundhouses and hillforts developed; we know about it through archaeology.
  • The Romans invaded in AD 43, faced resistance such as Boudica's revolt, built towns, roads, villas and Hadrian's Wall, and left evidence in buildings, objects and writing; Roman rule ended around AD 410.
  • Angles, Saxons and Jutes settled in England after the Romans left, forming kingdoms, while Scots came from Ireland; Anglo-Saxons farmed, worshipped pagan gods then became Christian, and left evidence such as Sutton Hoo and place names.
  • Vikings from Scandinavia raided Lindisfarne in 793, conquered much of England, were resisted by Alfred the Great, and settled the Danelaw; many were farmers and traders, as finds at Jorvik and -by place names show.
  • A local history study starts with a question and uses old maps, census records, photographs, buildings, memorials and memories; comparing sources builds an account that links the area to national history.

Common mistakes in History

  • Thinking "old" always means hundreds of years ago. Living memory can be just 50 or 60 years.
  • Thinking Stone Age people lived alongside dinosaurs. Dinosaurs died out about 66 million years before humans existed.
  • Thinking all Britons welcomed the Romans. Many resisted, while others adopted Roman ways.
  • Thinking the Anglo-Saxons were uncivilised because they followed the Romans. They produced fine metalwork, books, laws and kingdoms.
  • Thinking Vikings only raided and wore horned helmets. Many were farmers and traders, and there is no evidence they wore horned helmets in battle.
  • Thinking nothing historical happened locally. Every area has a history connected to wider events.

Lessons in this school

  1. Changes within living memory
    Some changes have happened so recently that people alive today remember them.
  2. The Stone Age to the Iron Age
    For thousands of years before the Romans, people in Britain lived in the Stone Age, the Bronze Age and the Iron Age.
  3. Roman Britain
    The Romans ruled much of Britain for nearly 400 years.
  4. Anglo-Saxons and Scots
    After the Romans left, Anglo-Saxons settled in England and Scots moved into what became Scotland.
  5. Vikings
    From the late 700s, Vikings from Scandinavia raided and then settled in Britain.
  6. A local history study
    History happened in your own area too.
  7. A theme beyond 1066
    Some topics are best studied across many centuries.
  8. Ancient Egypt
    Ancient Egypt was one of the earliest great civilisations, built along the River Nile.
  9. Ancient Greece
    The ancient Greeks developed ideas that still shape our world, from democracy to the Olympic Games.
  10. The Maya
    The Maya built a great civilisation in Central America, with cities, writing and astronomy.
  11. Events beyond living memory
    Some important events happened long before anyone alive today was born.
  12. Significant people
    Some people's actions changed the world.
  13. Sources and evidence
    Historians use sources as evidence to find out about the past.
  14. Timelines and chronology
    Chronology means putting events in order of time.
