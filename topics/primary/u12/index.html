Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the four countries of the United Kingdom.

England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. From “The United Kingdom” →

In plain English: The United Kingdom is made up of four countries. Key idea: The UK is England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, with capitals London, Edinburgh, Cardiff and Belfast; it is surrounded by the North Sea, English Channel, Irish Sea and Atlantic Ocean.

Which is the largest country in the world?

Russia. From “Countries and capitals” →

In plain English: There are about 195 countries in the world, each with its own capital and government. Key idea: Countries have their own governments and borders, and capitals are where governments are based; key European capitals include Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Rome and Dublin; in the Americas, Washington, D.C., Ottawa and Brasília show capitals are not always the largest cities.

Give two reasons why settlements grew in a particular place.

Water supply, defence, fertile farmland, transport routes or resources. From “Settlements” →

In plain English: A settlement is a place where people live. Key idea: Settlements range from hamlets to cities in the settlement hierarchy; they grew near water, defence, farmland, transport routes and resources; and they contain residential, commercial, industrial, recreational and transport land uses.

What is the highest mountain in the UK?

Ben Nevis in Scotland, about 1,345 m. From “Mountains” →

In plain English: Mountains are high, steep landforms found on every continent. Key idea: Fold mountains form when plates push together and volcanic mountains from eruptions; Everest is the world's highest and Ben Nevis the UK's highest; mountains are colder and wetter higher up and are used for farming, tourism and hydroelectric power.

What does a map key do?

It explains what each symbol on the map means. From “Maps and compasses” →

In plain English: Maps help us find our way and understand places. Key idea: Maps use symbols explained in a key; compass points include the four and eight points with north usually at the top; scale shows how map distances relate to real distances.

  3. What is the capital of France? (Foundation recall)

  4. Which sea lies between Great Britain and Ireland? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Irish Sea. The Irish Sea separates the two islands to the west of Great Britain.

  5. Which ocean lies between the UK and North America? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The Atlantic Ocean. Ships sailing west from the UK to Canada cross the Atlantic.

  6. What is the capital of Brazil? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Brasília. Brasília was built as a new capital in the 1950s and 1960s.

  7. Which statement about Great Britain is correct? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is the island containing England, Scotland and Wales. The UK includes Northern Ireland, which is not on the island of Great Britain.

  8. Why does a globe show the shapes of continents more accurately than a flat map? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is a round model like the Earth, so shapes are not stretched. Flattening a round surface onto paper stretches some areas.

  9. Which statement about capital cities is true? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A capital is not always the largest city in its country. Washington, D.C., Ottawa and Canberra are examples.

Key takeaways

  • The UK is England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, with capitals London, Edinburgh, Cardiff and Belfast; it is surrounded by the North Sea, English Channel, Irish Sea and Atlantic Ocean.
  • The seven continents are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe and Oceania; the five oceans are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic; the UK is in Europe in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Countries have their own governments and borders, and capitals are where governments are based; key European capitals include Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Rome and Dublin; in the Americas, Washington, D.C., Ottawa and Brasília show capitals are not always the largest cities.
  • Latitude measures north or south from the Equator (0°) and longitude east or west from the Prime Meridian at Greenwich (0°); the Earth's rotation creates time zones of about 15° each, measured from GMT.
  • Comparing places looks at physical and human geography, finding similarities and differences between a UK locality and a contrasting one, using evidence and avoiding stereotypes.
  • Settlements range from hamlets to cities in the settlement hierarchy; they grew near water, defence, farmland, transport routes and resources; and they contain residential, commercial, industrial, recreational and transport land uses.

Common mistakes in Geography

  • Thinking the UK and Great Britain mean the same thing. Great Britain is the island of England, Scotland and Wales; the UK also includes Northern Ireland.
  • Thinking flat maps show sizes exactly. Flat maps stretch some areas; a globe shows the true shapes.
  • Assuming a country's largest city is its capital. Washington, D.C., Ottawa, Canberra and Brasília are not their countries' biggest cities.
  • Confusing latitude and longitude. Latitude lines run across, like a ladder's rungs; longitude lines run from pole to pole.
  • Thinking a whole country is like one village or city. Every country contains many different kinds of places.
  • Thinking cities appeared fully formed. Most grew slowly from small settlements over hundreds of years.

Geography: questions students ask

What is human geography?

The study of features made or used by people, such as towns, roads and jobs.

What is the difference between an import and an export?

An import is brought into a country; an export is sold to another country.

What is the source of a river?

The place where it begins, often in hills or mountains.

What are tectonic plates?

Huge moving pieces of the Earth's crust.
