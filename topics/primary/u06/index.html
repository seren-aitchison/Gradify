Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a blurb?

The short description on the back of a book. From “Reading for pleasure” →

In plain English: Reading for pleasure means choosing to read because you enjoy it. Key idea: The more you read, the more words and ideas you meet, and the easier reading becomes: a few minutes every day matters more than a long time once a week.

What is context?

The words around a word that give clues to its meaning. From “Understanding new words” →

In plain English: Good readers meet new words all the time. Key idea: To work out a new word, look for clues in the sentences around it, break the word into parts, then check with a dictionary.

What does PEE stand for?

Point, Evidence, Explain. From “Making inferences” →

In plain English: Inference means working out something the writer hasn't said directly, using clues in the text. Key idea: An inference is a sensible conclusion based on clues in the text, and it must always be backed up with evidence.

What is a metaphor?

Saying something is something else, such as "Her smile was sunshine." From “The author's choice of language” →

In plain English: Writers choose their words carefully to create pictures, feelings and effects in the reader's mind. Key idea: To explain a word choice, name the word, say what it suggests, and describe the effect on the reader.

What is a verse?

A group of lines in a poem. From “Poetry” →

In plain English: Poems use rhythm, rhyme, sound and imagery to create feelings and pictures in very few words. Key idea: Poets choose sounds, rhythms and images carefully; ask how each choice makes you feel and what it helps you picture.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Reading and Comprehension

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Where would you usually find a book's blurb? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    On the back cover. The blurb is on the back cover and tells you what the book is about, to help you decide whether to read it.

  2. What does a full stop tell you to do when reading aloud? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Pause briefly. A full stop marks the end of a sentence, so you pause briefly before the next one.

  3. "The tiny kitten was so minuscule it fitted in my hand." What does "minuscule" mean? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Very small. The clues "tiny" and "fitted in my hand" show that minuscule means very small.

  4. In the five finger test, you find 2 words you don't know on a page. What does that suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The book is about right for you. Two or three unknown words means the book is "just right": challenging enough to learn from but not too hard.

  5. Which reason for choosing a book shows the best thinking? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    "I liked the first book in this series". Knowing you enjoyed the first book is a good reason to think you will enjoy the next one. Length and colour don't tell you whether you'll like it.

  6. Which is the best example of fluent reading? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Reading smoothly and changing your voice for speech. Fluency is accurate, smooth and expressive. Racing or reading word by word makes it harder to understand.

  7. Why might reading lots of stories help with science and history too? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    You meet more words and ideas you can use in other subjects. Wide reading builds vocabulary and background knowledge, which help you understand what you read in every subject.

  8. Jo reads "Help me," whispered the boy, in a loud, cheerful voice. What should she change? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Read "Help me" quietly, because the boy whispered. The word "whispered" tells you how the boy spoke, so a fluent reader would say "Help me" quietly and perhaps worried.

  9. "The knight drew his sword." What does "drew" mean in this context? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Pulled out. "Drew" can mean made a picture, but with a sword it means pulled the sword out. Context tells you which meaning fits.

Key takeaways

  • The more you read, the more words and ideas you meet, and the easier reading becomes: a few minutes every day matters more than a long time once a week.
  • Fluent reading is accurate, smooth and expressive, and it frees your mind to understand what you read.
  • To work out a new word, look for clues in the sentences around it, break the word into parts, then check with a dictionary.
  • For a retrieval question, underline the key words, scan the text to find them, read that part carefully and use the text to answer.
  • An inference is a sensible conclusion based on clues in the text, and it must always be backed up with evidence.
  • A good prediction is based on clues in the text, not just a wild guess, and it can change as you learn more.

Common mistakes in Reading and Comprehension

  • Thinking only long chapter books "count". Picture books, comics, poems and non-fiction all build reading skills and enjoyment.
  • Thinking fast means fluent. Racing through and ignoring punctuation makes it harder to understand; aim for the speed of natural talking.
  • Skipping every word you don't know. Skipping one or two is fine, but if you skip many, you lose the meaning, so stop and use the clues.
  • Answering from your own knowledge instead of the text. If the text says red squirrels eat fungi, write that, even if you think they eat something else.
  • Making a guess with no evidence. "Mia is hungry" can't be supported by the text, so it isn't a good inference.
  • Predicting something with no link to the text, such as "Ben goes to the moon." Base your prediction on the clues the writer has given.

Lessons in this school

  1. Reading for pleasure
    Reading for pleasure means choosing to read because you enjoy it.
  2. Reading fluently
    Fluent readers read accurately, at a good speed and with expression, so they can think about what the words mean.
  3. Understanding new words
    Good readers meet new words all the time.
  4. Retrieving information
    Retrieval means finding information that is written in a text.
  5. Making inferences
    Inference means working out something the writer hasn't said directly, using clues in the text.
  6. Predicting
    Predicting means using what you have read so far to suggest what might happen next.
  7. Summarising
    Summarising means giving the main ideas of a text in a few words, leaving out the details.
  8. The author's choice of language
    Writers choose their words carefully to create pictures, feelings and effects in the reader's mind.
  9. Fiction and non-fiction
    Fiction is made-up writing such as stories; non-fiction gives real information.
  10. Poetry
    Poems use rhythm, rhyme, sound and imagery to create feelings and pictures in very few words.
  11. Reading a play
    A play script is written to be performed.
  12. Answering comprehension questions
    Comprehension questions test how well you understand a text.
