Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a phoneme?

The smallest sound in a word, such as /m/ in "map". From “Letters and sounds” →

In plain English: Phonics teaches the link between the sounds we hear in words and the letters we write. Key idea: A phoneme is a sound; a grapheme is the letter or letters that write it. Reading means turning graphemes into phonemes.

What is segmenting?

Breaking a word into its sounds so you can spell it. From “Segmenting to spell” →

In plain English: Segmenting is the opposite of blending: you break a spoken word into its sounds so that you can write each one. Key idea: Segmenting means splitting a word into its sounds, then writing one grapheme for each sound.

Name a way to practise a tricky spelling.

Look, say, cover, write, check. From “Tricky words” →

In plain English: Some common words don't follow the usual phonics patterns, or use patterns children haven't learned yet. Key idea: Most of a tricky word still follows phonics; only one part is tricky, so learn that part.

What does the prefix "re-" mean?

Again or back. From “Prefixes” →

In plain English: A prefix is a group of letters added to the start of a word to change its meaning, like "un-" in "unhappy". Key idea: Adding a prefix never changes the spelling of the root word: just put the prefix on the front, even if letters double up.

How can you remember "hear"?

It has an "ear" in it. From “Homophones” →

In plain English: Homophones are words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings, like "there", "their" and "they're". Key idea: Choose a homophone by its meaning: check the sentence, and test shortened forms by saying them in full.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Phonics and Spelling

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. How many phonemes are in the word "dog"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    3. "Dog" has three sounds: /d/ /o/ /g/. Each sound is one phoneme.

  2. Blend the sounds /m/ /a/ /p/. What word do they make? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    map. /m/ /a/ /p/ pushed together in order make "map". Keep the sounds in left-to-right order.

  3. How many sounds are in the word "lamp"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4. "Lamp" breaks into /l/ /a/ /m/ /p/: four sounds, each written with one letter.

  4. Which is a grapheme? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The letters "sh" in "ship". A grapheme is the written letter or letters for one sound. "sh" is two letters writing one sound.

  5. Why should you say "mmm" rather than "muh" for the letter m? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pure sounds blend into words more easily. Extra "uh" sounds get in the way when you push sounds together, so pure sounds make blending accurate.

  6. Which sounds do you blend to read "stamp"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    /s/ /t/ /a/ /m/ /p/. Every grapheme in "stamp" is one letter, so blend all five sounds. Missing out the /m/ gives a different word.

  7. Why does English need graphemes with more than one letter? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It has more sounds than letters. English has about 44 sounds but only 26 letters, so some sounds are written with two or three letters, like "sh" and "igh".

  8. Ava reads "black" as "back". What did she most likely do? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She missed out the /l/ sound. "Black" has /b/ /l/ /a/ /ck/. Leaving out the /l/ gives "back", so she needs to say every sound.

  9. Which word has the same number of sounds as letters? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    frog. "Frog" is /f/ /r/ /o/ /g/: four sounds and four letters. The others each have a two-letter grapheme (sh, ck, th).

Key takeaways

  • A phoneme is a sound; a grapheme is the letter or letters that write it. Reading means turning graphemes into phonemes.
  • To read an unfamiliar word, say each sound from left to right and blend them, faster and faster, until you hear the word.
  • Segmenting means splitting a word into its sounds, then writing one grapheme for each sound.
  • In a digraph or trigraph, the letters work together to make one sound; read them as a team, not one at a time.
  • Most of a tricky word still follows phonics; only one part is tricky, so learn that part.
  • When a sound has several spellings, think about where it comes in the word and which spelling you have seen in similar words.

Common mistakes in Phonics and Spelling

  • Adding "uh" to sounds, so "c-a-t" becomes "cuh-a-tuh", which blends to "cuhatuh" instead of "cat". Keep the sounds pure.
  • Guessing a word from its first letter or a picture. Look at every letter, all the way through the word, and blend.
  • Missing sounds in the middle of words, like writing "tet" for "tent". Say the word slowly and touch a finger for every sound.
  • Sounding out each letter in a digraph separately, so "ship" becomes "s-h-i-p". The s and h make one sound together: /sh/.
  • Thinking tricky words have to be learned as a whole picture. Usually most letters still follow phonics, so sound out what you can and focus on the tricky bit.
  • Always using the first spelling you learned, such as writing "rane" or "rayn" for "rain". Check which spelling fits the position, and learn common words.

Lessons in this school

  1. Letters and sounds
    Phonics teaches the link between the sounds we hear in words and the letters we write.
  2. Blending to read
    Blending means pushing sounds together to hear the word.
  3. Segmenting to spell
    Segmenting is the opposite of blending: you break a spoken word into its sounds so that you can write each one.
  4. Digraphs and trigraphs
    Some sounds are written with two letters (digraphs, like "sh") or three letters (trigraphs, like "igh").
  5. Tricky words
    Some common words don't follow the usual phonics patterns, or use patterns children haven't learned yet.
  6. Alternative spellings of sounds
    Many sounds in English can be spelled in more than one way, and some spellings make more than one sound.
  7. Adding suffixes
    A suffix is a group of letters added to the end of a word to change its meaning or how it is used, like "-ing", "-ed", "-ness" and "-ful".
  8. Prefixes
    A prefix is a group of letters added to the start of a word to change its meaning, like "un-" in "unhappy".
  9. Plurals
    Plural means more than one.
  10. Homophones
    Homophones are words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings, like "there", "their" and "they're".
  11. Silent letters
    Some words contain letters that we write but don't say, like the k in "knee" and the b in "lamb".
  12. Spelling rules and exceptions
    Spelling in English follows many patterns, but almost every rule has exceptions.

Phonics and Spelling: questions students ask

What is blending?

Pushing sounds together to read a word.

What is a digraph?

Two letters that make one sound, such as "ch".

Where does "ay" usually come in a word?

At the end, as in "play".

Why does the phonics screening check include made-up words like "jound"?

They can only be read by blending. A child can't have memorised a made-up word, so reading it correctly shows they can blend.
