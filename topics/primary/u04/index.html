Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How many centimetres are in a metre?

100. From “Length and height” →

In plain English: Length tells us how long something is, and height how tall. Key idea: Line up the zero mark with the start of the object and read where it ends; there are 10 millimetres in every centimetre.

How many minutes are in an hour?

60. From “Telling the time” →

In plain English: Clocks tell us the time in hours and minutes. Key idea: Count the minute hand in fives from the 12; the hour hand shows the hour it has just passed.

How many metres are in 3.5 km?

3,500. From “Converting units” →

In plain English: Converting units means changing a measurement from one unit to another, such as metres to centimetres. Key idea: Bigger unit to smaller unit: multiply. Smaller unit to bigger unit: divide.

What do the angles on a straight line add up to?

180°. From “Angles” →

In plain English: An angle is an amount of turn, measured in degrees. Key idea: A right angle is 90°, a straight line is 180° and a full turn is 360°; the angles in a triangle always add up to 180°.

How many does one tally gate stand for?

5. From “Tally charts and pictograms” →

In plain English: Tally charts and pictograms are simple ways to collect and show information. Key idea: Count tallies in fives, using the gates, then add the single lines.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Measurement, Geometry and Statistics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which unit is best for measuring the length of a football pitch? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Metres. A football pitch is about a hundred metres long, so metres are sensible. Grams measure mass, not length.

  2. Which is the best unit for the mass of an apple? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Grams. An apple is light, around 100 to 200 grams. Kilograms suit heavy things like a person, and litres measure liquids.

  3. How many pence are in £1? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    100. There are 100 pence in every pound, so £1 = 100p and £2 = 200p.

  4. A crayon starts at 0 and ends at 7 cm and 4 small marks. How long is it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    7 cm 4 mm. Each small mark is a millimetre, so the crayon is 7 cm and 4 mm long, which is 74 mm.

  5. Jay measures a leaf with its tip at the 2 cm mark and its end at 9 cm. How long is the leaf? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    7 cm. He didn't start at zero, so subtract: 9 − 2 = 7 cm. Reading 9 cm forgets the starting point.

  6. A scale goes 0, 1 kg, 2 kg with 10 steps between each number. What is each step worth? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    100 g. 1 kg is 1,000 g, and 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100, so each step is 100 g.

  7. Which length is longest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    1.2 m. Change them all to centimetres: 120 cm, 115 cm, 105 cm and 110 cm. 1.2 m is longest.

  8. A 2 kg bag of flour has 650 g used. How much is left? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    1,350 g. Change 2 kg to 2,000 g. 2,000 − 650 = 1,350 g, which is 1.35 kg.

  9. Ben has three 20p coins, two 50p coins and one £1 coin. How much does he have? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    £2.60. 3 × 20p = 60p, 2 × 50p = £1, and £1 more: 60p + £1 + £1 = £2.60.

Key takeaways

  • Line up the zero mark with the start of the object and read where it ends; there are 10 millimetres in every centimetre.
  • On a scale, work out what each small mark is worth by dividing the gap between two numbers by the number of steps between them.
  • £1 = 100p, and the two digits after the decimal point in pounds are the pence.
  • Count the minute hand in fives from the 12; the hour hand shows the hour it has just passed.
  • Perimeter is the total length of the edges of a shape: add all the sides, making sure you have counted every one.
  • The area of a rectangle is its length times its width, and the answer is in square units such as cm² or m².

Common mistakes in Measurement, Geometry and Statistics

  • Starting to measure from the edge of the ruler or from 1 instead of from 0. If you start at 1, the object looks 1 cm longer than it is.
  • Counting every mark as 1 or as 10. Always work out what one step is worth first.
  • Writing both £ and p together, like £1.50p. Use one or the other: £1.50 or 150p.
  • Treating time like ordinary numbers, for example working out 6:20 − 4:45 as 175. There are 60 minutes in an hour, not 100, so count on through the hours instead.
  • Forgetting the unlabelled sides of a shape. Mark each side as you add it so none is missed.
  • Adding the sides instead of multiplying, which finds the half-perimeter, or forgetting to halve for a triangle.

Lessons in this school

  1. Length and height
    Length tells us how long something is, and height how tall.
  2. Mass and capacity
    Mass is how heavy something is; capacity is how much a container can hold.
  3. Money
    In the UK we use pounds (£) and pence (p), with 100p in every pound.
  4. Telling the time
    Clocks tell us the time in hours and minutes.
  5. Perimeter
    The perimeter is the distance all the way round the edge of a shape.
  6. Area
    Area is the amount of flat space a shape covers, measured in squares.
  7. Volume
    Volume is the amount of space a solid object takes up.
  8. Converting units
    Converting units means changing a measurement from one unit to another, such as metres to centimetres.
  9. 2-D shapes
    2-D shapes are flat shapes with length and width, like squares, triangles and circles.
  10. 3-D shapes
    3-D shapes are solid shapes with length, width and height, like cubes, cylinders and pyramids.
  11. Angles
    An angle is an amount of turn, measured in degrees.
  12. Lines of symmetry
    A shape has a line of symmetry if one half is the mirror image of the other.
  13. Position and direction
    Position and direction describe where things are and how they move.
  14. Coordinates
    Coordinates are pairs of numbers that give the exact position of a point on a grid.
  15. Tally charts and pictograms
    Tally charts and pictograms are simple ways to collect and show information.
  16. Bar charts and tables
    Bar charts and tables organise information so it is easy to compare.
  17. Line graphs
    A line graph shows how something changes over time, such as temperature through a day or a plant's height over weeks.
  18. The mean
    The mean is a kind of average: it shares a total out equally.
