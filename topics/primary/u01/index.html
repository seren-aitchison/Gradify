Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

When you count a group of objects, which number tells you how many there are?

The last number you say. From “Counting objects to 20” →

In plain English: Counting tells us how many things there are. Key idea: Say one number for each object, and the last number you say tells you how many there are altogether.

How do you write seventy-five in digits?

75. From “Reading and writing numbers to 100” →

In plain English: Numbers can be written in digits, like 47, or in words, like forty-seven. Key idea: In a two-digit number, the first digit tells you the tens and the second tells you the ones; in words, say the tens first, then the ones.

Partition 64 into tens and ones.

60 + 4. From “Tens and ones” →

In plain English: The value of a digit depends on where it is in a number. Key idea: In a two-digit number, the first digit tells you how many tens and the second tells you how many ones: 46 = 40 + 6.

What does the sign > mean?

Is greater than. From “Comparing and ordering numbers” →

In plain English: To compare numbers we decide which is bigger or smaller, and write it with the signs <, > or =. Key idea: The wide end of < or > points at the bigger number, the narrow end at the smaller one.

How do you find the rule for a sequence?

Work out the difference between numbers next to each other. From “Counting in steps” →

In plain English: Counting in steps means adding the same amount each time, such as counting in twos, fives or tens. Key idea: A sequence goes up or down by the same step each time; find the step by working out the difference between two numbers next to each other.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Number and Place Value

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Mia counts some shells: "one, two, three, four, five, six." How many shells are there? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    6. The last number Mia says is six, so there are six shells. The first number, one, only tells you where she started.

  2. Which number comes straight after fifteen when you count? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Sixteen. Counting goes in order: thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Fourteen comes just before fifteen, and fifty is a much bigger number.

  3. What is one more than 26? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    27. One more is the next counting number, so 26 becomes 27. Thirty-six would be ten more, and 25 is one less.

  4. Leo counts 9 cars, then counts again starting with a different car. What should he find? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    9 cars again. The total doesn't change when you start in a different place, so Leo should count 9 again. If he gets a different number, he has made a mistake somewhere.

  5. Count back from 52. Which numbers come next? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    51, 50, 49. Counting back gives one less each time: 51, 50, 49. The option 53, 54, 55 counts forwards, and 42, 32, 22 jumps back in tens.

  6. What is one less than 60? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    59. When you count back from 60 the ones go from 0 to 9 and the tens drop from 6 to 5, giving 59. Sixty-nine is a common slip.

  7. Sam counts 10 pencils but touches one pencil twice. What answer will he probably get? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    11. Touching one pencil twice adds an extra number, so Sam says eleven even though there are only ten pencils.

  8. Ava counts on from 88 in ones and says "88, 89, 80". What has she done wrong? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    She went back to 80 instead of going on to 90. After 89 the ones go back to 0 but the tens must go up by one, from 8 to 9, so the next number is 90.

  9. Ben hears "sixteen" and writes 61. What is the best way to help him? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Show that sixteen is ten and six, so it starts with a 1. Sixteen means ten and six, so the tens digit is 1 and the ones digit is 6. The order of the word doesn't change the order of the digits.

Key takeaways

  • Say one number for each object, and the last number you say tells you how many there are altogether.
  • One more is the next counting number; one less is the number just before. Counting on and counting back are the same numbers in opposite directions.
  • In a two-digit number, the first digit tells you the tens and the second tells you the ones; in words, say the tens first, then the ones.
  • Even numbers end in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. Odd numbers end in 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9.
  • In a two-digit number, the first digit tells you how many tens and the second tells you how many ones: 46 = 40 + 6.
  • Adding or taking away 10 changes the tens digit; adding or taking away 100 changes the hundreds digit.

Common mistakes in Number and Place Value

  • Counting too fast, so your voice and your finger get out of step. Slow down and touch each object as you say its number.
  • Saying "twenty-nine, twenty-ten" instead of "thirty". After 9 ones, the next number starts a new ten.
  • Writing "fourteen" as 41 because you hear "four" first. In the teens the word says the ones first, but the digits are still written tens first: 1 then 4.
  • Thinking a number is odd because its first digit is odd. 34 starts with 3 but ends in 4, so it is even: only the last digit counts.
  • Thinking the 3 in 34 means 3. It means 3 tens, or 30.
  • Writing "three hundred and five" as 3005 or 35. Each place gets exactly one digit: H 3, T 0, O 5.

Lessons in this school

  1. Counting objects to 20
    Counting tells us how many things there are.
  2. Counting forwards and backwards
    Counting forwards makes numbers bigger and counting backwards makes them smaller.
  3. Reading and writing numbers to 100
    Numbers can be written in digits, like 47, or in words, like forty-seven.
  4. Odd and even numbers
    Every whole number is either odd or even.
  5. Tens and ones
    The value of a digit depends on where it is in a number.
  6. Hundreds, tens and ones
    Three-digit numbers have a hundreds digit as well as tens and ones.
  7. Thousands and beyond
    The place value pattern keeps going: ten hundreds make a thousand, ten thousands make ten thousand, and so on up to millions.
  8. Comparing and ordering numbers
    To compare numbers we decide which is bigger or smaller, and write it with the signs <, > or =.
  9. Rounding numbers
    Rounding gives a simpler number that is close to the real one, such as saying "about 50" instead of 47.
  10. Counting in steps
    Counting in steps means adding the same amount each time, such as counting in twos, fives or tens.
  11. Negative numbers
    Negative numbers are numbers less than zero, such as −3.
  12. Roman numerals
    The ancient Romans wrote numbers with letters: I, V, X, L, C, D and M.

Number and Place Value: questions students ask

What is the 8 worth in 58,201?

8,000 (eight thousand).

What is 85 rounded to the nearest 10?

90 (halfway rounds up).

Which shows eighty-two in digits?

82. Eighty-two is 8 tens and 2 ones, written 82. Writing 28 swaps the tens and ones.

Which is the correct spelling of 40?

forty. Forty has no u, even though four does. This is one of the most common spelling mistakes in maths.
