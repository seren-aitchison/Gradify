Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does the atomic number tell you?

The number of protons in the atom. From “Atomic structure and isotopes” →

In plain English: All matter is made of atoms, which contain protons, neutrons and electrons. Key idea: Atoms have a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons; the atomic number gives the protons, the mass number gives protons plus neutrons, isotopes differ in neutron number, and relative atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average of isotope masses.

What is a covalent bond?

A shared pair of electrons between two atoms. From “Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding” →

In plain English: Atoms join together in three main ways. Key idea: Ionic bonding transfers electrons from metals to non-metals, forming ions held by electrostatic forces in a lattice; covalent bonding shares electron pairs between non-metals; and metallic bonding holds positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.

How is aluminium extracted?

By electrolysis, because it is more reactive than carbon. From “Reactivity series and extraction of metals” →

In plain English: Metals differ in how readily they react. Key idea: The reactivity series orders metals by how readily they form positive ions, predicting displacement reactions; oxidation is gain of oxygen and reduction loss of oxygen; metals below carbon are extracted by reduction with carbon, and those above carbon by electrolysis.

What does the gradient of a product–time graph show?

The rate of reaction. From “Rates of reaction” →

In plain English: Some reactions are very fast and others very slow. Key idea: Rate of reaction is the amount of reactant used or product formed per unit time, increased by higher temperature, concentration, pressure and surface area and by catalysts; collision theory explains these as more frequent collisions, more energetic collisions or a lower activation energy.

What is the test for carbon dioxide?

Bubble it through limewater, which turns milky. From “Tests for gases” →

In plain English: Simple tests identify common gases. Key idea: Hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint, oxygen relights a glowing splint, carbon dioxide turns limewater milky and chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper; tests should use small volumes, with chlorine tested in a fume cupboard.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Chemistry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the relative charge of a neutron? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    0. Applying the standard result: Neutrons are neutral.

  2. What was the plum pudding model? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it. Applying the standard result: Thomson's model mixed charges.

  3. What are Group 1 elements called? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Alkali metals. Applying the standard result: Group 1 forms alkalis.

  4. An atom of sodium has atomic number 11 and mass number 23. How many neutrons does it have? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    12. Applying the standard result: 23 − 11 = 12.

  5. In the alpha scattering experiment, what did most alpha particles passing straight through show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Most of the atom is empty space. Applying the standard result: Little was in their way.

  6. What forms when sodium reacts with water? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Sodium hydroxide and hydrogen. Applying the standard result: Alkali metals form hydroxides.

  7. Why do isotopes of the same element react in the same way? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They have the same electron arrangement. Applying the standard result: Chemistry depends on electrons.

  8. Why was the plum pudding model replaced? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It could not explain alpha particles bouncing back from the foil. Applying the standard result: New evidence did not fit.

  9. Bromine water is added to potassium iodide solution. What happens? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Iodine is displaced because bromine is more reactive. Applying the standard result: Reactivity decreases down Group 7.

Key takeaways

  • Atoms have a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons; the atomic number gives the protons, the mass number gives protons plus neutrons, isotopes differ in neutron number, and relative atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average of isotope masses.
  • The atomic model developed from Dalton's solid spheres to Thomson's plum pudding, Rutherford's nuclear model after the alpha scattering experiment, Bohr's electron shells and Chadwick's neutron, showing how scientific models change when new evidence cannot be explained.
  • The periodic table orders elements by atomic number into periods and groups, building on Mendeleev's table with gaps; group members share outer electron numbers and properties, with Group 1 reactivity increasing down the group, Group 7 reactivity decreasing, and Group 0 being unreactive.
  • Ionic bonding transfers electrons from metals to non-metals, forming ions held by electrostatic forces in a lattice; covalent bonding shares electron pairs between non-metals; and metallic bonding holds positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
  • Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct when molten or dissolved; small molecules have low melting points and do not conduct; giant covalent structures such as diamond and graphite have very high melting points, with graphite and graphene conducting; and metals conduct and are malleable, while alloys are harder.
  • Mass is conserved in chemical reactions because atoms are only rearranged, so balanced equations have equal atoms on each side; apparent changes in mass usually involve gases, and relative formula mass is the sum of relative atomic masses in a formula.

Common mistakes in Chemistry

  • Thinking isotopes have different chemical properties. They have the same electron arrangement, so they react the same way.
  • Saying Rutherford discovered the electron. Thomson discovered the electron; Rutherford's work led to the nuclear model.
  • Saying all groups become more reactive down the table. Group 1 does, but Group 7 becomes less reactive.
  • Thinking ionic compounds are made of molecules. They form giant lattices of ions, not separate molecules.
  • Saying covalent bonds break when water boils. Boiling overcomes the weak forces between molecules, not the bonds within them.
  • Changing a formula to balance an equation, such as writing H₂O₂ for water. Only change the numbers in front.

Chemistry: questions students ask

Who discovered the electron?

J. J. Thomson, in 1897.

Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his table?

For elements not yet discovered, whose properties he predicted.

Between which types of element does ionic bonding occur?

Between metals and non-metals.

Why does solid sodium chloride not conduct electricity?

Its ions are fixed in the lattice and cannot move.
