Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

State the principle of conservation of energy.

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred, stored or dissipated. From “Energy stores and transfers” →

In plain English: Energy is never used up; it is stored in different ways and transferred between stores. Key idea: Energy is held in stores and transferred by work, heating and radiation; in a closed system the total is conserved, and Ek = 0.5mv² and Ep = mgh let you calculate two of the stores.

What happens to the resistance of an LDR in bright light?

It decreases. From “Resistance and I–V characteristics” →

In plain English: Resistance tells us how hard it is for current to flow through a component. Key idea: V = IR links potential difference, current and resistance; resistors are ohmic, filament lamps increase in resistance as they heat up, diodes conduct one way, and thermistors and LDRs fall in resistance with heat and light.

What is specific heat capacity?

The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. From “Specific heat capacity and latent heat” →

In plain English: Heating a substance either raises its temperature or changes its state. Key idea: Specific heat capacity tells you the energy to warm 1 kg by 1 °C (ΔE = mcΔθ), specific latent heat tells you the energy to change the state of 1 kg (E = mL), and flat parts of a heating curve are changes of state.

What is a typical walking speed?

About 1.5 m/s. From “Speed, velocity and acceleration” →

In plain English: Motion can be described with distance, speed, velocity and acceleration, and shown on graphs. Key idea: Speed is distance over time, velocity adds direction, acceleration is change in velocity over time; the gradient of a distance–time graph is speed, and on a velocity–time graph the gradient is acceleration and the area is distance.

What is the period of a wave?

The time for one complete wave to pass a point. From “Properties of waves” →

In plain English: Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter. Key idea: Transverse waves oscillate at right angles to energy transfer and longitudinal waves along it; waves are described by amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period, and v = fλ links speed, frequency and wavelength.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Physics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which energy store increases when a spring is stretched? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Elastic potential. Stretching or squashing an object increases its elastic potential store.

  2. What is the unit of power? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Watt. Power is measured in watts, where one watt is one joule per second.

  3. Which of these is a non-renewable energy resource? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Natural gas. Natural gas is a fossil fuel that forms over millions of years, so it will run out.

  4. How is energy transferred when a current flows through a heater? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    By electrical work. A current flowing transfers energy by electrical work, which then heats the element.

  5. A 2000 W kettle runs for 90 s. How much energy does it transfer? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    180 000 J. E = P × t = 2000 × 90 = 180 000 J transferred by the kettle.

  6. In a coal power station, what does the steam directly turn? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A turbine. Steam drives the turbine, and the turbine turns the generator to produce electricity.

  7. A car's speed doubles from 10 m/s to 20 m/s. What happens to its kinetic energy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It becomes four times larger. Kinetic energy depends on speed squared, so doubling the speed multiplies Ek by 2² = 4.

  8. A student claims a pulley system is 120% efficient. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The claim is wrong: useful output cannot exceed input. Conservation of energy means useful output is never more than total input, so efficiency is at most 1.

  9. Why does a grid with lots of wind power still need some other resources? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Wind output varies with the weather. On calm days wind output falls, so reliable or stored supplies must meet demand.

Key takeaways

  • Energy is held in stores and transferred by work, heating and radiation; in a closed system the total is conserved, and Ek = 0.5mv² and Ep = mgh let you calculate two of the stores.
  • Work done equals force times distance, power is the rate of energy transfer in watts, and efficiency compares useful output to total input, which is always less than 100% in real devices.
  • Renewable resources are replenished and produce little carbon dioxide but can be unreliable, while fossil fuels and nuclear are reliable but non-renewable, with fossil fuels driving climate change.
  • Current is the rate of flow of charge (Q = It), potential difference is energy transferred per coulomb (E = QV), ammeters go in series and voltmeters go in parallel.
  • V = IR links potential difference, current and resistance; resistors are ohmic, filament lamps increase in resistance as they heat up, diodes conduct one way, and thermistors and LDRs fall in resistance with heat and light.
  • In series the current is the same, p.d. is shared and resistances add; in parallel the p.d. is the same, currents add and total resistance is less than the smallest branch resistance.

Common mistakes in Physics

  • Saying energy is "used up" or "lost". Energy is conserved; it is dissipated into less useful stores, usually the thermal store of the surroundings.
  • Dividing input by output when calculating efficiency. Always divide useful output by total input, so the answer is less than 1.
  • Calling nuclear power renewable because it emits no carbon dioxide. Uranium is a finite resource, so nuclear fuel is non-renewable.
  • Connecting a voltmeter in series with a component. A voltmeter measures the difference between two points, so it must be connected across (in parallel with) the component.
  • Thinking a curved I–V graph shows constant resistance. Only a straight line through the origin shows constant resistance; calculate R = V ÷ I at each point.
  • Adding resistances in parallel as if they were in series. Adding a parallel branch lowers the total resistance, so the total must be smaller than the smallest resistor.

Lessons in this school

  1. Energy stores and transfers
    Energy is never used up; it is stored in different ways and transferred between stores.
  2. Work, power and efficiency
    Work done, power and efficiency let us measure how much energy is transferred, how fast, and how usefully.
  3. Energy resources
    Our energy comes from renewable and non-renewable resources used for transport, electricity generation and heating.
  4. Circuits, current and potential difference
    Electric circuits let charge flow to transfer energy.
  5. Resistance and I–V characteristics
    Resistance tells us how hard it is for current to flow through a component.
  6. Series and parallel circuits
    Components can be joined in series or in parallel, and each arrangement follows its own rules.
  7. Mains electricity and the National Grid
    Homes in the UK are supplied with alternating current through a three-core cable and the National Grid.
  8. Density and states of matter
    The particle model explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases and the idea of density.
  9. Specific heat capacity and latent heat
    Heating a substance either raises its temperature or changes its state.
  10. Atomic structure and radioactive decay
    Atoms have a tiny dense nucleus surrounded by electrons, and some nuclei are unstable and decay.
  11. Half-life and the uses and dangers of radiation
    Radioactive sources decay at a rate described by their half-life.
  12. Scalars, vectors and resultant forces
    Physical quantities are either scalars or vectors, and forces are vectors that combine into a resultant.
  13. Speed, velocity and acceleration
    Motion can be described with distance, speed, velocity and acceleration, and shown on graphs.
  14. Newton's laws of motion
    Newton's three laws explain how forces change the motion of objects.
  15. Stopping distances and momentum
    A vehicle's stopping distance depends on the driver and on the vehicle and road.
  16. Forces, springs and Hooke's law
    Forces can stretch, squash or bend objects, and springs follow a simple rule up to a limit.
  17. Properties of waves
    Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter.
  18. The electromagnetic spectrum
    Electromagnetic waves form a continuous spectrum, all travelling at the same speed in a vacuum.
  19. Magnetism and electromagnetism
    Magnets and electric currents both produce magnetic fields, and a current in a field can feel a force.
  20. Physics equations: recall and use
    AQA GCSE Physics expects you to recall some equations and use others from the equation sheet.
  21. Physics required practicals
    AQA GCSE Physics includes ten required practicals that can be examined in written papers.

Physics: questions students ask

What is the equation for gravitational potential energy?

Ep = mass × gravitational field strength × height (Ep = mgh).

What is one watt equal to?

One joule of energy transferred per second.

Why is coal called non-renewable?

It is used far faster than it forms, so it will run out.

What two conditions are needed for a current to flow?

A closed circuit and a source of potential difference.
