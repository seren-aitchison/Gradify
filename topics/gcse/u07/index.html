Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Define ecology.

The scientific study of the interactions that determine the distribution and abundance of organisms. From “What is ecology?” →

In plain English: Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment, and of what determines where organisms live and how many there are. Key idea: Ecology explains distribution and abundance through interactions: between organisms and the physical environment, and between organisms themselves.

Name the four spheres of the Earth system.

Atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere. From “Ecology and environmental science” →

In plain English: Environmental science combines ecology with geology, chemistry, physics and the social sciences to understand and manage human impacts on Earth's systems. Key idea: Environmental science studies the Earth system as connected spheres, and how human activity changes the flows between them.

What are Jenny's five factors of soil formation?

Climate, organisms, relief (topography), parent material and time. From “Soils” →

In plain English: Soil is a living mixture of weathered rock, organic matter, water, air and organisms, and it controls which plants can grow. Key idea: Texture sets drainage and nutrient-holding capacity: sandy soils drain freely but are infertile; clay soils hold water and nutrients but can be waterlogged; loams balance both.

How do marram grass leaves reduce water loss?

They roll up, trapping humid air; stomata are sunken in pits with hairs, reducing the diffusion gradient. From “Adaptation to the environment” →

In plain English: Adaptations are inherited features that increase an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Key idea: Adaptations are inherited traits shaped by natural selection, not changes an individual makes because it needs them.

What is the natural biome of most of lowland Britain?

Temperate deciduous forest. From “Terrestrial biomes” →

In plain English: Biomes are the major types of terrestrial ecosystem, each defined by its climate and dominant vegetation. Key idea: Each biome reflects climate: temperature and rainfall set the dominant plant growth forms, and the plants in turn shape the animals, soils and productivity.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Ecology

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is a biotic factor affecting a population of rabbits? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The number of foxes in the area. Foxes are living organisms (predators), so they are a biotic factor; the others are physical or chemical conditions.

  2. All the bluebells in one wood form a (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    population. A population is all the individuals of one species in one area; the wood's bluebells are one species.

  3. Which approach changes one factor and compares the outcome with untreated controls? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A manipulative experiment. Manipulation with controls is what lets an experiment test whether a factor causes an effect.

  4. A campaign leaflet says "ecology tells us we must ban all new building on farmland". What is the best response? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ecology can predict effects on wildlife, but whether to ban building is a value judgement. Ecology provides evidence about consequences; deciding what to do also involves values, costs and other interests.

  5. A researcher measures how quickly leaf litter decomposes and releases nitrogen in a forest. Which level of organisation is this mainly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ecosystem. Nutrient cycling links the living community with its abiotic environment, so it is an ecosystem-level process.

  6. Surveys show more wildflower species in meadows that are cut for hay than in unmanaged ones. What is the best next step to test whether cutting causes the difference? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Randomly assign plots to be cut or left uncut and compare species richness over several years. Random assignment to treatments removes other differences between meadows, so a difference can be attributed to cutting.

  7. Why is the red squirrel's decline in Britain a good example of a typical ecological explanation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Several interacting causes, competition, disease and history, combine to explain it. Ecological patterns usually arise from several interacting biotic and abiotic causes; single-factor explanations are rarely enough.

  8. Which is an emergent property of a population, absent from any single individual? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its age structure. Age structure describes how individuals are distributed among age classes, which only exists for the group as a whole.

  9. A study finds bird species are randomly spaced within 10 m plots but clumped across a 10 km landscape. What is the best interpretation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The pattern depends on scale, so both descriptions can be correct. Spatial pattern is scale-dependent: within small plots local spacing may look random while habitat patches create clumping at landscape scale.

Key takeaways

  • Ecology explains distribution and abundance through interactions: between organisms and the physical environment, and between organisms themselves.
  • Each level contains the ones below it: populations are made of organisms, communities of populations, and an ecosystem is a community plus its physical environment.
  • Correlation from field observation suggests hypotheses; manipulative experiments with controls and replicates are the strongest test of a cause.
  • Environmental science studies the Earth system as connected spheres, and how human activity changes the flows between them.
  • Abiotic factors act through physiology: temperature through enzyme rates, water and salt through osmosis, pH through enzymes and nutrient availability, light through photosynthesis.
  • Latitude sets the energy received; the circulation it drives sets where rain falls: wet at the equator, dry near 30°, wetter again in the mid-latitudes.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Ecology

  • Using "ecological" to mean "environmentally friendly". In the science, ecological simply means "to do with the interactions of organisms and environment".
  • Confusing community and ecosystem. A community is living organisms only; an ecosystem includes the community and its abiotic environment.
  • Generalising from a one-season, one-site study. Ecological systems vary from place to place and year to year, so short, small studies may miss the pattern.
  • Treating environmental science as only "green" campaigning. It is an evidence-based discipline, and its findings sometimes show a popular measure to be ineffective.
  • Saying that temperature is a resource. Organisms respond to temperature, but one organism doesn't use temperature up so that less is left for another.
  • Thinking rainfall alone decides vegetation. Seasonality, temperature and evaporation matter too: the same rainfall supports forest in a cool climate but only grassland or scrub in a hot one, where evaporation is greater.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is ecology?
    Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment, and of what determines where organisms live and how many there are.
  2. Levels of ecological organisation
    Ecology works at a ladder of levels, from the individual organism to the whole biosphere.
  3. Ecology as a science: hypotheses and scale
    Ecologists test ideas by observation, experiment and models, in systems that are large, variable and slow.
  4. Ecology and environmental science
    Environmental science combines ecology with geology, chemistry, physics and the social sciences to understand and manage human impacts on Earth's systems.
  5. Abiotic factors
    Abiotic factors are the physical and chemical conditions that determine where organisms can live.
  6. Climate and the distribution of life
    Climate, the long-term pattern of temperature and rainfall, is the main control on where major types of vegetation occur.
  7. Soils
    Soil is a living mixture of weathered rock, organic matter, water, air and organisms, and it controls which plants can grow.
  8. Water and aquatic environments
    Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface and has unusual properties that shape life in it.
  9. Tolerance ranges and limiting factors
    Every species can survive, grow and reproduce only within a range of each environmental factor.
  10. Adaptation to the environment
    Adaptations are inherited features that increase an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  11. Habitats and the ecological niche
    A habitat is where an organism lives; its niche is its role there: the conditions it tolerates, the resources it uses and how it interacts with other species.
  12. Biotic interactions: an overview
    Organisms affect each other in a small number of basic ways, classified by whether each partner gains, loses or is unaffected.
  13. Terrestrial biomes
    Biomes are the major types of terrestrial ecosystem, each defined by its climate and dominant vegetation.
  14. Aquatic biomes
    Aquatic biomes are defined by salinity, depth, light and water movement rather than by climate.
  15. Biogeography and the distribution of species
    Biogeography explains why species are found where they are, at scales from continents to islands.
  16. Humans as an ecological force
    People now alter ecosystems on a planetary scale, through land use, harvesting, pollution, species introductions and climate change.

Foundations of Ecology: questions students ask

What is the difference between a community and an ecosystem?

A community is all the living populations in an area; an ecosystem is the community plus its abiotic environment.

Why can't an observational study on its own prove a cause?

It shows a correlation, but another factor could be responsible; only manipulating the factor with controls tests causation.

What is the difference between positive and negative feedback?

Positive feedback amplifies a change; negative feedback counteracts it and stabilises the system.

Why does temperature affect the growth rate of a plant?

It changes the rate of enzyme-controlled reactions such as photosynthesis and respiration; extremes denature enzymes.
