Key terms and formulas at a glance

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a megacity?

An urban area with more than 10 million people. From “Global urbanisation” →

In plain English: More than half the world's people now live in towns and cities, and the fastest growth is in lower-income and newly emerging countries. Key idea: Urbanisation is a rising share of people in urban areas, driven by rural–urban migration and natural increase, and it is fastest in LICs and NEEs, where most of the world's megacities are now found.

What is gentrification?

Wealthier people moving into a poorer area, raising prices and changing its character. From “UK cities: change and regeneration” →

In plain English: UK cities have changed as industry declined, populations moved and new jobs appeared. Key idea: The UK's population is concentrated in cities that grew with industry; deindustrialisation left inner-city deprivation and brownfield sites, and regeneration schemes such as Stratford's Olympic Park aim to bring new jobs, housing and environments.

What is intermediate technology?

Simple, affordable technology that local people can use and maintain. From “Reducing the development gap” →

In plain English: The development gap is the difference in standards of living between the world's richest and poorest countries. Key idea: Uneven development has physical, economic and historical causes; strategies such as aid, intermediate technology, fair trade, microfinance, debt relief and tourism can narrow the gap, but each has limits.

What is water security?

Having enough clean, reliable water for health, farming and industry. From “Food, water and energy: global patterns” →

In plain English: Food, water and energy are vital for human development, but they are unevenly distributed around the world. Key idea: Food, water and energy are unevenly distributed, so resource security varies; the UK imports much of its food, has a water deficit in the south-east and is moving from coal to gas and renewables.

Which graph is best for showing the age and sex structure of a population?

A population pyramid. From “Graphs, statistics and data presentation” →

In plain English: Geographers present and analyse data to reveal patterns and trends. Key idea: Match the graph to the data, use mean, median, mode, range and interquartile range to summarise data, and describe trends with evidence while remembering that correlation does not prove cause.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Geography: Human Environments and Skills

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is a push factor encouraging people to leave rural areas? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Repeated crop failure. Push factors are problems in the place people leave, such as crop failure, while the others are pull factors.

  2. What is meant by the informal economy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Work that is not taxed or regulated. Informal jobs such as street selling are not registered, taxed or protected by law.

  3. Which term describes the decline of manufacturing in a region? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Deindustrialisation. Deindustrialisation is the loss of manufacturing industry, as happened in many UK cities from the 1960s.

  4. Why do cities with many young migrants also have high natural increase? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Young adults are at the age when most people have children. Migrants are mostly young adults of child-bearing age, so the birth rate in the city rises.

  5. Why are favelas often built on steep hillsides? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The land is unused and nobody else wants to build there. Steep, risky land is often left empty, so migrants can build on it, though it is at risk of landslides.

  6. Why did many UK inner cities become deprived from the 1960s onwards? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Factories and docks closed, causing unemployment. The closure of industry removed jobs, so incomes fell and buildings were left empty.

  7. Which statement best explains why most new megacities are in LICs and NEEs? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Rapid rural–urban migration and high natural increase. LICs and NEEs combine high migration with high natural increase, so their cities grow fastest.

  8. Which is the strongest argument for upgrading a favela rather than clearing it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is cheaper and keeps communities and livelihoods together. Upgrading costs less and lets residents keep their homes, social networks and jobs nearby.

  9. A regeneration scheme builds expensive riverside flats in a poor area. What is the most likely negative impact? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Existing residents may be priced out of the area. Expensive housing can raise local prices and rents, a process called gentrification.

Key takeaways

  • Urbanisation is a rising share of people in urban areas, driven by rural–urban migration and natural increase, and it is fastest in LICs and NEEs, where most of the world's megacities are now found.
  • Cities in LICs and NEEs offer jobs and services but rapid growth creates squatter settlements, poor services and informal work; upgrading schemes such as Favela Bairro can improve living conditions at lower cost than clearance.
  • The UK's population is concentrated in cities that grew with industry; deindustrialisation left inner-city deprivation and brownfield sites, and regeneration schemes such as Stratford's Olympic Park aim to bring new jobs, housing and environments.
  • Sustainable cities conserve water and energy, protect green space, recycle waste and provide good public transport, as in Freiburg and Curitiba, though cost and habits limit how far any city can go.
  • Development is measured by economic indicators such as GNI per head, social indicators such as infant mortality and life expectancy, and composite indexes such as HDI; the demographic transition model links falling birth and death rates to development.
  • Uneven development has physical, economic and historical causes; strategies such as aid, intermediate technology, fair trade, microfinance, debt relief and tourism can narrow the gap, but each has limits.

Common mistakes in Geography: Human Environments and Skills

  • Confusing urbanisation with urban growth. Urbanisation means the proportion of people in urban areas rises; a city can grow in size without the country urbanising if the rural population grows just as fast.
  • Describing squatter settlements as places with no positive features. Many have strong communities, small businesses and residents who improve their homes over time.
  • Assuming regeneration helps everyone equally. New homes and jobs may suit incoming professionals better than existing residents, who can be priced out.
  • Treating "sustainable" as meaning only environmentally friendly. Sustainability also includes social and economic needs, such as affordable housing and jobs.
  • Judging development from GNI per head alone. Wealth may be unevenly shared, so social measures and the HDI give a fuller picture.
  • Assuming all aid is equally effective. Aid can be lost to corruption or create dependence; small, local projects using intermediate technology are often more sustainable.

Lessons in this school

  1. Global urbanisation
    More than half the world's people now live in towns and cities, and the fastest growth is in lower-income and newly emerging countries.
  2. Cities in lower-income countries
    Rapid growth brings both opportunities and challenges to cities in LICs and NEEs.
  3. UK cities: change and regeneration
    UK cities have changed as industry declined, populations moved and new jobs appeared.
  4. Sustainable urban living
    A sustainable city meets the needs of people today without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  5. Measuring development
    Countries differ greatly in wealth and quality of life.
  6. Reducing the development gap
    The development gap is the difference in standards of living between the world's richest and poorest countries.
  7. Economic change in an emerging economy
    Some LICs and NEEs are experiencing rapid economic development that changes their economy, environment and people's lives.
  8. The changing UK economy
    The UK has moved from an economy based on manufacturing to a post-industrial economy based on services and knowledge.
  9. Food, water and energy: global patterns
    Food, water and energy are vital for human development, but they are unevenly distributed around the world.
  10. Resource management strategies
    Countries use large-scale schemes and small, local projects to increase supply and use resources more sustainably.
  11. Map skills and grid references
    Ordnance Survey maps and atlas maps are essential tools in geography.
  12. Graphs, statistics and data presentation
    Geographers present and analyse data to reveal patterns and trends.
  13. Fieldwork enquiry
    AQA GCSE Geography students must carry out two fieldwork enquiries in contrasting environments, one physical and one human.
  14. Issue evaluation and decision making
    Paper 3 of AQA GCSE Geography includes an issue evaluation based on a pre-release resource booklet.
