Hedonic wellbeing

Holistic approach

Determinants of health
The personal, social, economic and environmental factors that influence health

Lifestyle factor

The probability of an event in a group over a stated period

Testing people without symptoms to identify those at higher risk of a condition

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Give the WHO definition of health.

A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (WHO Constitution, 1946). From “What is health?” →

In plain English: Health can be defined negatively, as the absence of disease, or positively, as complete wellbeing or the ability to adapt and cope. Key idea: How health is defined decides what counts as a health problem and what services aim to do: treat disease, promote wellbeing or help people adapt and function.

What is a QALY?

A quality-adjusted life year: length of life multiplied by a quality-of-life score, where one year in full health equals one QALY. From “Wellbeing and how it is measured” →

In plain English: Wellbeing is how people feel and function, and how they judge their lives. Key idea: Wellbeing measures ask people how they feel and function; quality-of-life measures such as EQ-5D turn health states into numbers that can be compared and combined with length of life as QALYs.

What are the four explanations of health inequalities in the Black Report?

Artefact, natural or social selection, cultural/behavioural, and materialist/structural. From “Health inequalities” →

In plain English: Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable and unfair differences in health between groups. Key idea: Health follows a social gradient that is mainly driven by material and structural conditions; equal provision does not produce equal outcomes when need is unequal.

Give three questions to ask when judging a health claim.

Who is saying it (and do they have a conflict of interest)? What kind of evidence supports it? How big is the effect in absolute terms? Is it balanced and up to date? From “Judging health information” →

In plain English: Health information is everywhere: in news, adverts, social media and conversation. Key idea: Trust a health claim more when it comes from an accountable source, rests on good human evidence, reports absolute effects and harms, and agrees with other studies.

What is the difference between sensitivity and specificity?

Sensitivity: the proportion of people with the condition who test positive. Specificity: the proportion of people without it who test negative. From “Screening: benefits and harms” →

In plain English: Screening tests apparently healthy people to find disease, or the risk of it, early. Key idea: Even an accurate test produces many false positives when a condition is rare, which is why screening is followed by diagnostic tests and why its harms must be weighed against its benefits.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement is a negative definition of health? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Health is the absence of disease or illness. A negative definition says what health is not (disease); the others describe something present, so they are positive or functional definitions.

  2. Which factor would the biomedical model focus on when explaining heart disease? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Narrowed coronary arteries. The biomedical model explains illness through bodily changes such as atherosclerosis; work, income and isolation are psychological or social factors.

  3. Which of these is an example of eudaimonic wellbeing? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Feeling that your life has purpose. Eudaimonic wellbeing concerns meaning, purpose and functioning; momentary cheerfulness is hedonic, and income and diagnosis are not wellbeing measures.

  4. A man with well-controlled epilepsy works full time, plays football and says he feels well. Which definition is least likely to class him as healthy? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The negative (absence of disease) definition. He has a diagnosed condition, so a strictly negative definition excludes him, while adaptive, functional and wellbeing definitions focus on how he copes and lives.

  5. A GP treats a patient's depression with medication and also refers them to a debt advice service and a talking therapy. Which model does this best reflect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Biopsychosocial. The care addresses biological (medication), psychological (therapy) and social (debt) factors together.

  6. A council wants to know whether a community gardening project improved residents' mental wellbeing. Which measure is the most suitable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    WEMWBS or SWEMWBS before and after the project. WEMWBS measures positive mental wellbeing directly and is designed for evaluating projects; the others measure health status or unrelated outcomes.

  7. What is the strongest criticism of the word "complete" in the WHO definition? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Almost nobody meets it for long, so it risks medicalising ordinary life and excludes people living well with chronic conditions. "Complete" wellbeing is an ideal few reach; with an ageing population and common long-term conditions, it would label most people unhealthy.

  8. Which is the best evaluation of the biomedical model? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It is highly effective for many acute diseases but underplays psychological and social causes of illness. Most effective treatments come from it, but it can't explain why poverty or stress affect health, and it is still central to medicine.

  9. Which is the strongest criticism of using only life-satisfaction scores to judge policy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    People adapt to poor circumstances and still report moderate satisfaction, so serious need can be hidden. Adaptation and differences in how people use scales mean subjective scores can mask objective deprivation, so they are best used alongside objective indicators.

Key takeaways

  • How health is defined decides what counts as a health problem and what services aim to do: treat disease, promote wellbeing or help people adapt and function.
  • The biomedical model asks "what is wrong with this body?"; the biopsychosocial model asks "what is happening to this person?"; the social model asks "what is happening to this population, and why?"
  • Wellbeing measures ask people how they feel and function; quality-of-life measures such as EQ-5D turn health states into numbers that can be compared and combined with length of life as QALYs.
  • Physical, intellectual, emotional and social health are linked: a problem in one usually spreads to the others, so care that addresses only one aspect is often incomplete.
  • Health is shaped by layers of determinants, from fixed biology through individual behaviour to living conditions and national policy; behaviour is itself shaped by the outer layers.
  • Health follows a social gradient that is mainly driven by material and structural conditions; equal provision does not produce equal outcomes when need is unequal.

Common mistakes in Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

  • Treating the WHO definition as the only "correct" one. It is the most quoted, but it is one definition among several, and its word "complete" is widely criticised.
  • Thinking the biopsychosocial model rejects biology or medicine. It adds psychological and social factors to biological ones; it doesn't replace them.
  • Assuming a high wellbeing score means the absence of illness. People with long-term conditions can report high wellbeing, and people without any diagnosis can report low wellbeing.
  • Confusing "holistic" with "alternative" medicine. In health and social care, holistic means attending to the whole person; it is part of mainstream evidence-based care.
  • Adding up percentages as if determinants act separately. Determinants interact: poverty increases smoking, and smoking deepens poverty.
  • Confusing equality with equity. Equality gives everyone the same; equity gives according to need so that outcomes become fairer.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is health?
    Health can be defined negatively, as the absence of disease, or positively, as complete wellbeing or the ability to adapt and cope.
  2. Models of health: biomedical and biopsychosocial
    A model of health is a way of explaining what causes ill health and what should be done about it.
  3. Wellbeing and how it is measured
    Wellbeing is how people feel and function, and how they judge their lives.
  4. Holistic health: physical, mental and social
    A holistic approach treats a person as a whole, recognising that physical, intellectual, emotional and social aspects of health affect one another.
  5. The determinants of health
    The determinants of health are the factors that make people healthy or unhealthy, from genes to government policy.
  6. Health inequalities
    Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable and unfair differences in health between groups.
  7. Lifestyle factors and health
    Smoking, diet, physical activity and alcohol are the four lifestyle factors with the largest effects on health in the UK.
  8. The wider determinants: housing, work and environment
    The wider determinants are the social, economic and environmental conditions in which people live.
  9. Judging health information
    Health information is everywhere: in news, adverts, social media and conversation.
  10. Levels of evidence
    Not all evidence is equally trustworthy.
  11. Understanding risk: absolute and relative
    Risks are often reported in ways that make them sound bigger or smaller than they are.
  12. Screening: benefits and harms
    Screening tests apparently healthy people to find disease, or the risk of it, early.
  13. Measuring the nation's health
    Governments and researchers describe a population's health with measures of death, illness and wellbeing.
  14. Leading causes of death and ill health in the UK
    Knowing what kills people and what makes them ill shapes public health priorities.

Foundations of Health and Wellbeing: questions students ask

What did Huber and colleagues (2011) propose as a new definition of health?

Health as the ability to adapt and to self-manage in the face of social, physical and emotional challenges.

Who proposed the biopsychosocial model, and when?

George Engel, in 1977 (Science).

What are the four ONS personal wellbeing questions about?

Life satisfaction, feeling that what you do is worthwhile, happiness yesterday and anxiety yesterday, each scored 0–10.

What does PIES stand for?

Physical, intellectual, emotional and social aspects of health and wellbeing.
