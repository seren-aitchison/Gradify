Key terms and formulas at a glance

Working memory
The limited system that holds and manipulates a small amount of information for a few seconds
Retrieval practice
Recalling information from memory without looking, as a way to learn it
Spacing effect
Better long-term memory from study spread across sessions than from the same time in one block
Interleaving
Mixing different topics or problem types within one practice session
Flashcard
A card with a question or prompt on one side and the answer on the other
Dual coding
Combining words with meaningful visuals so information has two routes into memory
Mark scheme
The guidance examiners use to award marks for each question
Command word
The instruction word in a question that tells you what kind of answer is needed
Extended response
A longer answer, usually 6 marks or more, marked by levels of quality
Minutes per mark
The paper's time divided by its total marks, used to plan how long to spend on each question
Stress response
The body's reaction to a challenge, involving hormones such as adrenaline

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the main limit of working memory?

It holds only a few chunks of new information (about four) for a few seconds unless they are rehearsed. From “How memory works” →

In plain English: Revision is really about memory: getting information into long-term memory and being able to get it back out under exam conditions. Key idea: Every time you successfully pull something out of long-term memory, it becomes easier to retrieve next time. Good revision is built around retrieving, not just rereading.

What is massed practice?

Studying a topic all in one block without gaps, in other words cramming. From “Spaced practice and revision timetables” →

In plain English: Spaced practice means spreading study of a topic over several sessions with gaps in between, instead of cramming it all at once. Key idea: Spread each topic across several short sessions over weeks, and plan the return visits in advance, so forgetting is interrupted before it wins.

What makes a good flashcard front?

A single clear question or prompt that forces you to recall one idea. From “Flashcards and the Leitner system” →

In plain English: Flashcards are a simple way to combine retrieval practice with spacing. Key idea: Flashcards work because they force retrieval, and the Leitner system adds spacing automatically, sending your weakest cards round most often.

What is the difference between "describe" and "explain"?

Describe says what happens or what something is like; explain gives reasons why or how. From “Command words” →

In plain English: Command words are the instruction words at the start of an exam question, such as "describe", "explain" and "evaluate". Key idea: Underline the command word before answering. Describe gives what, explain gives why, and evaluate needs a judgement backed by reasons.

Why should you show working in calculations?

Method marks can be awarded for correct working even if the final answer is wrong. From “Timing and exam technique” →

In plain English: Knowing the content is only half of it: in the exam you also need to read questions carefully, manage time and avoid careless errors. Key idea: Allocate time by marks, not by how much you know. Leaving a high-mark question unfinished costs far more than a slightly shorter answer to a low-mark one.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Revision and Exam Skills

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which memory store holds the knowledge you build up over years of school? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Long-term memory. Long-term memory is the large, lasting store. Working memory holds only a few items for seconds.

  2. Which of these is retrieval practice? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Writing everything you remember about a topic before checking. Retrieval practice means recalling without looking. Reading, highlighting and watching are all input, not retrieval.

  3. What is the spacing effect? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Spreading study over separate sessions improves long-term memory. The spacing effect is about time: the same total study is remembered better when it is spread out.

  4. A student says, "I read my notes three times and they looked really familiar, so I'm ready." What is the problem? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Familiarity is recognition, but the exam needs recall from memory. Recognising material on a page is easier than recalling it without prompts. The student should test themselves before deciding they are ready.

  5. In Roediger and Karpicke's study, what happened one week after learning? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The retrieval group remembered much more than the rereaders. Rereading gave a small advantage at five minutes, but after a week the students who had practised retrieval remembered far more.

  6. A student has four weeks until a chemistry mock. Which plan uses spacing best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Visit each topic in week 1, again in week 2 and again in week 4. Revisiting topics across the four weeks interrupts forgetting. Doing each once, or all at the end, is massed practice.

  7. Which revision plan best fits what is known about memory? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Short self-tests on each topic spread across several weeks. Spaced, active retrieval slows forgetting. Cramming, highlighting and copying feel productive but give weaker long-term recall.

  8. Why do many students prefer rereading even though retrieval practice works better? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Rereading feels fluent and easy, which is mistaken for learning. Rereading creates a feeling of fluency. Retrieval feels harder, but that difficulty is what makes it effective; it is a "desirable difficulty".

  9. Which is the weakest feature of this plan: "Every evening, 5pm to 11pm, revise one subject, no breaks"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Six hours without breaks is unrealistic and will soon be abandoned. Overlong sessions with no rest cause fatigue and poor concentration. Shorter focused sessions with breaks are more effective and more sustainable.

Key takeaways

  • Every time you successfully pull something out of long-term memory, it becomes easier to retrieve next time. Good revision is built around retrieving, not just rereading.
  • Testing yourself is not just checking what you know. The act of recalling strengthens the memory, so frequent low-stakes self-testing is one of the best uses of revision time.
  • Spread each topic across several short sessions over weeks, and plan the return visits in advance, so forgetting is interrupted before it wins.
  • Use worked examples while a method is new, then switch to mixed (interleaved) practice so you learn to choose the right method as well as carry it out.
  • Flashcards work because they force retrieval, and the Leitner system adds spacing automatically, sending your weakest cards round most often.
  • Visuals and summaries help when you produce them from memory and they show connections. Copying them neatly from a book is mainly colouring in.

Common mistakes in Revision and Exam Skills

  • Thinking that if notes feel familiar you know them. Recognising information when you see it is much easier than recalling it from a blank page, which is what an exam asks you to do.
  • Looking at the answer too soon. If you glance at your notes the moment you hesitate, you skip the effortful part that does the work. Give yourself time to struggle, then check.
  • Making a timetable so packed that it collapses in the first week. A plan that leaves time for meals, exercise, friends and sleep is one you will actually follow.
  • Staying on blocked practice because it feels good. Getting every question right when they are all the same type can hide the fact that you couldn't pick the method on a mixed paper.
  • Turning the card over before really trying to answer, or saying "I knew that" after reading the back. Say or write the answer first, then check honestly.
  • Highlighting as a revision method. Dunlosky's review rated highlighting and underlining as low utility: it feels productive but rarely improves later recall, and can make you focus on isolated facts.

Lessons in this school

  1. How memory works
    Revision is really about memory: getting information into long-term memory and being able to get it back out under exam conditions.
  2. Retrieval practice
    Retrieval practice means bringing information back to mind from memory, usually by testing yourself.
  3. Spaced practice and revision timetables
    Spaced practice means spreading study of a topic over several sessions with gaps in between, instead of cramming it all at once.
  4. Interleaving and worked examples
    Interleaving means mixing different types of problem or topic in one practice session, and worked examples show every step of a solution.
  5. Flashcards and the Leitner system
    Flashcards are a simple way to combine retrieval practice with spacing.
  6. Mind maps, dual coding and summaries
    Mind maps, diagrams and summaries can help you organise a topic, but only if you make them in a way that makes you think.
  7. Past papers and mark schemes
    Past papers are the closest thing to the real exam, and mark schemes show exactly what examiners reward.
  8. Command words
    Command words are the instruction words at the start of an exam question, such as "describe", "explain" and "evaluate".
  9. Planning extended answers
    Six-mark science questions, nine-mark geography questions and long English answers reward organised, linked writing.
  10. Timing and exam technique
    Knowing the content is only half of it: in the exam you also need to read questions carefully, manage time and avoid careless errors.
  11. Exam stress and wellbeing
    Most students feel nervous before exams, and a little stress can sharpen focus.
  12. Making a revision plan
    This lesson brings the school together: you audit what you need to know, choose methods that work, build a spaced and interleaved timetable, and check and adjust it as exams approach.

Revision and Exam Skills: questions students ask

What is the testing effect?

Being tested on something makes you remember it better later than spending the same time restudying it.

What is a faded worked example?

A sequence of examples where steps are removed one at a time, so you gradually complete more of the solution yourself.

In the Leitner system, what happens to a card you answer wrongly?

It goes back to Box 1, which is reviewed most often.
