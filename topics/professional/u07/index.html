Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What does it mean for data to be fit for purpose?

Its quality is good enough for the use it will be put to. From “Data literacy” →

In plain English: Almost every job now involves reading, using or questioning data. Key idea: Data literacy is the ability to read, use, communicate and question data; knowing data types and checking accuracy, completeness, uniqueness, consistency, timeliness and validity helps you judge whether data is fit for purpose.

How do you calculate percentage change?

New minus old, divided by old, multiplied by 100. From “Descriptive statistics for work” →

In plain English: A few simple statistics summarise most workplace data. Key idea: The mean, median and mode summarise typical values, with the median more robust to outliers; range, interquartile range and standard deviation describe spread, and percentage change and percentage points must be calculated from the correct base and not confused.

If a risk rises from 1 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000, what is the absolute increase?

1 in 1,000. From “Spotting misleading statistics” →

In plain English: Statistics can mislead by accident or by design. Key idea: Statistics mislead through confusing correlation with causation, relative with absolute change, small or selected samples, survivorship bias and visual tricks; asking who produced a claim, what was measured, compared with what and whether something else explains it exposes most problems.

What password approach does the NCSC suggest?

Three random words. From “Cyber security at work” →

In plain English: Most cyber attacks on organisations exploit everyday behaviour. Key idea: Phishing is the most common attack on organisations, alongside ransomware and payment fraud; checking senders and links, verifying payment changes by a known route, strong passwords, two-step verification, prompt updates and quick reporting protect people and organisations.

What are the four WCAG principles?

Perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. From “Digital accessibility” →

In plain English: Digital content must work for everyone, including disabled people. Key idea: UK law requires reasonable adjustments and public bodies must meet accessibility regulations; WCAG's perceivable, operable, understandable and robust principles guide design, and headings, alt text, descriptive links, good contrast and not relying on colour make everyday documents accessible.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Data and Digital Skills at Work

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is an example of ordinal data? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Satisfaction ratings from poor to excellent. Applying the standard result: Ordinal categories have a natural order.

  2. Which function would total sales only for the North region? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    SUMIF. Applying the standard result: SUMIF adds values that meet a condition.

  3. What is the median of 2, 9, 4, 7 and 3? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    4. Sorted, the values are 2, 3, 4, 7, 9, so the middle value is 4.

  4. A manager's dashboard shows different staff numbers from the HR system. Which quality dimension is most clearly at issue? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Consistency. Consistency asks whether data matches across systems.

  5. A formula =B4*C1 works in row 4 but gives wrong answers when copied down. What is the most likely fix? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Make C1 absolute as $C$1. The rate cell must stay fixed while the row reference changes.

  6. Staff turnover rose from 8% to 10%. How should this be described? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A rise of 2 percentage points, or 25%. The difference is 2 points; 2 divided by 8 is a 25% relative increase.

  7. You are asked to base a staffing decision on a survey that only 12 of 200 staff answered. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Use it cautiously and say that the low response limits it. Low completeness means results may not reflect the whole group.

  8. A finance team relies on a large forecasting spreadsheet built by one person. Which step best reduces the risk of errors? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Have a colleague review it and document assumptions. Independent review and documentation catch errors one person misses.

  9. A report gives the mean salary in a firm of 20 staff, where the owner earns ten times the others. Which change would improve it? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Report the median salary as well. The median is not distorted by the one very high earner.

Key takeaways

  • Data literacy is the ability to read, use, communicate and question data; knowing data types and checking accuracy, completeness, uniqueness, consistency, timeliness and validity helps you judge whether data is fit for purpose.
  • Formulas with correct relative and absolute references, functions such as SUMIF and XLOOKUP and pivot tables make spreadsheets powerful; clean table structure, separated inputs and calculations, validation, checks and peer review prevent common and costly errors.
  • The mean, median and mode summarise typical values, with the median more robust to outliers; range, interquartile range and standard deviation describe spread, and percentage change and percentage points must be calculated from the correct base and not confused.
  • Choose bar charts for comparison, line charts for time, scatter plots for relationships and tables for exact values; clear titles, labels and minimal clutter aid understanding, while zero-based bar axes, consistent scales and not relying on colour keep charts honest and accessible.
  • Effective data stories start from the audience and a one-sentence big idea, move from context to finding, insight and recommendation, highlight what matters and are honest about uncertainty, with detail kept in reserve.
  • Statistics mislead through confusing correlation with causation, relative with absolute change, small or selected samples, survivorship bias and visual tricks; asking who produced a claim, what was measured, compared with what and whether something else explains it exposes most problems.

Common mistakes in Data and Digital Skills at Work

  • Assuming data is correct because it is in a system or a spreadsheet. Always ask where it came from and check its quality before relying on it.
  • Typing fixed numbers such as rates directly into formulas. Put them in one labelled cell and use an absolute reference so they can be changed safely.
  • Confusing percentage points with percentages. A rise from 10% to 12% is 2 percentage points but a 20% increase.
  • Using a pie chart for many similar categories. People judge angles poorly, so use a sorted bar chart instead.
  • Showing every chart from your analysis. The audience needs the few findings that support the message, not the whole exploration.
  • Being impressed by a large relative change. Always ask for the absolute numbers: doubling a tiny risk is still a tiny risk.

Data and Digital Skills at Work: questions students ask

What is the difference between a relative and an absolute reference?

A relative reference changes when copied; an absolute one stays fixed.

Why does the ONS usually report median rather than mean earnings?

A few very high earners pull the mean upwards, so the median is more typical.

Why should a bar chart's value axis start at zero?

Because bar length represents the value, so a cut axis exaggerates differences.

What is the big idea in a data story?

A one-sentence statement of your key point and why it matters.
