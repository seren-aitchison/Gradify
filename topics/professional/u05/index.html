Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

According to goal-setting theory, which goals produce the highest performance?

Specific, challenging goals that people are committed to and get feedback on. From “Setting goals: SMART objectives and OKRs” →

In plain English: Clear goals direct effort and make progress visible. Key idea: Specific, challenging goals with commitment and feedback improve performance; SMART objectives are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, while OKRs pair an inspiring objective with a few measurable key results that track outcomes rather than tasks.

What is time-blocking?

Assigning specific tasks to fixed slots in your calendar. From “Planning your week” →

In plain English: A short weekly plan connects big goals to daily action. Key idea: A regular weekly review links goals to action; time-blocking puts priorities into the calendar at peak-energy times with batches and buffers, and allowing for the planning fallacy by using past durations makes plans realistic.

What is an implementation intention?

An "if-then" plan linking a specific situation to a specific action. From “Procrastination” →

In plain English: Procrastination is common and costly but is not simply laziness. Key idea: Procrastination is voluntary delay despite expecting to be worse off, driven by mood repair, aversive or vague tasks, low self-efficacy and distant rewards; clear next actions, implementation intentions, interim deadlines, short starts and self-compassion help reduce it.

What are the three parts of Ury's positive no?

Yes to your priorities, no to the request, yes to an alternative or the relationship. From “Saying no and managing expectations” →

In plain English: Taking on everything leads to overload and missed commitments. Key idea: Over-commitment harms quality and trust; assertive refusals acknowledge the request, give an honest reason, say no clearly and offer an alternative, and expectations are managed by agreeing scope and deadlines, asking what to drop and renegotiating early.

Which is more effective for long-term memory: re-reading or self-testing?

Self-testing (retrieval practice). From “How adults learn” →

In plain English: Continuing to learn is part of professional life. Key idea: Adults learn best when learning is relevant, problem-centred and self-directed; Kolb's cycle moves from experience through reflection and conceptualisation to experimentation, and retrieval and spaced practice are well supported, while matching teaching to learning styles is not.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Productivity and Personal Effectiveness

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In goal-setting theory, which kind of goal usually leads to the best performance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A specific and challenging goal. Locke and Latham found specific, difficult goals outperform "do your best" goals.

  2. Which task is important but not urgent for a team leader? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Planning next year's skills training. Planning and development work matters but rarely has an immediate deadline.

  3. What is the planning fallacy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Underestimating how long our own tasks will take. Kahneman and Tversky described the tendency to underestimate task completion times.

  4. Which of these is the best-written key result for the objective "Make onboarding painless for new starters"? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    90% of new starters rate onboarding 4 out of 5 or higher. A key result is a measurable outcome; the other options are tasks or vague intentions.

  5. What is the recommended action for Quadrant 3 tasks? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Delegate, batch or decline them. Urgent but unimportant tasks should not crowd out important work.

  6. Where should you schedule your most demanding work in a time-blocked week? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    In your peak energy hours. Demanding work goes best when attention and energy are highest.

  7. A new analyst faces a complex task she has never done before. What does goal-setting research suggest? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Set a learning goal to find effective strategies first. For complex, unfamiliar tasks, learning goals often work better than tough performance targets.

  8. A manager is in constant crisis mode, firefighting problems every day. Which change is most likely to help in the long term? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Protect time each week for prevention and planning. Investing in important, non-urgent prevention work reduces future crises.

  9. A colleague's time-blocked plan collapses by Tuesday every week. What is the most likely fix? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Add buffer time and plan to fill only part of the week. Plans without slack break at the first overrun or interruption.

Key takeaways

  • Specific, challenging goals with commitment and feedback improve performance; SMART objectives are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, while OKRs pair an inspiring objective with a few measurable key results that track outcomes rather than tasks.
  • Urgent tasks demand attention now, while important tasks advance goals; the four-quadrant matrix helps you do crises, schedule important non-urgent work, delegate or batch urgent trivia and drop time-wasters, and investing in Quadrant 2 reduces future crises.
  • A regular weekly review links goals to action; time-blocking puts priorities into the calendar at peak-energy times with batches and buffers, and allowing for the planning fallacy by using past durations makes plans realistic.
  • Interruptions and task switching raise stress, errors and attention residue; deep work is focused, demanding work that creates value, and it can be protected with focus blocks, switched-off notifications, timed work intervals and team norms.
  • Procrastination is voluntary delay despite expecting to be worse off, driven by mood repair, aversive or vague tasks, low self-efficacy and distant rewards; clear next actions, implementation intentions, interim deadlines, short starts and self-compassion help reduce it.
  • Information overload harms decisions and wellbeing; processing each email with delete, do, delegate or defer, batching email at set times, switching off notifications and sending fewer, clearer messages keep email under control.

Common mistakes in Productivity and Personal Effectiveness

  • Writing key results as a to-do list. A key result is a measurable outcome that shows success, not an activity you plan to carry out.
  • Treating every urgent request as important. Ask whether the task advances your goals or responsibilities before letting it push out planned work.
  • Filling every hour of the calendar. A plan with no buffer collapses at the first interruption, so leave unscheduled time.
  • Believing you can multitask on demanding work. The brain switches between tasks rather than doing both at once, and each switch carries a cost.
  • Calling procrastination laziness or a time management failure alone. It is often about managing uncomfortable emotions, so strategies should make starting easier and less aversive.
  • Using the inbox as a to-do list. Important actions get buried among new messages, so move them to a task list and clear the inbox.
