Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What makes a decision strategic?

It is long-term, affects the whole organisation, commits major resources and is hard to reverse. From “What is strategy?” →

In plain English: Strategy is the long-term direction of an organisation: the choices about where it competes and how it will win. Key idea: Strategy sets long-term direction through choices and trade-offs about where and how to compete; it operates at corporate, business and functional levels.

Name Porter's five forces.

Threat of new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among competitors. From “Porter's five forces” →

In plain English: Porter's five forces framework analyses the competitive structure of an industry to judge how attractive it is. Key idea: Five forces analysis judges industry attractiveness: strong entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes and rivalry all reduce average profitability, and firms seek positions where the forces are weakest.

What is the difference between a resource and a capability?

A resource is something the firm has; a capability is what it can do by combining resources. From “Resources and capabilities” →

In plain English: The resource-based view explains competitive advantage by what a firm has and can do, not just by the industry it is in. Key idea: The RBV explains advantage by firm-specific resources and capabilities, especially intangible ones that are hard to copy; dynamic capabilities let firms renew them as markets change.

What does "stuck in the middle" mean?

A firm that tries to pursue both low cost and differentiation but achieves neither. From “Porter's generic strategies” →

In plain English: Porter argued that a firm can achieve competitive advantage through lower cost or differentiation, across a broad market or a narrow focus. Key idea: Advantage comes from lower cost or valued differentiation, across a broad market or a focused segment; firms must make clear choices to avoid being stuck in the middle.

What is a strategic alliance?

Co-operation between firms without forming a new company, such as sharing technology or routes. From “Mergers, acquisitions and alliances” →

In plain English: Firms can grow organically or by combining with others through mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures and strategic alliances. Key idea: Firms combine through mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures and alliances for synergies and speed, but many acquisitions destroy value through overpaying, culture clash and failed integration.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Strategy

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a strategic decision? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Acquiring a rival company in another country. Acquisitions commit major resources over the long term and affect the whole organisation.

  2. Which statement is a vision rather than a mission? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "To be the world's most trusted name in electric bikes by 2035". A vision describes a desired future state; the others are a mission, a value and an objective.

  3. According to Mintzberg, what is an emergent strategy? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A pattern of action that develops without being explicitly intended. Emergent strategy forms from patterns in decisions over time.

  4. A car maker decides how its marketing department will support a move into electric vehicles. Which level of strategy is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Functional. Supporting the business strategy within one department is functional-level strategy.

  5. A charity's mission is to reduce child poverty. It is offered sponsorship by a payday lender. How should the mission guide the decision? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It sets boundaries: the sponsorship may conflict with the charity's purpose and values. Missions and values limit acceptable options, especially where a partner's activities harm the people served.

  6. A software firm plans to sell to large banks, but a feature built for one client is adopted rapidly by small businesses, and the firm refocuses on them. What is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Emergent strategy becoming part of realised strategy. The new focus wasn't intended but emerged from customer response and was then adopted.

  7. Two airlines both cut costs by copying each other's check-in technology. Using Porter, what is the best analysis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They are improving operational effectiveness, which rivals can copy, rather than building a distinct strategy. Porter argues imitable efficiency gains don't give lasting advantage; strategy needs a different set of activities and trade-offs.

  8. A bank's mission statement praises customer care, but its bonuses reward selling products regardless of need. What is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The statement is weak, because behaviour follows rewards rather than the published words. The Ashridge model stresses alignment of purpose, standards and values; misaligned rewards undermine a mission.

  9. When is a highly planned approach to strategy most suitable? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    In stable environments where large, coordinated, long-term investments are needed. Planning works best when conditions are predictable and coordination of big commitments, such as power stations, is essential.

Key takeaways

  • Strategy sets long-term direction through choices and trade-offs about where and how to compete; it operates at corporate, business and functional levels.
  • Mission states purpose, vision states aspiration, values guide behaviour, and objectives make them measurable; together they frame strategic choice.
  • Realised strategy blends deliberate plans with emergent patterns; planning gives direction and coordination, while emergence allows learning and adaptation.
  • Five forces analysis judges industry attractiveness: strong entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes and rivalry all reduce average profitability, and firms seek positions where the forces are weakest.
  • Industries move from introduction through growth and shake-out to maturity and decline; the key success factors change from innovation to efficiency, and strategy must change with them.
  • SWOT separates internal strengths and weaknesses from external opportunities and threats; a TOWS matrix turns them into strategic options.

Common mistakes in Strategy

  • Confusing strategy with being efficient. Doing things better than rivals (operational effectiveness) matters, but rivals can copy it; strategy requires a distinct position and deliberate trade-offs.
  • Assuming a published mission statement shows what an organisation actually does. Statements are often generic; judge them against decisions, resource allocation and behaviour.
  • Treating emergent strategy as having no strategy. Emergent strategy is a consistent pattern that develops through action and learning, often recognised and then formalised by managers.
  • Confusing substitutes with competitors. A substitute meets the same need in a different way (trains for domestic flights); a competitor offers a similar product (another airline).
  • Assuming decline is inevitable or that stage durations are predictable. Innovation can renew an industry (as streaming renewed music), and stages can last years or decades.
  • Listing opportunities that are really actions, such as "open new stores". Opportunities are external conditions; actions are strategic options that come from matching factors in TOWS.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is strategy?
    Strategy is the long-term direction of an organisation: the choices about where it competes and how it will win.
  2. Mission, vision and objectives
    Mission and vision statements express why an organisation exists and what it hopes to become, and strategic objectives turn them into targets.
  3. Planned and emergent strategy
    Some strategies are deliberately planned; others emerge from patterns of action.
  4. Porter's five forces
    Porter's five forces framework analyses the competitive structure of an industry to judge how attractive it is.
  5. The industry life cycle
    Industries, like products, pass through stages of introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
  6. SWOT analysis
    SWOT analysis summarises an organisation's internal strengths and weaknesses and the external opportunities and threats it faces.
  7. Resources and capabilities
    The resource-based view explains competitive advantage by what a firm has and can do, not just by the industry it is in.
  8. The value chain
    Porter's value chain breaks a firm's activities into primary and support activities to show where value and cost are created.
  9. Core competences and the VRIO framework
    Not every strength gives lasting advantage.
  10. Porter's generic strategies
    Porter argued that a firm can achieve competitive advantage through lower cost or differentiation, across a broad market or a narrow focus.
  11. The Ansoff matrix
    The Ansoff matrix sets out four directions for growth based on whether products and markets are existing or new.
  12. The Boston Matrix and portfolio analysis
    Portfolio analysis helps a firm decide how to allocate resources between its products or business units.
  13. Mergers, acquisitions and alliances
    Firms can grow organically or by combining with others through mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures and strategic alliances.
  14. Blue ocean strategy
    Blue ocean strategy argues that firms can create uncontested market space rather than competing head-on.
  15. Strategy implementation
    A strategy only creates value once it is put into practice.
  16. The balanced scorecard
    The balanced scorecard measures performance from four perspectives, not just financial results.
