Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name the four factors of production.

Land, labour, capital and enterprise. From “What is a business?” →

In plain English: A business is an organisation that combines resources to produce goods or services that meet customers' needs, usually in exchange for payment. Key idea: A business is defined by what it does, combining resources to create goods or services that customers value, not by whether it makes a profit or by its size.

What is the third sector?

Independent, not-for-private-profit organisations, such as charities, voluntary and community groups and social enterprises, that reinvest surpluses in a social purpose. From “The private, public and third sectors” →

In plain English: The UK economy is organised into three broad sectors by who owns organisations and why they exist: the private sector, the public sector and the third (voluntary and community) sector. Key idea: The sectors differ in who owns the organisation and what it is for: profit for private owners, public service for the state, and social purpose for the third sector.

What is an asset lock?

A legal restriction on a CIC that prevents its assets and profits being transferred away from its community purpose, including a cap on dividends. From “Social enterprises and co-operatives” →

In plain English: Not every business is owned by investors seeking returns. Key idea: Social and mutual models change who owns a business and therefore what it aims for: members' or communities' benefit rather than returns to outside investors.

What is a niche market?

A small, specialised segment of a larger market, with distinct needs that a few firms target. From “Markets and competition” →

In plain English: A market is where buyers and sellers exchange goods or services. Key idea: The more intense the competition, the less control a firm has over its price; businesses therefore seek competitive advantage, through lower costs or differentiation, to protect their margins.

Give one business opportunity created by an ageing population.

Higher demand for health and care services, accessible products, retirement housing or later-life financial services. From “Technology and social change” →

In plain English: Technological and social change create new markets and destroy old ones. Key idea: Technological and social change reshape demand and the way products are made and sold; firms that monitor and adapt early gain, while those that defend an outdated model decline.

Quick quiz: test yourself on The Business Environment

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which of these is a service rather than a good? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A car insurance policy. A service is intangible: insurance provides cover and peace of mind rather than a physical object, whereas bread, phones and trainers are tangible goods.

  2. Which is an objective rather than an aim? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Raise online sales by 15% by the end of the next financial year. Only the first has a measure and a deadline. The others are broad aims or values: useful for direction but impossible to check.

  3. Which group is an external stakeholder? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A local residents' association. Employees, managers and owners are internal stakeholders; the local community is external to the organisation.

  4. A florist buys flowers and wrapping for £8 and sells a bouquet for £30. Which statement is correct? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The florist adds £22 of value per bouquet, from which wages, rent and profit must be paid. Added value is £30 − £8 = £22. It is not profit, because the florist's labour, rent and other overheads have not yet been paid; skill and presentation are how the value is added.

  5. A restaurant sets the objective "improve customer satisfaction soon". Which change does most to make it SMART? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Raise the average online review score from 4.1 to 4.5 by June. Only the first adds a measurable target and a deadline. The other changes leave it vague, so nobody could tell whether it had been met.

  6. A regulator has the power to fine a company but has shown little interest in its new product. Using Mendelow's matrix, how should the company treat it? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Keep it satisfied. High power and low interest means keep satisfied: if the regulator's interest is aroused it becomes a key player, so the company should avoid giving it reasons for concern.

  7. Which is the strongest reason for defining a business without reference to profit? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Charities, social enterprises and public bodies also combine resources to produce valued goods and services. The defining activity, producing goods or services for customers using combined resources, is shared by organisations with very different goals. Losses or measurement problems don't change what a business is.

  8. A family-owned brewery turns down a takeover that would have doubled the owners' wealth. What best explains this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Its owners value independence and continuity, objectives that aren't purely financial. Objectives reflect who owns the business and what they value. Turning down an offer is a rational choice when independence matters more to the owners than wealth; nothing prevents the sale legally.

  9. Which is the best evaluation of section 172 of the Companies Act 2006? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It requires directors to consider stakeholders, but the duty is owed to the company for its members, so shareholders' interests remain primary. Section 172 is an "enlightened shareholder value" duty: directors must have regard to stakeholders while promoting success for the members. Only the company (or shareholders in a derivative claim) can enforce it.

Key takeaways

  • A business is defined by what it does, combining resources to create goods or services that customers value, not by whether it makes a profit or by its size.
  • Objectives turn purpose into measurable targets; a SMART objective can be checked, assigned and reviewed, which is what makes it useful for control.
  • Stakeholders' interests often conflict, so managers must decide whose interests to prioritise, and a power–interest map helps them decide whom to consult, satisfy or simply inform.
  • The sectors differ in who owns the organisation and what it is for: profit for private owners, public service for the state, and social purpose for the third sector.
  • Sole traders and ordinary partners have unlimited liability because the business has no separate legal identity: the owners' personal assets are at risk for business debts.
  • Incorporation separates the company from its owners: shareholders have limited liability, the company has continuity, and in a plc ownership (shareholders) is often divorced from control (directors).

Common mistakes in The Business Environment

  • Confusing added value with profit. Added value is selling price minus bought-in inputs; profit is what remains after all costs, including wages and overheads, have also been paid.
  • Treating a mission statement as an objective. "To delight our customers" is an aim or mission; without a measure and a deadline it can't guide decisions or show whether the business succeeded.
  • Assuming stakeholder positions are fixed. Interest and power change with the decision and over time; a matrix is a snapshot to revisit.
  • Mixing up the public sector with public limited companies. A plc such as BP is in the private sector: "public" refers to its shares being offered to the public, not to state ownership.
  • Thinking a partnership must be formally registered to exist. An ordinary partnership arises whenever people run a business together for profit, even without a written agreement, with the legal consequences that follow.
  • Assuming every plc is listed on the stock market. "Plc" means a company may offer shares to the public; listing is a separate step.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is a business?
    A business is an organisation that combines resources to produce goods or services that meet customers' needs, usually in exchange for payment.
  2. Business purpose and objectives
    Every organisation needs to know what it is for and what it is trying to achieve.
  3. Stakeholders
    Stakeholders are the individuals and groups who affect or are affected by a business.
  4. The private, public and third sectors
    The UK economy is organised into three broad sectors by who owns organisations and why they exist: the private sector, the public sector and the third (voluntary and community) sector.
  5. Sole traders and partnerships
    Most UK businesses are run by one person or a small group without a separate legal identity.
  6. Private and public limited companies
    A company is a separate legal person, owned by shareholders and run by directors, whose owners have limited liability.
  7. Social enterprises and co-operatives
    Not every business is owned by investors seeking returns.
  8. Franchising
    Franchising lets a business grow by licensing others to run outlets under its brand and system.
  9. PESTLE analysis
    Businesses operate in an external environment they can't control but must anticipate.
  10. Markets and competition
    A market is where buyers and sellers exchange goods or services.
  11. The economic environment
    Every business is affected by the state of the wider economy.
  12. The legal and regulatory environment
    Law sets the rules within which businesses compete.
  13. Technology and social change
    Technological and social change create new markets and destroy old ones.
  14. Globalisation and international business
    Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, technology and people.
  15. Business size and growth
    Businesses range from one-person enterprises to global corporations.
  16. Why businesses fail
    Many new businesses close within a few years, and even large, famous firms collapse.

The Business Environment: questions students ask

How is added value calculated?

Selling price minus the cost of bought-in materials and services.

What does SMART stand for?

Specific, measurable, achievable, realistic (or relevant) and time-bound.

What is the difference between a shareholder and a stakeholder?

A shareholder owns shares in a company; a stakeholder is anyone affected by or able to affect the business. All shareholders are stakeholders, but not all stakeholders are shareholders.

What does unlimited liability mean?

The owners are personally responsible for all the business's debts, so their personal assets can be taken to pay creditors.
