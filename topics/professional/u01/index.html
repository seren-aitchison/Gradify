Key terms and formulas at a glance

Noise
Anything that distorts or blocks a message between sender and receiver, whether physical, semantic, psychological or cultural
Active listening
Listening for the speaker's full meaning, including feelings, and showing understanding through attention, paraphrase, reflection and summary
Funnel technique
Sequencing questions from broad open questions through probing questions to closed questions that confirm detail
Paralanguage
The vocal features that accompany speech, such as tone, pitch, pace, volume and pauses
Active voice
A sentence in which the subject does the action: "The manager signed the contract"
Pyramid principle
Minto's method of structuring writing with the main answer first, supported by grouped arguments and then evidence
Call to action
The specific action you want the reader to take, with a deadline
Register
The level of formality in language, chosen to suit the audience and situation
Agenda
A list of the items a meeting will cover, ideally phrased as outcomes with owners and timings
Action log
A running list of actions from meetings, each with an owner, deadline and status
Key message
The single main point the audience should remember and act on

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What one-sentence test shows you are ready to write a message?

Completing "After reading this, I want [who] to [know, decide or do what] by [when]." From “Communication at work: purpose and audience” →

In plain English: Every message at work exists to make something happen: a decision, an action, a change in understanding. Key idea: Decide purpose and audience before content. The same facts, ordered and worded for a different reader, become a different message.

Why avoid multiple questions?

Bundling several questions together confuses the listener, who usually answers only the last or easiest one. From “Questioning skills” →

In plain English: Good questions uncover facts, test assumptions and help people think. Key idea: Use open questions to explore, probing questions to deepen, and closed questions to confirm. Avoid leading and multiple questions when accuracy matters.

What does MECE mean?

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive: points don't overlap and together cover everything important. From “Structuring reports and briefings” →

In plain English: A report or briefing exists to support a decision. Key idea: Lead with the answer: executive summary and recommendation first, then the supporting arguments, then the detail.

When should you not hold a meeting?

When the purpose is only to share information that could be read, or when the person who must decide can't attend. From “Running effective meetings” →

In plain English: Meetings take a large share of professional time and are often judged a poor use of it. Key idea: Every meeting needs a purpose, an agenda of outcomes, the right people and a chair who turns discussion into decisions and actions.

What is the illusion of transparency?

Overestimating how visible our nervousness or other feelings are to other people. From “Delivering a presentation” →

In plain English: Delivery turns a well-planned talk into one people remember. Key idea: Rehearse aloud, pause on key points, and treat nerves as energy: the audience notices them far less than you do.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Professional Communication

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. In the transmission model, what does "noise" refer to? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Anything that distorts or blocks the message on its way to the receiver. Noise is interference of any kind: physical, semantic, psychological or cultural. Feedback is the reply; encoding is the sender's process; the channel is the medium.

  2. Which behaviour is part of active listening? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Summarising the speaker's main points before moving on. Summarising checks and shows understanding. Planning replies and jumping to solutions are barriers; total silence gives the speaker no sign you have understood.

  3. Which is an open question? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    "What were the main challenges on the project?". Open questions invite an explanation. The others invite yes/no, suggest an answer (leading) or limit the answer to two choices.

  4. You must tell a busy operations director that a supplier will deliver late. What should come first in your email? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The new delivery date, its impact and any decision you need from them. A senior reader needs the news and its consequence first so they can act; background can follow or be attached for those who want it.

  5. A team member says, "I'm worried the new system will make my job harder and nobody's asked us." Which response best shows active listening? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    "So you're concerned about the effect on your work and that the team wasn't consulted?". The paraphrase checks both the content (effect on work) and the concern (not consulted). The others reassure, generalise or redirect without showing understanding.

  6. You are investigating a complaint and need an accurate account from a witness. Which opening is best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    "Tell me in your own words what you saw that afternoon.". An open, neutral question gives the witness's own account. The others lead, bundle several questions or suggest an answer, all of which reduce accuracy and fairness.

  7. A project update is accurate, complete and grammatically perfect, yet the steering group takes no decision. What is the most likely reason? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It never made clear what decision was needed from them, or by when. Accuracy isn't enough: a message without a clear purpose and call to action leaves the reader unsure what to do. Channel and jargon are secondary here.

  8. A manager uses every listening technique in a one-to-one but types emails throughout. What is the most likely effect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The employee concludes they aren't really being listened to. Attention is visible. Divided attention undermines the techniques, because speakers judge listening by behaviour as much as by words.

  9. A manager asks a struggling employee, "Why did you do it that way?" and the employee becomes defensive. Which rewording is most likely to help? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    "What led you to choose that approach?". "What led you to…" keeps curiosity without the accusing tone "why" can carry. The others lead, blame or are closed.

Key takeaways

  • Decide purpose and audience before content. The same facts, ordered and worded for a different reader, become a different message.
  • Check your understanding before you respond: a paraphrase or summary lets the speaker correct you and shows you were listening.
  • Use open questions to explore, probing questions to deepen, and closed questions to confirm. Avoid leading and multiple questions when accuracy matters.
  • When words and non-verbal signals conflict, people tend to trust the non-verbal signals for feelings and attitudes. That is the defensible version of Mehrabian's finding.
  • Plain English means the reader can find what they need, understand it and act on it the first time they read it.
  • Lead with the answer: executive summary and recommendation first, then the supporting arguments, then the detail.

Common mistakes in Professional Communication

  • Writing for yourself: putting things in the order you discovered them, or using the vocabulary of your own team, rather than in the order and words the reader needs.
  • Jumping straight to advice. Solving the problem you assume someone has, before they've finished, often solves the wrong problem and signals that you weren't listening.
  • Asking a leading question in an investigation or interview ("You saw him take it, didn't you?"), which can contaminate the answer and undermine fairness.
  • Treating body-language "rules" as lie detection. Research gives little support to the idea that people can reliably spot deception from non-verbal cues.
  • Thinking plain English means informal or imprecise. It is compatible with formal, technical and legal writing; it removes unnecessary difficulty, not necessary detail.
  • Writing the report in the order you did the work (background, method, every finding, then at last the conclusion), so the reader must reach the end to find out what you recommend.

Professional Communication: questions students ask

Who coined the term "active listening"?

Carl Rogers and Richard Farson, in 1957.

What is the funnel technique?

Starting with open questions, narrowing with probing questions, and finishing with closed questions to confirm details.

What does Mehrabian's research actually show?

When words and non-verbal cues about feelings conflict, people rely more on tone and facial expression. It is not a rule for all communication.

What average sentence length do plain English guides recommend?

About 15 to 20 words, with one main idea per sentence.
