Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Give three behaviours associated with professionalism.

Any three of competence, reliability, integrity, accountability, respect and confidentiality. From “What professionalism means” →

In plain English: Professionalism is about how you do your job, not just what job you do. Key idea: Professionalism means competence, reliability, integrity, accountability, respect and confidentiality in any role; professional bodies set standards through qualifications, codes of conduct, CPD and chartered status, and some professions are regulated by law.

What is a perceived conflict of interest?

One that others could reasonably believe exists, even if you are not influenced. From “Integrity and conflicts of interest” →

In plain English: A conflict of interest can undermine trust even when nothing goes wrong. Key idea: Conflicts of interest can be actual, potential or perceived and should be declared, registered and managed by stepping back from decisions; the Bribery Act 2010 makes giving or receiving bribes, bribing foreign officials and failing to prevent bribery offences.

What is victimisation under the Equality Act?

Treating someone badly because they made or supported a discrimination complaint. From “Discrimination, harassment and victimisation” →

In plain English: The Equality Act defines several forms of prohibited conduct. Key idea: Direct discrimination is less favourable treatment because of a protected characteristic; indirect discrimination is an unjustified practice that disadvantages a group; harassment violates dignity, victimisation punishes complaints, and employers must take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment.

Name two rights workers have.

Any two of minimum wage, paid holiday, rest breaks, discrimination and whistleblowing protection. From “Employment status” →

In plain English: Your rights at work depend on your legal employment status. Key idea: Employees, workers and the self-employed have different levels of rights, with employees having the most; status depends on the reality of the relationship, judged by tests such as personal service, control, mutuality of obligation and financial risk, as the Uber case showed.

How much carer's leave are employees entitled to?

One week of unpaid leave every 12 months. From “Family-friendly rights” →

In plain English: UK law gives working parents and carers rights to leave and pay. Key idea: Parents have rights to maternity, paternity, adoption and shared parental leave and pay, plus unpaid parental leave; carers have carer's leave and time off for dependants, and unfavourable treatment or dismissal because of pregnancy or family leave is unlawful.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Ethics, Professionalism and Employment Rights

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which behaviour best shows accountability? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Admitting your own error and fixing it. Accountability means owning decisions and outcomes.

  2. Which of these is one of the Seven Principles of Public Life? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Openness. The seven are selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty and leadership.

  3. Which approach judges actions mainly by their results? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Consequentialism. Consequentialism, such as utilitarianism, focuses on outcomes.

  4. Which is a feature of a legally regulated profession? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Practising without registration can be unlawful. Regulators such as the GMC control who may practise.

  5. What does impartiality require of a civil servant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Serving the government of the day whatever its party. Civil servants must serve successive governments equally.

  6. A manager calls cutting safety checks "just an efficiency decision". What does this illustrate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Ethical fading. Framing it purely as efficiency hides the ethical dimension.

  7. A consultant is asked to advise on a technical area she has never worked in. What is the most professional response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Explain her limits and suggest someone with that expertise. Competence includes knowing and being honest about the limits of your expertise.

  8. A company has a detailed ethics code, but senior managers regularly break it without consequence. What is the most likely effect? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Staff become cynical and treat the code as window dressing. Codes work only when leaders follow and enforce them.

  9. A salesperson is told to tell customers a product has "no known issues" when two faults are being investigated. Which test most clearly shows the problem? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Transparency: would he mind customers knowing the truth?. If he would not want the truth known, the action is likely unethical.

Key takeaways

  • Professionalism means competence, reliability, integrity, accountability, respect and confidentiality in any role; professional bodies set standards through qualifications, codes of conduct, CPD and chartered status, and some professions are regulated by law.
  • Codes of conduct turn values into expected behaviour on honesty, competence, confidentiality, conflicts of interest and fairness; the Nolan Principles and Civil Service Code set public standards, but codes need judgement, leadership and enforcement to work.
  • Consequentialist, duty-based and virtue ethics give different lenses on decisions; ethical behaviour needs awareness, judgement, motivation and action, while ethical fading can hide the moral side, and simple tests of transparency, effect and fairness help check choices.
  • Conflicts of interest can be actual, potential or perceived and should be declared, registered and managed by stepping back from decisions; the Bribery Act 2010 makes giving or receiving bribes, bribing foreign officials and failing to prevent bribery offences.
  • Whistleblowing is reporting wrongdoing in the public interest; qualifying disclosures based on reasonable belief are protected under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998, workers should usually raise concerns internally or with a prescribed person, and dismissal for whistleblowing is automatically unfair.
  • The Equality Act 2010 protects applicants, employees and workers from discrimination on nine protected characteristics at every stage of employment, places a public sector equality duty on public bodies and is enforced by the EHRC and through employment tribunals.

Common mistakes in Ethics, Professionalism and Employment Rights

  • Equating professionalism with formal dress or a job title. It is shown through behaviour, such as honesty, reliability and owning mistakes.
  • Assuming that anything not banned by the code is acceptable. Codes cannot list every case, so use their spirit and your judgement.
  • Framing a decision only in business terms. Ask explicitly whether it has an ethical dimension, or ethical fading may lead you astray.
  • Thinking a conflict only matters if you are actually influenced. Perceived conflicts also damage trust, so declare them too.
  • Believing you must have proof before speaking up. The law requires a reasonable belief, not proof.
  • Thinking the Act only protects current employees. Job applicants and some former employees are also protected.

Lessons in this school

  1. What professionalism means
    Professionalism is about how you do your job, not just what job you do.
  2. Professional codes of conduct
    Codes of conduct turn values into expected behaviour.
  3. Ethical decision making
    Ethical dilemmas at work rarely come labelled.
  4. Integrity and conflicts of interest
    A conflict of interest can undermine trust even when nothing goes wrong.
  5. Whistleblowing
    Whistleblowers expose wrongdoing that might otherwise stay hidden.
  6. The Equality Act 2010
    The Equality Act 2010 is the main law protecting people from discrimination in Great Britain.
  7. Discrimination, harassment and victimisation
    The Equality Act defines several forms of prohibited conduct.
  8. Reasonable adjustments
    Employers must remove barriers that disabled people face at work.
  9. Inclusion and unconscious bias
    Legal compliance is a minimum; inclusion is about everyone being able to contribute fully.
  10. Employment status
    Your rights at work depend on your legal employment status.
  11. The contract of employment
    The contract of employment sets out the rights and duties of employer and employee.
  12. Working time, pay and holiday
    Core rights on hours, pay and holiday apply to almost all workers.
  13. Family-friendly rights
    UK law gives working parents and carers rights to leave and pay.
  14. Discipline and grievance: the ACAS Code
    Problems at work should be handled fairly and consistently.
  15. Dismissal and redundancy basics
    Ending employment fairly protects both employees and employers.
  16. Getting help: ACAS and advice services
    Knowing where to get help early can resolve problems before they escalate.
