Key terms and formulas at a glance

Management

Interpersonal roles

Hawthorne effect
A change in behaviour caused by people knowing they are being studied

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What are the four functions of management?

Planning, organising, leading and controlling. From “What is management?” →

In plain English: Management is getting things done through other people and through the organisation's resources. Key idea: Management is a cycle of planning, organising, leading and controlling; the balance of technical, human and conceptual skill a manager needs shifts with their level.

What is the Hawthorne effect?

People changing their behaviour because they know they are being observed. From “The human relations movement” →

In plain English: The human relations movement shifted attention from tasks to people. Key idea: The human relations movement argued that productivity depends on social factors, group norms and recognition as well as pay and physical conditions.

What is the central claim of contingency theory?

There is no one best way to organise; the right structure depends on the situation. From “Contingency theory and organisational design” →

In plain English: Contingency theory says there is no one best way to organise: the right structure depends on the situation. Key idea: Structure should fit the situation: mechanistic for stable conditions, organic for uncertain and changing ones.

What is transactional leadership?

Leadership based on exchanging rewards for performance and correcting failure. From “Leadership theories” →

In plain English: Leadership theories try to explain what makes a leader effective. Key idea: Leadership theory moved from who leaders are, to what they do, to what fits the situation, to how leaders transform followers' goals.

What are Schein's three levels of culture?

Artefacts, espoused values and basic underlying assumptions. From “Organisational culture” →

In plain English: Organisational culture is "the way we do things around here". Key idea: Culture is shared assumptions as much as stated values; behaviour follows the deep assumptions, which is why culture is powerful and slow to change.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Management and Organisational Behaviour

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which management function involves comparing actual results with targets and correcting deviations? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Controlling. Controlling closes the cycle: it measures performance against the plan and takes corrective action, which then feeds into new plans.

  2. Which idea is most closely associated with Taylor? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Time and motion study to find the one best way. Time and motion study was central to scientific management. Unity of command and the scalar chain are Fayol's principles.

  3. In which group is the resource allocator role? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Decisional. Deciding who gets resources is a decision, so it is one of the four decisional roles alongside entrepreneur, disturbance handler and negotiator.

  4. A team leader on a factory line spends most of the day solving machine problems and showing staff how to set up jobs. Which skill is this manager mainly using? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Technical skill. Knowing how the work itself is done is technical skill, which Katz found matters most at first-line level.

  5. A burger chain sets exact timings for every step of making a burger and trains staff to follow them. Which theory does this most reflect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Scientific management. Measuring tasks, setting a standard method and training staff to follow it is Taylor's approach applied to modern service work.

  6. A sales director reads industry reports and attends trade fairs to learn what competitors are doing. Which role is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Monitor. Gathering information from inside and outside the organisation is the monitor role. Spokesperson would involve passing information out.

  7. A chief executive is praised for detailed budgets and tight cost control but staff say the firm has no sense of direction. Using Kotter's distinction, what is missing? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Leadership: setting a direction and aligning people behind it. Kotter separates management (coping with complexity through plans and control) from leadership (coping with change by setting direction). The CEO shows strong management but weak leadership.

  8. A software firm adopts strict unity of command but its projects need staff to work for several project leaders at once. What is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Fayol's principle assumes stable, simple work and conflicts with project-based structures. Classical principles assumed one best way for stable conditions. Project work often needs matrix structures, where staff report to more than one manager; contingency theory explains why.

  9. A student argues that Mintzberg proves Fayol was wrong. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Mintzberg described how managers spend their time; Fayol prescribed what management should achieve, so the views complement each other. Fayol's functions are about purpose; Mintzberg's roles describe observed activity. A manager allocating resources is still planning and controlling, just in fragmented, spoken bursts.

Key takeaways

  • Management is a cycle of planning, organising, leading and controlling; the balance of technical, human and conceptual skill a manager needs shifts with their level.
  • Taylor focused on the efficiency of individual tasks on the shop floor; Fayol focused on how the whole organisation should be managed from above. Both assumed there was one best way.
  • Mintzberg's roles describe what managers actually spend their time doing: interpersonal, informational and decisional work, done in short, interrupted bursts.
  • The human relations movement argued that productivity depends on social factors, group norms and recognition as well as pay and physical conditions.
  • Structure sets who reports to whom and how work is grouped; the trade-off is between control and specialisation on one side and speed and flexibility on the other.
  • Centralisation buys consistency and control; decentralisation buys speed and local responsiveness. Most firms centralise some decisions and decentralise others.

Common mistakes in Management and Organisational Behaviour

  • Treating management and leadership as the same thing. Kotter's distinction is useful: management copes with complexity (plans, budgets, control); leadership copes with change (direction, alignment, inspiration). Organisations need both.
  • Saying classical theory is obsolete. Its ideas live on in fast-food production lines, call-centre scripts, lean timings and warehouse picking targets, where work is still measured and standardised.
  • Thinking the roles are separate jobs done one after another. They form an integrated whole: one meeting can involve several roles at once.
  • Overstating the evidence. The studies had tiny samples, changed several things at once and were partly reinterpreted to fit Mayo's ideas. Re-analyses of the original lighting data find the "Hawthorne effect" much weaker than the legend.
  • Assuming flat is always better. A flat structure with spans too wide for the work, such as complex professional tasks, can leave staff unsupported and managers overloaded.
  • Confusing delegation with abdication. A manager who delegates a task still answers for it and must give the subordinate enough authority, resources and support.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is management?
    Management is getting things done through other people and through the organisation's resources.
  2. Classical management theory: Taylor and Fayol
    Classical management theory, developed around 1900–1920, tried to find the one best way to run work and organisations.
  3. Mintzberg's managerial roles
    Henry Mintzberg watched what managers actually did and found work very different from the tidy functions of classical theory.
  4. The human relations movement
    The human relations movement shifted attention from tasks to people.
  5. Organisational structure
    Structure is how an organisation divides, groups and co-ordinates its work.
  6. Centralisation and decentralisation
    Centralisation and decentralisation describe where decisions are made.
  7. Bureaucracy and Weber
    Max Weber described bureaucracy as the most rational and efficient form of organisation for large-scale administration.
  8. Contingency theory and organisational design
    Contingency theory says there is no one best way to organise: the right structure depends on the situation.
  9. Motivation: content theories
    Content theories of motivation ask what needs drive people at work.
  10. Motivation: process theories
    Process theories explain how people decide how much effort to put in.
  11. Leadership theories
    Leadership theories try to explain what makes a leader effective.
  12. Leadership styles
    A leadership style is a typical way a leader makes decisions and treats followers.
  13. Teams and group working
    Much work is done in groups and teams.
  14. Communication in organisations
    Communication is how information and meaning pass between people in an organisation.
  15. Organisational culture
    Organisational culture is "the way we do things around here".
  16. Power and politics in organisations
    Power is the capacity to influence others, and organisational politics is the use of power to pursue interests.
  17. Managing change
    Organisations must change to survive, but change often fails.
  18. Decision-making in organisations
    Managers make decisions under uncertainty and with limited time and information.

Management and Organisational Behaviour: questions students ask

What is a differential piece rate?

Paying a higher rate per unit to workers who meet the standard output, and a lower rate to those who don't.

What are Mintzberg's three groups of managerial roles?

Interpersonal, informational and decisional.

What did the bank wiring room show?

Informal groups set their own output norms and pressured members to keep to them.

What is a tall structure?

A hierarchy with many levels of management and narrow spans of control.
