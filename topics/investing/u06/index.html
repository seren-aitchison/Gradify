Key terms and formulas at a glance

Intrinsic value
An estimate of what an investment is worth, based on the cash it is expected to generate
Free cash flow
Cash generated by operations after the investment needed to sustain and grow the business
Dividend discount model
A model valuing a share as the present value of its expected future dividends
Gordon growth model
A formula valuing a share as D1 ÷ (r − g) when dividends grow at a constant rate for ever
Cost of equity
The return shareholders require for investing in a company's shares
Earnings per share

Price-to-earnings ratio

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is a margin of safety?

The gap between estimated intrinsic value and the price paid, protecting against forecasting error. From “Price and value” →

In plain English: The price of a share is what you pay; its value is what the business is worth to an owner. Key idea: Price is what the market charges and value is an estimate of what the cash flows are worth; intrinsic, relative and asset-based methods each estimate value, and a margin of safety protects against forecasting error.

What is the value of a constant dividend paid for ever?

The dividend divided by the required return. From “The dividend discount model” →

In plain English: The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of the dividends it is expected to pay. Key idea: The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of expected dividends; it suits steady payers but not firms that pay little or return cash through buybacks.

Why is diluted EPS usually lower than basic EPS?

It assumes options and convertibles become shares, spreading profit over more shares. From “Earnings per share” →

In plain English: Earnings per share (EPS) is the profit attributable to each ordinary share. Key idea: Basic EPS is profit for ordinary shareholders divided by the weighted average number of shares; diluted EPS allows for options and convertibles, and EPS growth should be checked against buybacks, one-offs and cash flow.

What is book value?

Shareholders' equity: total assets minus total liabilities. From “Price-to-book” →

In plain English: The price-to-book ratio compares a company's market value with the net assets shown in its accounts. Key idea: Price-to-book compares market value with accounting net assets; it is linked to ROE and works best for financial and asset-heavy firms, but understates the worth of companies built on intangibles.

How many years of earnings does CAPE average?

Ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. From “Valuing the whole market: CAPE” →

In plain English: The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, or CAPE, compares a market's price with its average real earnings over ten years. Key idea: CAPE divides a market's real price by its ten-year average real earnings; high values have historically been followed by lower long-run returns, but it is a poor guide to short-term timing.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Valuation

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes intrinsic value? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An estimate of worth based on the cash the business should generate. Intrinsic value is an opinion built from forecasts of future cash flows, not an observed price.

  2. In a DCF valuation, what is discounted to today? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Forecast free cash flows and a terminal value. A DCF discounts expected future cash flows, including a terminal value, back to their present value.

  3. Under the dividend discount model, what determines a share's value? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Its expected future dividends and the required return. The DDM discounts expected dividends at the required return; nothing else enters the formula directly.

  4. An analyst values a company by comparing its price-to-earnings ratio with five similar listed firms. Which approach is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Relative valuation. Comparing multiples with peers is relative valuation; it tells you whether a share is cheap compared with others, not in absolute terms.

  5. In a DCF, the analyst raises the discount rate from 8% to 10% and leaves the cash flows unchanged. What happens to the valuation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It falls, because each future cash flow is worth less today. Present values fall as the discount rate rises, and the effect is largest for distant cash flows.

  6. Why does an investor who plans to sell after three years reach the same DDM value as one who holds for ever? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The future sale price itself reflects the dividends expected after the sale. The buyer pays for the remaining dividends, so the sale price is the present value of the later dividends.

  7. Which investor most needs a wide margin of safety? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    One valuing a young loss-making firm with uncertain future revenues. The less predictable the cash flows, the larger the possible forecasting error and so the bigger the cushion needed.

  8. A DCF shows the terminal value is 75% of the total value. What is the best conclusion? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The valuation rests mainly on long-run assumptions, so test them hard. A high terminal-value share is common but means long-run growth and discount rate assumptions drive the answer.

  9. For which company is the dividend discount model least suitable? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A fast-growing technology firm that pays no dividend. With no current dividends, the model needs speculative forecasts of when and how much the firm will pay.

Key takeaways

  • Price is what the market charges and value is an estimate of what the cash flows are worth; intrinsic, relative and asset-based methods each estimate value, and a margin of safety protects against forecasting error.
  • A DCF values a business as the present value of its forecast free cash flows plus a terminal value, so the result depends heavily on the discount rate and long-run growth assumptions.
  • The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of expected dividends; it suits steady payers but not firms that pay little or return cash through buybacks.
  • The Gordon growth model values a share as next year's dividend divided by the required return minus growth; it is simple but very sensitive to the gap between r and g.
  • The cost of equity is the return shareholders require, estimated with CAPM, the dividend growth approach or a bond yield plus premium, and it combines with the after-tax cost of debt to give WACC.
  • Basic EPS is profit for ordinary shareholders divided by the weighted average number of shares; diluted EPS allows for options and convertibles, and EPS growth should be checked against buybacks, one-offs and cash flow.

Common mistakes in Valuation

  • Assuming a share that has fallen a long way must now be cheap. A lower price says nothing about value on its own; the business may have become worth less.
  • Presenting a DCF value to the penny as if it were precise. Show a range, because modest changes in inputs produce large changes in value.
  • Concluding that a company paying no dividend is worthless under the DDM. Its value comes from dividends it may pay in future, or from other payouts, which the model must forecast.
  • Using the dividend just paid in place of next year's dividend. The formula needs D1, so multiply D0 by (1 + g) first.
  • Treating the cost of equity as the dividend a company pays. It is the total return shareholders require, from dividends and growth, and it is an opportunity cost rather than a cash payment.
  • Assuming rising EPS always means a better business. EPS can rise through buybacks or one-off gains while underlying profits and returns are flat.

Lessons in this school

  1. Price and value
    The price of a share is what you pay; its value is what the business is worth to an owner.
  2. Discounted cash flow valuation
    A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation estimates what a business is worth by forecasting its future free cash flows and discounting them to today.
  3. The dividend discount model
    The dividend discount model values a share as the present value of the dividends it is expected to pay.
  4. The Gordon growth model
    The Gordon growth model values a share whose dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate for ever.
  5. The required return and the cost of equity
    Every valuation needs a discount rate, the return investors require for bearing the risk.
  6. Earnings per share
    Earnings per share (EPS) is the profit attributable to each ordinary share.
  7. The price-to-earnings ratio
    The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio compares a share's price with its earnings per share.
  8. Dividend yield and dividend cover
    Dividend yield shows the income a share pays relative to its price, and dividend cover shows how comfortably profits support that dividend.
  9. Price-to-book
    The price-to-book ratio compares a company's market value with the net assets shown in its accounts.
  10. Enterprise value and EV/EBITDA
    Enterprise value measures the value of the whole business, to shareholders and lenders together.
  11. Valuing a bond
    A bond's price is the present value of its coupons and its final repayment, discounted at the market yield.
  12. Valuing the whole market: CAPE
    The cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, or CAPE, compares a market's price with its average real earnings over ten years.
  13. Growth and value investing
    Growth and value are two broad styles of choosing shares: one pays up for fast-growing companies, the other buys shares that look cheap on measures such as price-to-book and P/E.
  14. The limits of valuation
    Every valuation method rests on uncertain forecasts and assumptions, and markets can stay away from estimated value for long periods.

Valuation: questions students ask

What is a terminal value in a DCF?

An estimate of the value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period.

What does the dividend discount model say a share is worth?

The present value of all its expected future dividends.

What condition must hold for the Gordon model to work?

The required return must be greater than the growth rate.

Why is the cost of debt adjusted for tax in WACC?

Interest is tax-deductible for companies, so the effective cost is the interest rate × (1 − tax rate).
