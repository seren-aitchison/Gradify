Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Who holds the assets of an authorised UK fund?

An independent depositary or trustee, separate from the fund manager. From “Why pool investments?” →

In plain English: A fund pools money from many investors and invests it as one portfolio. Key idea: A fund pools many investors' money into one portfolio, giving diversification, scale, professional management and access at a cost, with assets held separately from the manager.

What is an authorised participant?

A large dealer that creates or redeems ETF shares by exchanging them with the fund for the underlying securities. From “Exchange-traded funds” →

In plain English: Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are funds whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, usually tracking an index. Key idea: ETFs are listed funds traded during the day whose prices are kept near NAV by creation and redemption; investors should check structure, counterparty risk, reporting fund status and whether a product is a fund or a note.

What does a market-capitalisation weighted index do?

It weights each company by its total market value, so larger companies count for more. From “Index tracking funds” →

In plain English: Index tracking funds, or passive funds, aim to match the return of a market index at low cost. Key idea: Index trackers aim to match an index at low cost, usually through market-cap weighting, giving broad diversification but no chance of beating the market and exposure to index concentration.

What did Sharpe (1991) show about the average active return?

Before costs it must equal the market return, so after costs it must be lower than passive. From “The evidence on active and passive funds” →

In plain English: Decades of research compare active funds with index trackers. Key idea: Because active investors as a group hold the market, the average active pound must underperform passive after costs; the evidence shows most active funds lag over long periods with little persistence.

What rate of SDRT applies to electronic purchases of most UK shares?

0.5% of the purchase price. From “Dealing costs, spreads and stamp duty” →

In plain English: Buying and selling investments brings costs beyond annual charges. Key idea: Dealing costs include commissions, bid-offer spreads and 0.5% SDRT on most UK share purchases, with exemptions for AIM shares and ETFs; frequent trading multiplies these costs.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Funds, Index Investing and Costs

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What does an investor in a fund own? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A proportional share of the fund's whole portfolio. Units or shares in a fund represent a proportion of all its holdings, so value rises and falls with the portfolio.

  2. What happens when investors put new money into an open-ended fund? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The manager creates new units at the current NAV per unit. Open-ended funds issue new units at NAV, so demand changes the fund's size, not its price.

  3. When you buy shares in an investment trust, from whom do you usually buy them? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Another investor selling shares in the market. Investment trusts are closed-ended, so their shares change hands on the stock exchange.

  4. Why can a fund offer diversification to someone with only £100 a month? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Pooled money buys many holdings at shared cost. Pooling spreads costs and lets each pound buy a slice of every holding.

  5. What is the main legal difference between a unit trust and an OEIC? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A unit trust is a trust with a trustee; an OEIC is a company with a depositary. Both are open-ended and FCA-authorised, but one uses a trust structure and the other a company structure.

  6. A trust borrows to invest so that it has £120 of assets for every £100 of shareholder funds. Its assets fall 10%. Ignoring interest, what happens to NAV? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It falls 12%. Assets fall to £108; after repaying £20 of borrowing, NAV is £88, a 12% fall from £100.

  7. A fund manager goes out of business. What happens to the fund's investments? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    They stay with the depositary and are not used to pay the manager's debts. Ring-fenced custody by an independent depositary protects fund assets if the manager fails.

  8. Why did some open-ended property funds suspend dealing in 2016 and 2020? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Selling buildings quickly to meet redemptions was impossible without harming remaining investors. Daily dealing clashes with assets that take months to sell, so suspensions protected remaining holders.

  9. Why are investment trusts often preferred over open-ended funds for illiquid assets such as infrastructure? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The manager never has to sell assets to meet redemptions. A fixed share capital means investors sell to each other, so the portfolio is not forced into fire sales.

Key takeaways

  • A fund pools many investors' money into one portfolio, giving diversification, scale, professional management and access at a cost, with assets held separately from the manager.
  • Unit trusts and OEICs are open-ended funds that create and cancel units as money flows in and out, priced daily at net asset value per unit using forward pricing.
  • Investment trusts are closed-ended listed companies whose shares can trade at discounts or premiums to NAV, which can use gearing, and which are overseen by an independent board.
  • ETFs are listed funds traded during the day whose prices are kept near NAV by creation and redemption; investors should check structure, counterparty risk, reporting fund status and whether a product is a fund or a note.
  • Factsheets and key information documents show a fund's objective, benchmark, holdings, risk score, costs and performance; read them critically, comparing performance with the benchmark and costs with alternatives.
  • Active managers try to earn alpha through selection and timing, but they charge more, may be closet trackers with low active share, and face manager, style and concentration risks.

Common mistakes in Funds, Index Investing and Costs

  • Thinking that a fund protects you from market losses. Pooling spreads specific risk, but if the market falls, the fund falls too, and FSCS does not compensate for investment losses.
  • Thinking an open-ended fund's price rises because many people are buying it. New money simply creates new units at NAV; the price only moves with the value of the underlying assets.
  • Assuming a wide discount is free money. Discounts can stay wide or widen further, especially in falling markets, so a discount is only an advantage if it narrows or at least does not widen.
  • Treating every exchange-traded product as a diversified fund. ETCs and ETNs are debt of an issuer, and leveraged or inverse products behave very differently from a simple tracker over time.
  • Reading a risk score of 3 as meaning the fund cannot fall much. The score is based largely on past volatility, which can change, and even low-scored funds can lose money.
  • Assuming a higher fee buys better management. Fees are certain while outperformance is not, and some higher-fee funds hold portfolios very close to the index.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why pool investments?
    A fund pools money from many investors and invests it as one portfolio.
  2. Unit trusts and OEICs
    Unit trusts and open-ended investment companies (OEICs) are the main types of open-ended fund in the UK.
  3. Investment trusts
    Investment trusts are closed-ended funds structured as listed companies.
  4. Exchange-traded funds
    Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) are funds whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, usually tracking an index.
  5. Fund factsheets and key information documents
    Fund factsheets and key information documents summarise a fund's objective, risks, costs and past performance.
  6. Active fund management
    Active fund managers try to beat a benchmark by choosing which assets to hold and when.
  7. Index tracking funds
    Index tracking funds, or passive funds, aim to match the return of a market index at low cost.
  8. The efficient market hypothesis
    The efficient market hypothesis says that market prices reflect available information, so it is hard to beat the market consistently.
  9. Tracking error and replication methods
    Index funds never match their index exactly.
  10. The evidence on active and passive funds
    Decades of research compare active funds with index trackers.
  11. Ongoing charges and platform fees
    Investors pay a fund's own charges and, usually, a platform's charges for holding it.
  12. How fees compound
    Small annual charges compound over time into large differences in final value.
  13. Dealing costs, spreads and stamp duty
    Buying and selling investments brings costs beyond annual charges.
  14. Accumulation and income units
    Many funds offer income units, which pay out dividends and interest, and accumulation units, which reinvest them automatically.
  15. Choosing a platform
    An investment platform is where most UK investors buy and hold funds and shares.
