What is bounded rationality?

The idea that people make decisions with limited time, information and mental capacity, so they use short cuts. From “Rational investors and their limits” →

In plain English: Traditional finance assumes investors are rational, use all available information and maximise expected utility. Key idea: Traditional finance assumes rational investors, but people have bounded rationality and use heuristics that create systematic biases, which behavioural finance studies and investors can guard against.

What did Barber and Odean (2001) find about men and women investors?

Men traded about 45% more than women and reduced their net returns by more. From “Overconfidence” →

In plain English: Overconfidence leads people to overrate their knowledge, skill and control. Key idea: Overconfidence, through miscalibration, the better-than-average effect, illusion of control and self-attribution, leads investors to trade too much and diversify too little, lowering returns.

What did Greenwood and Shleifer (2014) find about investor expectations?

They follow recent returns and are negatively related to subsequent returns. From “Recency and availability in markets” →

In plain English: Recency bias and the availability heuristic lead people to give too much weight to recent and memorable events. Key idea: The availability heuristic and recency bias make recent and vivid events seem more likely to continue, leading investors to chase recent winners and flee after falls; long-run evidence and a written plan counter them.

What is confirmation bias?

Seeking and favouring information that supports existing beliefs while discounting contrary evidence. From “Confirmation bias” →

In plain English: Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and favour information that supports what we already believe. Key idea: Confirmation bias makes investors favour supporting evidence and ignore warnings, reinforced by echo chambers; a written thesis, a deliberate opposing case and pre-mortems help keep views honest.

What did Barber and Odean (2008) find about attention-grabbing shares?

Individual investors tend to buy them, and they tend to underperform afterwards. From “News, noise and social media” →

In plain English: Investors face a constant stream of news, opinions and social media posts. Key idea: Much financial news is noise; attention-grabbing shares and social media hype can drive short-term prices and poor decisions, and finfluencer promotions are regulated, so check sources and firms with the FCA.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Behavioural Finance

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement describes the rational investor of traditional finance? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Uses all relevant information and chooses the highest expected utility. The rational investor weighs all information, updates correctly and maximises expected utility.

  2. In prospect theory, against what are gains and losses judged? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A reference point such as the purchase price. Prospect theory says people judge outcomes relative to a reference point, not by final wealth.

  3. Which describes the better-than-average effect? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Most people rate their own skill as above average. Rating oneself above average is a common form of overconfidence.

  4. Which is an example of an emotional bias rather than a cognitive bias? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Selling in a panic because a loss feels unbearable. Emotional biases arise from feelings such as fear and regret; the others are information-processing errors.

  5. Why might an investor who checks their portfolio daily feel shares are riskier than one who checks yearly? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Daily checks reveal many more small losses, which loss aversion amplifies. Over short periods shares fall often, so frequent checking magnifies the pain of losses.

  6. An investor says her winning shares show her skill, but her losing shares were 'just bad luck'. Which bias is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Self-attribution bias. Taking credit for success and blaming luck for failure is self-attribution bias.

  7. Why does it matter that investor biases are systematic rather than random? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Systematic errors can be anticipated, and designed against, by investors and regulators. Predictable errors can be anticipated, so they can be guarded against with rules and design.

  8. An investor's shares have fallen 30% and she says she will sell 'once they get back to what I paid'. What is the main bias, and the better question to ask? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Reference dependence; she should ask whether she would buy them today. The purchase price is a reference point; the relevant question is the shares' future prospects.

  9. Which is the most effective way for an investor to test whether they really have stock-picking skill? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Record every decision and compare results with a tracker over years. A full record against a fair benchmark over a long period avoids selective memory and luck.

Key takeaways

  • Traditional finance assumes rational investors, but people have bounded rationality and use heuristics that create systematic biases, which behavioural finance studies and investors can guard against.
  • Prospect theory shows people judge outcomes against a reference point, feel losses about twice as strongly as gains, avoid risk for gains and seek it to escape losses, which shapes many investment mistakes.
  • Overconfidence, through miscalibration, the better-than-average effect, illusion of control and self-attribution, leads investors to trade too much and diversify too little, lowering returns.
  • Anchoring makes investors rely too much on initial numbers, such as purchase prices and past highs, and adjust too little to new information; valuing from fundamentals and asking whether you would buy today reduces it.
  • Herding is following others rather than one's own analysis; information cascades and career risk make it common, and it can drive prices away from value and lead investors to buy high and sell low.
  • The availability heuristic and recency bias make recent and vivid events seem more likely to continue, leading investors to chase recent winners and flee after falls; long-run evidence and a written plan counter them.

Common mistakes in Behavioural Finance

  • Assuming that being clever or well informed removes bias. Biases are features of how all people think, including experts; good processes help more than intelligence.
  • Judging an investment by whether it is above or below the price you paid. The purchase price is irrelevant to future returns; what matters is the investment's prospects from today.
  • Treating a run of good results as proof of skill. In a rising market most portfolios gain; compare your returns with a tracker before concluding you have an edge.
  • Believing a share must be cheap because it is far below its previous high. Past prices reflect past expectations; what matters is the value of future cash flows.
  • Treating popularity as evidence of value. A share being widely bought means its price already reflects that enthusiasm; it says nothing about whether it is cheap.
  • Expecting next year's returns to look like last year's. Short-run returns are highly variable and tell you little about the next period.

    Overconfidence leads people to overrate their knowledge, skill and control.
  4. Anchoring and investment decisions
    Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on a first number when making estimates.
  5. Herding
    Herding is the tendency to follow what others are doing.
  6. Recency and availability in markets
    Recency bias and the availability heuristic lead people to give too much weight to recent and memorable events.
  7. The disposition effect
    The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winning investments too soon and hold losing ones too long.
  8. Mental accounting
    Mental accounting is the habit of treating money differently depending on where it came from or which mental 'pot' it is in.
  9. Confirmation bias
    Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and favour information that supports what we already believe.
  10. Investor psychology in bubbles
    Bubbles are periods when prices rise far above fundamental value, driven largely by investor psychology.
  11. Market timing
    Market timing means moving money in and out of markets to avoid falls and catch rises.
  12. News, noise and social media
    Investors face a constant stream of news, opinions and social media posts.
  13. Pound-cost averaging and automatic investing
    Pound-cost averaging means investing a fixed sum at regular intervals.
  14. Rules, checklists and investment journals
    Because knowing about biases is not enough to avoid them, investors benefit from systems that structure decisions.

Behavioural Finance: questions students ask

What is loss aversion?

The tendency for losses to hurt more than equal gains please, by roughly two to one.

What is miscalibration?

Being too precise in estimates, so that confidence ranges are too narrow.

What is anchoring?

Relying too heavily on a first number when making an estimate and adjusting too little from it.

What is an information cascade?

A chain of decisions in which people copy earlier choices and ignore their own information.
