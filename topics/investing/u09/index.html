What is bounded rationality?

The idea that people make decisions with limited time, information and mental capacity, so they use short cuts. From “Rational investors and their limits” →

What did Barber and Odean (2001) find about men and women investors?

Men traded about 45% more than women and reduced their net returns by more. From “Overconfidence” →

What did Greenwood and Shleifer (2014) find about investor expectations?

They follow recent returns and are negatively related to subsequent returns. From “Recency and availability in markets” →

What is confirmation bias?

Seeking and favouring information that supports existing beliefs while discounting contrary evidence. From “Confirmation bias” →

What did Barber and Odean (2008) find about attention-grabbing shares?

Individual investors tend to buy them, and they tend to underperform afterwards. From “News, noise and social media” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Rational investors and their limits
    Traditional finance assumes investors are rational, use all available information and maximise expected utility.
  2. Loss aversion and prospect theory
    Prospect theory describes how people actually value gains and losses.
  3. Overconfidence
    Overconfidence leads people to overrate their knowledge, skill and control.
  4. Anchoring and investment decisions
    Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on a first number when making estimates.
  5. Herding
    Herding is the tendency to follow what others are doing.
  6. Recency and availability in markets
    Recency bias and the availability heuristic lead people to give too much weight to recent and memorable events.
  7. The disposition effect
    The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winning investments too soon and hold losing ones too long.
  8. Mental accounting
    Mental accounting is the habit of treating money differently depending on where it came from or which mental 'pot' it is in.
  9. Confirmation bias
    Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and favour information that supports what we already believe.
  10. Investor psychology in bubbles
    Bubbles are periods when prices rise far above fundamental value, driven largely by investor psychology.
  11. Market timing
    Market timing means moving money in and out of markets to avoid falls and catch rises.
  12. News, noise and social media
    Investors face a constant stream of news, opinions and social media posts.
  13. Pound-cost averaging and automatic investing
    Pound-cost averaging means investing a fixed sum at regular intervals.
  14. Rules, checklists and investment journals
    Because knowing about biases is not enough to avoid them, investors benefit from systems that structure decisions.

Key terms

Bounded rationality
Rational decision-making limited by time, information and mental capacity
Prospect theory
A model of decision-making in which outcomes are valued as gains or losses relative to a reference point
Overconfidence
Overestimating one's knowledge, skill or the precision of one's forecasts
Anchoring
Relying too heavily on an initial number when making an estimate
Herding
Following the actions of other investors rather than one's own analysis
Availability heuristic
Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind
Disposition effect
The tendency to sell winning investments too early and hold losing ones too long
Mental accounting
Treating money differently depending on its source or intended use
Confirmation bias
Favouring information that supports existing beliefs and discounting evidence against them
Bubble
A period when prices rise far above fundamental value, sustained by expectations of further rises
Market timing
Moving money in and out of markets to try to avoid falls and catch rises
Noise
Information or trading not related to fundamental value
Pound-cost averaging
Investing a fixed sum at regular intervals regardless of price
Investment policy statement
A written plan setting out goals, risk tolerance, asset allocation and rules for managing investments
