Key terms and formulas at a glance

Diversification
Spreading investments across assets that do not move perfectly together to reduce risk
Correlation coefficient
A measure from −1 to +1 of how closely two variables move together
Specific risk
Risk unique to a company or sector, which diversification can reduce
Average covariance
The typical co-movement between pairs of holdings, which sets the floor for a diversified portfolio's risk
Home bias
The tendency to hold a disproportionately large share of investments in one's home country
Modern portfolio theory
Markowitz's framework for choosing portfolios by expected return and variance, taking correlations into account
Efficient frontier
The set of portfolios giving the highest expected return for each level of risk
CAPM
A model in which expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium
Beta
A measure of an asset's sensitivity to movements in the overall market
Factor
A characteristic of securities associated with differences in average returns
Strategic asset allocation
A long-term target mix of asset classes set to match an investor's goals and risk
Tactical asset allocation
Temporary deviations from the strategic asset mix to exploit expected short-term opportunities

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is diversification called a free lunch?

It reduces risk without reducing expected return. From “Why diversification works” →

In plain English: Diversification means spreading money across many investments that do not all move together. Key idea: Diversification lowers risk without lowering expected return, because imperfectly correlated holdings partly offset each other; it cannot remove market-wide risk.

What level does portfolio risk approach as holdings increase?

The average covariance between holdings: market risk. From “How many holdings are enough?” →

In plain English: Adding holdings reduces specific risk quickly at first and then more slowly. Key idea: Risk falls quickly with the first holdings, then levels off at market risk; dozens of well-spread shares or a broad fund remove most specific risk.

What is the efficient frontier?

The set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk. From “The efficient frontier” →

In plain English: The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk. Key idea: The efficient frontier shows the best risk–return trade-offs; with cash, investors mix one optimal risky portfolio with cash to reach their chosen risk.

Name four equity factors.

Any four of: market, size, value, momentum, quality or profitability, low volatility. From “Factor investing” →

In plain English: Factors are characteristics of securities, such as value, size, momentum, quality and low volatility, that have historically been associated with returns beyond the market. Key idea: Factors such as value, size, momentum and quality have historically earned premiums, but they come and go for long periods and may shrink once widely exploited.

Why does a portfolio drift?

Its assets earn different returns, so their weights change. From “Rebalancing” →

In plain English: As markets move, a portfolio drifts away from its target mix. Key idea: Rebalancing keeps a portfolio's risk at its chosen level by trimming what has risen and topping up what has fallen; once or twice a year, or at thresholds, is usually enough.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Diversification and Portfolio Theory

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What determines how much diversification reduces risk? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    How closely the holdings' returns move together (their correlation). Lower correlation means more offsetting and more risk reduction.

  2. What correlation gives no diversification benefit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    +1. Perfectly correlated assets move in step, so combining them does not reduce volatility.

  3. Which is an example of systematic risk? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A rise in interest rates that affects all companies. Market-wide shocks such as interest-rate changes are systematic.

  4. Two assets each have 20% volatility. If they are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), what is the volatility of a 50/50 portfolio? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    20%: with perfect correlation there is no diversification benefit. With ρ = 1 the portfolio volatility equals the weighted average, so no risk is removed.

  5. Which pair would usually offer the largest diversification benefit? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Global shares and high-quality short-dated government bonds. Pairs from different asset classes have lower correlations; the others overlap heavily.

  6. An investor holds one pharmaceutical company's shares. Its main drug fails a trial and the price halves. What type of risk caused the loss? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Specific risk. The event is unique to that company and could have been diversified away.

  7. In a severe global crash most of an investor's holdings fall together. Which is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Diversification still helped against specific risk, but rising correlations in crises limit its protection against market-wide falls. Diversification is not insurance against systematic falls; bonds and cash may help more in such periods.

  8. Why did many 60/40 share–bond portfolios fall sharply in 2022? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Rising inflation and interest rates turned the share–bond correlation positive, so both fell together. Correlation depends on the economic regime; inflation shocks hurt both.

  9. Why is holding a large share of savings in an employer's shares particularly risky? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The employee's income and savings depend on the same company, so a failure hits both at once. Concentration combines human capital and financial capital risk in one company.

Key takeaways

  • Diversification lowers risk without lowering expected return, because imperfectly correlated holdings partly offset each other; it cannot remove market-wide risk.
  • Correlation from −1 to +1 measures co-movement; lower correlation means greater diversification benefit, but correlations shift over time and rise in crises.
  • Specific risk can be diversified away and earns no premium; systematic risk cannot be diversified and is what investors are paid to bear.
  • Risk falls quickly with the first holdings, then levels off at market risk; dozens of well-spread shares or a broad fund remove most specific risk.
  • Home bias concentrates UK investors in a small, sector-skewed market; global diversification spreads risk, though sterling spending needs justify some domestic assets, especially bonds.
  • MPT judges assets by their contribution to portfolio risk and return; it is the foundation of diversification and asset allocation, but its outputs are only as good as uncertain inputs.

Common mistakes in Diversification and Portfolio Theory

  • Thinking you are diversified because you own many funds. If they all hold the same large companies, the overlap leaves you concentrated.
  • Assuming correlation means causation, or that a historical correlation will hold in the next crisis.
  • Holding a large stake in your employer's shares because you "know the company". Your income already depends on it.
  • Counting holdings rather than exposures. Ten funds can hold the same companies; thirty shares can sit in one sector.
  • Assuming a global index fund is evenly spread around the world. Cap-weighting puts most of it in the US.
  • Treating an optimiser's output as precise. Small changes in estimated returns can swing the "optimal" weights dramatically.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why diversification works
    Diversification means spreading money across many investments that do not all move together.
  2. Correlation between assets
    Correlation measures how closely two assets' returns move together, on a scale from −1 to +1.
  3. Specific and systematic risk
    Total risk divides into specific risk, unique to a company or sector, and systematic risk, which affects the whole market.
  4. How many holdings are enough?
    Adding holdings reduces specific risk quickly at first and then more slowly.
  5. Home bias and global diversification
    Investors everywhere tend to hold far more of their own country's shares than its share of world markets would suggest.
  6. Modern portfolio theory
    Modern portfolio theory, developed by Harry Markowitz, treats a portfolio as a whole: investors should choose combinations of assets that give the highest expected return for each level of risk, using
  7. The efficient frontier
    The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk.
  8. The capital asset pricing model (CAPM)
    The CAPM says an asset's expected return equals the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market risk premium.
  9. Beta
    Beta measures how sensitive an investment is to movements in the overall market.
  10. Factor investing
    Factors are characteristics of securities, such as value, size, momentum, quality and low volatility, that have historically been associated with returns beyond the market.
  11. Strategic asset allocation
    Strategic asset allocation is the long-term mix of asset classes, such as shares, bonds and cash, chosen to fit an investor's goals and risk tolerance.
  12. Tactical asset allocation
    Tactical asset allocation means deliberately moving away from the strategic mix for a period to exploit expected short-term opportunities, for example holding more cash when shares look expensive.
  13. Rebalancing
    As markets move, a portfolio drifts away from its target mix.
  14. Lifestyling and glide paths
    Lifestyling automatically moves a pension from higher-risk to lower-risk assets as the planned retirement date approaches, following a glide path.
  15. Multi-asset funds and model portfolios
    Multi-asset funds hold a mix of shares, bonds and other assets in one fund, rebalancing automatically.

Diversification and Portfolio Theory: questions students ask

What does a correlation of zero mean?

No linear relationship between the two assets' returns.

Why does specific risk earn no premium?

Because it can be diversified away cheaply, so markets do not compensate investors for bearing it.

What is home bias?

Holding a disproportionate share of investments in one's own country.

Who developed modern portfolio theory, and when?

Harry Markowitz, in 1952.
