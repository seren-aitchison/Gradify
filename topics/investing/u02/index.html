Key terms and formulas at a glance

Financial system
The institutions, markets and infrastructure that move money between savers and borrowers, make payments and manage risk
Maturity transformation
Funding long-term loans with short-term deposits or borrowing
Central bank
The institution responsible for a country's monetary policy and the stability of its financial system
Monetary policy
Central bank action on interest rates and money to control inflation and support the economy
Primary market
The market where newly created securities are sold by issuers to raise capital
Stock exchange
A regulated marketplace where securities are listed and traded
Bid price
The highest price buyers are currently willing to pay, at which an investor can sell
Broker
A firm that executes trades on behalf of clients as their agent
Settlement
The exchange of securities for cash that completes a trade
Market depth
The volume that can be traded near the current price without moving it significantly
Index
A measure of the combined performance of a defined group of securities
GDP
Gross domestic product: the total value of goods and services produced in an economy over a period

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three functions of the financial system.

Any three of: channelling saving to investment, payments, pricing and sharing risk, providing liquidity, producing information through prices. From “The role of the financial system” →

In plain English: The financial system moves money from people who have more than they need now to people and firms who can use it productively, and prices the risk of doing so. Key idea: The financial system channels savings to productive uses, runs payments, prices and shares risk, and provides liquidity; investors' returns come from the real economy it connects them to.

What is quantitative easing?

The central bank creating reserves to buy assets, mainly government bonds, to push down long-term interest rates. From “Interest rates and monetary policy” →

In plain English: Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and the central bank's balance sheet to keep inflation on target. Key idea: Bank Rate and QE work through borrowing costs, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations; higher rates help savers and hurt existing bond prices and borrowers.

At which price do you buy a share: the bid or the offer?

The offer (ask) price, which is the higher of the two. From “How a trade works: orders, bid and offer” →

In plain English: Every share has two prices at any moment: the bid, at which you can sell, and the offer (or ask), at which you can buy. Key idea: You buy at the offer and sell at the bid, so the spread is a real cost; limit orders control price at the risk of not trading.

What is price discovery?

The process by which trading combines many participants' information into a market price. From “Liquidity and price discovery” →

In plain English: Markets do two jobs at once: they let investors trade quickly and cheaply (liquidity) and they combine the information of many traders into a price (price discovery). Key idea: Liquid markets are tight, deep and resilient; trading combines dispersed information into prices quickly, but liquidity can vanish in stress.

What is stagflation?

Weak or falling growth combined with high inflation. From “Inflation, growth and asset prices” →

In plain English: Inflation and economic growth affect each asset class differently. Key idea: Growth favours shares, falling inflation favours bonds, rising inflation hurts both; the mix of assets matters because no single asset suits every environment.

Quick quiz: test yourself on How Financial Markets Work

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a core function of the financial system? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Channelling savings to borrowers who can invest them productively. Moving money from savers to productive borrowers is central; tax and pricing goods are not financial system functions.

  2. Which intermediary runs pooled investment funds for investors? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    An asset manager. Asset managers run funds; building societies take deposits and lend, insurers cover risks.

  3. Which Bank of England body sets Bank Rate? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Monetary Policy Committee. The MPC sets Bank Rate; the FPC handles system-wide risks and the PRA supervises firms.

  4. A company issues new bonds to fund a wind farm and a pension fund buys them. What has the financial system done? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Linked savers' money with a productive investment, with the wind farm's income paying the bond interest. The pension fund's savers lend to the company; their return depends on the project's cash flows.

  5. Why can a bank offer instant-access savings while lending for 25-year mortgages? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It relies on only a small share of depositors withdrawing at any time, backed by reserves, regulation and deposit insurance. Maturity transformation works because withdrawals are spread out; it fails if many withdraw at once.

  6. A bank approves a £10,000 personal loan and credits the customer's current account. What has happened to deposits in the banking system? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    They have increased by £10,000. Lending creates a matching new deposit; repayment later destroys it.

  7. Which statement best evaluates the role of finance in the 2007–09 crisis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    When the system failed, lending to households and firms fell sharply and the real economy suffered, showing both its value and its risks. The crisis showed how dependent the economy is on functioning credit and how failures spread beyond finance.

  8. What does the 2007 run on Northern Rock show about intermediaries? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Maturity transformation is valuable but fragile: a loss of confidence can force even a solvent-looking lender to fail. Northern Rock relied heavily on short-term wholesale funding; when markets froze, confidence collapsed.

  9. Which is the best evaluation of the lender of last resort role? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It prevents temporary cash shortages at solvent banks from becoming failures, but it can encourage risk-taking if banks expect rescue. The classic principle (often attributed to Bagehot) is to lend freely to solvent banks against good collateral; moral hazard is the main criticism.

Key takeaways

  • The financial system channels savings to productive uses, runs payments, prices and shares risk, and provides liquidity; investors' returns come from the real economy it connects them to.
  • Intermediaries make finance work at scale by pooling money and risk and transforming maturities, but that same transformation creates the risk of runs and failures.
  • The Bank of England sets monetary policy, protects financial stability and supervises banks through the PRA; commercial banks create most money by lending.
  • Bank Rate and QE work through borrowing costs, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations; higher rates help savers and hurt existing bond prices and borrowers.
  • Primary markets raise new capital for issuers; secondary markets let investors trade existing securities, and their liquidity makes primary issues possible.
  • Exchanges list securities, match trades and enforce disclosure; London's Main Market hosts larger companies, while AIM's lighter rules suit smaller firms but bring higher risk.

Common mistakes in How Financial Markets Work

  • Thinking financial markets create returns out of nothing. Over the long run, returns come from the cash flows of real businesses, borrowers and assets.
  • Assuming money in a bank sits in a vault. Banks lend most of it; deposit protection (FSCS) and regulation exist because they do.
  • Believing banks simply lend out existing savers' money. Lending creates new deposits; savers' deposits and capital matter for funding and safety, but the order is the other way round.
  • Expecting markets to move on a rate decision itself. Prices react to the difference between the decision and what was expected; a widely predicted rise may barely move them.
  • Thinking that buying a company's shares on a platform gives the company money. Only primary issues raise money for the issuer.
  • Treating AIM shares as being as safe and liquid as FTSE 100 shares because both trade on the LSE.

Lessons in this school

  1. The role of the financial system
    The financial system moves money from people who have more than they need now to people and firms who can use it productively, and prices the risk of doing so.
  2. Savers, borrowers and financial intermediaries
    Intermediaries such as banks, insurers, pension funds and asset managers stand between savers and borrowers.
  3. Banks and the Bank of England
    The Bank of England is the UK's central bank.
  4. Interest rates and monetary policy
    Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and the central bank's balance sheet to keep inflation on target.
  5. Primary and secondary markets
    In the primary market, companies and governments sell new securities to raise money; in the secondary market, investors trade existing securities with each other.
  6. Stock exchanges: the LSE, AIM and global markets
    A stock exchange provides a regulated marketplace where shares and other securities are listed and traded.
  7. How a trade works: orders, bid and offer
    Every share has two prices at any moment: the bid, at which you can sell, and the offer (or ask), at which you can buy.
  8. Brokers, market makers and investment platforms
    Investors reach the market through firms.
  9. Clearing, settlement and custody
    After a trade is agreed, it has to be completed: the buyer's money and the seller's securities must change hands safely.
  10. Liquidity and price discovery
    Markets do two jobs at once: they let investors trade quickly and cheaply (liquidity) and they combine the information of many traders into a price (price discovery).
  11. Stock market indices
    An index measures the performance of a group of securities, such as the 100 largest UK companies.
  12. The economic cycle and markets
    Economies move through expansions and recessions.
  13. Inflation, growth and asset prices
    Inflation and economic growth affect each asset class differently.
  14. Currencies and exchange rates
    When a UK investor buys overseas assets, their return in pounds depends on the exchange rate as well as on the asset.
  15. Bull and bear markets
    A bull market is a sustained rise in prices; a bear market is a fall of 20% or more from a peak.
  16. Bubbles and crashes
    A bubble occurs when prices rise far above any reasonable estimate of value, driven by enthusiasm and the expectation of further rises; a crash is a sudden, steep fall.

How Financial Markets Work: questions students ask

Where do investors' long-run returns ultimately come from?

The real economy: company profits, interest paid by borrowers and rents from assets.

What is maturity transformation?

Funding long-term loans, such as mortgages, with short-term deposits or borrowing.

When did the Bank of England gain operational independence for interest rates?

1997, confirmed by the Bank of England Act 1998.

Does buying an existing share on a platform give the company money?

No; the money goes to the selling investor in the secondary market.
