Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How large is an emergency fund usually recommended to be?

About 3 to 6 months of essential spending. From “The personal financial plan” →

In plain English: A financial plan brings together your goals, money and investments into one picture. Key idea: A personal financial plan covers budgeting, an emergency fund, debt, protection, goals and investing, usually tackled in a sensible order and reviewed regularly, especially after life events.

What does the "100 minus your age" rule suggest for a 40-year-old?

60% in shares. From “Choosing an asset allocation” →

In plain English: Asset allocation, the mix of shares, bonds and cash, is the most important decision for most investors. Key idea: Asset allocation drives most portfolio risk and return; it should reflect time horizon, attitude to risk, capacity for loss and the goal; rules of thumb like "100 minus age" are starting points, and the best mix is one you can stick with.

What is the annual Lifetime ISA bonus?

25% of contributions, up to £1,000 on £4,000 a year. From “Investing early in adult life” →

In plain English: The years from 18 to 30 are when time is the investor's greatest asset. Key idea: Starting early gives compounding more time, so small regular amounts grow significantly; workplace pensions, Lifetime ISAs and stocks and shares ISAs suit young adults, balancing pension, deposit and emergency savings.

Who can book a free Pension Wise appointment?

People aged 50 and over with a defined contribution pension. From “Approaching retirement” →

In plain English: The ten years before retirement are a critical time for planning. Key idea: Approaching retirement, estimate needs using standards such as the Retirement Living Standards, check the State Pension forecast, manage sequence-of-returns risk, trace old pensions and use free Pension Wise guidance.

What is rebalancing?

Restoring a portfolio to its target asset allocation. From “Reviewing and monitoring a portfolio” →

In plain English: A portfolio needs regular, but not constant, attention. Key idea: Annual reviews check goals, allocation, costs, allowances and life changes; rebalancing on a calendar or threshold restores the target mix to control risk; checking too often encourages overtrading and myopic loss aversion.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Building Your Plan

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which should usually come first in a financial plan? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A budget showing income and spending. A budget is the foundation of every other decision.

  2. Which element of an IPS states the goal and when it is needed? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Objectives and time horizon. Objectives set out what the money is for and when.

  3. Where should money needed for a house deposit in 18 months usually be kept? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    In cash savings. Short horizons cannot ride out market falls.

  4. Why should expensive credit card debt usually be paid off before investing? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Its interest rate is usually higher than likely investment returns. Clearing 24% debt is like a guaranteed 24% return.

  5. Why is capacity for loss included in an IPS? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It shows how much loss the investor can afford without harming their life. Capacity for loss differs from willingness to take risk.

  6. What is the main criticism of the "100 minus age" rule? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It ignores individual circumstances and may be too cautious for long lives. Rules of thumb are only starting points and ignore personal circumstances.

  7. Who most needs protection insurance early in their plan? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Someone with children who depend on their income. Protection matters most where others rely on your earnings.

  8. Markets fall 20%. According to a good IPS, what should the investor do first? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Check whether goals or circumstances have changed before acting. The IPS guides decisions by goals, not market moves.

  9. Why is an allocation the investor can stick with so important? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Selling in a panic after falls can lock in losses. Behaviour can matter as much as the mix chosen.

Key takeaways

  • A personal financial plan covers budgeting, an emergency fund, debt, protection, goals and investing, usually tackled in a sensible order and reviewed regularly, especially after life events.
  • An investment policy statement records objectives, risk, constraints, asset allocation, implementation and rebalancing rules, helping investors stick to a plan and avoid emotional decisions.
  • Asset allocation drives most portfolio risk and return; it should reflect time horizon, attitude to risk, capacity for loss and the goal; rules of thumb like "100 minus age" are starting points, and the best mix is one you can stick with.
  • Portfolios can be built from a few broad funds such as global equity and bond index funds or a multi-asset fund; compare costs, diversification, approach and tracking; many investors use low-cost index funds as a core because most active funds underperform after fees.
  • Lump-sum investing has usually beaten phased investing because markets tend to rise, but pound-cost averaging reduces regret; for most people, regular automatic investing from income is the most important habit.
  • Starting early gives compounding more time, so small regular amounts grow significantly; workplace pensions, Lifetime ISAs and stocks and shares ISAs suit young adults, balancing pension, deposit and emergency savings.

Common mistakes in Building Your Plan

  • Investing before building any cash buffer. Without an emergency fund, a surprise bill may force you to sell investments at a bad time.
  • Rewriting the plan every time markets fall. Change the IPS when goals or circumstances change, not because of short-term market moves.
  • Using the same asset allocation for every goal. Short-term goals need safer assets than long-term goals.
  • Thinking more funds always means more diversification. Overlapping funds often hold the same companies.
  • Believing pound-cost averaging always gives better returns. It reduces regret but, on average, lump sums have done better.
  • Waiting until you earn more before starting. Small amounts invested early can grow more than larger amounts invested later.

    Pension drawdown lets retirees keep their pension invested and withdraw income flexibly.

    A portfolio needs regular, but not constant, attention.

    Some decisions are complex enough that paying for advice is worthwhile.
  13. Record keeping and paperwork
    Good records make investing, tax returns and later-life planning far easier.

There is more time to recover from market falls.

What is the ongoing charges figure?

The annual cost of running a fund as a percentage of its value.

What is pound-cost averaging?

Investing equal amounts at regular intervals, buying more units when prices are low.
