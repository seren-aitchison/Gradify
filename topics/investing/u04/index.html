Key terms and formulas at a glance

Holding period return
The total return over the period an investment is held, including income, as a percentage of the starting value
Arithmetic mean return
The simple average of periodic returns
Total return
The return from price changes plus income, assuming income is reinvested
Nominal return
A return measured in money terms, without adjusting for inflation
Volatility
The degree of variation in an investment's returns over time
Drawdown
The percentage fall in value from a peak to a subsequent trough
Sequence of returns risk
The risk that the order of returns, combined with withdrawals or contributions, harms the final outcome
Systematic risk
Risk affecting the whole market, which diversification cannot remove
Risk aversion
A preference for certain outcomes over uncertain ones with the same expected value
Equity risk premium
The extra return expected from equities over a risk-free asset
Real return
Return after adjusting for inflation
Sharpe ratio
Excess return over the risk-free rate divided by the standard deviation of returns

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What must a holding period return include besides price change?

Income received, such as dividends or interest. From “Holding period return” →

In plain English: The holding period return measures what an investment earned over the whole time it was held, combining price change and income. Key idea: Holding period return includes income; annualise multi-year returns with the compound formula before comparing them.

Is income reinvested in accumulation units taxable outside a wrapper?

Yes, in the tax year it arises, even though no cash is received. From “Total return and reinvested income” →

In plain English: Total return combines price changes with income. Key idea: Total return = price change + income; reinvesting income compounds it and has driven much of the long-run return on shares.

What gain recovers a 30% drawdown?

About 42.9%. From “Drawdowns and recovery” →

In plain English: A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a subsequent low. Key idea: Drawdowns describe the pain of losses; a fall of x needs a gain of x ÷ (1 − x) to recover, and recoveries can take years.

State the risk–return trade-off.

Investors require higher expected returns for bearing more risk. From “The risk–return trade-off” →

In plain English: Investors require higher expected returns to hold riskier assets. Key idea: Higher expected returns are compensation for bearing risk; risk widens the range of outcomes and guarantees nothing.

Give the formula for the Sharpe ratio.

(Portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation of returns. From “Risk-adjusted return: the Sharpe ratio” →

In plain English: Comparing returns without considering risk can mislead. Key idea: The Sharpe ratio is excess return divided by volatility: a way to compare reward per unit of risk, not a forecast.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Risk and Return

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. A fund rises from £100 to £108 and pays £2 of income in a year. What is its holding period return? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    10%. (8 + 2) ÷ 100 = 10%; leaving out income would give 8%.

  2. An investment returns +10% and then −10%. What is the arithmetic mean return? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    0%. (10 − 10) ÷ 2 = 0%, although the investor ends 1% down.

  3. Which measure includes reinvested dividends? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Total return. Total return includes income; price return excludes it.

  4. An advert says a fund "returned 80% over ten years". What is the fairest way to compare it with a savings rate? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Annualise it: 1.80.1 − 1 is about 6% a year. Annualising gives about 6.1% a year, the comparable figure; simple division overstates it.

  5. Two funds both average 7% a year arithmetically. Fund A's returns vary little; Fund B's swing widely. Which probably grew more? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Fund A, because lower volatility means less drag on compound growth. With the same arithmetic mean, the less volatile fund has the higher geometric mean.

  6. A retired investor needs income from their fund. Which unit type pays income out? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Income units. Income units distribute; accumulation units reinvest within the price.

  7. Two funds report five-year returns of 40% and 45%. Which further information matters most before concluding the second is better? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Whether both include income and are net of charges, and how volatile each was. Like-for-like comparison needs the same basis (total return, net of fees) and context on risk.

  8. Which average should a fund factsheet use to describe its five-year past performance? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The geometric (annualised) return, because it reflects actual growth. Investors experience compound growth; GIPS and good practice use time-weighted, geometrically linked returns.

  9. Why does a chart of the FTSE 100 level since 2000 understate what investors in a UK index fund earned? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It shows only price changes, and dividends, which were a substantial part of returns, are excluded. Dividends of several per cent a year over two decades add a great deal; total return indices show this.

Key takeaways

  • Holding period return includes income; annualise multi-year returns with the compound formula before comparing them.
  • The geometric mean is the true compound growth rate and is always below the arithmetic mean when returns vary; the gap grows with volatility.
  • Total return = price change + income; reinvesting income compounds it and has driven much of the long-run return on shares.
  • Judge investments by their real return after charges and tax: the change in what your money can buy.
  • Standard deviation measures the spread of returns; it is a useful, standard risk measure but misses fat tails and the difference between gains and losses.
  • Drawdowns describe the pain of losses; a fall of x needs a gain of x ÷ (1 − x) to recover, and recoveries can take years.

Common mistakes in Risk and Return

  • Dividing a cumulative return by the number of years. That ignores compounding and overstates the annual rate.
  • Projecting wealth by compounding an arithmetic average return. That overstates the likely outcome when returns are volatile.
  • Comparing a fund's total return with an index's price return, or judging long-run share performance from price charts alone.
  • Comparing returns from a high-inflation period with those from a low-inflation period without adjusting for inflation.
  • Treating a low-volatility history as proof of low risk. Some risks, such as default or illiquidity, appear rarely and suddenly.
  • Judging a fund's risk only from its annualised return. Two funds with the same return can have very different maximum drawdowns.

Lessons in this school

  1. Holding period return
    The holding period return measures what an investment earned over the whole time it was held, combining price change and income.
  2. Arithmetic and geometric average returns
    The arithmetic average of yearly returns is not the rate at which money actually grew.
  3. Total return and reinvested income
    Total return combines price changes with income.
  4. Nominal and real returns in practice
    Nominal returns are measured in pounds; real returns measure the change in purchasing power.
  5. Volatility and standard deviation
    Volatility measures how widely returns vary around their average, and standard deviation is the usual statistic for it.
  6. Drawdowns and recovery
    A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a subsequent low.
  7. Sequence of returns risk
    When money is being added or withdrawn, the order of returns matters, not just their average.
  8. Types of investment risk
    Investment risk is not one thing.
  9. The risk–return trade-off
    Investors require higher expected returns to hold riskier assets.
  10. The equity risk premium
    The equity risk premium is the extra return shares are expected to earn over a risk-free asset.
  11. The long-run evidence: shares, bonds and cash
    More than a century of data on UK and global markets shows shares beating bonds and cash on average, with much larger swings.
  12. Risk-adjusted return: the Sharpe ratio
    Comparing returns without considering risk can mislead.
  13. The range of outcomes and probability
    Investment results are uncertain, so it is better to think in ranges and probabilities than in single forecasts.
  14. Survivorship bias in performance data
    Survivorship bias arises when failed or closed funds and companies drop out of the data, leaving only the survivors.

Risk and Return: questions students ask

What is the approximate gap between arithmetic and geometric means?

About half the variance: σ²/2.

What is the difference between price return and total return?

Total return includes reinvested income; price return does not.

What is money illusion?

Thinking about money in nominal terms and ignoring inflation.

About what share of outcomes lie within one standard deviation of the mean in a normal distribution?

About 68%, roughly two-thirds.
