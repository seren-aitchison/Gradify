Key terms and formulas at a glance

Saving
Setting money aside in a form that keeps its nominal value, such as a bank account, with low risk and quick access
Net income
Take-home pay after tax, National Insurance and other deductions
Emergency fund
Easily accessible cash kept to cover unexpected costs or a loss of income
APR
Annual percentage rate: the yearly cost of borrowing including interest and compulsory fees, used to compare credit
Premium
The regular payment made to an insurer in exchange for cover
Bank Rate
The Bank of England's main policy interest rate, set by the Monetary Policy Committee
Principal
The original sum invested or borrowed
Doubling time
The time for a quantity growing at a constant compound rate to double
Inflation
The rate at which the general level of prices rises over time
Time value of money
The principle that money available now is worth more than the same amount in the future
Present value
The value today of a future cash flow, discounted at a required rate of return
Financial goal
A specific amount needed by a specific date for a stated purpose

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the main difference between saving and investing?

Saving keeps the amount you put in safe; investing accepts that the value can fall in return for a higher expected long-run return. From “Saving and investing: what is the difference?” →

In plain English: Saving keeps money safe and available; investing puts money to work in assets whose value can rise or fall, in return for the chance of a higher long-run return. Key idea: Save for the short term and emergencies; invest only money you will not need for at least five years and can afford to see fall in value.

Why is repaying a 20% APR credit card like earning 20%?

Each pound repaid saves 20p a year in interest, with certainty. From “Debt before investing” →

In plain English: Paying off expensive debt is a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to its interest rate. Key idea: Repaying high-interest debt is a risk-free return at the debt's interest rate; clear priority and expensive debts before investing.

What is the compound interest formula for a lump sum?

FV = P(1 + r)^t, with r the rate per period and t the number of periods. From “Simple and compound interest” →

In plain English: Simple interest is paid only on the original sum; compound interest is paid on the interest already earned as well. Key idea: Compound growth multiplies money by the same factor each period, so time is the investor's greatest ally and the borrower's enemy.

What is the future value of a lump sum formula?

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n. From “The time value of money” →

In plain English: A pound today is worth more than a pound in the future, because today's pound can be invested, is certain, and is not eroded by inflation. Key idea: A pound today beats a pound tomorrow because it can earn a return, is not eroded by inflation and carries no risk of non-payment.

Why did some UK property funds suspend dealing in 2016 and 2020?

They held buildings that take months to sell, so they could not meet redemption requests quickly. From “Time horizon and liquidity” →

In plain English: Time horizon is how long until money is needed; liquidity is how quickly an asset can become cash without loss. Key idea: Match the asset to the need: money needed soon must be liquid and stable; only long-horizon money can be tied up or exposed to large swings.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Money Foundations

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which statement best describes investing? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Buying assets whose value can rise or fall, in the hope of higher long-run returns. Investing means accepting that value can fall; a guarantee of capital is a feature of saving, not investing.

  2. Which of these is usually classed as essential spending in a budget? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Council tax. Council tax is a legal obligation tied to your home; the others are discretionary and could be cut.

  3. What is the main purpose of an emergency fund? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    To cover unexpected costs or loss of income without borrowing or selling investments. It is a safety buffer; return matters much less than access and safety.

  4. Priya needs £5,000 for university fees in 12 months. Which is most suitable? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A savings account with easy access or a fixed term ending before the fees are due. Twelve months is too short to absorb a market fall, so money needed soon belongs in savings.

  5. Using the 50/30/20 rule on net income of £3,000 a month, how much would go to saving and debt repayment? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    £600. 20% of £3,000 is £600; 50% (£1,500) is needs and 30% (£900) is wants.

  6. Which is the most suitable home for an emergency fund? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    An easy-access savings account with an authorised UK bank. Emergency money must be instantly available and stable in value; a five-year fix locks it up, and equities and crypto can fall sharply.

  7. Which is the strongest reason a 25-year-old might invest part of their pension savings in shares rather than cash? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A long horizon allows time to recover from falls, and cash has historically lost ground to inflation over decades. Long horizons let investors ride out volatility in exchange for higher expected real returns; shares can still fall over ten years and dividends are never guaranteed.

  8. A Londoner's rent and essentials take 65% of net pay. What is the best judgement about the 50/30/20 rule for them? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Treat it as a guide and adapt the shares, still aiming to save something regularly. Budget rules are heuristics; circumstances such as high housing costs mean the split must flex, but the habit of regular saving still matters.

  9. Who most needs an emergency fund at the upper end of the usual range? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A self-employed parent whose household depends on their variable income. Variable income and dependants raise both the chance and the cost of a shock, so a larger buffer is justified.

Key takeaways

  • Save for the short term and emergencies; invest only money you will not need for at least five years and can afford to see fall in value.
  • Investing starts with a reliable monthly surplus; a budget turns that surplus from luck into a plan.
  • Build an accessible cash buffer of roughly three to six months of essential spending before investing, so a shock never forces you to sell investments or borrow at high cost.
  • Repaying high-interest debt is a risk-free return at the debt's interest rate; clear priority and expensive debts before investing.
  • Insure against rare, catastrophic losses you could not absorb; use savings, not insurance, for small predictable costs.
  • Compare savings with AER and borrowing with APR; both convert different payment patterns into one annual figure.

Common mistakes in Money Foundations

  • Thinking investing is just "saving with a better rate". Investments carry no promise of getting your money back, and the higher expected return is payment for accepting that risk.
  • Budgeting from gross salary. Tax, National Insurance and pension deductions can take a large share of gross pay, so a budget based on gross income will overstate what you have.
  • Counting investments as the emergency fund. A share fund may be down 20% exactly when you lose your job, because recessions hit both at once.
  • Treating a 0% balance transfer as free. When the promotional period ends the rate often jumps, and missing a payment can end the deal early; plan to clear it before then.
  • Buying cover without checking what you already have through work, or assuming the State will replace lost income. State sickness benefits are far below most people's earnings.
  • Comparing a savings account's AER with a loan's representative APR as if they were the same thing. Many people are offered a higher APR than the representative one, and introductory bonuses can inflate an AER for the first year only.

Lessons in this school

  1. Saving and investing: what is the difference?
    Saving keeps money safe and available; investing puts money to work in assets whose value can rise or fall, in return for the chance of a higher long-run return.
  2. Budgeting and cash flow
    Before anyone can invest, they need money left over each month.
  3. The emergency fund
    An emergency fund is easily accessible cash set aside for unexpected costs or loss of income.
  4. Debt before investing
    Paying off expensive debt is a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to its interest rate.
  5. Protecting your finances: insurance and resilience
    Insurance transfers the cost of rare but serious events to an insurer in return for a premium.
  6. Interest rates: AER, APR and the base rate
    Interest is the price of money: what borrowers pay and savers earn.
  7. Simple and compound interest
    Simple interest is paid only on the original sum; compound interest is paid on the interest already earned as well.
  8. Doubling time and the rule of 72
    The rule of 72 is a mental shortcut: dividing 72 by an annual percentage rate gives the approximate number of years for money to double.
  9. Inflation and real returns
    Inflation is the general rise in prices over time.
  10. The time value of money
    A pound today is worth more than a pound in the future, because today's pound can be invested, is certain, and is not eroded by inflation.
  11. Present value and discounting
    Discounting is compounding in reverse: it converts a future sum into its value today.
  12. Setting financial goals
    A plan starts with goals.
  13. Time horizon and liquidity
    Time horizon is how long until money is needed; liquidity is how quickly an asset can become cash without loss.
  14. Attitude to risk and capacity for loss
    Attitude to risk is how much uncertainty you are willing to accept; capacity for loss is how much you can afford to lose without harming your life.
  15. Cash savings accounts
    Cash accounts differ in access, rate and protection.
  16. Investing, speculation and gambling
    Investing, speculation and gambling all involve uncertainty, but they differ in what drives the expected return.
