Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is information asymmetry in financial services?

When firms know much more about a product's risks and costs than the customers buying it. From “Why financial services are regulated” →

In plain English: Financial products are complex, and mistakes or misconduct can cost people their savings. Key idea: Financial services are regulated because of information asymmetry, long timescales, conflicts of interest and systemic risk; regulation aims to protect consumers, keep markets fair and firms sound, and promote competition, but it has costs and cannot remove investment risk.

What is the main difference between advice and guidance?

Advice gives a personal recommendation; guidance gives general information without telling you what to do. From “Advice, guidance and execution-only” →

In plain English: Investors can get regulated advice, free guidance or make their own decisions. Key idea: Regulated advice gives a suitable personal recommendation with protection, guidance gives general information without a recommendation, and execution-only lets investors act on their own; independent advisers consider the whole market, and since 2013 advisers charge fees rather than commission.

How long does a firm normally have to respond to a complaint?

Eight weeks. From “The Financial Ombudsman Service” →

In plain English: The Financial Ombudsman Service settles disputes between consumers and financial firms. Key idea: Complain to the firm first and allow eight weeks; then the free, independent Financial Ombudsman Service can decide what is fair and order compensation, binding on the firm if accepted; the FSCS covers failed firms, and courts are a more formal alternative.

Does a firm's absence from the Warning List prove it is safe?

No, new scam firms appear all the time, so check the register too. From “Red flags and the FCA Warning List” →

In plain English: Recognising warning signs can stop a scam before money is lost. Key idea: Red flags include unsolicited contact, guaranteed high returns, pressure, unusual payment methods and false claims of regulation; the FCA Warning List and ScamSmart help investors check firms, but absence from the list does not prove safety.

What should a CFA member do if local law is less strict than the Code?

Follow the stricter standard set by the Code. From “The CFA Institute Code of Ethics” →

In plain English: Professional bodies set standards for investment professionals. Key idea: Professional bodies set codes of ethics; the CFA Institute Code requires integrity and putting clients first, and its seven Standards cover professionalism, market integrity, duties to clients and employers, analysis, conflicts and membership responsibilities.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Regulation, Protection and Ethics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is a conflict of interest for an adviser? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Being tempted to recommend products that pay the adviser more. Commission can bias recommendations away from what suits the client.

  2. Which body is part of the Bank of England? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Prudential Regulation Authority. The PRA sits within the Bank of England and supervises banks and insurers.

  3. Which is one of the Consumer Duty's cross-cutting rules? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Avoid causing foreseeable harm to customers. The three rules are good faith, avoiding foreseeable harm and supporting customers.

  4. Why are long timescales a reason to regulate pensions? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Problems may only appear decades later, when it is too late to fix them. Consumers cannot learn from experience when outcomes arrive so late.

  5. What is a clone firm? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    A fraudster pretending to be an authorised firm by copying its details. Cloning exploits the trust people place in genuine, authorised firms.

  6. Which practice would breach the consumer support outcome? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Making it much harder to leave a service than to join it. Unreasonable barriers to switching or cancelling harm customers.

  7. Which statement best describes the limits of regulation? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It controls how firms behave but cannot stop investments falling in value. Market risk remains even in well-regulated markets.

  8. Why should you use the contact details on the Financial Services Register? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Scammers may give false contact details while using a real firm's name. Using the register's details avoids clone firms.

  9. What does the price and value outcome require? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    A reasonable relationship between price and the benefits customers receive. Fair value means price matches benefits; it does not mean the lowest price.

Key takeaways

  • Financial services are regulated because of information asymmetry, long timescales, conflicts of interest and systemic risk; regulation aims to protect consumers, keep markets fair and firms sound, and promote competition, but it has costs and cannot remove investment risk.
  • The PRA, part of the Bank of England, regulates the safety of banks and insurers, while the FCA regulates conduct across around 40,000 firms; consumers should check the Financial Services Register and use its contact details to avoid clone firms.
  • The Consumer Duty, in force from July 2023, requires firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers through three cross-cutting rules and four outcomes: products and services, price and value, consumer understanding and consumer support.
  • Regulated advice gives a suitable personal recommendation with protection, guidance gives general information without a recommendation, and execution-only lets investors act on their own; independent advisers consider the whole market, and since 2013 advisers charge fees rather than commission.
  • Financial promotions must be communicated or approved by authorised firms and be fair, clear and not misleading; finfluencers promoting investments for payment without approval may break the law; investors should check who is behind content and whether risks are explained.
  • The FSCS compensates customers of failed authorised firms, protecting deposits up to £120,000 and investments up to £85,000 per person per firm; it does not cover market losses, unauthorised firms or most crypto.

Common mistakes in Regulation, Protection and Ethics

  • Thinking regulation means an investment is safe. Regulation controls how firms behave; it does not guarantee returns.
  • Assuming a firm is genuine because it uses a real company's name. Clone firms copy names of authorised firms, so check contact details on the register.
  • Thinking the Consumer Duty guarantees good investment returns. It sets standards for firms' conduct, not for market performance.
  • Treating guidance as advice. Guidance services do not recommend what you should do, so you cannot complain that a recommendation was unsuitable.
  • Assuming popular online content is reliable. Popularity and lifestyle images say nothing about the quality or legality of the promotion.
  • Thinking the FSCS protects against investments falling in value. It covers firm failure, not market losses.

Regulation, Protection and Ethics: questions students ask

What does the PRA regulate?

The safety and soundness of banks, building societies, insurers and major investment firms.

What standard must financial promotions meet?

They must be fair, clear and not misleading.

What is the FSCS protection limit for investments?

Up to £85,000 per person per firm.

Does it cost a consumer anything to use the Financial Ombudsman Service?

No, it is free for consumers.
