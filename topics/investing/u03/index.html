Key terms and formulas at a glance

Money market fund
A fund that invests in very short-term, high-quality debt and deposits, aiming to preserve capital and provide liquidity
Coupon
The fixed annual interest paid on a bond, expressed as a percentage of its nominal value
Gilt
A bond issued by the UK government through the Debt Management Office
Investment grade
Bonds rated BBB− or above (Baa3 or above at Moody's), judged to have relatively low default risk
Yield to maturity
The discount rate that equates a bond's price with the present value of its remaining cash flows
Duration
A measure of a bond's sensitivity to interest-rate changes, related to the weighted average timing of its cash flows
Index-linked gilt
A UK government bond whose coupons and repayment are adjusted for inflation
Share
A unit of ownership in a company
Ordinary share
The main class of share, carrying votes and a variable share of profits
Ex-dividend date
The date from which a buyer of a share no longer receives the declared dividend
Rights issue
An offer of new shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, usually at a discount
Rental yield
Annual rent as a percentage of the property's value, gross or net of costs

Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Name three instruments in the cash asset class.

Any three of: bank deposits, Treasury bills, money market funds, very short-term commercial paper. From “Cash as an asset class” →

In plain English: In a portfolio, "cash" means deposits and very short-term, high-quality instruments such as Treasury bills and money market funds. Key idea: Cash is the low-risk, liquid benchmark asset: excellent for stability and spending needs, poor at beating inflation over the long run.

What is the lowest investment-grade rating on the S&P scale?

BBB−. From “Corporate bonds and credit ratings” →

In plain English: Companies borrow by issuing bonds that pay more than gilts to compensate for the risk of default. Key idea: Credit ratings grade default risk; investment grade runs down to BBB− (Baa3), and the credit spread over gilts is the payment for credit and liquidity risk.

What is breakeven inflation?

The inflation rate at which conventional and index-linked bonds of the same maturity give equal returns. From “Index-linked bonds” →

In plain English: Index-linked bonds adjust their coupons and repayment for inflation, protecting real value. Key idea: Index-linked gilts protect against inflation if held to maturity, but their prices move with real yields, so they can fall heavily when real rates rise.

What majority does a special resolution need?

At least 75% of votes cast. From “Shareholder rights and corporate actions” →

In plain English: Shareholders have legal rights to vote, receive information and be offered new shares first. Key idea: Shareholders vote, receive information and have pre-emption rights; in a rights issue, take up or sell the rights rather than let them lapse.

Why can't gold be valued by discounting cash flows?

It pays no income. From “Commodities and gold” →

In plain English: Commodities are raw materials such as oil, metals and agricultural products. Key idea: Commodities pay no income; returns come from price changes and roll yield; gold may diversify but has had long periods of poor real returns.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Asset Classes

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which is a cash-type instrument issued by the UK government? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A Treasury bill. Treasury bills are short-term government debt; long gilts are bonds.

  2. What does a bondholder receive at maturity on a conventional bond? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The nominal (face) value. Conventional bonds repay their nominal value at maturity.

  3. How often do conventional gilts usually pay coupons? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Every six months. Conventional gilts pay half the annual coupon every six months.

  4. Why might a long-term investor still hold some cash? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    For liquidity, stability in falls and the ability to rebalance or meet needs without selling. Cash's value lies in liquidity and stability, not long-run growth.

  5. A company bond pays 7% while a gilt of similar maturity pays 4%. What is the main reason? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Investors demand extra return for the company's higher credit risk. The extra yield (credit spread) compensates for the risk of default.

  6. For a higher-rate taxpayer holding bonds outside an ISA, why can a low-coupon gilt bought below £100 be tax-efficient? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Most of the return comes as a capital gain, which is exempt from CGT on gilts. The pull to £100 at maturity is a CGT-free gain; only the small coupon is taxed.

  7. Which is the best evaluation of a 30-year-old holding 40% of their pension in cash for safety? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It reduces short-term falls but is likely to cost substantial long-run real growth, given the near-zero historical real return on cash. The safety of cash in nominal terms comes at the cost of growth over long horizons.

  8. Which risk remains for a UK investor holding a conventional gilt to maturity? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Inflation risk: fixed payments may buy less than expected. Held to maturity, price swings no longer matter, but unexpected inflation reduces real value.

  9. Why are gilt yields used as the "risk-free rate" for sterling assets, even though gilt prices fluctuate? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The UK government's sterling default risk is regarded as very low, so the yield reflects time and inflation rather than credit risk. Risk-free here means free of default risk, not of price movement.

Key takeaways

  • Cash is the low-risk, liquid benchmark asset: excellent for stability and spending needs, poor at beating inflation over the long run.
  • A bond pays a fixed coupon on its nominal value and repays that value at maturity; its price moves with interest rates and the issuer's creditworthiness.
  • Gilts are UK government bonds issued by the DMO, the benchmark risk-free sterling asset; their capital gains are free of CGT while coupons are taxable.
  • Credit ratings grade default risk; investment grade runs down to BBB− (Baa3), and the credit spread over gilts is the payment for credit and liquidity risk.
  • Bond prices are present values of fixed cash flows, so they move inversely to yields; yield to maturity is the bond's annual return if held to redemption.
  • Duration is a bond's interest-rate sensitivity: price change ≈ −modified duration × yield change; long, low-coupon bonds carry the most interest-rate risk.

Common mistakes in Asset Classes

  • Leaving large amounts of uninvested cash on a platform earning little interest. Check what rate, if any, the platform pays.
  • Confusing the coupon rate with the yield. The coupon is fixed on nominal value; the yield depends on the price you pay.
  • Thinking gilts cannot lose value. Their prices move with interest rates, and long gilts fell heavily in 2022.
  • Treating a high yield as free income. A high yield signals high risk of default or price falls.
  • Believing a falling bond price means the bond is "losing money" for a holder who will keep it to maturity. The price recovers to £100 at redemption if the issuer pays.
  • Assuming a "government bond fund" is low risk without checking its duration. A long gilt fund can be as volatile as a share fund.

Lessons in this school

  1. Cash as an asset class
    In a portfolio, "cash" means deposits and very short-term, high-quality instruments such as Treasury bills and money market funds.
  2. What a bond is
    A bond is a loan to a government or company that is traded as a security.
  3. Gilts
    Gilts are bonds issued by the UK government.
  4. Corporate bonds and credit ratings
    Companies borrow by issuing bonds that pay more than gilts to compensate for the risk of default.
  5. Bond prices and yields
    Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions.
  6. Duration and interest-rate risk
    Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates.
  7. Index-linked bonds
    Index-linked bonds adjust their coupons and repayment for inflation, protecting real value.
  8. What a share is
    A share is a unit of ownership in a company.
  9. Ordinary and preference shares
    Most shares are ordinary shares, with votes and a variable share of profits.
  10. Dividends and capital growth
    Shareholders earn returns in two ways: dividends paid from profits and growth in the share price.
  11. Shareholder rights and corporate actions
    Shareholders have legal rights to vote, receive information and be offered new shares first.
  12. Property as an investment
    Property can be owned directly, as a home or buy-to-let, or indirectly through funds and listed companies.
  13. Real estate investment trusts (REITs)
    A REIT is a listed company that owns income-producing property and, in return for tax advantages, must pay out most of its rental profits.
  14. Commodities and gold
    Commodities are raw materials such as oil, metals and agricultural products.
  15. Alternative investments: private equity and hedge funds
    Alternatives are investments outside listed shares, bonds and cash: private equity, hedge funds, infrastructure, private credit and others.
  16. Crypto-assets
    Crypto-assets are digital tokens recorded on distributed ledgers (blockchains), such as bitcoin and ether.
  17. Derivatives: an introduction
    A derivative is a contract whose value depends on another asset, such as a share, index, currency or commodity.

Asset Classes: questions students ask

What is reinvestment risk?

The risk that maturing money can only be reinvested at a lower rate.

What is a bond's coupon?

The fixed annual interest it pays, as a percentage of nominal value.

Who issues gilts?

The UK Debt Management Office, on behalf of HM Treasury.

What is a credit spread?

The extra yield a corporate bond pays over a government bond of similar maturity.
