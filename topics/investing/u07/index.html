Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is vertical analysis?

Expressing each item as a percentage of a base figure, such as revenue, for the same period. From “Why analyse financial statements?” →

In plain English: Financial statement analysis turns published figures into information users can act on. Key idea: Analysis serves different users' decisions; ratios, trends and common-size statements are the main tools, and figures only become meaningful when compared with previous years, competitors or targets.

How is the working capital cycle calculated?

Inventory days plus receivables days minus payables days. From “Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle” →

In plain English: Efficiency ratios show how quickly a business turns inventory into sales, collects cash from customers and pays suppliers. Key idea: Inventory, receivables and payables days measure working capital efficiency; the cycle shows how long cash is tied up, and shortening it frees cash.

Why can inflation distort ROCE?

Historical cost assets may be understated, making capital employed low and ROCE appear high. From “Limitations of ratio analysis” →

In plain English: Ratio analysis is powerful but has important limitations. Key idea: Ratios depend on accounting choices, year-end timing, one-off items and business models; analysts should adjust where possible and combine ratios with other information.

What is non-controlling interest?

The part of a subsidiary's equity not owned by the parent. From “Goodwill on acquisition” →

In plain English: When a parent buys a subsidiary, it usually pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets: the excess is goodwill. Key idea: Goodwill is consideration plus NCI less the fair value of net assets acquired; NCI at fair value gives full goodwill, at proportionate share gives partial goodwill; goodwill is tested annually for impairment.

How is the NCI's share of profit calculated?

NCI percentage multiplied by the subsidiary's profit after tax for the post-acquisition period, after relevant adjustments. From “The consolidated statement of profit or loss” →

In plain English: The consolidated statement of profit or loss combines the income and expenses of the parent and subsidiaries for the period. Key idea: Add 100% of results from the acquisition date, remove intra-group trading, interest and dividends, adjust for PURP and impairment, and split profit between owners of the parent and the NCI.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which user is most interested in a company's ability to repay a loan? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A lender. Lenders focus on liquidity, cash flow, gearing and interest cover.

  2. Revenue is £500,000 and gross profit is £150,000. What is the gross margin? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    30%. Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue: 150,000 ÷ 500,000 = 30%.

  3. Current assets are £600,000 and current liabilities £400,000. What is the current ratio? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1.5. Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities: 600,000 ÷ 400,000 = 1.5.

  4. Profit rose from £400,000 to £460,000. What is the percentage increase? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    15%. Percentage change = change ÷ original: (460,000 − 400,000) ÷ 400,000 = 15%.

  5. A company's gross margin fell from 42% to 36% while revenue rose. Which is the most likely explanation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Price cuts to win sales, or higher purchase costs. Gross margin reflects prices and cost of sales, not overheads, interest or dividends.

  6. A supermarket has a current ratio of 0.7. What is the best interpretation? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    This can be normal: supermarkets sell for cash quickly and pay suppliers later. Low ratios are common in retail where inventory turns over fast and sales are for cash.

  7. A company's gross margin is 25%. An analyst calls this poor. What is missing from the analysis? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Comparison with the company's previous years and with the industry. Whether 25% is good depends on industry norms and the trend; supermarkets have low margins, software firms high ones.

  8. A company has an operating margin of 10% and asset turnover of 1.8. A rival has a margin of 20% and turnover of 0.8. Which has the higher ROCE? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The first, with ROCE of 18% against 16%. ROCE = margin × turnover: 10% × 1.8 = 18%; 20% × 0.8 = 16%.

  9. A fast-growing company reports record profits but can't pay its suppliers. What is the most likely cause? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Overtrading: cash is tied up in receivables and inventory as sales grow. Rapid growth can consume working capital faster than profits generate cash.

Key takeaways

  • Analysis serves different users' decisions; ratios, trends and common-size statements are the main tools, and figures only become meaningful when compared with previous years, competitors or targets.
  • Margins show profit per pound of revenue; ROCE shows profit per pound of capital and equals margin times asset turnover; analyse which driver changed.
  • The current and quick ratios measure short-term liquidity; norms depend on the industry, and both very low and very high ratios can signal problems.
  • Inventory, receivables and payables days measure working capital efficiency; the cycle shows how long cash is tied up, and shortening it frees cash.
  • Gearing and interest cover measure financial risk; EPS, P/E, dividend yield and dividend cover show returns and market expectations for shareholders.
  • Good interpretation explains the causes of ratio changes, links ratios together, compares with others and reaches a balanced conclusion supported by evidence.

Common mistakes in Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

  • Drawing conclusions from one ratio for one year. Ratios need context: the industry, the company's strategy, the economy and the trend over several years.
  • Comparing ROCE across companies that calculate capital employed differently. Check definitions, and note that revaluations or old, heavily depreciated assets can distort ROCE.
  • Treating the quick ratio as always more reliable. It excludes inventory, but receivables may also be slow or doubtful; check receivables days and cash flows too.
  • Using year-end balances without considering seasonality. A year-end at a quiet point may make inventory or receivables look unusually low; averages give a better picture.
  • Assuming high gearing is always bad. Debt is often cheaper than equity and can raise returns to shareholders; the risk depends on the stability of profits and cash flows.
  • Listing ratios and saying only whether each went up or down. Examiners and users need explanations of why ratios changed and what this means for the business.

Lessons in this school

  1. Why analyse financial statements?
    Financial statement analysis turns published figures into information users can act on.
  2. Profitability ratios
    Profitability ratios measure how well a company turns revenue and capital into profit.
  3. Liquidity ratios
    Liquidity ratios show whether a company can meet its short-term obligations.
  4. Efficiency ratios and the working capital cycle
    Efficiency ratios show how quickly a business turns inventory into sales, collects cash from customers and pays suppliers.
  5. Gearing and investor ratios
    Gearing ratios show how far a company is financed by debt, and investor ratios show returns to shareholders.
  6. Interpreting ratios in context
    Ratios are only useful when interpreted in context.
  7. Limitations of ratio analysis
    Ratio analysis is powerful but has important limitations.
  8. Analysing cash flows
    Cash flow analysis shows whether a company generates enough cash to sustain itself and how it funds investment and returns to shareholders.
  9. Performance in not-for-profit organisations
    Charities, public bodies and other not-for-profit organisations exist to achieve a mission, not to make profit.
  10. Groups and control
    A group is a parent company and the subsidiaries it controls.
  11. Goodwill on acquisition
    When a parent buys a subsidiary, it usually pays more than the fair value of the identifiable net assets: the excess is goodwill.
  12. The consolidated statement of financial position
    The consolidated statement of financial position adds together the parent's and subsidiaries' assets and liabilities and shows the equity attributable to the parent and to non-controlling interests.
  13. Intra-group trading and unrealised profit
    When group companies trade with each other, the sales, purchases, balances and any profit left in inventory must be eliminated on consolidation.
  14. The consolidated statement of profit or loss
    The consolidated statement of profit or loss combines the income and expenses of the parent and subsidiaries for the period.
  15. Associates and joint arrangements
    Investments that give significant influence or joint control aren't consolidated line by line.
  16. Creative accounting and earnings management
    Creative accounting uses the flexibility in accounting rules, and sometimes breaks them, to present a better picture than reality.
  17. Narrative reporting and the strategic report
    Annual reports contain much more than the financial statements.

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts: questions students ask

How is ROCE calculated?

Operating profit divided by capital employed (equity plus non-current liabilities), times 100.

Why are inventories excluded from the quick ratio?

They are the least liquid current asset and may take time to sell.

How is interest cover calculated?

Operating profit divided by finance costs.

What does "linkage" mean in ratio interpretation?

Connecting changes in different ratios to a common cause, such as an expansion affecting margins, ROCE and gearing.
