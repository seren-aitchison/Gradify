Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

How did Coleridge distinguish poetry from prose?

Prose is words in their best order; poetry is the best words in the best order. From “What makes a poem?” →

In plain English: Poetry is language organised by the line as well as the sentence, using sound, rhythm and compression to make meaning. Key idea: A poem organises language by the line and attends closely to sound, pattern and compression; readers should ask what each of these contributes to meaning.

What is a trochee?

A foot of a stressed followed by an unstressed syllable, as in garden. From “Stress and the metrical foot” →

In plain English: English metre is built from patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables. Key idea: Metre is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; feet are its units, and lines are named by foot and number of feet.

What is the rhyme scheme of a Shakespearean sonnet?

ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. From “The sonnet” →

In plain English: The sonnet is a fourteen-line poem with a strict rhyme scheme and a turn in thought. Key idea: A sonnet is fourteen lines with a set rhyme scheme and a volta; Petrarchan sonnets divide 8 + 6, Shakespearean sonnets 4 + 4 + 4 + 2.

Who is the silent listener in "My Last Duchess"?

An envoy from a count whose daughter the Duke hopes to marry. From “The dramatic monologue” →

In plain English: A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by a character to a silent listener at a dramatic moment, revealing more than the speaker intends. Key idea: A dramatic monologue lets a character speak at a dramatic moment to a silent listener, revealing more than they intend; the reader's judgement comes from what the speaker gives away.

What is the rhyme scheme of terza rima?

ABA BCB CDC and so on, each middle rhyme starting the next tercet. From “Stanza forms” →

In plain English: A stanza is a group of lines forming a unit in a poem. Key idea: Stanza forms shape a poem's movement: couplets give point, terza rima pushes forward, ottava rima sets up and punctures, the villanelle circles back.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Poetry and Poetics

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which poet described poetry as "emotion recollected in tranquillity"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Wordsworth. The phrase comes from Wordsworth's Preface to Lyrical Ballads, in the 1802 version.

  2. What is a persona in poetry? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A character or mask the poet adopts as speaker. A persona is a voice the poet takes on, as in a dramatic monologue.

  3. Which type of imagery appeals to the sense of smell? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Olfactory. Olfactory imagery relates to smell; auditory to sound, tactile to touch, kinaesthetic to movement.

  4. In "London", what does repeating "charter'd" across two lines most clearly suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    That even the river is controlled by commerce and law. Chartered means legally granted or owned; applying it to the river as well as the streets shows control reaching into nature.

  5. Keats's "Ode on a Grecian Urn" speaks directly to the urn. Which technique is this? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Apostrophe. Addressing an object that can't answer is apostrophe.

  6. Owen describes soldiers "Bent double, like old beggars under sacks". What is the main effect? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It presents young soldiers as broken and degraded, undermining heroic images of war. The simile reduces soldiers to aged beggars, contradicting the glory the poem's title quotes.

  7. A student says a prose poem proves line breaks don't matter in poetry. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Prose poems show the line isn't essential, but in lineated poems the line remains a key source of meaning. The prose poem complicates any single definition, but that doesn't make the line irrelevant where poets use it.

  8. In Blake's "The Chimney Sweeper", the child accepts that duty keeps him safe. What is the most convincing reading? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Blake creates irony: the child's trust exposes the cruelty of a society that exploits him. Distinguishing speaker from poet reveals the gap between the child's innocence and the poet's protest.

  9. A student says the worm in "The Sick Rose" simply means a garden pest. What is the strongest response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The worm works as a symbol whose secrecy and night-time love suggest hidden corruption, not just a pest. The poem's language of night, bed and joy invites symbolic readings; reducing it to a literal pest misses its suggestiveness.

Key takeaways

  • A poem organises language by the line and attends closely to sound, pattern and compression; readers should ask what each of these contributes to meaning.
  • The speaker is a constructed voice that may differ sharply from the poet; the gap between them is often where irony and meaning lie.
  • Imagery appeals to the senses and carries feeling and ideas; analyse what each image suggests and how images form patterns across the poem.
  • Identify the sound pattern precisely, then explain what it does in context: imitating, linking, slowing or speeding, or creating contrast.
  • Metre is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; feet are its units, and lines are named by foot and number of feet.
  • Iambic pentameter is five iambs; skilled poets vary it constantly, and the meaning often lies in where and why the pattern is broken.

Common mistakes in Poetry and Poetics

  • Treating a poem as prose with line breaks added. The line, the sound and the form are part of what the poem says, not decoration around a message.
  • Assuming a poem written in the first person tells us the poet's own feelings or experiences. Even confessional poetry shapes and selects; treat biography as context, not as the meaning.
  • Listing images without saying what they do. Identify the image, then explain its connotations and its effect in context.
  • Claiming a sound has a fixed meaning, such as "sibilance always sounds sinister". Sounds take their effect from context; the same s sounds can be soothing or menacing.
  • Marking stress by the metre instead of by how the words are actually said. Start with natural pronunciation; the metre is the underlying pattern, and real lines vary from it.
  • Saying a line "is in iambic pentameter so it sounds like a heartbeat" and stopping there. Comment on how this line's rhythm, and its departures from the pattern, shape its meaning.

Lessons in this school

  1. What makes a poem?
    Poetry is language organised by the line as well as the sentence, using sound, rhythm and compression to make meaning.
  2. The speaker and the poetic voice
    Every poem has a speaker, the voice that says the words, who isn't necessarily the poet.
  3. Imagery in poetry
    Imagery is language that appeals to the senses.
  4. Rhyme and sound patterning
    Poets organise sound as carefully as meaning.
  5. Stress and the metrical foot
    English metre is built from patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables.
  6. Iambic pentameter
    Iambic pentameter, five iambs per line, has been the dominant metre of English poetry since Chaucer.
  7. Other metres and metrical variation
    English poetry uses many metres besides iambic pentameter.
  8. Free verse
    Free verse has no regular metre or rhyme scheme, but it isn't formless: it builds rhythm through lines, repetition and syntax.
  9. The sonnet
    The sonnet is a fourteen-line poem with a strict rhyme scheme and a turn in thought.
  10. The ballad
    The ballad is a narrative poem or song, often in quatrains, rooted in oral tradition.
  11. The ode
    The ode is a lyric poem of praise or meditation in an elevated style, usually addressed to someone or something.
  12. The elegy
    An elegy is a poem of mourning.
  13. The dramatic monologue
    A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by a character to a silent listener at a dramatic moment, revealing more than the speaker intends.
  14. The lyric
    The lyric is a short poem expressing a single speaker's thoughts or feelings.
  15. Blank verse
    Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter, the metre of Shakespeare's plays, Milton's Paradise Lost and Wordsworth's Prelude.
  16. Narrative and epic poetry
    Narrative poems tell stories; epics tell them on a heroic scale.
  17. Stanza forms
    A stanza is a group of lines forming a unit in a poem.
  18. Enjambment, caesura and the line
    The relationship between the line and the sentence is one of poetry's main resources.
  19. Analysing a poem: a method
    Close reading a poem means looking carefully at how its language, form and structure create meaning.
  20. Comparing poems
    Comparing poems sharpens understanding of each by showing what they share and how they differ.

Poetry and Poetics: questions students ask

Why do critics usually write "the speaker" rather than the poet's name?

Because the voice in a poem is constructed and may differ from the poet's own views.

What is the difference between a simile and a metaphor?

A simile compares using "like" or "as"; a metaphor states that one thing is another.

What is eye rhyme?

Words that look as if they rhyme but sound different, such as love and move.

What is a feminine ending?

An extra unstressed syllable at the end of a line.
