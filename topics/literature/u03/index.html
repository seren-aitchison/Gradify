Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why is a play script sometimes called a blueprint?

Because it is completed by actors, directors and designers in performance. From “Drama as performance” →

In plain English: A play is written to be performed, so the text is only part of the work. Key idea: Drama is completed in performance: meaning depends on actors, staging, space and audience, so reading a play means imagining how it could be performed.

What is dramatic irony?

When the audience knows something that characters on stage don't. From “Dramatic structure” →

In plain English: Plays are shaped by structure: how action is divided and how tension builds and resolves. Key idea: Dramatic structure divides action into acts and scenes and builds tension through exposition, rising action and climax to falling action and resolution; irony, foreshadowing and subplots add meaning.

Which characters typically speak in prose in Shakespeare?

Comic and lower-status characters, and characters who are mad or speaking informally. From “Shakespeare's language” →

In plain English: Shakespeare moves between blank verse, rhyme and prose for dramatic effect. Key idea: Shakespeare uses blank verse, rhyme and prose for different characters and moods, and shifts between them meaningfully; imagery, puns, antithesis and rhetoric carry character and theme.

Who disguises herself as Cesario in Twelfth Night?

Viola. From “Shakespearean comedy” →

In plain English: Shakespeare's comedies blend romance, wit, disguise and festivity. Key idea: Shakespearean comedy combines lovers, disguise, wit, fools and marriage endings; the green world allows transformation, and darker elements often complicate the happy resolution.

What is an epigram?

A short, witty and often paradoxical saying. From “Wilde, Shaw and the Victorian stage” →

In plain English: The late Victorian and Edwardian stage saw a revival of serious and witty drama. Key idea: Victorian theatre was dominated by melodrama and the well-made play; Wilde's witty comedies satirised society through paradox, while Shaw's plays of ideas used drama to argue about social problems.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Drama and Shakespeare

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which feature most distinguishes drama from prose fiction? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    It is written to be performed before an audience. Drama's meaning is completed in performance, usually without a narrator.

  2. What is anagnorisis? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The moment when the tragic hero recognises the truth. Anagnorisis is the moment of recognition, when the hero discovers the truth.

  3. Which play is a comedy of manners? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The Importance of Being Earnest. Wilde's play mocks upper-class manners through witty dialogue.

  4. Two productions of the same play present one character as comic and as tragic, without changing the text. What does this show? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Performance choices shape meaning beyond the words. Delivery, casting and staging can produce different interpretations of the same lines.

  5. In Oedipus the King, Oedipus discovers he has killed his father. Which term describes this moment? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Anagnorisis. The discovery of the terrible truth is recognition, anagnorisis.

  6. A play features twins mistaken for each other, disguise and a series of marriages at the end. Which type is it most likely? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Romantic comedy. Mistaken identity and marriages are conventions of romantic comedy, as in Twelfth Night.

  7. A student writes, "The reader feels tension as Macbeth looks at his hands." What is the best improvement? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Discuss the audience and how staging, pauses and sound effects create tension. Drama analysis should consider the audience and theatrical effects, not just the reader.

  8. A student says Othello's hamartia is simply that he is "evil". What is the best response? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Hamartia means an error of judgement; Othello's trust in Iago and jealousy lead him astray, but he isn't simply evil. Aristotle's hero is neither wholly good nor bad; the error brings about the fall.

  9. Why do some critics find the ending of Twelfth Night unsettling? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Malvolio's humiliation and bitter exit leave tensions that the marriages don't resolve. Malvolio's vow of revenge darkens the festive resolution.

Key takeaways

  • Drama is completed in performance: meaning depends on actors, staging, space and audience, so reading a play means imagining how it could be performed.
  • Tragedy shows a significant figure's fall through hamartia, reversal and recognition, arousing pity and fear; its forms have changed from Greek drama to modern tragedies of ordinary lives.
  • Comedy moves from confusion to happy resolution through misunderstanding, disguise and wit; romantic comedy, satire, comedy of manners and farce use humour differently, often with serious social purposes.
  • Dramatic structure divides action into acts and scenes and builds tension through exposition, rising action and climax to falling action and resolution; irony, foreshadowing and subplots add meaning.
  • Character in drama is revealed through speech, action and others' reactions; subtext carries unspoken meaning, and patterns of dialogue show shifts in power and relationships.
  • Stagecraft (set, lighting, sound, costume, props and movement) shapes meaning in performance; stage directions may be explicit in the text or implicit in the dialogue.

Common mistakes in Drama and Shakespeare

  • Writing about a play as if it were a novel. Avoid phrases such as "the reader learns"; consider the audience, the stage and how a moment might be played.
  • Translating hamartia simply as a "fatal flaw" of character. Aristotle's term suggests an error or misjudgement, which may involve ignorance rather than vice.
  • Treating comedy as trivial. Many comedies satirise society, question gender roles or contain darker elements that complicate their happy endings.
  • Applying Freytag's pyramid rigidly to every play. It describes many classical and Shakespearean tragedies, but modern plays often use different shapes, circular structures or fragmented time.
  • Taking characters' words at face value. In drama, characters lie, hide feelings and say the opposite of what they mean; look for contradictions between words and actions.
  • Ignoring stage directions when writing about a play. They are part of the text and often carry key meanings, especially in modern drama.

Lessons in this school

  1. Drama as performance
    A play is written to be performed, so the text is only part of the work.
  2. Tragedy
    Tragedy shows the fall of a significant figure through a combination of character and circumstance.
  3. Comedy
    Comedy aims to amuse and usually ends happily, often in marriage or reconciliation.
  4. Dramatic structure
    Plays are shaped by structure: how action is divided and how tension builds and resolves.
  5. Character and dialogue in drama
    In drama, character is revealed mainly through dialogue and action.
  6. Stagecraft and stage directions
    Stagecraft is everything that turns a script into a performance: set, lighting, sound, costume, props and movement.
  7. Shakespeare's theatre
    Shakespeare wrote for particular theatres, companies and audiences.
  8. Shakespeare's language
    Shakespeare moves between blank verse, rhyme and prose for dramatic effect.
  9. Soliloquy and aside
    Soliloquies and asides let characters share their thoughts with the audience.
  10. Shakespearean tragedy
    Shakespeare's great tragedies, including Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth, centre on a hero whose choices and flaws lead to catastrophe.
  11. Shakespearean comedy
    Shakespeare's comedies blend romance, wit, disguise and festivity.
  12. The history plays and the late romances
    Beyond tragedy and comedy, Shakespeare wrote English history plays and, late in his career, romances.
  13. Marlowe and Shakespeare's contemporaries
    Shakespeare worked alongside other major playwrights.
  14. Restoration and eighteenth-century drama
    After the theatres reopened in 1660, English drama changed: women appeared on stage, indoor theatres dominated, and the witty comedy of manners flourished.
  15. Wilde, Shaw and the Victorian stage
    The late Victorian and Edwardian stage saw a revival of serious and witty drama.
  16. Ibsen, Chekhov and modern realism
    In the late nineteenth century, Ibsen and Chekhov transformed drama with realism: ordinary settings, everyday speech and serious social and psychological themes.
  17. Modernist and absurdist drama
    Twentieth-century dramatists broke with realism.
  18. Post-war and contemporary British drama
    British drama since 1945 has included the "angry young men", Pinter's comedy of menace, political and feminist theatre, and the in-yer-face drama of the 1990s.

Drama and Shakespeare: questions students ask

What is peripeteia?

A reversal of fortune in a tragedy.

How do Shakespeare's comedies typically end?

With marriages, reunions or reconciliation.

What are the five parts of Freytag's pyramid?

Exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and catastrophe or resolution.

What is subtext?

The meaning beneath a character's words: what they really think or feel but don't say.
