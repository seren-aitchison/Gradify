Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

Why does the play present Banquo favourably?

James I claimed descent from Banquo, so Shakespeare made him noble and showed his line of kings. From “Macbeth: context” →

In plain English: Macbeth was written around 1606 for a new king who was fascinated by witchcraft and who traced his ancestry to Banquo. Key idea: Macbeth reflects James I's interests in kingship, Banquo's line and witchcraft, and post-Gunpowder Plot anxieties about treason; use context to explain the play's choices.

How does Macduff respond when told to "dispute it like a man"?

"I shall do so; / But I must also feel it as a man." From “Macbeth: gender and Lady Macbeth” →

In plain English: Macbeth repeatedly connects violence with masculinity and questions what it means to be a man or a woman. Key idea: Lady Macbeth rejects femininity to drive the murder, while the play links manhood to violence; Macduff's grief offers a fuller model, and Lady Macbeth's collapse suggests the cost of denying feeling.

Which family does the creature observe and learn from?

The De Lacey family. From “Frankenstein: the creature and sympathy” →

In plain English: The creature begins benevolent but is turned to violence by rejection. Key idea: Shelley gives the creature an eloquent voice and shows him rejected at every turn, creating sympathy that is complicated, but not cancelled, by his crimes.

How does Scrooge describe himself on Christmas morning?

"As light as a feather ... as happy as an angel ... as merry as a school-boy." From “A Christmas Carol: Scrooge's transformation” →

In plain English: Over five staves, Scrooge changes from a cold miser to a generous, joyful man. Key idea: Each ghost moves Scrooge a stage further, from regret to compassion to fear and resolution, and Dickens marks his change by reversing the imagery of hardness and isolation.

What do Gilbert and Gubar call Bertha?

Jane's "dark double", who acts out the rage Jane suppresses. From “Jane Eyre: Bertha Mason and critical readings” →

In plain English: Bertha Mason, Rochester's first wife, is hidden in the attic of Thornfield. Key idea: Bertha is presented as a Gothic, dehumanised figure; feminist critics read her as Jane's suppressed rage, while postcolonial critics show how Jane's freedom depends on a silenced colonial woman.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which king was Shakespeare's patron when Macbeth was written? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    James I. James became patron of the King's Men after succeeding to the English throne in 1603.

  2. How is Macbeth presented at the start of the play? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    As a brave and loyal warrior. The Captain calls him "brave Macbeth" for his ferocity in battle for Duncan.

  3. Which metre distinguishes the witches' chants? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Trochaic tetrameter. Their four-beat, falling rhythm sets them apart from the nobles' iambic pentameter.

  4. How does the play show disorder in nature after Duncan's murder? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Darkness covers the day and Duncan's horses eat each other. Unnatural events show the Great Chain of Being disrupted by regicide.

  5. What does "I am in blood / Stepped in so far" suggest about Macbeth's state? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    He feels committed to further violence. The image of wading through a river of blood suggests turning back is as hard as going on.

  6. Why is Macbeth's first line, "So foul and fair a day I have not seen", significant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It echoes the witches before he has met them. The echo links Macbeth to the witches' world of confusion from the start.

  7. Which change to Holinshed best reflects Shakespeare's audience? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Making Banquo innocent of Duncan's murder. In Holinshed Banquo is an accomplice; James I's ancestor had to be innocent.

  8. How does Banquo's response to the prophecies affect our view of Macbeth? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It shows Macbeth chose to act where Banquo did not. Banquo receives prophecies too but does not murder, highlighting Macbeth's agency.

  9. Which reading of Banquo's ghost is best supported by the feast scene? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It may be a manifestation of Macbeth's guilt, since only he sees it. Only Macbeth sees the ghost, which supports a psychological reading.

Key takeaways

  • Macbeth reflects James I's interests in kingship, Banquo's line and witchcraft, and post-Gunpowder Plot anxieties about treason; use context to explain the play's choices.
  • Shakespeare presents ambition as a force that, once acted upon, demands ever more violence and leaves its holder fearful, isolated and finally nihilistic; Macbeth chooses each step knowingly.
  • The supernatural creates moral confusion and tempts Macbeth with equivocal truths, but the play leaves room for his choices; the dagger and ghost can be read as projections of his guilt.
  • Lady Macbeth rejects femininity to drive the murder, while the play links manhood to violence; Macduff's grief offers a fuller model, and Lady Macbeth's collapse suggests the cost of denying feeling.
  • Blood, darkness, sleep and clothing imagery trace the move from heroism to guilt and disorder; changes between verse and prose signal loss of control.
  • Frankenstein combines Romantic, Gothic and scientific contexts with a nested structure of three narrators, which gives the creature a central voice and invites readers to judge each narrator's reliability.

Common mistakes in Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose

  • Bolting on paragraphs of history. Every contextual point should be tied to a specific moment, character or quotation.
  • Blaming the witches or Lady Macbeth entirely. The text gives Macbeth knowledge and choice, especially in the later murders.
  • Saying the witches make Macbeth kill Duncan. They never instruct him to murder; they predict, and he interprets.
  • Calling Lady Macbeth simply evil or a "fiend-like queen". That is Malcolm's verdict; her guilt, breakdown and death make her more complex.
  • Spotting an image without saying how it develops. Show how the meaning of blood, darkness or sleep changes through the play.
  • Treating Victor as a reliable narrator. He tells the story from his point of view, minimising his responsibility and seeking sympathy.

Lessons in this school

  1. Macbeth: context
    Macbeth was written around 1606 for a new king who was fascinated by witchcraft and who traced his ancestry to Banquo.
  2. Macbeth: ambition and power
    Macbeth traces how ambition, once unleashed, drives a brave soldier to murder and tyranny.
  3. Macbeth: the supernatural
    The witches, the dagger, Banquo's ghost and the apparitions create an atmosphere of evil and uncertainty.
  4. Macbeth: gender and Lady Macbeth
    Macbeth repeatedly connects violence with masculinity and questions what it means to be a man or a woman.
  5. Macbeth: language and imagery
    Shakespeare builds the meaning of Macbeth through recurring images of blood, darkness, sleep and ill-fitting clothes.
  6. Frankenstein: context and form
    Frankenstein grew out of a ghost-story contest in 1816 and the scientific and political ferment of the Romantic period.
  7. Frankenstein: creation and responsibility
    Victor Frankenstein creates life and then abandons it.
  8. Frankenstein: the creature and sympathy
    The creature begins benevolent but is turned to violence by rejection.
  9. Frankenstein: nature, science and the sublime
    Shelley sets her story among Alpine glaciers, Arctic ice and the Scottish islands.
  10. A Christmas Carol: context
    A Christmas Carol was written in six weeks in 1843 as a response to poverty and child labour in industrial Britain.
  11. A Christmas Carol: Scrooge's transformation
    Over five staves, Scrooge changes from a cold miser to a generous, joyful man.
  12. A Christmas Carol: social responsibility
    Dickens uses A Christmas Carol to argue that everyone is responsible for the welfare of others.
  13. Jane Eyre: context
    Jane Eyre, published in 1847 under the pseudonym Currer Bell, drew on Charlotte Brontë's own experience of harsh schooling and work as a governess.
  14. Jane Eyre: independence and identity
    Jane Eyre is driven by a struggle for independence, equality and self-respect against social, religious and romantic pressures.
  15. Jane Eyre: Bertha Mason and critical readings
    Bertha Mason, Rochester's first wife, is hidden in the attic of Thornfield.
  16. Jekyll and Hyde: context
    Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886) reflects late-Victorian anxieties about respectability, science, evolution and the city.
  17. Jekyll and Hyde: duality
    Stevenson explores the idea that every person contains good and evil, and the dangers of trying to separate them.
  18. Jekyll and Hyde: narrative and setting
    Stevenson withholds the truth by telling most of the story through a cautious lawyer and by setting it in a foggy, divided London.

Set Texts: Drama and Nineteenth-Century Prose: questions students ask

What image does Macbeth use for his ambition in Act 1 Scene 7?

"Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on th' other", a rider who jumps too far.

What paradox do the witches chant in Act 1 Scene 1?

"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."

What does Lady Macbeth ask the spirits to do?

"Unsex me here", removing her feminine qualities and filling her with cruelty.

How does clothing imagery present Macbeth's kingship?

His titles fit poorly, "like a giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief", showing he is unfit to rule.
