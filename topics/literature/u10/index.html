The speaker, the situation, the subject and the overall tone. From “Approaching an unseen poem” →

What three areas can texts be compared on?

Ideas and attitudes, methods (language, form, structure) and contexts. From “Comparative analysis: principles” →

Lessons in this school

  1. Approaching an unseen poem
    Unseen poetry questions test whether you can read a poem you have never met and write about it with insight under time pressure.
  2. Approaching unseen prose
    Unseen prose extracts test your ability to analyse narrative, character and style quickly.
  3. Approaching unseen drama
    Drama is written to be performed, so an unseen scene must be read with the stage in mind.
  4. Comparative analysis: principles
    Comparing texts reveals more about each than reading them separately.
  5. Structuring a comparison
    A clear structure helps a comparative essay stay balanced and focused.
  6. Comparing across periods
    Comparing texts from different periods shows how contexts shape writing and how writers respond to one another across time.
  7. Reading the question
    Many essays lose marks by answering the question the writer wanted rather than the one set.
  8. Building an argument
    A literature essay is an argument, not a list of observations.
  9. Paragraphing: point, evidence, analysis
    Well-built paragraphs carry an essay's argument.
  10. Embedding quotations
    Quotations work best when they are short and woven into your own sentences.
  11. Writing about context
    Context can deepen interpretation when it is linked to the text.
  12. Writing about writers' methods
    The highest marks go to essays that explain how writers create meaning.
  13. Introductions and conclusions
    Introductions and conclusions frame an essay's argument.
  14. Academic style and register
    Clear, precise academic style helps your argument persuade.
  15. Referencing and academic integrity
    Referencing gives credit to others' ideas and lets readers check your sources.
  16. The coursework essay
    Coursework, or non-exam assessment, gives time to research and write an extended, independent essay.
  17. Revising for literature exams
    Effective revision for literature exams combines knowing texts deeply with practising timed analytical writing.

Key terms

Unseen
A text you have not studied before, presented in an exam
Narrative voice
The perspective and personality of the narrator telling the story
Subtext
The underlying meaning beneath what characters say
Comparative analysis
Analysing two or more texts together to illuminate both
Integrated structure
Each paragraph compares both texts on one point
Intertextuality
The relationship between texts through echo, allusion, parody or response
Command word
A word in a question that tells you what to do, such as "explore" or "evaluate"
Thesis
A clear, arguable statement answering the question
Topic sentence
The first sentence of a paragraph stating its main point
Embedded quotation
A quotation woven grammatically into the writer's own sentence
Context
The circumstances in which a text was written and read
Form
The type of text and its conventions
Synthesis
Bringing ideas together to show how they relate
Register
The level of formality in language
