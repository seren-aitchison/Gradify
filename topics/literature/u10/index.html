Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What should you identify on a first reading of an unseen poem?

The speaker, the situation, the subject and the overall tone. From “Approaching an unseen poem” →

In plain English: Unseen poetry questions test whether you can read a poem you have never met and write about it with insight under time pressure. Key idea: Read an unseen poem at least twice, annotate language, form, structure and tone, find the turning point, and plan three or four points that answer the question, beginning with an overview.

What three areas can texts be compared on?

Ideas and attitudes, methods (language, form, structure) and contexts. From “Comparative analysis: principles” →

In plain English: Comparing texts reveals more about each than reading them separately. Key idea: Good comparison focuses on how texts present ideas, compares attitudes, methods and contexts, identifies similarities and differences, and keeps both texts in balance throughout.

What is a thesis?

A clear statement of your overall answer to the question. From “Reading the question” →

In plain English: Many essays lose marks by answering the question the writer wanted rather than the one set. Key idea: Identify the focus and command words, treat statements as invitations to debate with a judgement, write a thesis that answers the question, and keep its key words visible throughout the essay.

What is the context of reception?

How readers and audiences in different periods have responded to a text. From “Writing about context” →

In plain English: Context can deepen interpretation when it is linked to the text. Key idea: Context includes historical, biographical, literary and reception contexts; integrate brief, precise context to explain specific choices and responses, avoiding irrelevant facts and generalisations.

Why use tentative language in literature essays?

Because texts allow several interpretations, and tentative language shows thoughtful judgement. From “Academic style and register” →

In plain English: Clear, precise academic style helps your argument persuade. Key idea: Write in a formal, precise register, use tentative and evaluative language, write about texts in the literary present tense, refer to writers by surname and present titles correctly.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. What is the first step in reading an unseen poem? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Read it through for overall sense. An initial reading gives the overall meaning.

  2. Which narrator uses "I"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A first-person narrator. First-person narrators tell the story from their own perspective.

  3. In an unseen scene, a character alone on stage speaks her private thoughts aloud. What is this? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    A soliloquy. Soliloquies give the audience access to a character's inner thoughts.

  4. Why should an unseen answer be organised by ideas rather than line by line? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It builds an argument that answers the question. Thematic organisation shows analysis rather than paraphrase.

  5. What is the effect of narrowing focus from a wide landscape to a single figure? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It directs attention and builds interest in that figure. Structural focus guides the reader's attention.

  6. What effect might a long pause in a stage direction create? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Tension and unspoken emotion. Silence can carry meaning and build tension.

  7. A poem's last line is much shorter than the rest. What might a strong response say? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The broken pattern emphasises a final change or loss. Departures from a pattern create emphasis and meaning.

  8. Which comment shows analysis rather than retelling? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The short sentence "She waited." slows the pace and heightens tension. Analysis explains a method and its effect.

  9. The audience knows a letter has been hidden, but the character searching for it does not. What technique is this? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Dramatic irony. The audience's greater knowledge creates tension.

Key takeaways

  • Read an unseen poem at least twice, annotate language, form, structure and tone, find the turning point, and plan three or four points that answer the question, beginning with an overview.
  • In unseen prose, identify narrative voice and the extract's position, then analyse setting, characterisation, language and structure, always linking methods to their effects on the reader.
  • Read unseen drama as a script for performance, analysing stage directions, dialogue, subtext, dramatic irony and shifts of power, and explain how the scene creates tension and audience response.
  • Good comparison focuses on how texts present ideas, compares attitudes, methods and contexts, identifies similarities and differences, and keeps both texts in balance throughout.
  • An integrated structure, with each paragraph comparing both texts on one point, is usually strongest; topic sentence, evidence from both, comparison and evaluation build a clear argument framed by a comparative introduction and conclusion.
  • Cross-period comparison shows how contexts shape texts and how later writers respond to earlier ones; use precise context to explain choices, consider intertextuality and avoid generalising about whole periods.

Common mistakes in Unseen, Comparative and Essay Writing

  • Working through the poem line by line. Organise your answer by ideas so that it builds an argument.
  • Retelling the plot instead of analysing. Every point should explain how and why the writer creates an effect.
  • Ignoring stage directions. They are part of the text and show how meaning is created in performance.
  • Writing about one text then the other with a single comparative sentence at the end. Integrate comparison throughout.
  • Using a block structure that discusses one text fully before the other. It weakens comparison.
  • Using context as a list of facts. Context must help explain the writer's choices and the text's meaning.

What is subtext?

The meaning beneath what characters actually say.

What is the weakness of a block structure?

It discusses each text separately, giving little real comparison.

The way texts echo, allude to, parody or respond to other texts.
