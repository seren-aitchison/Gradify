Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What did the Russian Formalists mean by literariness?

The features that make language literary, above all the way it draws attention to its own form. From “What is literature?” →

In plain English: Literature is hard to define, but every definition tries to say what makes some writing worth reading closely and repeatedly. Key idea: No single test defines literature. Imaginative content, self-conscious language and cultural value each capture part of it, and the study of literature asks how and why particular texts reward close and repeated reading.

What makes contextual knowledge relevant in an essay?

It explains the meaning or force of a specific detail in the text and supports an interpretation. From “Text and context” →

In plain English: Context is the set of circumstances in which a text was written and has been read. Key idea: Context should explain something in the text. Use it to show why a word, character or choice had a particular force, not as a free-standing history lesson.

Name the sense each imagery type appeals to: auditory, olfactory, gustatory.

Auditory: sound; olfactory: smell; gustatory: taste. From “Imagery” →

In plain English: Imagery is language that appeals to the senses. Key idea: Analyse imagery by naming the sense it appeals to, explaining what it suggests, and, where an image recurs, tracking how its meaning develops through the text.

Name three features that create tone in writing.

Any three of: diction, syntax, rhythm, sound, imagery, punctuation. From “Tone and mood” →

In plain English: Tone is the attitude a text's voice takes towards its subject and reader; mood is the atmosphere and feeling the text creates. Key idea: Describe tone with precise adjectives and evidence: "the narrator's tone is wry rather than angry, because…". Where tone changes, say where, how and why.

What is dramatic irony?

When the audience or reader knows something a character does not. From “Irony” →

In plain English: Irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what happens. Key idea: To analyse irony, state both levels (what is said or expected, and what is meant or happens), show the signals that reveal the gap, and explain what the irony makes the reader think or feel.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Foundations of Literary Study

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. Which critic argued that art "defamiliarises" familiar things so that we perceive them afresh? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Viktor Shklovsky. Shklovsky's essay "Art as Technique" (1917) is the classic statement of defamiliarisation, a key idea in Russian Formalism.

  2. Which of these is a form rather than a genre? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    The sonnet. The sonnet is a fixed poetic form defined by its shape (fourteen lines, set rhyme schemes); the others are genres defined by conventions of content and effect.

  3. Who introduced the term "implied author"? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Wayne C. Booth. Booth introduced it in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961) to describe the authorial presence inferred from a work.

  4. A student says a cereal advert that uses rhyme and alliteration must be literature. Which reply is best? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Patterned language is one feature of literature but not enough on its own to make a text literary. The Formalist test identifies literariness, but literature is also defined by purpose and value; many patterned texts are not literature.

  5. A novel opens in a crumbling castle during a storm, with a locked room nobody may enter. Which genre's conventions is it using? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Gothic. Remote, decaying buildings, extreme weather and forbidden secrets are classic Gothic conventions.

  6. In a first-person story, the narrator insists he is calm and fair while describing how he spied on and punished a neighbour. What should a reader most likely conclude? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The narrator is unreliable, and the gap between his claims and actions is the point. When a narrator's self-description conflicts with what he reports, the implied author invites us to doubt him.

  7. Which is the strongest objection to defining literature purely as "writing a culture values"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It cannot explain why some texts reward close reading more than others, so it may make value seem arbitrary. A purely institutional definition captures how canons are formed, but critics argue it leaves out the qualities of texts that make readers return to them.

  8. Why is Northanger Abbey often cited in discussions of genre? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It raises Gothic expectations and then deflates them, making genre itself the novel's subject. Austen parodies the Gothic, showing how much readers' responses depend on genre expectations.

  9. Which statement best represents Barthes's argument in "The Death of the Author"? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Meaning is produced in reading, so the author's intention cannot be the final authority. Barthes shifted authority from the writer to the reader and to language itself, rejecting the author as the guarantee of a single meaning.

Key takeaways

  • No single test defines literature. Imaginative content, self-conscious language and cultural value each capture part of it, and the study of literature asks how and why particular texts reward close and repeated reading.
  • Genre works by expectation: writers create meaning both by fulfilling conventions and by breaking them, so ask what the reader expects at each point and what the writer does with that expectation.
  • Always write "the speaker" or "the narrator", not the author's name, when describing what a text's voice says or feels; reserve the author's name for choices about how the text is made.
  • Context should explain something in the text. Use it to show why a word, character or choice had a particular force, not as a free-standing history lesson.
  • The canon is not a natural list of the best books: it is made and remade by readers, critics, publishers and institutions, and arguments about it are arguments about value and representation.
  • Every observation should answer three questions: what is the technique, what is its effect, and why does it matter to the passage's meaning?

Common mistakes in Foundations of Literary Study

  • Thinking literature means only novels. Poetry, drama, essays, letters, diaries, sermons and oral forms such as ballads are all studied as literature.
  • Listing genre features without saying why they matter. Name the convention, then explain its effect or how the writer adapts it.
  • Writing "Wordsworth feels lonely" about "I wandered lonely as a cloud". Write "the speaker describes feeling lonely", then discuss how Wordsworth presents it.
  • Bolting on context ("In Victorian times there was lots of poverty") in a separate paragraph. Integrate it with a quotation and an interpretive point.
  • Treating "canonical" as meaning "objectively best". It means valued and taught by particular institutions, for reasons that can be examined.
  • "Feature-spotting": naming techniques ("there is alliteration") without explaining their effect or linking them to meaning.

Lessons in this school

  1. What is literature?
    Literature is hard to define, but every definition tries to say what makes some writing worth reading closely and repeatedly.
  2. Genre and form
    Genre is the kind of text something is; form is the shape it takes.
  3. Author, narrator and reader
    Every text involves someone who wrote it, a voice that speaks it and someone who reads it.
  4. Text and context
    Context is the set of circumstances in which a text was written and has been read.
  5. The literary canon
    The canon is the set of works treated as central to a literature, taught in schools and universities and set in exams.
  6. Close reading: a method
    Close reading is the careful, detailed analysis of a short passage to show how its language and form produce meaning.
  7. Diction and register
    Diction is a writer's choice of words; register is the level of formality and the kind of language those choices create.
  8. Imagery
    Imagery is language that appeals to the senses.
  9. Metaphor, simile and personification
    Figures of speech say one thing in terms of another.
  10. Symbol and motif
    A symbol is a concrete thing that stands for something beyond itself; a motif is an element that recurs through a text.
  11. Tone and mood
    Tone is the attitude a text's voice takes towards its subject and reader; mood is the atmosphere and feeling the text creates.
  12. Syntax and sentence structure
    Syntax is the arrangement of words into sentences.
  13. The sound of language
    Writers use the sounds of words as well as their meanings.
  14. Ambiguity and multiple meanings
    Literary language often means more than one thing at once.
  15. Irony
    Irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what happens.
  16. Intertextuality and allusion
    No text is written in isolation: works echo, quote, rewrite and argue with earlier ones.
  17. Using quotations as evidence
    Quotations are the evidence of a literature essay.
  18. From annotation to argument
    Annotation is where analysis begins, but an essay needs an argument.

Foundations of Literary Study: questions students ask

What is a literary convention?

An established feature of a genre that readers have learned to expect, such as the ghost in revenge tragedy.

What is the implied author?

The sense of the author's values and design that a reader builds up from the work as a whole, distinct from the narrator and from the real person.

What is the context of reception?

How a text has been read and valued by different audiences at different times, including today.

What did Gilbert and Gubar's The Madwoman in the Attic contribute to canon debates?

It analysed nineteenth-century women writers and showed how they had worked within, and against, a male-dominated tradition, helping widen the canon.
