Five sample flashcards

Answer each from memory, then open it to check. Still foggy? Each card has the plain-English version.

What is the subtitle of Blake's combined Songs?

"Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul". From “Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience” →

In plain English: William Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794) pairs poems showing "the two contrary states of the human soul". Key idea: Blake's Songs pair innocent and experienced visions to show contrary states of the soul; "The Tyger" questions a creator of both lamb and tiger, and "London" exposes the suffering caused by commerce, church, state and the "mind-forg'd manacles" of accepted thinking.

What does the inscription on Ozymandias's pedestal say?

"Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!" From “Shelley: Ozymandias and the political poems” →

In plain English: Percy Bysshe Shelley was a radical poet who attacked tyranny and believed in the power of poetry to change the world. Key idea: "Ozymandias" uses a ruined statue and ironic inscription to show the impermanence of tyrannical power; Shelley's poems of 1819 respond to Peterloo with calls for nonviolent mass resistance, reflecting his belief in poetry's political force.

To whom is the Duke speaking?

An envoy negotiating his marriage to a count's daughter. From “Browning: My Last Duchess” →

In plain English: Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess" is the most famous example of the dramatic monologue. Key idea: In this dramatic monologue the Duke unintentionally reveals his jealousy, pride and probable murder of his wife; enjambed couplets and a silent listener create a controlled voice and dramatic irony.

Which line from Binyon's "For the Fallen" is read at remembrance services?

"They shall grow not old, as we that are left grow old". From “First World War poetry: an overview” →

In plain English: The First World War produced a body of poetry that shaped how later generations remember the war. Key idea: First World War poetry moved from early idealism (Brooke, Binyon) to the realism and protest of soldier poets (Owen, Sassoon); the canon centred on protest poets has been widened to include women and other voices.

What animal crosses no man's land in "Break of Day in the Trenches"?

A rat. From “Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas” →

In plain English: Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas both died in 1917 to 1918 and wrote war poetry very different from Owen's and Sassoon's. Key idea: Rosenberg's "Break of Day in the Trenches" uses a rat and a poppy to explore the absurdity and fragility of war with ironic understatement; Thomas's poems such as "Adlestrop" approach war indirectly through landscape, memory and stillness.

Quick quiz: test yourself on Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry

9 questions from the course, from foundation recall to stretch. Pick an answer, then say whether you knew it or guessed.

  1. When were Songs of Innocence and of Experience first published together? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    1794. Innocence appeared in 1789, and the combined collection in 1794.

  2. Who wrote Lyrical Ballads with Wordsworth? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Wordsworth and Coleridge published Lyrical Ballads together in 1798.

  3. What crime does the Mariner commit? (Foundation recall)
    Show the answer

    He shoots the albatross. Killing the albatross triggers the curse.

  4. What does the repetition of "charter'd" in "London" suggest? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    The city and even the river are owned and controlled. Charters granted ownership and rights, implying control by commerce.

  5. What does the daffodils poem suggest about memory? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    Memory can recreate past joy in solitude. The "inward eye" recalls the daffodils and renews pleasure.

  6. Why is the Mariner's blessing of the water-snakes significant? (Exam style)
    Show the answer

    It marks the beginning of his redemption. When he blesses them, he can pray and the albatross falls.

  7. Why are Blake's Songs written in simple, childlike forms? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    The simplicity heightens the shock of their darker social and spiritual meanings. The contrast between form and content sharpens the critique.

  8. In the boat-stealing episode, what does the "huge peak" teach the boy? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    Awe and a sense of moral fear before nature. Nature disciplines and shapes his moral imagination.

  9. Why might the frame narrative with the Wedding-Guest matter? (Stretch)
    Show the answer

    It shows the tale's lasting power to change its hearer. The guest leaves "a sadder and a wiser man", affected by the tale.

Key takeaways

  • Blake's Songs pair innocent and experienced visions to show contrary states of the soul; "The Tyger" questions a creator of both lamb and tiger, and "London" exposes the suffering caused by commerce, church, state and the "mind-forg'd manacles" of accepted thinking.
  • Wordsworth sought ordinary language and subjects, defined poetry as powerful feeling recollected in tranquillity, and presented nature and memory as sources of spiritual renewal and moral growth.
  • The Rime is a supernatural ballad with a frame narrative in which the Mariner's killing of the albatross brings punishment, and his unconscious blessing of the water-snakes brings redemption, leading to the moral of loving "All things both great and small".
  • "Ozymandias" uses a ruined statue and ironic inscription to show the impermanence of tyrannical power; Shelley's poems of 1819 respond to Peterloo with calls for nonviolent mass resistance, reflecting his belief in poetry's political force.
  • Keats's odes explore the longing to escape suffering through art and imagination, the coldness of permanence in art, and finally, in "To Autumn", acceptance of change and mortality, embodying his negative capability.
  • "The Charge of the Light Brigade" uses galloping rhythm and repetition to celebrate obedient courage despite a blunder; In Memoriam uses its enclosed ABBA stanza to trace grief for Hallam through doubt, shaped by scientific ideas, towards faith.

Common mistakes in Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry

  • Reading Innocence as simply good and Experience as simply bad. Blake presents both as limited perspectives, and Innocence can be naive or exploited.
  • Reading Wordsworth's nature poems as simple description. They are about the mind's relationship with nature over time.
  • Reducing the poem to its final moral. The poem's imagery, isolation and lasting guilt suggest more complex meanings.
  • Treating "Ozymandias" only as a poem about time. It is also a pointed attack on tyranny and political pride.
  • Treating "Beauty is truth, truth beauty" as Keats's simple final answer. The line is spoken by the urn and its meaning is debated.
  • Reading "The Charge of the Light Brigade" as an anti-war poem in the modern sense. It acknowledges a blunder but celebrates heroism.

Lessons in this school

  1. Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience
    William Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794) pairs poems showing "the two contrary states of the human soul".
  2. Wordsworth: nature and memory
    William Wordsworth made nature and memory central to English poetry.
  3. Coleridge: The Rime of the Ancient Mariner
    Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner is a supernatural ballad of crime, punishment and redemption.
  4. Shelley: Ozymandias and the political poems
    Percy Bysshe Shelley was a radical poet who attacked tyranny and believed in the power of poetry to change the world.
  5. Keats: the odes
    In 1819, John Keats wrote a series of odes that explore beauty, art, mortality and the desire to escape suffering.
  6. Tennyson: The Charge of the Light Brigade and In Memoriam
    Alfred, Lord Tennyson was Poet Laureate and the most popular poet of the Victorian age.
  7. Browning: My Last Duchess
    Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess" is the most famous example of the dramatic monologue.
  8. Christina Rossetti
    Christina Rossetti was one of the most important Victorian poets, combining religious devotion, intense feeling and technical mastery.
  9. Thomas Hardy's poetry
    Thomas Hardy turned from novels to poetry and wrote about loss, time, chance and war in a distinctive voice.
  10. First World War poetry: an overview
    The First World War produced a body of poetry that shaped how later generations remember the war.
  11. Wilfred Owen
    Wilfred Owen wrote some of the most powerful poems of the First World War, focused on "the pity of War".
  12. Siegfried Sassoon
    Siegfried Sassoon was a decorated officer who became one of the war's fiercest critics.
  13. Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas
    Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas both died in 1917 to 1918 and wrote war poetry very different from Owen's and Sassoon's.
  14. Women's poetry of the First World War
    Women wrote a large body of poetry about the First World War, often from the home front, hospitals and ambulance units.
  15. War poetry after 1918
    Poets have continued to write about war, from the Second World War to modern conflicts, often in dialogue with the First World War poets.

Set Texts: Romantic, Victorian and War Poetry: questions students ask

How did Wordsworth define poetry in the Preface?

"The spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings", taking its origin "from emotion recollected in tranquillity".

What moral does the Mariner give at the end?

"He prayeth best, who loveth best / All things both great and small."

What is negative capability?

Being "capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact & reason".

Whom does In Memoriam mourn?

Arthur Henry Hallam, Tennyson's close friend, who died in 1833.
