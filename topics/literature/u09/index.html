Lessons in this school

  1. Blake: Songs of Innocence and of Experience
    William Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794) pairs poems showing "the two contrary states of the human soul".
  2. Wordsworth: nature and memory
    William Wordsworth made nature and memory central to English poetry.
  3. Coleridge: The Rime of the Ancient Mariner
    Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner is a supernatural ballad of crime, punishment and redemption.
  4. Shelley: Ozymandias and the political poems
    Percy Bysshe Shelley was a radical poet who attacked tyranny and believed in the power of poetry to change the world.
  5. Keats: the odes
    In 1819, John Keats wrote a series of odes that explore beauty, art, mortality and the desire to escape suffering.
  6. Tennyson: The Charge of the Light Brigade and In Memoriam
    Alfred, Lord Tennyson was Poet Laureate and the most popular poet of the Victorian age.
  7. Browning: My Last Duchess
    Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess" is the most famous example of the dramatic monologue.
  8. Christina Rossetti
    Christina Rossetti was one of the most important Victorian poets, combining religious devotion, intense feeling and technical mastery.
  9. Thomas Hardy's poetry
    Thomas Hardy turned from novels to poetry and wrote about loss, time, chance and war in a distinctive voice.
  10. First World War poetry: an overview
    The First World War produced a body of poetry that shaped how later generations remember the war.
  11. Wilfred Owen
    Wilfred Owen wrote some of the most powerful poems of the First World War, focused on "the pity of War".
  12. Siegfried Sassoon
    Siegfried Sassoon was a decorated officer who became one of the war's fiercest critics.
  13. Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas
    Isaac Rosenberg and Edward Thomas both died in 1917 to 1918 and wrote war poetry very different from Owen's and Sassoon's.
  14. Women's poetry of the First World War
    Women wrote a large body of poetry about the First World War, often from the home front, hospitals and ambulance units.
  15. War poetry after 1918
    Poets have continued to write about war, from the Second World War to modern conflicts, often in dialogue with the First World War poets.

Key terms

Contrary states
Blake's idea that innocence and experience are opposed but complementary views of life
Romanticism
A late eighteenth and early nineteenth-century movement valuing feeling, imagination, nature and the individual
Frame narrative
A story set within another story, as the Mariner's tale within the wedding
Irony
A contrast between what is said or expected and what is meant or happens
Ode
A lyric poem of address, often celebrating its subject
Dactyl
A metrical foot of one stressed and two unstressed syllables
Dramatic monologue
A poem spoken by a character to a silent listener that reveals the speaker's personality
Petrarchan sonnet
A sonnet divided into an octave and a sestet, with a turn between them
Elegy
A poem of mourning for the dead
Western Front
The line of trenches across Belgium and France where British forces fought Germany
Pararhyme
Half-rhyme in which consonants match but vowels differ
Satire
Using humour, irony or exaggeration to criticise people or institutions
Free verse
Poetry without a regular metre or rhyme scheme
Home front
The civilian population and activities of a country at war
