Free GCSE Core course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

YouthGradify's free GCSE Core course runs from Revision and Exam Skills to Foundations of Health and Wellbeing: 149 lessons in 9 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for teenagers, 13 to 18.

Study material, not a qualification or an exam board resource. YouthGradify helps 13 to 16 year olds in England learn and revise for GCSEs. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by teachers or examiners, so they may contain errors: check important points with your teacher, your class notes and your exam board's current specification. It is not endorsed by AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR or Ofqual, and its model answers are illustrations, not mark schemes. The life skills school is general education, not financial, medical or legal advice: talk to a trusted adult, your school, your GP or a service such as Childline (0800 1111) if you need help.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Revision and Exam Skills

12 lessons

Revision and Exam Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. How memory works: Revision is really about memory: getting information into long-term memory and being able to get it back out under exam conditions.
  2. Retrieval practice: Retrieval practice means bringing information back to mind from memory, usually by testing yourself.
  3. Spaced practice and revision timetables: Spaced practice means spreading study of a topic over several sessions with gaps in between, instead of cramming it all at once.
  4. Interleaving and worked examples: Interleaving means mixing different types of problem or topic in one practice session, and worked examples show every step of a solution.
  5. Flashcards and the Leitner system: Flashcards are a simple way to combine retrieval practice with spacing.
  6. Mind maps, dual coding and summaries: Mind maps, diagrams and summaries can help you organise a topic, but only if you make them in a way that makes you think.
  7. Past papers and mark schemes: Past papers are the closest thing to the real exam, and mark schemes show exactly what examiners reward.
  8. Command words: Command words are the instruction words at the start of an exam question, such as "describe", "explain" and "evaluate".
  9. Planning extended answers: Six-mark science questions, nine-mark geography questions and long English answers reward organised, linked writing.
  10. Timing and exam technique: Knowing the content is only half of it: in the exam you also need to read questions carefully, manage time and avoid careless errors.
  11. Exam stress and wellbeing: Most students feel nervous before exams, and a little stress can sharpen focus.
  12. Making a revision plan: This lesson brings the school together: you audit what you need to know, choose methods that work, build a spaced and interleaved timetable, and check and adjust it as exams approach.

School 2: Foundations of Literary Study

18 lessons

Foundations of Literary Study: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is literature?: Literature is hard to define, but every definition tries to say what makes some writing worth reading closely and repeatedly.
  2. Genre and form: Genre is the kind of text something is; form is the shape it takes.
  3. Author, narrator and reader: Every text involves someone who wrote it, a voice that speaks it and someone who reads it.
  4. Text and context: Context is the set of circumstances in which a text was written and has been read.
  5. The literary canon: The canon is the set of works treated as central to a literature, taught in schools and universities and set in exams.
  6. Close reading: a method: Close reading is the careful, detailed analysis of a short passage to show how its language and form produce meaning.
  7. Diction and register: Diction is a writer's choice of words; register is the level of formality and the kind of language those choices create.
  8. Imagery: Imagery is language that appeals to the senses.
  9. Metaphor, simile and personification: Figures of speech say one thing in terms of another.
  10. Symbol and motif: A symbol is a concrete thing that stands for something beyond itself; a motif is an element that recurs through a text.
  11. Tone and mood: Tone is the attitude a text's voice takes towards its subject and reader; mood is the atmosphere and feeling the text creates.
  12. Syntax and sentence structure: Syntax is the arrangement of words into sentences.
  13. The sound of language: Writers use the sounds of words as well as their meanings.
  14. Ambiguity and multiple meanings: Literary language often means more than one thing at once.
  15. Irony: Irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what happens.
  16. Intertextuality and allusion: No text is written in isolation: works echo, quote, rewrite and argue with earlier ones.
  17. Using quotations as evidence: Quotations are the evidence of a literature essay.
  18. From annotation to argument: Annotation is where analysis begins, but an essay needs an argument.

School 3: Drama and Shakespeare

18 lessons

Drama and Shakespeare: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Drama as performance: A play is written to be performed, so the text is only part of the work.
  2. Tragedy: Tragedy shows the fall of a significant figure through a combination of character and circumstance.
  3. Comedy: Comedy aims to amuse and usually ends happily, often in marriage or reconciliation.
  4. Dramatic structure: Plays are shaped by structure: how action is divided and how tension builds and resolves.
  5. Character and dialogue in drama: In drama, character is revealed mainly through dialogue and action.
  6. Stagecraft and stage directions: Stagecraft is everything that turns a script into a performance: set, lighting, sound, costume, props and movement.
  7. Shakespeare's theatre: Shakespeare wrote for particular theatres, companies and audiences.
  8. Shakespeare's language: Shakespeare moves between blank verse, rhyme and prose for dramatic effect.
  9. Soliloquy and aside: Soliloquies and asides let characters share their thoughts with the audience.
  10. Shakespearean tragedy: Shakespeare's great tragedies, including Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth, centre on a hero whose choices and flaws lead to catastrophe.
  11. Shakespearean comedy: Shakespeare's comedies blend romance, wit, disguise and festivity.
  12. The history plays and the late romances: Beyond tragedy and comedy, Shakespeare wrote English history plays and, late in his career, romances.
  13. Marlowe and Shakespeare's contemporaries: Shakespeare worked alongside other major playwrights.
  14. Restoration and eighteenth-century drama: After the theatres reopened in 1660, English drama changed: women appeared on stage, indoor theatres dominated, and the witty comedy of manners flourished.
  15. Wilde, Shaw and the Victorian stage: The late Victorian and Edwardian stage saw a revival of serious and witty drama.
  16. Ibsen, Chekhov and modern realism: In the late nineteenth century, Ibsen and Chekhov transformed drama with realism: ordinary settings, everyday speech and serious social and psychological themes.
  17. Modernist and absurdist drama: Twentieth-century dramatists broke with realism.
  18. Post-war and contemporary British drama: British drama since 1945 has included the "angry young men", Pinter's comedy of menace, political and feminist theatre, and the in-yer-face drama of the 1990s.

School 4: The Human Body

17 lessons

The Human Body: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cells, tissues and organs: The body is built in levels: cells form tissues, tissues form organs and organs work together in systems.
  2. Homeostasis: Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite changes outside and inside the body.
  3. Body temperature regulation: The body keeps its core temperature close to 37 °C by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  4. Blood glucose regulation: Blood glucose must stay within narrow limits because the brain depends on it and high levels damage blood vessels.
  5. The nervous system: The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates fast responses.
  6. The endocrine system: The endocrine system controls the body with hormones carried in the blood.
  7. The heart: The heart is a muscular pump that drives blood through two circulations.
  8. Blood vessels and blood pressure: Arteries, capillaries and veins carry blood around the body, and blood pressure keeps it moving.
  9. Blood: Blood transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and wastes, defends against infection and seals wounds.
  10. The respiratory system: The respiratory system brings air into the lungs so oxygen can enter the blood and carbon dioxide can leave.
  11. Gas exchange and breathing: Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; gas exchange moves oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out.
  12. The digestive system: The digestive system breaks food down into molecules small enough to be absorbed into the blood.
  13. The liver: The liver is the body's chemical processing centre, with hundreds of functions.
  14. The kidneys and fluid balance: The kidneys filter the blood, remove wastes and control the body's water and salt balance.
  15. The immune system: The immune system defends the body against pathogens.
  16. The musculoskeletal system: Bones, joints and muscles support the body, protect organs and make movement possible.
  17. The skin: The skin is the body's largest organ, forming a barrier between the body and the outside world.

School 5: Chemistry

19 lessons

Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Atomic structure and isotopes: All matter is made of atoms, which contain protons, neutrons and electrons.
  2. The development of the atomic model: Scientists' ideas about atoms changed as new evidence appeared.
  3. The periodic table and groups: The periodic table arranges the elements so that patterns in their properties can be seen.
  4. Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding: Atoms join together in three main ways.
  5. Structure and properties of substances: The way particles are bonded determines a substance's properties.
  6. Conservation of mass and relative formula mass: In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged but not created or destroyed.
  7. Moles and concentration: Chemists count particles using the mole.
  8. Reactivity series and extraction of metals: Metals differ in how readily they react.
  9. Acids, alkalis and salts: Acids and alkalis are among the most common chemicals in the laboratory and everyday life.
  10. Electrolysis: Electrolysis uses electricity to break down ionic compounds.
  11. Exothermic and endothermic reactions: Chemical reactions transfer energy to or from their surroundings.
  12. Rates of reaction: Some reactions are very fast and others very slow.
  13. Reversible reactions and equilibrium: Some reactions can go both forwards and backwards.
  14. Crude oil and hydrocarbons: Crude oil is the source of most fuels and many chemicals.
  15. Chemical analysis and chromatography: Chemists need to know what substances are present and whether they are pure.
  16. Tests for gases: Simple tests identify common gases.
  17. The Earth's atmosphere and climate change: The Earth's atmosphere has changed dramatically over billions of years, and human activity is changing it again.
  18. Using resources: water and life cycle assessment: Humans use the Earth's resources for food, warmth, shelter and products.
  19. Chemistry required practicals: GCSE Chemistry includes required practical activities that develop practical skills and can be assessed in exams.

School 6: Physics

21 lessons

Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Energy stores and transfers: Energy is never used up; it is stored in different ways and transferred between stores.
  2. Work, power and efficiency: Work done, power and efficiency let us measure how much energy is transferred, how fast, and how usefully.
  3. Energy resources: Our energy comes from renewable and non-renewable resources used for transport, electricity generation and heating.
  4. Circuits, current and potential difference: Electric circuits let charge flow to transfer energy.
  5. Resistance and I–V characteristics: Resistance tells us how hard it is for current to flow through a component.
  6. Series and parallel circuits: Components can be joined in series or in parallel, and each arrangement follows its own rules.
  7. Mains electricity and the National Grid: Homes in the UK are supplied with alternating current through a three-core cable and the National Grid.
  8. Density and states of matter: The particle model explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases and the idea of density.
  9. Specific heat capacity and latent heat: Heating a substance either raises its temperature or changes its state.
  10. Atomic structure and radioactive decay: Atoms have a tiny dense nucleus surrounded by electrons, and some nuclei are unstable and decay.
  11. Half-life and the uses and dangers of radiation: Radioactive sources decay at a rate described by their half-life.
  12. Scalars, vectors and resultant forces: Physical quantities are either scalars or vectors, and forces are vectors that combine into a resultant.
  13. Speed, velocity and acceleration: Motion can be described with distance, speed, velocity and acceleration, and shown on graphs.
  14. Newton's laws of motion: Newton's three laws explain how forces change the motion of objects.
  15. Stopping distances and momentum: A vehicle's stopping distance depends on the driver and on the vehicle and road.
  16. Forces, springs and Hooke's law: Forces can stretch, squash or bend objects, and springs follow a simple rule up to a limit.
  17. Properties of waves: Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter.
  18. The electromagnetic spectrum: Electromagnetic waves form a continuous spectrum, all travelling at the same speed in a vacuum.
  19. Magnetism and electromagnetism: Magnets and electric currents both produce magnetic fields, and a current in a field can feel a force.
  20. Physics equations: recall and use: AQA GCSE Physics expects you to recall some equations and use others from the equation sheet.
  21. Physics required practicals: AQA GCSE Physics includes ten required practicals that can be examined in written papers.

School 7: Foundations of Ecology

16 lessons

Foundations of Ecology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is ecology?: Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment, and of what determines where organisms live and how many there are.
  2. Levels of ecological organisation: Ecology works at a ladder of levels, from the individual organism to the whole biosphere.
  3. Ecology as a science: hypotheses and scale: Ecologists test ideas by observation, experiment and models, in systems that are large, variable and slow.
  4. Ecology and environmental science: Environmental science combines ecology with geology, chemistry, physics and the social sciences to understand and manage human impacts on Earth's systems.
  5. Abiotic factors: Abiotic factors are the physical and chemical conditions that determine where organisms can live.
  6. Climate and the distribution of life: Climate, the long-term pattern of temperature and rainfall, is the main control on where major types of vegetation occur.
  7. Soils: Soil is a living mixture of weathered rock, organic matter, water, air and organisms, and it controls which plants can grow.
  8. Water and aquatic environments: Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface and has unusual properties that shape life in it.
  9. Tolerance ranges and limiting factors: Every species can survive, grow and reproduce only within a range of each environmental factor.
  10. Adaptation to the environment: Adaptations are inherited features that increase an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  11. Habitats and the ecological niche: A habitat is where an organism lives; its niche is its role there: the conditions it tolerates, the resources it uses and how it interacts with other species.
  12. Biotic interactions: an overview: Organisms affect each other in a small number of basic ways, classified by whether each partner gains, loses or is unaffected.
  13. Terrestrial biomes: Biomes are the major types of terrestrial ecosystem, each defined by its climate and dominant vegetation.
  14. Aquatic biomes: Aquatic biomes are defined by salinity, depth, light and water movement rather than by climate.
  15. Biogeography and the distribution of species: Biogeography explains why species are found where they are, at scales from continents to islands.
  16. Humans as an ecological force: People now alter ecosystems on a planetary scale, through land use, harvesting, pollution, species introductions and climate change.

School 8: Geography: Human Environments and Skills

14 lessons

Geography: Human Environments and Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Global urbanisation: More than half the world's people now live in towns and cities, and the fastest growth is in lower-income and newly emerging countries.
  2. Cities in lower-income countries: Rapid growth brings both opportunities and challenges to cities in LICs and NEEs.
  3. UK cities: change and regeneration: UK cities have changed as industry declined, populations moved and new jobs appeared.
  4. Sustainable urban living: A sustainable city meets the needs of people today without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  5. Measuring development: Countries differ greatly in wealth and quality of life.
  6. Reducing the development gap: The development gap is the difference in standards of living between the world's richest and poorest countries.
  7. Economic change in an emerging economy: Some LICs and NEEs are experiencing rapid economic development that changes their economy, environment and people's lives.
  8. The changing UK economy: The UK has moved from an economy based on manufacturing to a post-industrial economy based on services and knowledge.
  9. Food, water and energy: global patterns: Food, water and energy are vital for human development, but they are unevenly distributed around the world.
  10. Resource management strategies: Countries use large-scale schemes and small, local projects to increase supply and use resources more sustainably.
  11. Map skills and grid references: Ordnance Survey maps and atlas maps are essential tools in geography.
  12. Graphs, statistics and data presentation: Geographers present and analyse data to reveal patterns and trends.
  13. Fieldwork enquiry: AQA GCSE Geography students must carry out two fieldwork enquiries in contrasting environments, one physical and one human.
  14. Issue evaluation and decision making: Paper 3 of AQA GCSE Geography includes an issue evaluation based on a pre-release resource booklet.

School 9: Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

14 lessons

Foundations of Health and Wellbeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is health?: Health can be defined negatively, as the absence of disease, or positively, as complete wellbeing or the ability to adapt and cope.
  2. Models of health: biomedical and biopsychosocial: A model of health is a way of explaining what causes ill health and what should be done about it.
  3. Wellbeing and how it is measured: Wellbeing is how people feel and function, and how they judge their lives.
  4. Holistic health: physical, mental and social: A holistic approach treats a person as a whole, recognising that physical, intellectual, emotional and social aspects of health affect one another.
  5. The determinants of health: The determinants of health are the factors that make people healthy or unhealthy, from genes to government policy.
  6. Health inequalities: Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable and unfair differences in health between groups.
  7. Lifestyle factors and health: Smoking, diet, physical activity and alcohol are the four lifestyle factors with the largest effects on health in the UK.
  8. The wider determinants: housing, work and environment: The wider determinants are the social, economic and environmental conditions in which people live.
  9. Judging health information: Health information is everywhere: in news, adverts, social media and conversation.
  10. Levels of evidence: Not all evidence is equally trustworthy.
  11. Understanding risk: absolute and relative: Risks are often reported in ways that make them sound bigger or smaller than they are.
  12. Screening: benefits and harms: Screening tests apparently healthy people to find disease, or the risk of it, early.
  13. Measuring the nation's health: Governments and researchers describe a population's health with measures of death, illness and wellbeing.
  14. Leading causes of death and ill health in the UK: Knowing what kills people and what makes them ill shapes public health priorities.
