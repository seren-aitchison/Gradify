Free Art and Design course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

ArtGradify's free Art and Design course runs from The Visual Language of Art and Design to Art in Society and Professional Practice: 183 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for artists and design students.

Study material, not a qualification or an assessment. ArtGradify teaches art, design and art history for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against your exam board's specification, your teacher and museum sources. Works of art are described in words rather than reproduced: look at them in person or on the holding museum's website. Nothing here is a mark or a prediction of a grade, and the copyright lessons explain general principles, not legal advice: for a real question about your rights, ask a qualified adviser.

Schools and lessons

School 1: The Visual Language of Art and Design

18 lessons

The Visual Language of Art and Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is art and design?: Art and design are the making of things that are looked at, used and thought about.
  2. How art and design are assessed: the four assessment objectives: GCSE and A level Art and Design are marked against the same four assessment objectives: develop, refine, record and present.
  3. Looking closely: an approach to visual analysis: Visual analysis is the disciplined description and interpretation of what a work looks like.
  4. Line: Line is the most basic visual element: a mark with length and direction, or an edge the eye perceives.
  5. Shape and form: Shape is a flat, two-dimensional area; form is three-dimensional, with height, width and depth.
  6. Tone and value: Tone (or value) is the lightness or darkness of a colour or surface.
  7. Colour as a visual element: Colour is the visual element with the most immediate emotional impact.
  8. Texture: Texture is the surface quality of a work, how it feels or looks as if it would feel.
  9. Space: Space is the area around, between and within forms, real in sculpture and architecture and illusory in pictures.
  10. Pattern: Pattern is the regular repetition of a motif across a surface.
  11. Balance and symmetry: Balance is the distribution of visual weight in a composition.
  12. Contrast and emphasis: Emphasis makes one part of a work dominant, and contrast is the main way to achieve it.
  13. Rhythm, repetition and movement: Rhythm is created by repeating visual elements at intervals, and movement is the path the eye takes through a work or the illusion of motion within it.
  14. Proportion and scale: Proportion is the size relationship of parts to each other and to the whole; scale is size relative to a standard, usually the human body.
  15. Unity and variety: Unity is the sense that all parts of a work belong together; variety is the difference that keeps it interesting.
  16. Composition and the picture plane: Composition is the arrangement of elements within the format of a work.
  17. The rule of thirds and the golden ratio: The rule of thirds and the golden ratio are proportional systems often recommended for composition.
  18. Gestalt principles in art and design: Gestalt psychology studies how we perceive wholes rather than separate parts.

School 2: Drawing

17 lessons

Drawing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Drawing as thinking and recording: Drawing is the foundation of art and design practice: a way of seeing, thinking, planning and communicating, not only of making finished pictures.
  2. Drawing materials: graphite, charcoal, ink and pastel: Each drawing medium has its own range of marks, tones and handling.
  3. Mark-making: Mark-making is the vocabulary of lines, dots, strokes and textures an artist produces with a tool.
  4. Measuring and sighting: Sighting is the practice of measuring proportions and angles by eye with a pencil held at arm's length.
  5. Contour and blind contour drawing: Contour drawing follows the edges of a form slowly and attentively; blind contour drawing does so without looking at the paper.
  6. Negative space: Negative space is the space around and between objects.
  7. Tonal drawing and chiaroscuro: Tonal drawing builds form with masses of light and dark rather than lines.
  8. Hatching and cross-hatching: Hatching builds tone with parallel lines; cross-hatching layers sets of lines at angles.
  9. One-point perspective: Linear perspective is a geometrical system for showing depth on a flat surface, in which parallel lines receding from the viewer converge on vanishing points.
  10. Two- and three-point perspective: When a viewer sees an object corner-on, its receding edges converge on two vanishing points; looking steeply up or down adds a third.
  11. Atmospheric perspective: Atmospheric or aerial perspective shows distance through changes in colour, tone and detail caused by the air between viewer and object.
  12. Foreshortening: Foreshortening is the apparent shortening of a form that points towards or away from the viewer.
  13. Drawing the figure: proportion and gesture: Figure drawing combines gesture, which captures movement and weight, with proportion, which captures the relative sizes of the body's parts.
  14. Anatomy for artists: Artistic anatomy is the study of the skeleton and muscles that shape the surface of the body.
  15. Drawing the portrait: Portrait drawing depends on accurate proportions of the head and on capturing individual character.
  16. Expressive and experimental drawing: Expressive and experimental drawing moves beyond accurate depiction to explore materials, processes, scale, chance and the body.
  17. Drawing from imagination and memory: Drawing from imagination and memory builds images that are not in front of the artist.

School 3: Colour Theory

15 lessons

Colour Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Light and colour perception: Colour is not a property of objects alone but an experience produced by light, surfaces and the visual system together.
  2. Additive and subtractive colour: Colour can be mixed by adding lights or by subtracting wavelengths with pigments, dyes and inks.
  3. Hue, value and saturation: Any colour can be described by three properties: hue, value and saturation (chroma).
  4. The colour wheel: The colour wheel arranges hues in a circle to show their relationships.
  5. Munsell and other colour systems: Colour systems give every colour a precise address so it can be specified, matched and communicated.
  6. Pigments and colour mixing: Paint colours come from pigments with their own chemistry, history and handling.
  7. Colour harmonies: Colour harmonies are combinations of hues chosen by their positions on the colour wheel.
  8. Complementary colours and simultaneous contrast: Colours change appearance according to their neighbours.
  9. Itten's seven colour contrasts: Johannes Itten, who taught the preliminary course at the Bauhaus, classified seven kinds of colour contrast.
  10. Warm and cool colour: Colour temperature describes whether a colour feels warm (towards red-orange) or cool (towards blue-green).
  11. Colour and space: Colour can create, flatten or contradict pictorial space.
  12. Colour symbolism and culture: Colours carry meanings, but those meanings come from culture, history, materials and context rather than from colour itself.
  13. Colour in design and branding: Designers use colour to identify brands, organise information and guide action.
  14. Albers and the interaction of colour: Josef Albers taught that colour is the most relative medium in art.
  15. Colour and accessibility: About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour vision deficiency, and many more people read in poor light or on poor screens.

School 4: Painting Media and Techniques

17 lessons

Painting Media and Techniques: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What paint is: pigment and binder: Every paint is a pigment held in a binder, often thinned by a solvent or diluent.
  2. Egg tempera: Egg tempera, pigment bound with egg yolk, was the main panel painting medium in Europe from the Middle Ages to the late 15th century.
  3. Fresco: Fresco is painting into wet lime plaster so the colour becomes part of the wall.
  4. Oil paint: Oil paint, pigment bound in drying oil, became the dominant European easel medium from the 15th century.
  5. Watercolour and gouache: Watercolour is transparent paint bound with gum arabic, relying on the white of the paper for light; gouache is its opaque cousin.
  6. Acrylic paint: Acrylic paint, pigment in a polymer emulsion, was developed in the mid-20th century and is now the most common studio and school paint.
  7. Supports and grounds: A painting's support is what it is painted on; the ground is the prepared layer between support and paint.
  8. Brushes, knives and tools: Brush shape, fibre and size determine the marks a painter can make.
  9. Underpainting and glazing: Indirect painting builds a picture in stages: an underpainting establishes drawing and values, then transparent glazes and opaque layers add colour.
  10. Alla prima: Alla prima (Italian, "at the first attempt") means painting wet-into-wet in one session, without waiting for layers to dry.
  11. Impasto and texture: Impasto is paint applied thickly enough to stand up from the surface and keep the marks of brush or knife.
  12. Still life: Still life depicts arranged inanimate objects such as fruit, flowers, vessels and books.
  13. Landscape painting: Landscape painting depicts natural and built scenery.
  14. Portraiture: Portraiture represents particular people, but it has always been about more than likeness: status, identity, memory and power.
  15. Abstract painting: Abstract painting does not depict recognisable objects, or reduces them until they are no longer the subject.
  16. Health and safety in the studio: Art materials and tools can be hazardous if misused.
  17. Conservation and the life of a painting: Paintings change over time: varnish yellows, paint cracks, pigments fade and supports move.

School 5: Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D

16 lessons

Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What a print is: editions and proofs: A fine art print is an original work made by transferring ink from a matrix (block, plate, stone or screen) to paper, usually in a numbered edition.
  2. Relief printing: woodcut and linocut: In relief printing, the artist cuts away the areas that will print white and inks the raised surface.
  3. Intaglio: etching and engraving: Intaglio prints are made from lines and tones cut or bitten into a metal plate, inked into the grooves and printed under heavy pressure.
  4. Lithography: Lithography prints from a flat surface, using the fact that grease and water repel each other.
  5. Screen printing: Screen printing pushes ink through a fine mesh where a stencil leaves it open.
  6. Monoprinting: Monoprinting and monotype produce unique prints rather than editions.
  7. Carving: Carving is a subtractive process: the sculptor removes material from a block of stone, wood or other substance to reveal a form.
  8. Modelling: Modelling is an additive process in which a soft material such as clay or wax is built up and shaped.
  9. Casting: Casting reproduces a form by pouring liquid material into a mould.
  10. Construction and assemblage: Construction builds sculpture by joining parts, and assemblage combines found objects.
  11. Installation art: Installation art creates an environment or arrangement that the viewer enters or moves around, often for a specific space and time.
  12. Site-specific and land art: Site-specific art is made for and depends on a particular place; land art uses the landscape itself as material and setting.
  13. Ceramics: Ceramics are objects made from clay and hardened by firing.
  14. Textiles and fibre art: Textile art and design work with fibres, fabrics and threads through weaving, printing, dyeing, stitching and construction.
  15. Product and 3D design: Product design creates objects that people use, balancing function, form, materials, manufacture and sustainability.
  16. Architecture as an art: Architecture is the art and science of designing buildings and spaces.

School 6: Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art

14 lessons

Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The camera obscura and the invention of photography: Photography grew from the much older camera obscura and a series of nineteenth-century chemical experiments.
  2. The camera and the lens: Every camera, from a pinhole box to a smartphone, controls light falling onto a light-sensitive surface.
  3. Exposure: aperture, shutter speed and ISO: Exposure is the total amount of light that reaches the film or sensor.
  4. Composition in photography: Photographers compose by choosing what to include, where to stand and when to press the shutter.
  5. Documentary photography: Documentary photography records real people, places and events, often to inform or to argue for change.
  6. Photography as art: For many years photography had to argue for its place as an art form.
  7. The darkroom and analogue processes: Before digital cameras, photographs were developed and printed by hand in a darkroom, and many artists still use these methods.
  8. Editing and post-production: Most photographs are adjusted after they are taken, from simple crops to complex composites.
  9. Moving image and video art: Moving images began with experiments to capture motion and grew into cinema, television and video art.
  10. Raster and vector images: Digital images are stored either as grids of pixels or as mathematical shapes.
  11. Digital painting and illustration: Digital painting uses tablets and software to create images that can look like traditional media or something entirely new.
  12. 3D modelling and animation: 3D software lets artists build objects and worlds in virtual space, then light, animate and render them.
  13. Generative art and AI: Artists have long made work by following rules or letting systems produce results, and computers and artificial intelligence have extended this.
  14. Digital colour and resolution: Screens and printers make colour in different ways, and image size depends on pixels as well as inches.

School 7: Graphic and Communication Design

14 lessons

Graphic and Communication Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The anatomy of type: Letters have named parts and measurements that designers use to choose and set type precisely.
  2. Type classification: Typefaces are grouped into families by their history and visual features.
  3. Hierarchy and layout: Good layout tells readers what to look at first, second and third.
  4. Grids: Grids are invisible structures that help designers place elements consistently.
  5. Visual communication and semiotics: Semiotics is the study of signs and how they create meaning.
  6. Logos and visual identity: A logo is the most visible part of a wider visual identity that includes colour, type and imagery.
  7. Poster design: Posters must grab attention and communicate quickly, often from a distance.
  8. Illustration: Illustration uses images to explain, decorate or tell stories alongside text or for a client's purpose.
  9. Information design: Information design makes complex information clear through diagrams, charts, maps and signs.
  10. User interface design: User interface design shapes how people interact with apps, websites and devices.
  11. Packaging design: Packaging protects products, informs customers and helps sell goods on the shelf.
  12. Advertising and persuasion: Advertising uses images, words and psychology to persuade audiences.
  13. The design process: from brief to final: Designers move from a client's brief to a final outcome through a structured but flexible process.
  14. Sustainable design: Designers influence how much material, energy and waste a product or campaign creates.

School 8: Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance

15 lessons

Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Prehistoric art: The earliest surviving art includes cave paintings, carved figures and monuments made tens of thousands of years ago.
  2. Ancient Egyptian art: Ancient Egyptian art served religion, kingship and the afterlife, and kept a remarkably stable style for about three thousand years.
  3. Ancient Greek art: Greek artists developed a naturalistic, idealised image of the human body that shaped Western art for centuries.
  4. Roman art: Roman art borrowed heavily from Greece but developed its own realism, engineering and use of images for power.
  5. Art of ancient China: Chinese art has a continuous history of thousands of years, from ritual bronzes to ink landscape painting.
  6. Art of South Asia: The art of South Asia spans the Indus Valley, Buddhist, Hindu, Jain and Mughal traditions.
  7. Islamic art: Islamic art covers the art of lands where Islam was the main religion or culture, from Spain to India.
  8. Art of Africa: Africa has one of the longest and most varied art histories in the world.
  9. Art of the ancient Americas: Before European contact, civilisations across Mesoamerica and the Andes created monumental sculpture, cities, murals and textiles.
  10. Early Christian and Byzantine art: Early Christian artists adapted Roman forms to new beliefs, and the Byzantine Empire developed a rich art of mosaics and icons.
  11. Romanesque and Gothic: Medieval Europe produced two great architectural styles, Romanesque and Gothic, filled with sculpture, stained glass and manuscripts.
  12. The Early Renaissance in Florence: In fifteenth-century Florence, artists revived classical ideas and developed new ways to represent space and the body.
  13. The High Renaissance: Around 1490 to 1527, Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael and Venetian painters brought Renaissance ideals to their height.
  14. The Northern Renaissance: In Flanders, Germany and the Netherlands, artists developed detailed oil painting, printmaking and new subjects.
  15. Mannerism: After the High Renaissance, some artists deliberately broke its rules of balance and natural proportion.

School 9: Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism

19 lessons

Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The Baroque: Baroque art of the seventeenth century used drama, movement and strong light to move viewers emotionally.
  2. The Dutch Golden Age: In the seventeenth century the Dutch Republic produced vast numbers of paintings for a new middle-class market.
  3. Rococo: Rococo art of the eighteenth century was light, playful and decorative, made for aristocratic interiors.
  4. Neoclassicism: Neoclassicism revived the art of ancient Greece and Rome to express seriousness, virtue and reason.
  5. Romanticism: Romantic artists valued emotion, imagination and the power of nature over reason and order.
  6. Japanese woodblock prints: Ukiyo-e woodblock prints from Edo-period Japan showed actors, beauties and landscapes and later transformed Western art.
  7. Realism: In the mid-nineteenth century, Realist artists painted ordinary people and modern life truthfully, rejecting idealised history painting.
  8. The Pre-Raphaelites: The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood was a group of young British artists who rebelled against academic painting in 1848.
  9. Impressionism: The Impressionists painted modern life and the changing effects of light with quick, visible brushstrokes.
  10. Post-Impressionism: Post-Impressionism covers artists who built on Impressionism but pushed it towards structure, symbolism or expression.
  11. Arts and Crafts and Art Nouveau: In the late nineteenth century, designers reacted against poor-quality mass production and created new decorative styles.
  12. Fauvism and Expressionism: In the early twentieth century, artists used bold, unnatural colour and distorted form to express feeling.
  13. Cubism: Cubism, developed by Picasso and Braque from 1907, broke objects into planes and showed several viewpoints at once.
  14. Futurism and Constructivism: Futurism in Italy celebrated speed and machines, while Russian Constructivism aimed to build a new society through art and design.
  15. Dada: Dada was an anti-war, anti-art movement that began in Zurich in 1916 and spread to Berlin, Paris and New York.
  16. Surrealism: Surrealism sought to unlock the unconscious mind through dreams, chance and unexpected combinations.
  17. The Bauhaus: The Bauhaus was a German art and design school from 1919 to 1933 that shaped modern design worldwide.
  18. Abstract Expressionism: Abstract Expressionism emerged in New York in the 1940s and 1950s and made the city a new centre of the art world.
  19. Pop Art: Pop Art drew on advertising, comics, celebrities and consumer goods, blurring the line between high art and popular culture.

School 10: Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing

13 lessons

Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Minimalism: Minimalism stripped art down to simple geometric forms, industrial materials and the viewer's experience of space.
  2. Conceptual art: In conceptual art, the idea is more important than the finished object.
  3. Performance art: Performance art uses the artist's body and actions in time as the medium.
  4. Feminist art: From the late 1960s, feminist artists challenged how women were represented in art and excluded from the art world.
  5. Postmodernism: Postmodernism rejected the modernist belief in progress, originality and pure forms.
  6. The Young British Artists: In the 1990s, a group of young London artists became famous for shocking, entrepreneurial and media-savvy art.
  7. Street art: Street art moved from illegal graffiti to galleries and auction houses.
  8. Global contemporary art: Contemporary art is now made, shown and sold across the world.
  9. Formalism: Formalism analyses art through its visual form rather than its subject or context.
  10. Iconography and iconology: Iconography studies the subjects and symbols of art.
  11. The social history of art: The social history of art studies art in relation to the society that produced it.
  12. Feminist and postcolonial art history: From the 1970s, feminist and postcolonial art historians questioned whose art was studied and how others were represented.
  13. John Berger and ways of seeing: John Berger's 1972 BBC series and book Ways of Seeing changed how many people think about images.

School 11: The Psychology of Creativity

12 lessons

The Psychology of Creativity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is creativity?: Creativity researchers broadly agree that a creative idea must be both original and effective.
  2. The four Ps of creativity: Rhodes (1961) organised creativity research into four strands: the person, the process, the press (environment) and the product.
  3. Divergent thinking and its measurement: Divergent thinking tests ask people to produce many answers to open problems, such as unusual uses for a brick.
  4. Stages of the creative process: Graham Wallas (1926) proposed four stages of creative thought: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification.
  5. Insight and incubation: Insight is the sudden reorganisation of a problem that makes a solution obvious; incubation is the effect of stepping away.
  6. Knowledge, expertise and creativity: Creativity draws on deep knowledge, but expertise can also trap people in familiar solutions.
  7. Motivation and creativity: People tend to be most creative when they are interested in the work itself.
  8. Design fixation and how to break it: Designers often get stuck on the first ideas they see or have, copying features without realising it.
  9. Creative confidence: Many people believe they are "not creative" and stop trying.
  10. Constraints and creativity: A blank page can paralyse, while the right limits can spark invention.
  11. Creative climate in teams: Most design happens in teams, and the climate of a team strongly affects its creativity.
  12. Myths about creativity: Popular beliefs about creativity, such as right-brained creative people, lone geniuses and the mad artist, are widespread but often unsupported.

School 12: Art in Society and Professional Practice

13 lessons

Art in Society and Professional Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Museums and galleries: Museums and galleries collect, preserve and display art for the public.
  2. Curating and exhibitions: Curators select and arrange art to create meaning for audiences.
  3. The art market: Art is bought and sold in a global market worth tens of billions of pounds.
  4. Public art and patronage: Art is funded not only by buyers but by patrons, governments and sponsors.
  5. Copyright basics for artists: Copyright protects artists' work and controls how it is copied and used.
  6. Appropriation, influence and fair dealing: Artists have always borrowed from others, but where does influence end and infringement begin?
  7. Cultural property and restitution: Many museum collections include objects taken during colonialism or war.
  8. Forgery and authenticity: Forgeries have fooled experts and collectors for centuries.
  9. Representation and ethics: Artists make choices about whom they represent and how.
  10. Building a portfolio: A portfolio shows your skills, ideas and development as an artist.
  11. Applying to art school: There are several routes into art and design higher education.
  12. Careers in art and design: Art and design skills lead to many careers across the creative industries and beyond.
  13. Documenting and presenting your work: Good documentation lets your work be seen, assessed and remembered.
