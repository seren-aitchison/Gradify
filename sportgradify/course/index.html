Free Sport and Exercise Science course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

SportGradify's free Sport and Exercise Science course runs from Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System to Physical Activity: 205 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for athletes, coaches and sport students.

Education, not medical or personal training advice. SportGradify teaches sport and exercise science. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials. Nothing here assesses your own health or fitness, prescribes a training or diet plan, or replaces a qualified professional. If you have a health condition, are pregnant, or have symptoms such as chest pain, dizziness or breathlessness with exertion, check with your GP before starting new exercise; for an injury, see a physiotherapist or sports medicine doctor. In an emergency call 999.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System

19 lessons

Functional Anatomy and the Musculoskeletal System: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Anatomical language: planes, axes and positions: Anatomy has a precise shared language so that a movement can be described the same way by any coach, physiotherapist or scientist.
  2. Functions of the skeleton: The skeleton is far more than a frame.
  3. Bone structure and types of bone: Bones are living organs built from compact and spongy tissue, cartilage, marrow and a covering membrane.
  4. The axial and appendicular skeleton: The 206 bones of the adult skeleton divide into the axial skeleton, the central core that protects and supports, and the appendicular skeleton, the limbs and girdles that move.
  5. Bone remodelling and the effects of exercise on bone: Bone is constantly broken down and rebuilt, and it adapts to the loads placed on it.
  6. Classification of joints: A joint (articulation) is where two or more bones meet.
  7. Synovial joints: Synovial joints are the freely movable joints of sport.
  8. Movement terminology: Each joint action has a precise name.
  9. Joint analysis of sporting actions: Exam questions and real coaching both ask you to break a movement into joints, actions, planes and muscles.
  10. Ligaments, tendons and cartilage: Connective tissues hold the musculoskeletal system together and transmit its forces.
  11. Skeletal muscle structure: Skeletal muscle is organised in nested bundles, from the whole muscle down to the protein filaments that generate force.
  12. The sliding filament theory: Muscles shorten because actin and myosin filaments slide past each other, driven by repeated cycles of myosin cross-bridges.
  13. Muscle fibre types: Skeletal muscle contains fibres that differ in speed, force and fatigue resistance.
  14. Agonists, antagonists, fixators and synergists: Muscles rarely work alone.
  15. Types of muscle contraction: Muscles can produce force while shortening, lengthening or staying the same length.
  16. The major muscles used in sport: Analysing movement needs a working knowledge of the major muscles: where they are, what they do and when sport uses them.
  17. Motor units and the all-or-none law: Muscles are controlled by motor units, each a motor neurone and the fibres it supplies.
  18. Gradation of force: recruitment, summation and tetanus: A muscle can produce anything from a gentle touch to maximal force.
  19. Proprioceptors and the stretch reflex: Proprioceptors tell the nervous system the position, length and tension of muscles and joints.

School 2: The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise

18 lessons

The Cardiorespiratory System in Exercise: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Structure of the heart: The heart is two pumps side by side: the right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side to the body.
  2. The cardiac conduction system: Each heartbeat starts in the heart itself and spreads along a specialised conduction pathway.
  3. The cardiac cycle: The cardiac cycle is the sequence of filling and emptying in one heartbeat.
  4. Cardiac output, stroke volume and heart rate: Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps each minute, the product of stroke volume and heart rate.
  5. Regulation of heart rate during exercise: Heart rate is adjusted to demand by the cardiac control centre in the medulla, using information from receptors and acting through the autonomic nervous system and hormones.
  6. Heart rate responses to exercise: Heart rate tells a story through every session: an anticipatory rise, a rapid climb, a steady state or a continued rise, and recovery.
  7. Blood vessels and blood pressure: Arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules and veins each have a structure suited to their job.
  8. The vascular shunt and venous return: In exercise, blood is redistributed from organs to working muscles, and the return of blood to the heart is boosted.
  9. Blood composition and oxygen transport: Blood carries oxygen, carbon dioxide, fuel, heat and hormones.
  10. The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve: The oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve shows how saturated haemoglobin is at each partial pressure of oxygen.
  11. Structure of the respiratory system: The respiratory system moves air to a huge, thin gas-exchange surface.
  12. Mechanics of breathing: Breathing works by changing the volume of the chest cavity, so that pressure differences move air in and out.
  13. Lung volumes and capacities: Lung volumes describe how much air moves in each breath and how much stays in the lungs.
  14. Gas exchange at the lungs and muscles: Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion down partial pressure gradients, at the alveoli and at the muscles.
  15. Control of breathing during exercise: Breathing is adjusted automatically to match the body's needs.
  16. Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max): VO2max is the highest rate at which a person can take in, transport and use oxygen, the classic measure of aerobic capacity.
  17. Cardiovascular drift: In prolonged exercise at a steady workload, especially in the heat, heart rate creeps upwards while stroke volume falls.
  18. Cardiorespiratory adaptations to endurance training: Months of endurance training remodel the heart, blood, vessels, lungs and muscles.

School 3: Energy Systems and Exercise Metabolism

14 lessons

Energy Systems and Exercise Metabolism: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. ATP: the energy currency: Every muscle contraction is powered by adenosine triphosphate (ATP).
  2. The ATP–PC system: The ATP–PC (phosphocreatine) system resynthesises ATP fastest of all, powering maximal efforts of up to about 10 seconds.
  3. The anaerobic glycolytic system: When high-intensity exercise lasts longer than about 10 seconds, the anaerobic glycolytic system breaks down glucose without oxygen to resynthesise ATP quickly.
  4. The aerobic system: The aerobic system uses oxygen to break down carbohydrate and fat completely, giving a very large ATP yield for prolonged exercise.
  5. The energy continuum: All three energy systems work all the time; what changes is their relative contribution.
  6. Fuel use: carbohydrate and fat: Muscles burn a mixture of carbohydrate and fat, and the mixture shifts with intensity, duration, training and diet.
  7. Oxygen deficit: At the start of exercise, oxygen uptake lags behind demand.
  8. Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC): After exercise, oxygen uptake stays above resting levels while the body recovers.
  9. Lactate threshold and OBLA: As exercise intensity rises, blood lactate eventually climbs above resting levels and then accumulates rapidly.
  10. Restoring phosphocreatine and glycogen: Recovery means rebuilding fuel stores.
  11. Indirect calorimetry and the respiratory exchange ratio: Measuring oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output reveals both how much energy someone is using and which fuel they are burning.
  12. Energy expenditure, METs and efficiency: How much energy does an activity cost?
  13. Causes of fatigue: Fatigue is a fall in the ability to produce force or power.
  14. Metabolic adaptations to training: Training changes the metabolic machinery of muscle.

School 4: Exercise Physiology: Environment, Adaptation and Populations

15 lessons

Exercise Physiology: Environment, Adaptation and Populations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Thermoregulation during exercise: Exercising muscles produce large amounts of heat, which the body must lose to stay safe.
  2. Exercise in the heat and acclimatisation: Hot conditions reduce performance and increase the risk of heat illness.
  3. Exercise in the cold: Cold environments challenge the body to conserve heat.
  4. Altitude and hypoxia: At altitude, the air contains less oxygen pressure, challenging aerobic performance.
  5. Altitude training: Many endurance athletes train at altitude hoping to improve performance at sea level.
  6. Neuromuscular adaptations to strength training: Early strength gains happen before muscles grow much larger.
  7. Muscle hypertrophy: Over longer periods, resistance training increases muscle size.
  8. Overtraining and underperformance: Training too hard without enough recovery can reduce performance and harm health.
  9. Detraining: When training stops or is greatly reduced, fitness gains are gradually lost.
  10. Exercise and ageing: Ageing brings changes in muscle, bone, the heart and balance, but regular exercise slows many of them.
  11. Children and young athletes: Children are not small adults: they differ in physiology, growth and maturation.
  12. Female athletes and the menstrual cycle: Female athletes have been under-represented in sport science research.
  13. Relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S): Athletes who do not eat enough to fuel training can develop serious health and performance problems.
  14. Exercise in pregnancy: Physical activity during pregnancy benefits both parent and baby.
  15. Exercise for long-term conditions: Exercise is an important part of managing many long-term conditions.

School 5: Biomechanics

19 lessons

Biomechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Linear motion: distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration: Linear motion is movement in a straight or curved line where all parts of the body move the same distance in the same direction at the same time.
  2. Distance–time and velocity–time graphs: Graphs show how motion changes during a performance.
  3. Angular motion: Angular motion is movement around an axis, as when a gymnast somersaults or a limb rotates at a joint.
  4. Centre of mass and stability: The centre of mass is the point at which the mass of a body can be considered to be concentrated.
  5. Newton's laws of motion: Newton's three laws describe how forces change motion.
  6. Types of force in sport: Forces are pushes or pulls that can change the motion or shape of a body.
  7. Free-body diagrams: Free-body diagrams show all the external forces acting on a body at one instant.
  8. Impulse and momentum: Momentum is the quantity of motion a body has, and impulse is how a force changes it over time.
  9. Friction: Friction is the force that opposes two surfaces sliding over each other.
  10. Levers: The skeleton and muscles work together as lever systems to produce movement.
  11. Mechanical advantage of lever systems: Different lever classes favour either force or speed and range of movement.
  12. Projectile motion: A projectile is any body released into the air that is acted on only by weight and air resistance.
  13. Factors affecting projectile distance: The horizontal distance a projectile travels depends on its speed, angle and height of release, together with aerodynamic factors.
  14. Fluid mechanics: drag: Drag is the force that opposes a body moving through air or water.
  15. The Bernoulli principle and lift: Lift is a force that acts at right angles to the airflow and can keep projectiles in the air for longer or press vehicles onto the ground.
  16. Spin and the Magnus effect: Spinning balls curve in flight because of the Magnus effect.
  17. Moment of inertia and angular momentum: Moment of inertia is a body's resistance to changes in rotation, and angular momentum is the quantity of rotation it has.
  18. Conservation of angular momentum: Once a performer leaves the ground, their angular momentum stays the same unless an external torque acts.
  19. Biomechanical analysis and technology: Coaches and scientists use qualitative and quantitative methods to analyse movement and improve technique.

School 6: Training Principles and Programme Design

18 lessons

Training Principles and Programme Design: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Specificity: The principle of specificity states that training adaptations match the type of training performed.
  2. Progressive overload and FITT: To keep improving, the body must be worked harder than it is used to, and the workload must rise gradually.
  3. Reversibility: Fitness gains are lost when training stops or is greatly reduced.
  4. Individual differences, variation and recovery: People respond differently to the same training, and programmes need variety and rest to work well.
  5. The general adaptation syndrome and supercompensation: Training is a stress that the body responds to in a predictable sequence.
  6. Continuous training: Continuous training involves steady exercise without rest for at least 20 minutes.
  7. Fartlek training: Fartlek, Swedish for speed play, mixes changes of pace and terrain within one continuous session.
  8. Interval training and HIIT: Interval training alternates periods of work with periods of rest or low-intensity recovery.
  9. Resistance training: Resistance training uses weights, machines, bands or body weight to develop strength, power, muscular endurance and muscle size.
  10. Plyometrics: Plyometric training uses jumps, bounds and throws to develop power.
  11. Flexibility training: Flexibility is the range of movement possible at a joint.
  12. Circuit training: Circuit training uses a series of exercises at stations, performed one after another with short rests.
  13. Speed, agility and quickness: Speed, agility and quickness (SAQ) training develops fast movement, rapid changes of direction and quick reactions.
  14. Needs analysis: A needs analysis is the first step in designing a training programme.
  15. Monitoring training load: Monitoring training load helps coaches check that athletes are training enough to improve but not so much that they become injured or ill.
  16. Periodisation: Periodisation is the planned division of training into phases so that athletes peak for important competitions.
  17. Tapering: A taper is a planned reduction in training before a major competition to allow athletes to recover and perform at their best.
  18. Warm-up and cool-down: A warm-up prepares the body and mind for exercise, and a cool-down helps the transition back to rest.

School 7: Fitness Testing and Performance Analysis

15 lessons

Fitness Testing and Performance Analysis: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why test?: Fitness testing gives coaches and athletes objective information about current fitness.
  2. Validity, reliability and objectivity: A fitness test is only useful if it measures the right thing consistently and fairly.
  3. Pre-test procedures and health screening: Fitness testing places stress on the body, so testers must check that it is safe for each person.
  4. Ethics and informed consent in testing: Testing people raises ethical responsibilities.
  5. Health-related and skill-related fitness: Fitness is made up of health-related and skill-related components.
  6. Testing aerobic capacity: Aerobic capacity is measured most accurately as VO2max in a laboratory, but field tests are widely used to estimate it.
  7. Testing strength and power: Strength is the maximum force a muscle can produce, and power is how quickly force can be applied.
  8. Testing speed and agility: Speed and agility tests measure how quickly an athlete can sprint and change direction.
  9. Testing flexibility: Flexibility tests measure the range of movement at a joint.
  10. Body composition: Body composition describes the proportions of fat mass and fat-free mass in the body.
  11. The Wingate anaerobic test: The Wingate anaerobic test is a 30-second all-out cycle test used to measure anaerobic power and capacity.
  12. Normative data and interpreting results: Test scores mean little without something to compare them with.
  13. Laboratory versus field testing: Fitness can be tested in a controlled laboratory or in the field where sport is played.
  14. Performance and notational analysis: Performance analysis records and examines what happens in training and competition.
  15. Wearable technology and GPS: Wearable devices allow coaches to monitor athletes' movements and physiological responses in training and matches.

School 8: Sport and Exercise Nutrition

19 lessons

Sport and Exercise Nutrition: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Carbohydrates and dietary fibre: Carbohydrates are the body's main energy source, and dietary fibre is the part of plant foods the small intestine can't digest.
  2. Protein: Proteins are made of amino acids and are needed for growth, repair, enzymes, hormones and immunity.
  3. Fats: Fats provide energy, fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids, but the type matters.
  4. Vitamins: Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small amounts for health.
  5. Minerals: Minerals are inorganic elements needed for structure, fluid balance, nerve function and enzyme activity.
  6. Water and hydration: Water makes up about 60% of adult body weight and is essential for nearly every body process.
  7. The Eatwell Guide: The Eatwell Guide is the UK government's model of a healthy, balanced diet.
  8. Energy balance: Body weight changes when energy intake and energy expenditure are out of balance.
  9. Reading food labels: Food labels give the information people need to compare products and make healthier choices.
  10. Salt, sugar and saturated fat: Salt, sugar and saturated fat are the three nutrients UK diets most often contain too much of.
  11. Ultra-processed food: Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) make up more than half of the energy in UK diets.
  12. Nutrition in pregnancy and infancy: Good nutrition before and during pregnancy and in the first years of life supports healthy growth and development.
  13. Nutrition in childhood and adolescence: Children and teenagers need energy and nutrients for growth and development, but many UK children eat too much sugar and too little fibre.
  14. Nutrition in older age: Older adults need less energy but as many or more nutrients, and are at risk of both undernutrition and dehydration.
  15. Vegetarian and vegan diets: Plant-based diets are increasingly common in the UK.
  16. Malnutrition: Malnutrition means an imbalance of energy or nutrients, including undernutrition and overnutrition.
  17. Diet and chronic disease: Diet is one of the leading modifiable causes of chronic disease.
  18. Food insecurity: Food insecurity is a lack of reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
  19. Eating disorders: Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions affecting eating, weight and body image.

School 9: Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing

20 lessons

Motivation, Emotion and Wellbeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation: Motivation is what energises and directs behaviour.
  2. Drive theories: Drive theories explain motivation as a push to reduce internal tension caused by unmet needs.
  3. Incentive theories: Incentive theories explain motivation by the pull of external goals and rewards.
  4. Self-determination theory: Self-determination theory explains how social environments support or undermine motivation and wellbeing through three basic psychological needs.
  5. Goal-setting theory: Goal-setting theory explains how specific, challenging goals improve performance.
  6. Achievement motivation in context: Achievement motivation is shaped by context: classrooms, cultures, stereotypes and expectations.
  7. Theories of emotion: James–Lange and Cannon–Bard: Early theories of emotion asked whether bodily changes cause emotional feelings or occur alongside them.
  8. The two-factor theory: Schachter–Singer: Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory proposes that emotion depends on physiological arousal plus a cognitive label.
  9. Emotion regulation: Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they experience and express them.
  10. Stress: Stress arises when demands are perceived to exceed resources.
  11. The physiology of stress: Stress triggers bodily responses that prepare for action.
  12. Coping: Coping is how people manage stressful demands.
  13. Resilience: Resilience is the ability to adapt well in the face of adversity.
  14. Subjective wellbeing: Subjective wellbeing is how people evaluate and experience their own lives.
  15. Happiness: What makes people happy?
  16. Positive psychology in practice: Positive psychology studies what helps people thrive and develops interventions to increase wellbeing.
  17. Flow: Flow is a state of deep absorption in a challenging activity.
  18. Gratitude: Gratitude is noticing and appreciating the good things in life, including what others do for us.
  19. Mindfulness research: Mindfulness means paying attention to present-moment experience without judgement.
  20. Flourishing: Flourishing means living well: feeling good and functioning well.

School 10: Skill Acquisition and Coaching

20 lessons

Skill Acquisition and Coaching: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The nature of skill: Skill is a learned ability to produce a chosen result consistently and efficiently.
  2. Classifying skills: Skills can be classified on continua according to their features.
  3. Abilities: Abilities are mostly innate traits that underpin the learning of skills.
  4. Types of practice: How practice is organised affects how quickly and how well skills are learned.
  5. Transfer of skill: Learning one skill can help, hinder or not affect the learning of another.
  6. Stages of learning: Learners progress through recognisable stages as they acquire a skill.
  7. Learning curves and plateaus: Learning curves show how performance changes with practice.
  8. Theories of learning: Different theories explain how people learn movement skills.
  9. Guidance: Guidance is information given to help a learner perform a skill.
  10. Feedback: Feedback is information about a performance that helps the learner correct errors and stay motivated.
  11. Information-processing models: Information-processing models describe how performers take in information, make decisions and produce movement.
  12. Reaction time and Hick's law: Reaction time is the time from the onset of a stimulus to the start of a response.
  13. Memory in skill learning: Memory allows performers to store and recall information and motor programmes.
  14. Schema theory: Schema theory explains how performers adapt learned movements to new situations.
  15. Ecological and constraints-led approaches: Ecological approaches see skill as emerging from the interaction between the performer, the task and the environment.
  16. The role of the coach: Coaches have many roles beyond teaching technique.
  17. Coaching styles: Coaches can use different styles depending on the learner, the skill and the situation.
  18. Planning a coaching session: A well-planned session is safe, progressive and matched to participants' needs.
  19. Long-term athlete development: Long-term athlete development models aim to match training to children's growth and maturation.
  20. Safeguarding and ethics in coaching: Coaches have a duty to keep children and adults at risk safe and to behave ethically.

School 11: Injury Prevention and Rehabilitation

14 lessons

Injury Prevention and Rehabilitation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Acute and chronic injuries: Sports injuries are usually classified by how they happen and how quickly they develop.
  2. Soft-tissue injuries: Most sports injuries affect soft tissues: muscles, tendons and ligaments.
  3. Hard-tissue injuries: Hard-tissue injuries involve bones and joints.
  4. Concussion: Concussion is a brain injury that can follow any blow to the head or body.
  5. Risk factors for injury: Injuries rarely have a single cause.
  6. Injury prevention strategies: Many sports injuries can be prevented.
  7. Screening and movement assessment: Many teams screen athletes before a season to identify injury risk.
  8. Load management and injury: How much athletes train, and how quickly that changes, strongly influences injury risk.
  9. First aid in sport: Knowing what to do in the first minutes after an injury can save a life.
  10. The stages of tissue healing: Injured tissues heal in a predictable sequence.
  11. Rehabilitation: Rehabilitation restores an injured athlete's function and readiness to perform.
  12. Return to play: Deciding when an athlete can safely return to competition is one of the hardest judgements in sport.
  13. Recovery methods: Recovery between sessions allows athletes to adapt and perform.
  14. Psychological responses to injury: Injury affects athletes' minds as well as their bodies.

School 12: Physical Activity

14 lessons

Physical Activity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Physical activity, exercise and fitness: Physical activity, exercise and fitness are related but different ideas.
  2. The UK physical activity guidelines: The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 guidelines set out how much activity people of different ages need.
  3. Sedentary behaviour: Sedentary behaviour is sitting or lying with low energy use while awake, and it carries health risks separate from a lack of exercise.
  4. The benefits of physical activity: Regular physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases and improves mental health and quality of life.
  5. Short-term responses to exercise: When exercise starts, the body responds within seconds to deliver more oxygen to working muscles.
  6. Long-term adaptations to training: Regular training causes lasting changes in the body that improve health and performance.
  7. Energy systems: Muscles get energy from ATP, which is resupplied by three energy systems.
  8. Components of fitness: Fitness is made up of several components, some linked mainly to health and others to skill and performance.
  9. Measuring fitness: Fitness tests measure components of fitness so that progress can be tracked and training planned.
  10. Physical activity in children and young people: Physical activity supports children's growth, development, health and wellbeing, but fewer than half meet the guidelines.
  11. Physical activity in older adults: Staying active in later life helps maintain independence, prevent falls and protect mental health.
  12. Activity with long-term conditions: Physical activity helps prevent and manage many long-term conditions, but people may need tailored advice.
  13. Barriers to physical activity: Many people want to be more active but face barriers.
  14. Active environments and active travel: The places where people live, work and travel strongly affect how active they are.
