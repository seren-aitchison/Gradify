Law: a free course on LawGradify

LawGradify's free Law course runs from Legal Foundations and the English Legal System to SQE2 Skills Practice: 480 lessons in 19 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Legal reasoning). It's written for law students and trainee lawyers.

Study material, not legal advice. The law is stated for England and Wales unless a lesson says otherwise; Scotland and Northern Ireland differ (School 17). Completing a LawGradify course is not a legal qualification and does not authorise you to practise. Law changes: check current sources before you rely on anything here.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Legal Foundations and the English Legal System

30 lessons

Legal Foundations and the English Legal System: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is law?: Law is a system of rules, recognised and enforced by the state, that governs how people, organisations and the state itself behave.
  2. The nature and purposes of law: Law serves several purposes at once: keeping order, settling disputes, protecting rights, regulating activity and expressing a society's values.
  3. Sources of law: The sources of law in England and Wales are legislation, case law (common law and equity), retained or assimilated law from the UK's EU membership, and, less directly, custom, conventions and international law.
  4. Common law and equity: The common law is the law developed by judges in the royal courts from the twelfth century; equity grew up in the Court of Chancery to soften its rigidity.
  5. Civil and criminal law: Criminal law deals with wrongs against society, prosecuted by the state and punished; civil law deals with disputes between private parties, usually ended with compensation or another remedy.
  6. Public and private law: Public law governs the state's relationship with individuals and the organisation of the state itself; private law governs relationships between individuals and organisations.
  7. The English legal system: The English legal system is the law, institutions and people that make, interpret and apply law in England and Wales.
  8. Court structures and jurisdiction: Courts in England and Wales are divided into civil and criminal, and into trial (first instance) and appellate courts.
  9. The hierarchy of courts: Courts are arranged in a hierarchy that decides which decisions bind which courts.
  10. The doctrine of precedent: Under the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis, "to stand by things decided"), courts follow the legal principles in earlier decisions of higher courts so that like cases are treated alike.
  11. Ratio decidendi and obiter dicta: The ratio decidendi ("reason for deciding") is the legal principle necessary for the decision on the material facts, and it is the binding part of a judgment.
  12. Statutory interpretation: Statutory interpretation is how courts work out what an Act means when it is applied to facts.
  13. Parliamentary sovereignty: Parliamentary sovereignty means the King in Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no court or other body can set aside an Act of Parliament.
  14. The rule of law: The rule of law is the principle that everyone, including government, is subject to the law, and that law should be clear, public, stable and applied fairly by independent courts.
  15. Separation of powers: The separation of powers divides state power between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary so that no one branch holds unchecked power.
  16. Judicial independence: Judicial independence means judges decide cases free from pressure by government, Parliament, parties, the media or anyone else.
  17. Parliament and the legislative process: Parliament makes primary legislation through a structured process of readings, committee scrutiny and Royal Assent.
  18. Delegated legislation: Delegated (secondary) legislation is law made by a person or body other than Parliament, under powers given by an Act (the parent or enabling Act).
  19. The judiciary: The judiciary is the body of judges and magistrates who decide cases.
  20. Tribunals: Tribunals decide disputes in specialist areas, mostly between individuals and the state (benefits, immigration, tax) and some between private parties (employment).
  21. Alternative dispute resolution: Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) covers ways of settling disputes outside a court trial: negotiation, mediation, conciliation, early neutral evaluation, adjudication and arbitration.
  22. Legal institutions and regulators: A web of institutions supports the legal system: government departments, prosecutors, law reform bodies and professional regulators.
  23. The legal profession: The legal profession in England and Wales is split mainly between solicitors and barristers, with chartered legal executives and other authorised professionals.
  24. Access to justice: Access to justice means people can understand their rights, get advice and bring or defend claims fairly, whatever their means.
  25. Legal aid: Legal aid is public funding for legal advice and representation for people who cannot afford it.
  26. Legal research and authority: Legal research is finding the law that applies to a question and judging how much weight each source carries.
  27. Case law analysis: Case law analysis is reading a judgment to extract the material facts, the issues, the decision, the ratio, and how the case fits with others.
  28. Legal citation and referencing: Legal citation identifies sources precisely so a reader can find them.
  29. Legal writing: Legal writing must be accurate, clear and organised for its reader, whether that is an examiner, a judge or a client.
  30. Introduction to legal argument: Legal argument persuades a decision-maker that the law, applied to the facts, produces a particular result.

School 2: Constitutional and Administrative Law

30 lessons

Constitutional and Administrative Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The UK constitution: The UK constitution is uncodified: there is no single document setting out the rules of government.
  2. Constitutional conventions: Constitutional conventions are non-legal rules that regulate how constitutional actors behave.
  3. Constitutional statutes: Some statutes are recognised by the courts as "constitutional statutes" because they deal with the relationship between citizen and state or fundamental rights.
  4. Parliamentary sovereignty: Parliamentary sovereignty is the central principle of the UK constitution.
  5. The rule of law: The rule of law requires that government acts under law and that law is accessible, applied equally and enforced by independent courts.
  6. Separation of powers: The separation of powers in the UK is partial, but courts use it to police the boundaries between executive, legislature and judiciary.
  7. Executive power: The executive is the government: the Crown, the Prime Minister, Cabinet, ministers and the civil service.
  8. Royal prerogative: The royal prerogative is the residue of discretionary power left in the Crown.
  9. Parliament and legislative scrutiny: Parliament holds the government to account and scrutinises legislation.
  10. Devolution: Devolution transferred power from Westminster to legislatures and executives in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  11. The UK Supreme Court: The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal for civil cases across the UK and for criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  12. Judicial independence: Judicial independence is protected in the UK by statute, convention and institutional design.
  13. Human rights and constitutional protection: Rights in the UK are protected by the common law, statute (especially the Human Rights Act 1998), and parliamentary scrutiny.
  14. The Human Rights Act 1998: The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates most ECHR rights into UK law.
  15. The European Convention on Human Rights: The European Convention on Human Rights is a treaty of the Council of Europe, signed in 1950.
  16. Judicial review: Judicial review is the procedure by which the High Court supervises the lawfulness of decisions by public bodies.
  17. Grounds of judicial review: The grounds of judicial review were classified by Lord Diplock in the GCHQ case as illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, with proportionality as a possible future ground.
  18. Illegality: Illegality covers decisions where the public body misunderstood or exceeded its legal powers.
  19. Irrationality: Irrationality, or Wednesbury unreasonableness, allows the courts to quash decisions so unreasonable that no reasonable decision-maker could have made them.
  20. Procedural impropriety: Procedural impropriety covers failures to follow statutory procedures and the common law rules of natural justice: the rule against bias and the right to a fair hearing.
  21. Legitimate expectations: A legitimate expectation arises when a public body's promise or established practice leads someone to expect a particular procedure or outcome.
  22. Proportionality: Proportionality asks whether a measure that interferes with rights is justified: whether its aim is important enough, whether it is rationally connected to that aim, whether a less intrusive measure…
  23. Standing: To bring judicial review, a claimant must have a "sufficient interest" in the matter (Senior Courts Act 1981, s 31(3)).
  24. Justiciability: Some decisions are unsuitable for judicial resolution because they involve high policy, national security, foreign affairs or matters better decided by Parliament.
  25. Remedies: Judicial review remedies are discretionary.
  26. Administrative tribunals: Tribunals decide disputes between individuals and the state across areas such as social security, immigration, tax and employment.
  27. Public authorities: Who counts as a public authority matters for judicial review and for duties under the Human Rights Act.
  28. Government accountability: Government is accountable to Parliament, the courts, independent bodies and the public.
  29. Constitutional reform: The UK constitution has changed significantly since 1997: devolution, the Human Rights Act, House of Lords reform, the Supreme Court, Brexit and changes to the timing of elections.
  30. Contemporary constitutional challenges: The UK constitution faces pressures from Brexit's legacy, tensions within the Union, clashes between government and the courts, debates over the ECHR, and questions about executive power.

School 3: Contract Law

36 lessons

Contract Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to contract law: A contract is a legally binding agreement: an exchange of promises (or a promise for an act) that the law will enforce.
  2. Agreement and offer: An offer is an expression of willingness to contract on specified terms, made with the intention that it will become binding as soon as it is accepted.
  3. Acceptance: Acceptance is an unqualified, final agreement to all the terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror.
  4. Communication of acceptance: The general rule is that acceptance takes effect when it is communicated to the offeror.
  5. Consideration: Consideration is the price of a promise: something of value given or promised in return.
  6. Intention to create legal relations: An agreement is only a contract if the parties intended it to be legally binding.
  7. Certainty and completeness: Courts will only enforce an agreement whose terms are certain enough to know what each party must do.
  8. Contractual terms: Contractual terms are the promises and obligations that make up the contract.
  9. Express terms: Express terms are those the parties specifically agree, orally or in writing.
  10. Implied terms: Implied terms are not expressly agreed but are read into the contract by statute, by the courts as a matter of law for certain types of contract, as a matter of fact to give effect to the parties'…
  11. Conditions, warranties and innominate terms: Terms are classified by importance, and the classification decides whether the innocent party can terminate the contract for breach or can only claim damages.
  12. Representations and terms: Statements made before a contract may become terms (promises the maker guarantees) or remain representations (statements that induced the contract).
  13. Misrepresentation: A misrepresentation is a false statement of fact or law, made by one party to the other, which induces the other to enter into the contract.
  14. Fraudulent misrepresentation: A fraudulent misrepresentation is a false statement made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly, careless whether it is true or false.
  15. Negligent misrepresentation: Negligent misrepresentation is a false statement made carelessly, without reasonable grounds for believing it true.
  16. Innocent misrepresentation: An innocent misrepresentation is a false statement made with reasonable grounds for believing it to be true.
  17. Duress: Duress is illegitimate pressure that coerces a party into a contract.
  18. Undue influence: Undue influence is an equitable doctrine that sets aside transactions procured by the abuse of a relationship of trust and confidence.
  19. Mistake: The doctrine of mistake can make a contract void where a fundamental mistake prevents genuine agreement or makes the contract impossible to perform as both parties assumed.
  20. Illegality: Illegality covers contracts that involve a crime, a civil wrong or conduct contrary to public policy.
  21. Privity of contract: Privity means only the parties to a contract can enforce it or be bound by it.
  22. Exclusion clauses: An exclusion clause excludes or limits a party's liability for breach of contract, negligence or misrepresentation.
  23. Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977: UCTA 1977 controls exclusion and limitation clauses in business-to-business contracts (and non-consumer notices).
  24. Consumer Rights Act 2015: The Consumer Rights Act 2015 is the main statute for contracts between traders and consumers.
  25. Discharge by performance: A contract is discharged by performance when both parties have performed their obligations exactly.
  26. Breach of contract: A breach of contract occurs when a party, without lawful excuse, fails to perform an obligation, performs defectively or repudiates the contract.
  27. Anticipatory breach: Anticipatory breach occurs when, before performance is due, a party clearly indicates that it will not perform, or puts itself in a position where it cannot.
  28. Frustration: A contract is frustrated when, after it was made, an event without the fault of either party makes performance impossible, illegal or radically different from what was agreed.
  29. Contractual remedies: Remedies for breach of contract aim to put the innocent party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed.
  30. Damages: Contract damages compensate the innocent party for loss caused by the breach, usually by putting them in the position they would have been in if the contract had been performed (the expectation interest).
  31. Specific performance: Specific performance is an equitable order compelling a party to perform its contractual obligations.
  32. Injunctions: An injunction is an equitable order requiring a party to do something (mandatory) or refrain from doing something (prohibitory).
  33. Restitution: Restitution reverses unjust enrichment: where a defendant has been enriched at the claimant's expense in circumstances the law regards as unjust, the claimant may recover the value.
  34. Third-party rights: The Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 lets a person who isn't a party to a contract enforce a term in their own right in certain circumstances, reforming the strict privity rule.
  35. Consumer contracts: Consumer contracts are subject to extra protection on top of the Consumer Rights Act 2015, especially for contracts made at a distance or off-premises, and against unfair commercial practices.
  36. Digital and electronic contracts: Contracts can be made electronically, by email, click-wrap, browse-wrap and even smart contracts.

School 4: Tort Law

30 lessons

Tort Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to tort: A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which the law gives a remedy, usually damages.
  2. Negligence: Negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take care, causing damage that is not too remote.
  3. Duty of care: A duty of care is a legal obligation to take reasonable care not to cause certain kinds of harm to others.
  4. Neighbour principle: Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 is the foundation of the modern law of negligence: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably…
  5. Foreseeability: Reasonable foreseeability is central to negligence.
  6. Breach of duty: Breach of duty occurs when the defendant falls below the standard of care the law requires: that of the reasonable person in the defendant's position.
  7. Standard of care: The standard of care varies with the defendant's role and the circumstances.
  8. Causation in fact: Factual causation asks whether the defendant's breach actually caused the claimant's damage.
  9. Legal causation: Even if the breach factually caused the damage, the law may decide it was not the legal cause.
  10. Remoteness of damage: Even where breach caused damage, the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable.
  11. Novus actus interveniens: A novus actus interveniens is a new intervening act that breaks the chain of causation between the defendant's breach and the claimant's damage, so the defendant isn't liable for the later loss.
  12. Psychiatric injury: The law allows claims for a recognised psychiatric illness caused by negligence, but it restricts them, especially for "secondary victims" who witness harm to others, to limit the number of claims.
  13. Pure economic loss: Pure economic loss is financial loss not consequent on physical injury to the claimant or damage to their property.
  14. Professional negligence: Professional negligence is the failure of a professional (such as a solicitor, accountant, surveyor, architect or doctor) to exercise the skill and care of a reasonably competent member of their profession, causing loss.
  15. Occupiers' liability: Occupiers owe duties to people on their premises under two statutes: the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 for lawful visitors, and the Occupiers' Liability Act 1984 for others, mainly trespassers.
  16. Employers' liability: Employers owe their employees a personal, non-delegable duty to take reasonable care for their safety.
  17. Vicarious liability: Vicarious liability makes one person (usually an employer) liable for a tort committed by another (usually an employee), without any fault of their own.
  18. Product liability: People injured by defective products can sue under the Consumer Protection Act 1987, Part 1, which imposes strict liability on producers, or in negligence under Donoghue v Stevenson.
  19. Defamation: Defamation protects reputation.
  20. Private nuisance: Private nuisance is an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over or in connection with it.
  21. Public nuisance: Public nuisance is an act or omission that endangers the life, health, property or comfort of the public, or obstructs the public in exercising rights common to all.
  22. Trespass to the person: Trespass to the person protects bodily integrity and liberty.
  23. Assault: Assault, as a tort, is an act that causes the claimant reasonably to apprehend the immediate infliction of unlawful force on them.
  24. Battery: Battery is the intentional and direct application of unlawful force to another person.
  25. False imprisonment: False imprisonment is the unlawful, total restraint of another person's freedom of movement, for however short a time.
  26. Trespass to land: Trespass to land is the unjustified, direct and intentional interference with land in another's possession.
  27. Defences: Even where the elements of a tort are proved, the defendant may escape or reduce liability through a defence.
  28. Contributory negligence: Contributory negligence is a partial defence: where the claimant's own failure to take reasonable care for their safety contributed to their damage, damages are reduced to the extent the court thinks just and equitable.
  29. Voluntary assumption of risk: Volenti non fit injuria ("to a willing person, no injury is done") is a complete defence where the claimant, with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk, freely and voluntarily agreed to accept it.
  30. Remedies and damages: The main remedy in tort is compensatory damages, aimed at putting the claimant, as far as money can, in the position they would have been in if the tort hadn't been committed.

School 5: Criminal Law

37 lessons

Criminal Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to criminal liability: Criminal liability arises when a person commits the conduct an offence prohibits (actus reus) with the state of mind it requires (mens rea), without a valid defence.
  2. Actus reus: The actus reus is the external element of an offence: the conduct, circumstances and, for result crimes, the consequences.
  3. Mens rea: Mens rea is the mental element of an offence.
  4. Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea: For most offences, the actus reus and mens rea must coincide in time.
  5. Causation: For result crimes, the prosecution must prove that the defendant's conduct caused the prohibited result, both in fact and in law.
  6. Strict liability: Strict liability offences require no mens rea as to at least one element of the actus reus.
  7. Attempts: An attempt is an inchoate offence: the defendant tries to commit an offence but doesn't complete it.
  8. Secondary liability: A person who aids, abets, counsels or procures an offence is liable to be tried and punished as a principal.
  9. Joint enterprise: "Joint enterprise" describes cases where two or more people set out to commit one crime and, in the course of it, one commits a further crime.
  10. Corporate criminal liability: Companies can commit crimes.
  11. Homicide: Homicide is the killing of a human being by another.
  12. Murder: Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace with malice aforethought.
  13. Voluntary manslaughter: Voluntary manslaughter is a killing with the mens rea of murder that is reduced to manslaughter because a partial defence applies.
  14. Diminished responsibility: Diminished responsibility is a partial defence to murder under s 2 of the Homicide Act 1957, as amended.
  15. Loss of control: Loss of control is a partial defence to murder under ss 54–56 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, replacing the old defence of provocation.
  16. Involuntary manslaughter: Involuntary manslaughter is an unlawful killing where D lacked the mens rea for murder.
  17. Gross negligence manslaughter: Gross negligence manslaughter applies where D owes V a duty of care, breaches it, and the breach causes death in circumstances so bad that the jury considers it criminal.
  18. Unlawful act manslaughter: Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter applies where D intentionally does an act that is unlawful and dangerous, and it causes death.
  19. Non-fatal offences: The non-fatal offences against the person form a ladder of seriousness, from common assault and battery to wounding or causing GBH with intent.
  20. Assault and battery: Assault and battery are the least serious offences against the person.
  21. Actual bodily harm: Assault occasioning actual bodily harm under s 47 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 requires an assault or battery that causes ABH.
  22. Grievous bodily harm: Sections 20 and 18 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 cover the most serious non-fatal injuries.
  23. Sexual offences: The Sexual Offences Act 2003 sets out the main sexual offences, including rape, assault by penetration and sexual assault.
  24. Theft: Theft is defined by s 1 of the Theft Act 1968: dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.
  25. Robbery: Robbery is theft with force.
  26. Burglary: Burglary under s 9 of the Theft Act 1968 involves entering a building or part of a building as a trespasser, either with intent to commit certain offences (s 9(1)(a)) or then committing or attempting…
  27. Fraud: The Fraud Act 2006 created a general offence of fraud, committed in one of three ways: false representation, failing to disclose information, or abuse of position.
  28. Criminal damage: The Criminal Damage Act 1971 makes it an offence to destroy or damage property belonging to another, intending to or being reckless as to that result, without lawful excuse.
  29. Defences: Criminal defences either deny an element of the offence or justify or excuse conduct that would otherwise be criminal.
  30. Self-defence: Self-defence, defence of others and the prevention of crime allow the use of reasonable force.
  31. Insanity: Insanity is a defence based on the M'Naghten Rules (1843).
  32. Automatism: Automatism is a defence where D's acts were involuntary because of an external factor that caused a complete loss of voluntary control.
  33. Intoxication: Intoxication is not a defence as such, but evidence of intoxication may show that D lacked the mens rea for an offence.
  34. Duress: Duress excuses D who committed an offence because of a threat of death or serious injury.
  35. Necessity: Necessity covers situations where D commits an offence to avoid a greater evil.
  36. Consent: Consent is a defence to assault and battery, and in limited, recognised situations to more serious harm.
  37. Criminal attempts and inchoate offences: Inchoate offences criminalise conduct before a substantive offence is completed.

School 6: Criminal Law and Practice

27 lessons

Criminal Law and Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The criminal justice system: The criminal justice system investigates, prosecutes and tries offences, and punishes and rehabilitates offenders.
  2. Police powers: The Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE) and its Codes of Practice regulate police powers to stop, search, enter premises, seize evidence, arrest, detain and question.
  3. Stop and search: Stop and search allows police to search people and vehicles in public for prohibited items.
  4. Arrest: A police officer may arrest without warrant under s 24 of PACE if they have reasonable grounds to suspect an offence and believe arrest is necessary for a listed reason.
  5. Detention and questioning: After arrest, the custody officer decides whether to detain the suspect without charge.
  6. Police interviews: A police interview is the questioning of a suspect about their involvement in an offence, under caution and recorded.
  7. PACE: The Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 is the central statute on police powers and the treatment of suspects.
  8. Disclosure: Disclosure in criminal cases ensures the defence receives material that might undermine the prosecution or assist the defence.
  9. Bail: Bail is release from custody while proceedings continue.
  10. Charging decisions: Charging decisions are made by the police for minor offences and by the CPS for most others, applying the Code for Crown Prosecutors.
  11. Prosecution: Most prosecutions are brought by the Crown Prosecution Service, which must act fairly, independently and in the public interest.
  12. Magistrates' Court: Magistrates' courts deal with almost all criminal cases at first, and try most summary and many either-way offences.
  13. Crown Court: The Crown Court tries indictable offences before a judge and jury, and hears either-way offences sent or elected for trial.
  14. Allocation of offences: Either-way offences can be tried in the magistrates' court or the Crown Court.
  15. Plea and trial preparation: Effective case preparation means early identification of the issues, timely disclosure and pleas.
  16. Evidence: Criminal evidence law decides what facts must be proved, by whom, and what evidence can be used to prove them.
  17. Hearsay: Hearsay is a statement made out of court and offered to prove the truth of what it says.
  18. Confessions: A confession is any statement adverse to the person who made it.
  19. Identification evidence: Eyewitness identification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions.
  20. Bad character evidence: Evidence of a defendant's or witness's bad character, such as previous convictions, is admissible only through the gateways in the Criminal Justice Act 2003.
  21. Witnesses: Witnesses give oral evidence that is tested by cross-examination.
  22. Expert evidence: Expert evidence gives the court opinions on matters beyond ordinary knowledge, such as forensic science or medicine.
  23. Trial procedure: A criminal trial follows a set structure: the prosecution opens and calls evidence, the defence may submit there is no case to answer, then presents its evidence, and both sides make closing speeches.
  24. Sentencing: Sentencing is governed by the Sentencing Act 2020 (the Sentencing Code) and Sentencing Council guidelines.
  25. Appeals: Criminal appeals allow convictions and sentences to be challenged.
  26. Criminal legal aid: Criminal legal aid funds advice and representation for people suspected or accused of crimes.
  27. Professional conduct in criminal practice: Criminal defence work raises distinctive ethical issues.

School 7: Land Law

30 lessons

Land Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to land law: Land law governs the rights people can hold in land: who owns it, who can use it, and whose rights win when they conflict.
  2. Nature of land: "Land" in law means more than the soil.
  3. Estates in land: English law doesn't recognise outright ownership of land by anyone but the Crown.
  4. Freehold estates: The fee simple absolute in possession is the freehold estate: the largest estate in land, lasting indefinitely and freely transferable.
  5. Leasehold estates: The term of years absolute is the leasehold estate.
  6. Legal and equitable interests: Interests in land can be legal or equitable.
  7. Registered land: Most land in England and Wales is now registered under the Land Registration Act 2002.
  8. Unregistered land: A shrinking minority of land is still unregistered.
  9. Land registration: Land registration involves first registration of unregistered land and the registration of later dealings.
  10. Title investigation: Title investigation is the buyer's solicitor checking that the seller owns the land and can sell it free of unexpected burdens.
  11. Co-ownership: Co-ownership arises whenever two or more people own land at the same time.
  12. Joint tenancy: A joint tenancy treats co-owners as a single owner: each owns the whole, and no one has a separate share.
  13. Tenancy in common: Tenants in common each have a distinct share in the land, which they can sell, mortgage or leave by will.
  14. Severance: Severance converts a beneficial joint tenancy into a tenancy in common, so that the severing co-owner's share no longer passes by survivorship.
  15. Trusts of land: The Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996 governs trusts of land, including all co-owned land.
  16. Overreaching: Overreaching allows a buyer of trust land to take free of the beneficiaries' interests, which shift from the land into the purchase money.
  17. Easements: An easement is a right enjoyed by one piece of land (the dominant tenement) over another (the servient tenement), such as a right of way or a right to light.
  18. Covenants: A covenant is a promise in a deed.
  19. Mortgages: A mortgage is security for a debt: the borrower (mortgagor) grants the lender (mortgagee) rights over land that can be enforced if the loan isn't repaid.
  20. Proprietary estoppel: Proprietary estoppel prevents a landowner from going back on an assurance that someone would have rights in land, where that person relied on it to their detriment and it would be unconscionable to deny the right.
  21. Adverse possession: Adverse possession allows a person in long, factual possession of another's land, with the intention to possess, to acquire title.
  22. Leases: A lease gives exclusive possession of land for a term certain.
  23. Licences: A licence is a permission to be on land that would otherwise be a trespass.
  24. Landlord and tenant relationships: The landlord and tenant relationship is shaped by the lease, implied covenants and a large body of statute.
  25. Priorities and third-party rights: Priority rules decide whether a buyer or lender takes land subject to an existing third-party right.
  26. Restrictive covenants: A restrictive covenant is a promise not to do something on land, such as not to build or not to run a business.
  27. Freehold covenants: Positive freehold covenants, such as obligations to repair or contribute to costs, don't bind later owners of the burdened land.
  28. Land disputes: Land disputes include boundary disputes, rights of way, trespass, nuisance and disagreements between co-owners.
  29. Property transactions: A property transaction moves through three main stages: pre-contract, exchange of contracts and completion, followed by post-completion steps.
  30. Conveyancing fundamentals: Conveyancing is the legal work of transferring land.

School 8: Property Law and Practice

25 lessons

Property Law and Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Residential conveyancing: Residential conveyancing is the legal process of buying and selling homes.
  2. Commercial conveyancing: Commercial conveyancing covers buying, selling and leasing business premises.
  3. Client onboarding: Onboarding is the set of checks and steps a firm completes before acting: conflict checks, identifying the client and their authority, risk assessment and anti-money laundering checks, and setting…
  4. Identity verification: Verifying identity is a legal requirement under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017 and a key defence against property fraud.
  5. Source of funds and source of wealth: Conveyancers must understand where the money for a purchase comes from.
  6. Anti-money laundering: Property is a common target for money laundering.
  7. Instructions and client care: Taking full instructions at the start of a matter avoids problems later.
  8. Searches and enquiries: Searches and enquiries reveal information that a buyer can't get from inspecting the property or the title.
  9. Official copies and title documents: Title investigation starts with the official copies of the register and title plan for registered land, and the deeds for unregistered land.
  10. Contract packages: The seller's solicitor prepares a contract package for the buyer's solicitor at the start of the transaction.
  11. Raising enquiries: After reviewing the contract package and search results, the buyer's solicitor raises additional enquiries targeted at issues specific to the property.
  12. Mortgage finance: Most buyers fund their purchase with a mortgage.
  13. Exchange of contracts: Exchange of contracts makes the sale binding.
  14. Completion: Completion is when the purchase money is paid and the seller hands over the transfer deed and keys.
  15. Post-completion formalities: After completion, both solicitors have important tasks.
  16. Stamp Duty Land Tax: Stamp Duty Land Tax is payable by buyers of land in England and Northern Ireland.
  17. Land Registry applications: Registration completes the buyer's legal title and the lender's security.
  18. Leasehold transactions: Buying or selling a leasehold property, usually a flat, adds layers to conveyancing: checking the lease, the landlord's consent, service charges, ground rent, management information and the length of the lease.
  19. New-build property: Buying a newly built home involves a developer's standard documentation, a property that may not be finished at exchange, and extra checks on roads, drainage, warranties and estate charges.
  20. Remortgaging: A remortgage replaces an existing mortgage with a new one, without a sale.
  21. Transfers of equity: A transfer of equity adds or removes a person from the ownership of a property without a sale to an outsider: for example, adding a partner or transferring a home on separation.
  22. Commercial leases: Commercial leases are negotiated contracts with terms on rent, repair, use, alienation and ending the lease.
  23. Property taxation: Property transactions raise several taxes beyond SDLT: VAT on commercial property, capital gains tax on disposals, income tax on rents, inheritance tax, and the annual tax on enveloped dwellings.
  24. Professional conduct: Property work raises frequent conduct issues: conflicts, undertakings, contract races, referral arrangements, confidentiality and fraud prevention.
  25. Solicitors' accounts in property transactions: Property transactions involve large sums of client money: deposits, mortgage advances, purchase money and sale proceeds.

School 9: Equity and Trusts

27 lessons

Equity and Trusts: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Historical development of equity: Equity grew up to correct the rigidity of the common law.
  2. Equitable principles: Equity's maxims summarise the principles guiding equitable intervention.
  3. Trusts: A trust arises where a trustee holds property for the benefit of beneficiaries or for a permitted purpose.
  4. Express trusts: An express trust is deliberately created by a settlor, either by declaring themselves trustee or by transferring property to trustees.
  5. Implied trusts: Implied trusts arise without an express declaration.
  6. Resulting trusts: Resulting trusts carry the beneficial interest back to the person who provided the property.
  7. Constructive trusts: A constructive trust is imposed by law where it would be unconscionable for the legal owner to deny another's beneficial interest.
  8. Certainty of intention: The first of the three certainties is that the settlor must intend to create a trust: to impose a binding obligation on the trustee, not merely express a hope or wish.
  9. Certainty of subject matter: A trust must identify the property held on trust and, where relevant, each beneficiary's share.
  10. Certainty of objects: A private trust must have beneficiaries who can be identified with sufficient certainty.
  11. Constitution of trusts: A trust is completely constituted only when the trust property is vested in the trustees.
  12. Trustees' duties: Trustees must follow the trust terms, act in the beneficiaries' best interests, and meet the standards of care set by statute and equity.
  13. Trustees' powers: Trustees have powers to invest, delegate, maintain and advance, and appoint new trustees.
  14. Fiduciary duties: A fiduciary is someone who has undertaken to act for another in circumstances giving rise to a relationship of trust and confidence.
  15. Breach of trust: A trustee who breaches the trust is personally liable to restore the trust fund or compensate the beneficiaries.
  16. Equitable remedies: Equity provides remedies the common law can't: specific performance, injunctions, rescission, rectification and account of profits.
  17. Tracing: Tracing is the process of identifying new property as a substitute for the original, so that a claimant can assert proprietary rights in it.
  18. Knowing receipt: A person who receives trust property transferred in breach of trust, for their own benefit, is personally liable to account for it if their knowledge makes it unconscionable to retain the benefit.
  19. Dishonest assistance: A person who dishonestly assists a breach of trust or fiduciary duty is personally liable for the loss, even if they never received any trust property.
  20. Charitable trusts: Charitable trusts are purpose trusts for the public benefit.
  21. Purpose trusts: A private trust must generally have human beneficiaries who can enforce it: the beneficiary principle.
  22. Secret trusts: Secret trusts allow a testator to leave property by will to someone who has agreed to hold it on trust for another, without the trust appearing in the will.
  23. Trusts of land: Trusts of land are governed by the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996.
  24. Co-ownership: When couples or families share a home, equity decides who owns the beneficial interest and in what shares.
  25. Wills and testamentary trusts: Many trusts are created by will.
  26. Equitable interests: Equitable interests arise under trusts and other equitable doctrines.
  27. Remedies and defences: Claimants for breach of trust and fiduciary duty can bring personal claims against wrongdoers and recipients, and proprietary claims against identifiable assets.

School 10: Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates

20 lessons

Wills, Probate and Administration of Estates: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to succession law: Succession law decides what happens to a person's property on death.
  2. Testamentary freedom: English law gives testators broad freedom to leave their property as they wish.
  3. Capacity to make a will: A testator must have testamentary capacity when making a will, and must know and approve its contents.
  4. Formalities of wills: A will is valid only if it meets the formalities of s 9 of the Wills Act 1837.
  5. Revocation: A will can be revoked by a later will or codicil, by written declaration executed like a will, by destruction with intention to revoke, and (in England and Wales) by marriage or civil partnership.
  6. Alteration and rectification: Changes made to a will after execution are only valid if executed like a will.
  7. Interpretation of wills: When the meaning of a will is disputed, the court seeks to identify the testator's intention from the words used, read in context.
  8. Gifts and legacies: Gifts in a will are classified as specific, general, demonstrative, pecuniary or residuary.
  9. Intestacy: When someone dies without a valid will, or a will that doesn't dispose of everything, the intestacy rules in s 46 of the Administration of Estates Act 1925 decide who inherits.
  10. Personal representatives: Personal representatives (PRs) administer a deceased person's estate.
  11. Executors and administrators: Executors are appointed by the will; administrators are appointed by the court where there is no will or no executor able and willing to act.
  12. Grants of representation: A grant of representation is the court document confirming who has authority to deal with a deceased person's estate.
  13. Probate procedure: Applying for a grant is now mostly done online with HM Courts and Tribunals Service.
  14. Estate administration: After the grant, personal representatives collect the assets, pay debts, expenses and tax, and then distribute the estate.
  15. Inheritance tax: Inheritance tax is charged on the value of a person's estate on death and on some lifetime gifts.
  16. Estate liabilities: Personal representatives must pay the deceased's debts and the estate's expenses before distributing.
  17. Distribution of estates: Distribution is the final stage: PRs transfer gifts and residue to beneficiaries.
  18. Family provision claims: The Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 allows certain people to apply to court for reasonable financial provision from an estate where the will or intestacy doesn't make it.
  19. Proprietary estoppel in succession: Proprietary estoppel often arises in families where someone was promised they would inherit property, relied on that promise, and was then left out of the will.
  20. Professional responsibilities: Private client work raises conduct issues: identifying the client, assessing capacity and undue influence, conflicts when a beneficiary instructs, gifts to the drafter, confidentiality after death,…

30 lessons

Business Law and Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Business organisations: Businesses can be run through several legal structures: sole traders, partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability partnerships and companies.
  2. Sole traders: A sole trader runs a business personally, without a separate legal entity.
  3. Partnerships: A partnership is the relation between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit (Partnership Act 1890, s 1).
  4. Limited liability partnerships: A limited liability partnership is a body corporate with separate legal personality, created under the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000.
  5. Companies: A registered company is a legal person separate from its members, created by registration under the Companies Act 2006.

  7. Corporate personality: A registered company is a legal person separate from its shareholders and directors.

  9. Directors' duties: Directors owe general duties to the company, codified in ss 171–177 of the Companies Act 2006.
  10. Shareholders: Shareholders own the company but don't manage it.
  11. Share capital: Share capital is the money members invest in exchange for shares.
  12. Corporate governance: Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled.
  13. Company decision-making: Company decisions are made either by the board of directors or by the shareholders.
  14. Company meetings: Shareholders make decisions at general meetings unless they use written resolutions.
  15. Dividends: Dividends are distributions of profit to shareholders.
  16. Company accounts: Companies must keep accounting records, prepare annual accounts and reports, and file them at Companies House.
  17. Corporate finance: Companies raise money through equity (shares) or debt (loans, overdrafts and bonds).
  18. Business contracts: Business contracts are governed by general contract law, with commercial terms that allocate risk: payment, delivery, liability limits, boilerplate clauses and dispute resolution.
  19. Commercial transactions: Businesses use a range of commercial arrangements to sell goods and services: agency, distribution, franchising, licensing and joint ventures.
  20. Agency: Agency arises when one person (the agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (the principal) and to affect the principal's legal relations with third parties.
  21. Sale of goods: Business-to-business sales of goods are governed by the Sale of Goods Act 1979.
  22. Supply of goods and services: Contracts for services and for work and materials between businesses are governed by the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982.
  23. Consumer protection: Businesses dealing with consumers face stricter rules than in B2B contracts.
  24. Business taxation: Business structure affects tax.
  25. Insolvency: A company is insolvent when it can't pay its debts as they fall due or its liabilities exceed its assets.
  26. Liquidation: Liquidation (winding up) ends a company's life: a liquidator collects and sells its assets, distributes the proceeds to creditors in a statutory order, and the company is dissolved.
  27. Administration: Administration is a rescue procedure under Schedule B1 to the Insolvency Act 1986.
  28. Restructuring: Restructuring tools let financially distressed companies compromise their debts and continue trading.
  29. Corporate transactions: Corporate transactions include buying and selling companies or businesses, investments, mergers and joint ventures.

School 12: Dispute Resolution and Civil Litigation

26 lessons

Dispute Resolution and Civil Litigation: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Civil justice system: Civil disputes in England and Wales are resolved under the Civil Procedure Rules 1998 (CPR), which aim to deal with cases justly and at proportionate cost.
  2. Pre-action protocols: Before issuing proceedings, parties are expected to exchange information, try to settle and consider ADR.
  3. Letter before claim: The letter before claim is the claimant's formal statement of the claim before proceedings.
  4. Limitation: Limitation periods set time limits for bringing claims.
  5. Jurisdiction: Jurisdiction decides which court can hear a case.
  6. Commencing proceedings: Proceedings begin when the court issues a claim form.
  7. Particulars of claim: The particulars of claim set out the claimant's case: the facts relied on, the legal basis and the remedy sought.
  8. Defence and counterclaims: The defence responds to each allegation in the particulars of claim.
  9. Statements of case: Statements of case are the formal documents setting out each party's case: the claim form, particulars of claim, defence, counterclaim, reply and further information.
  10. Case management: Courts actively manage cases to further the overriding objective.
  11. Disclosure: Disclosure is the process by which parties reveal documents relevant to the issues.
  12. Evidence: Evidence proves the facts in dispute.
  13. Witness statements: Witness statements set out the evidence a witness will give at trial, in their own words.
  14. Expert witnesses: Expert evidence helps the court with matters outside its own knowledge.
  15. Interim applications: Interim applications ask the court for orders before trial, such as directions, injunctions, interim payments or security for costs.
  16. Summary judgment: Summary judgment lets the court decide a claim or issue without a trial where one side has no real prospect of success and there's no other compelling reason for a trial.
  17. Strike-out: The court can strike out all or part of a statement of case that discloses no reasonable grounds, is an abuse of process, or follows failure to comply with rules or orders.
  18. Settlement: Most civil cases settle.
  19. Mediation: Mediation is a confidential, voluntary process in which a neutral mediator helps the parties negotiate a settlement.
  20. Trial preparation: Preparing for trial means complying with final directions, completing the pre-trial checklist, preparing bundles and skeleton arguments, and making sure witnesses and counsel are ready.
  21. Trial procedure: At trial, each party presents its case through evidence and submissions, and the judge decides the facts and the law.
  22. Costs: In civil litigation, the general rule is that the losing party pays the winner's costs, but the court has a wide discretion.
  23. Enforcement: Winning a judgment doesn't guarantee payment.
  24. Appeals: An appeal challenges a decision of a lower court.
  25. Alternative dispute resolution: Alternative dispute resolution covers methods other than court trial: negotiation, mediation, early neutral evaluation, expert determination, adjudication, arbitration and ombudsman schemes.
  26. Professional conduct in litigation: Litigation tests a solicitor's duties most sharply.

School 13: Human Rights Law

23 lessons

Human Rights Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Human rights theory: Human rights theory asks what rights are, where they come from and why they matter.
  2. Universal human rights: International human rights law began with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948.
  3. European Convention on Human Rights: The ECHR protects civil and political rights across 46 Council of Europe states.
  4. Human Rights Act 1998: The Human Rights Act 1998 brings Convention rights into UK law.
  5. Convention rights: The Convention protects a set of core civil and political rights.
  6. Article 2: the right to life: Article 2 protects the right to life.
  7. Article 3: prohibition of torture: Article 3 prohibits torture and inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment.
  8. Article 4: slavery and forced labour: Article 4 prohibits slavery and servitude absolutely, and forbids forced or compulsory labour, subject to listed exceptions.
  9. Article 5: liberty and security: Article 5 protects against arbitrary detention.
  10. Article 6: fair trial: Article 6 guarantees a fair and public hearing within a reasonable time by an independent and impartial tribunal, in the determination of civil rights and obligations or criminal charges.
  11. Article 8: private and family life: Article 8 protects respect for private and family life, home and correspondence.
  12. Article 9: thought, conscience and religion: Article 9 protects freedom of thought, conscience and religion.
  13. Article 10: freedom of expression: Article 10 protects freedom of expression, including the press and the right to receive information.
  14. Article 11: assembly and association: Article 11 protects peaceful assembly and freedom of association, including forming and joining trade unions.
  15. Article 14: discrimination: Article 14 prohibits discrimination in the enjoyment of Convention rights.
  16. Protocol 1 Article 1: property: Article 1 of Protocol 1 protects the peaceful enjoyment of possessions.
  17. Positive obligations: Convention rights don't just stop the state interfering; they can require it to act.
  18. Proportionality: Proportionality is central to qualified rights.
  19. Derogations: Article 15 allows states to derogate from some Convention obligations in time of war or other public emergency threatening the life of the nation, to the extent strictly required.
  20. Remedies: Remedies for breaches of Convention rights include declarations of incompatibility, damages under the HRA, quashing unlawful decisions, and, at Strasbourg, findings of violation and just satisfaction.
  21. Public authorities: Section 6 of the HRA makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with Convention rights.
  22. Human rights litigation: Human rights claims can be brought as standalone HRA claims, through judicial review, or raised in other proceedings.
  23. International human rights frameworks: Beyond the ECHR, human rights are protected by the UN system, regional systems in the Americas and Africa, international criminal law, and customary international law.

School 14: European Union and International Law

20 lessons

European Union and International Law: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Foundations of EU law: The European Union grew from post-war economic cooperation into a union with its own legal order.
  2. EU institutions: The EU's institutions make, implement and interpret EU law.
  3. Sources of EU law: EU law has several sources.
  4. EU legislation: The main EU legislative acts are regulations, directives and decisions.
  5. Direct effect: Direct effect allows individuals to rely on EU provisions in national courts.
  6. Supremacy: Supremacy means EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law in member states.
  7. State liability: State liability allows individuals to claim damages from a member state for loss caused by its breach of EU law.
  8. Free movement: The internal market rests on the four freedoms: free movement of goods, persons, services and capital.
  9. EU citizenship: EU citizenship is held by every national of a member state.
  10. Competition law: EU competition law prohibits anti-competitive agreements (Article 101 TFEU), abuse of a dominant position (Article 102 TFEU) and controls mergers.
  11. UK-EU relationship: Since leaving the EU, the UK's relationship with it is governed mainly by the Withdrawal Agreement (with the Northern Ireland arrangements now known as the Windsor Framework) and the Trade and Cooperation Agreement.
  12. Retained and assimilated law: To avoid gaps on Brexit, the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 converted EU law into domestic "retained EU law".
  13. International law sources: Public international law governs relations between states and other international actors.
  14. Treaties: Treaties are written agreements between states (and international organisations) governed by international law.
  15. Customary international law: Customary international law arises from general state practice accepted as law (opinio juris).
  16. State responsibility: A state is responsible under international law for its internationally wrongful acts.
  17. International courts: International courts and tribunals settle disputes and develop international law.
  18. Jurisdiction in international law: Jurisdiction in international law concerns a state's power to make, apply and enforce laws.
  19. International human rights: International human rights law imposes obligations on states through treaties and custom, enforced mainly through monitoring rather than courts.
  20. International dispute resolution: International disputes can be resolved by diplomatic means or by legal methods.

School 15: Legal Ethics and Professional Conduct

25 lessons

Legal Ethics and Professional Conduct: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Professional ethics: Professional ethics are the standards of behaviour expected of lawyers because of their special role: they serve clients, but also the courts, the rule of law and the public.
  2. SRA Principles: The seven SRA Principles are the fundamental ethical requirements for everyone the SRA regulates.
  3. SRA Codes of Conduct: The SRA has two Codes of Conduct: one for individuals (solicitors, registered European and foreign lawyers) and one for firms.
  4. Client confidentiality: Solicitors must keep the affairs of current and former clients confidential unless disclosure is required or permitted by law or the client consents (Code, para 6.3).
  5. Legal professional privilege: Legal professional privilege (LPP) protects certain communications between lawyers and clients from disclosure, even to a court.
  6. Conflicts of interest: A solicitor must not act where there is a conflict of interests, or a significant risk of one, unless a narrow exception applies.
  7. Client money: Client money is money held or received by a firm for or on behalf of a client or third party (and certain other categories).
  8. Solicitors' accounts: The SRA Accounts Rules require firms to keep accurate accounting records, reconcile client accounts regularly and, in most cases, obtain an accountant's report.
  9. Client care: Client care is about giving clients the information and service they need: who will do the work, what it will cost, what will happen and how to complain.
  10. Competence: Solicitors must provide a proper standard of service, be competent to carry out their role and keep their professional knowledge and skills up to date (Code paras 3.2–3.6).
  11. Honesty and integrity: Honesty and integrity are among the most important requirements of being a solicitor.
  12. Independence: Solicitors must act with independence (Principle 3): their professional judgement must not be compromised by the interests of third parties, introducers, funders, employers or their own interests.
  13. Professional duties to the court: Solicitors and barristers owe duties to the court that override their duties to clients.
  14. Undertakings: An undertaking is a statement, given orally or in writing, by which a solicitor (or someone in a firm) agrees to do or not do something.
  15. Anti-money laundering: Solicitors are a target for money launderers because they handle large sums and give credibility to transactions.
  16. Financial crime: Beyond money laundering, solicitors must understand other financial crimes that can affect their practice or their clients: fraud, bribery, sanctions breaches, tax evasion facilitation and terrorist financing.
  17. Equality and diversity: Solicitors must act in a way that encourages equality, diversity and inclusion (Principle 6), and must not unfairly discriminate (Code para 1.1).
  18. Complaints: Clients who are unhappy with a firm's service have a right to complain, first to the firm and then to the Legal Ombudsman.
  19. Professional negligence: A solicitor who fails to exercise the reasonable care and skill of a competent solicitor and causes loss may be liable to the client in contract and tort, and sometimes to third parties.
  20. Regulatory compliance: Firms and individual solicitors must have systems to comply with regulatory requirements, cooperate with the SRA and report serious breaches.
  21. Duties to clients: Solicitors owe clients duties under the SRA Principles and Code, in contract, in tort, and as fiduciaries.
  22. Duties to third parties: Solicitors owe duties not only to clients but also to third parties, including the other side, witnesses and others affected by their work.
  23. Duties to the public: Solicitors serve the public interest: upholding the rule of law and the administration of justice, maintaining public trust in the profession, and protecting the public from harm.
  24. Professional boundaries: Professional boundaries keep the relationship between solicitor and client, and between colleagues, appropriate.
  25. Ethical decision-making: Ethical decision-making is a structured way of working out the right course of action when duties, interests and pressures conflict.

School 16: Legal Research, Writing and Practical Skills

20 lessons

Legal Research, Writing and Practical Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Finding primary legal sources: Primary sources are the law itself: legislation and case law.
  2. Finding secondary legal sources: Secondary sources explain, analyse and comment on the law: textbooks, practitioner works, encyclopaedias, journals and law reform reports.
  3. Legislation research: Researching legislation means finding the right provision, checking that it is in force and up to date, understanding how it has been amended, and interpreting it correctly using permitted aids.
  4. Case law research: Case law research means finding relevant judgments, identifying the binding authorities, and checking that they are still good law.
  5. Legal databases: Legal research relies on free and subscription databases.
  6. Westlaw and LexisNexis concepts: Westlaw and LexisNexis are the main subscription legal research platforms in the UK.
  7. Research methodology: Good legal research follows a method: define the question, identify the issues and facts, plan sources, search systematically, update, record the trail, and know when to stop.
  8. Case analysis: Case analysis goes beyond reading a single judgment.
  9. Legal memoranda: A legal memorandum is an internal document analysing a legal question for a supervisor or colleague.
  10. Opinion writing: An opinion is a formal written advice, traditionally from counsel, on a client's legal position and prospects.
  11. Client letters: Client letters explain the law and options in a way the client understands and can act on.
  12. Legal drafting: Legal drafting produces documents that create rights and obligations: contracts, wills, deeds and statements of case.
  13. Negotiation: Negotiation is the most common way legal disputes and transactions are resolved.
  14. Advocacy: Advocacy is presenting a client's case persuasively to a court or tribunal.
  15. Interviewing clients: Client interviews gather facts, understand the client's goals and give initial advice.
  16. Taking instructions: Taking instructions means confirming who the client is, what they want, the scope of the work and the authority to act.
  17. Legal problem solving: Legal problem solving turns facts into legal answers and practical advice.
  18. Applying IRAC: IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) is a simple structure for answering legal problems.
  19. Legal argumentation: Legal argument persuades a decision-maker using different kinds of reasoning: text, purpose, precedent, principle and policy.
  20. Professional communication: Lawyers communicate constantly by email, letter, phone and in meetings.

School 17: Scotland and Northern Ireland

16 lessons

Scotland and Northern Ireland: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Three legal systems in one UK: The United Kingdom has three legal systems: England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland.
  2. Scots law: a mixed legal system: Scots law is a "mixed" or "hybrid" legal system, drawing on both the civilian (Roman law) tradition of continental Europe and the common law tradition shared with England.
  3. The Scottish courts: Scotland has its own hierarchy of civil and criminal courts, with distinct names, procedures and personnel.
  4. The Scottish Parliament and devolved law: The Scotland Act 1998 created the Scottish Parliament, which can make primary legislation (Acts of the Scottish Parliament) on all matters not reserved to Westminster.
  5. Scots contract law and promise: Scots contract law shares many principles with English law, but differs in important ways: it has no doctrine of consideration, it enforces unilateral promises, and its rules on formation have been reformed by statute.
  6. Delict: the Scots law of wrongs: Delict is the Scots law of civil wrongs, equivalent in function to the English law of tort.
  7. Scots criminal law and procedure: Scots criminal law is largely common law, defined by the courts and the institutional writer Hume, with growing statutory reform.
  8. Scots property law and land registration: Scots property law has its own concepts and terminology.
  9. Succession in Scotland: Scots succession law limits testamentary freedom more than English law: a surviving spouse, civil partner and children have "legal rights" to part of the moveable estate whatever the will says.
  10. Northern Ireland's legal system: Northern Ireland has a common law legal system closely related to England's, but with its own courts, legal professions, legislature and a substantial body of separate legislation.
  11. The Northern Ireland courts: Northern Ireland has its own court system, structured much like England and Wales, with the UK Supreme Court as the final appeal court.
  12. The Northern Ireland Assembly and devolved law: The Northern Ireland Assembly makes laws for Northern Ireland on transferred matters, under a power-sharing system established by the Northern Ireland Act 1998.
  13. Northern Ireland criminal law and procedure: Northern Ireland's criminal law and procedure are close to England's but contained in separate legislation, with some important differences in practice and structure.
  14. Northern Ireland land law and conveyancing: Northern Ireland land law shares English common law roots but retains older features and has its own registration system and conveyancing practice.
  15. Cross-border issues within the UK: Because the UK has three legal systems, lawyers must decide which courts have jurisdiction, which law applies and how judgments are enforced when a matter crosses internal borders.
  16. Qualifying and practising in Scotland and Northern Ireland: Each UK jurisdiction has its own legal professions and qualification routes.

School 18: Routes to Qualification

13 lessons

Routes to Qualification: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Academic study and professional qualification: Studying law and qualifying as a lawyer are different things.
  2. Becoming a solicitor in England and Wales: Since September 2021, the main route to qualifying as a solicitor is the Solicitors Qualifying Examination (SQE) route.
  3. The Solicitors Qualifying Examination: The SQE is a centralised assessment set by the SRA and delivered by its assessment provider.
  4. SQE1: structure and approach: SQE1 consists of two assessments, FLK1 and FLK2, each of 180 single-best-answer questions.
  5. SQE2: structure and approach: SQE2 assesses practical legal skills through 16 stations: written assessments and oral assessments, set in five practice areas.
  6. Qualifying work experience: Qualifying work experience (QWE) is two years' full-time (or equivalent) experience of providing legal services that gives the opportunity to develop the competences in the SRA's Statement of Solicitor Competence.
  7. Character and suitability: Before admission, the SRA must be satisfied that a candidate is of suitable character to be a solicitor.
  8. Becoming a barrister in England and Wales: Barristers qualify through three components set by the Bar Standards Board: the academic component, the vocational component and pupillage (work-based learning).
  9. The academic component for the Bar: The academic component gives the foundation of legal knowledge that every barrister needs.
  10. The vocational component for the Bar: The vocational component prepares students for practice at the Bar.
  11. Pupillage: Pupillage is the final, work-based stage of Bar training.
  12. Other legal careers: Law offers many careers beyond solicitor and barrister, each with its own route and, often, its own regulator.
  13. Answering single-best-answer questions: Single-best-answer (SBA) questions present a scenario and five options, all of which may look plausible.

School 19: SQE2 Skills Practice

15 lessons

SQE2 Skills Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. How the SQE2 stations work: Each SQE2 station gives you a short case file, a task and a strict time limit, and expects the work a competent newly qualified solicitor would produce.
  2. Client interview: a residential purchase: In this simulation you interview a client who is buying her first home.
  3. Attendance note: recording the interview: After an interview, the SQE2 asks you to write an attendance note or legal analysis recording the meeting and your advice.
  4. Client interview: a contract dispute: In this simulation you interview a business owner whose supplier has delivered faulty goods.
  5. Advocacy: an interim application: In this simulation you make a short oral application to a district judge for relief from sanctions, after your client missed a deadline.
  6. Advocacy: a bail application: In this simulation you apply for bail in the magistrates' court for a client charged with an either-way offence.
  7. Case and matter analysis: a business dispute: A case and matter analysis station gives you a file and asks for a written analysis for a supervising partner: the legal issues, the evidence, the options and a recommendation, with any conduct issues identified.
  8. Case and matter analysis: an estate: In this simulation you analyse a probate file and advise the partner on who inherits, the executor's next steps and a potential claim against the estate.
  9. Legal research: a limitation question: Legal research stations give you a question from a partner and a set of sources (some irrelevant), and ask for a research note with a clear answer, the authorities and how they apply.
  10. Legal research: a property question: This research simulation asks you to find and apply the law on a neighbour's claimed right of way over a client's land, using a supplied set of sources.
  11. Legal writing: a letter of advice: A legal writing station asks for a letter to a client or another party.
  12. Legal writing: a letter to the other side: Letters to the other side must be accurate, firm and professional.
  13. Legal drafting: a clause in a commercial agreement: Drafting stations ask you to draft or amend a legal document, such as a clause in a commercial agreement, so that it achieves the client's aims precisely, using clear and consistent language.
