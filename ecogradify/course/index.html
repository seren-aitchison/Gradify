Free Ecology and Environmental Science course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

EcoGradify's free Ecology and Environmental Science course runs from Foundations of Ecology to Sustainability and Environmental Policy: 198 lessons in 11 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for ecology and environment students.

Study material, not a qualification or professional advice. EcoGradify teaches ecology and environmental science. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by subject experts, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, your exam board's specification and official sources such as the IPCC, JNCC and DEFRA. Figures for populations, emissions and policy change over time: each is given with its year. Nothing here is an ecological survey, an environmental impact assessment or legal advice on planning or wildlife law; for real sites, protected species or development, consult a qualified ecologist (for example a CIEEM member) and the relevant statutory body (Natural England, NatureScot, Natural Resources Wales or DAERA). In the field, follow your organiser's risk assessment and the Countryside Code.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Ecology

16 lessons

Foundations of Ecology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is ecology?: Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their environment, and of what determines where organisms live and how many there are.
  2. Levels of ecological organisation: Ecology works at a ladder of levels, from the individual organism to the whole biosphere.
  3. Ecology as a science: hypotheses and scale: Ecologists test ideas by observation, experiment and models, in systems that are large, variable and slow.
  4. Ecology and environmental science: Environmental science combines ecology with geology, chemistry, physics and the social sciences to understand and manage human impacts on Earth's systems.
  5. Abiotic factors: Abiotic factors are the physical and chemical conditions that determine where organisms can live.
  6. Climate and the distribution of life: Climate, the long-term pattern of temperature and rainfall, is the main control on where major types of vegetation occur.
  7. Soils: Soil is a living mixture of weathered rock, organic matter, water, air and organisms, and it controls which plants can grow.
  8. Water and aquatic environments: Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface and has unusual properties that shape life in it.
  9. Tolerance ranges and limiting factors: Every species can survive, grow and reproduce only within a range of each environmental factor.
  10. Adaptation to the environment: Adaptations are inherited features that increase an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  11. Habitats and the ecological niche: A habitat is where an organism lives; its niche is its role there: the conditions it tolerates, the resources it uses and how it interacts with other species.
  12. Biotic interactions: an overview: Organisms affect each other in a small number of basic ways, classified by whether each partner gains, loses or is unaffected.
  13. Terrestrial biomes: Biomes are the major types of terrestrial ecosystem, each defined by its climate and dominant vegetation.
  14. Aquatic biomes: Aquatic biomes are defined by salinity, depth, light and water movement rather than by climate.
  15. Biogeography and the distribution of species: Biogeography explains why species are found where they are, at scales from continents to islands.
  16. Humans as an ecological force: People now alter ecosystems on a planetary scale, through land use, harvesting, pollution, species introductions and climate change.

School 2: Population Ecology

18 lessons

Population Ecology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is a population?: A population is a group of individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time and able to interbreed.
  2. Population size and density: Population size is how many individuals there are; density is how many there are per unit area or volume.
  3. Dispersion patterns: Dispersion is how individuals are spaced within a population's range.
  4. Estimating population size: mark–release–recapture: Mark–release–recapture estimates the size of a mobile animal population from the proportion of marked animals in a second sample.
  5. Births, deaths, immigration and emigration: Populations change through four processes: births and immigration add individuals; deaths and emigration remove them.
  6. Exponential growth: With unlimited resources, a population grows at a constant per capita rate, so it increases faster and faster.
  7. Logistic growth and carrying capacity: As a population grows, resources per individual fall and growth slows towards a ceiling, the carrying capacity.
  8. Density-dependent and density-independent factors: Some factors that affect birth and death rates get stronger as a population becomes denser; others act regardless of density.
  9. Population cycles and fluctuations: Some populations rise and fall in regular cycles; others fluctuate irregularly.
  10. Life tables and survivorship curves: Life tables record how many individuals survive and die at each age.
  11. Life-history strategies: r and K selection: A life-history strategy is how an organism allocates resources to growth, survival and reproduction over its life.
  12. Age structure: Age structure is the proportion of individuals in each age class.
  13. Metapopulations: A metapopulation is a set of local populations in separate habitat patches, linked by occasional dispersal.
  14. Human population growth: The human population took until about 1800 to reach one billion and passed eight billion in 2022.
  15. The demographic transition: The demographic transition model describes how countries move from high birth and death rates to low ones as they develop.
  16. Small populations and extinction risk: Small populations face extra risks that can drive them to extinction even when the original threat has gone.
  17. Harvesting and maximum sustainable yield: Harvesting fish, game or timber removes individuals from a population that can replace them.
  18. Population viability analysis: Population viability analysis (PVA) uses models to estimate the chance that a population will survive for a given time.

School 3: Communities and Species Interactions

18 lessons

Communities and Species Interactions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Intraspecific and interspecific competition: Competition occurs when organisms use the same limited resource and harm each other in the process.
  2. The competitive exclusion principle: The competitive exclusion principle states that two species competing for exactly the same limiting resources can't coexist indefinitely.
  3. Resource partitioning and niche differentiation: Species that coexist often divide up resources between them.
  4. Predation: Predation is the consumption of one organism by another.
  5. Predator–prey dynamics: Predator and prey populations affect each other and can cycle together.
  6. Herbivory and plant defences: Herbivores eat plants, and plants have evolved many defences in response.
  7. Parasitism and disease: Parasites live on or in a host, taking resources and usually harming it without killing it quickly.
  8. Mutualism: Mutualism is an interaction in which both species benefit.
  9. Commensalism and facilitation: Not all interactions involve both costs and benefits.
  10. What is an ecological community?: An ecological community is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
  11. Keystone species and trophic cascades: Some species have effects on their communities far greater than their abundance would suggest.
  12. Species richness and evenness: Biodiversity within a community has two parts: how many species there are and how evenly individuals are spread among them.
  13. Island biogeography: The theory of island biogeography explains why larger, closer islands hold more species.
  14. Ecological succession: Succession is the change in the species composition of a community over time.
  15. Primary and secondary succession: Succession can begin on bare ground with no soil or on land where soil remains after disturbance.
  16. Climax communities and plagioclimax: Succession tends towards a relatively stable climax community, but human activity often holds communities at earlier stages.
  17. Disturbance and the intermediate disturbance hypothesis: Disturbances such as storms, fire and grazing disrupt communities and reset succession.
  18. Invasive non-native species: Invasive non-native species are introduced outside their natural range and cause harm to ecosystems, the economy or health.

School 4: Ecosystems and Energy Flow

16 lessons

Ecosystems and Energy Flow: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is an ecosystem?: An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with its physical environment, linked by flows of energy and cycles of matter.
  2. Producers, consumers and decomposers: Organisms in an ecosystem can be grouped by how they obtain energy and nutrients.
  3. Food chains and food webs: Food chains show who eats whom, and food webs show the network of feeding links.
  4. Trophic levels: Trophic levels group organisms by their position in food chains.
  5. Photosynthesis and energy capture: Almost all energy in ecosystems is captured by photosynthesis.
  6. Gross and net primary productivity: Primary productivity measures the rate at which producers capture energy and build biomass.
  7. Secondary productivity: Secondary productivity is the rate at which consumers build new biomass.
  8. Efficiency of energy transfer: Only a fraction of energy at one trophic level reaches the next.
  9. Ecological pyramids: Ecological pyramids show how numbers, biomass or energy change across trophic levels.
  10. Decomposition and detritus food webs: Most of the energy fixed by plants is never eaten by herbivores but enters the detritus food web when organisms die.
  11. Soil ecosystems: Soils are among the most diverse and important ecosystems on Earth.
  12. Productivity across biomes: Net primary productivity varies enormously across the planet.
  13. Ocean productivity: The oceans produce about half of global NPP, but their productivity varies greatly.
  14. Ecosystem stability and resilience: Ecosystems vary in how well they withstand and recover from disturbance.
  15. Ecosystem services: Ecosystems provide benefits that people depend on, from food and clean water to climate regulation and inspiration.
  16. Agricultural ecosystems: Agricultural ecosystems, or agroecosystems, are designed by people to channel productivity into food.

School 5: Biogeochemical Cycles

14 lessons

Biogeochemical Cycles: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cycling of matter: Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the Earth; it moves between living things, rocks, water and air.
  2. The water cycle: The water cycle moves water between oceans, atmosphere, land and living things, driven by solar energy.
  3. The carbon cycle: Carbon moves between the atmosphere, living things, oceans and rocks over timescales from days to millions of years.
  4. Carbon stores and fluxes: Quantifying where carbon is stored and how fast it moves lets scientists track human disturbance.
  5. Peatlands, forests and blue carbon: Some ecosystems store very large amounts of carbon, and protecting them is part of climate policy.
  6. The nitrogen cycle: Nitrogen is essential for proteins and DNA, but most of it is locked up as unreactive N₂ gas.
  7. Nitrogen fixation: Nitrogen fixation turns unreactive atmospheric N₂ into ammonia that life can use.
  8. Human alteration of the nitrogen cycle: Humans now add more reactive nitrogen to the Earth than all natural land processes combined.
  9. The phosphorus cycle: Phosphorus is essential for DNA, ATP and cell membranes, yet it cycles very slowly and has no significant gas phase.
  10. Eutrophication: Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, leading to algal blooms and oxygen loss.
  11. The sulfur cycle and acid deposition: Sulfur cycles through rocks, oceans, organisms and the atmosphere.
  12. Nutrient limitation: Productivity is often limited by the nutrient in shortest supply.
  13. Planetary boundaries: The planetary boundaries framework identifies limits within which humanity can operate safely.
  14. Coupled cycles and global change: Biogeochemical cycles do not operate in isolation: changes in one alter the others.

School 6: Biodiversity and Evolution

18 lessons

Biodiversity and Evolution: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Defining biodiversity: Biodiversity is the variety of life at every level.
  2. Genetic, species and ecosystem diversity: Biodiversity exists at three linked levels.
  3. Species concepts: Defining what a species is turns out to be difficult.
  4. Classification and naming: Classification organises the diversity of life, and scientific names allow precise communication.
  5. Variation and natural selection: Natural selection is the main mechanism of adaptive evolution.
  6. Adaptation and fitness: Adaptations are traits shaped by natural selection that improve survival and reproduction.
  7. Speciation: Speciation is the formation of new species.
  8. Coevolution: Coevolution occurs when interacting species drive each other's evolution.
  9. The history of life and mass extinctions: Life has evolved over more than 3.5 billion years, punctuated by mass extinctions.
  10. The latitudinal diversity gradient: For most groups of organisms, species richness rises from the poles to the tropics.
  11. Biodiversity hotspots: Biodiversity hotspots are regions rich in endemic species and under severe threat.
  12. Endemism: Endemic species are found nowhere else.
  13. Measuring biodiversity: diversity indices: Diversity indices combine species richness and evenness into a single number.
  14. Biodiversity in the UK: The UK is one of the most nature-depleted countries in the world.
  15. The global state of biodiversity: Biodiversity is declining worldwide at unprecedented rates.
  16. Drivers of biodiversity loss: Human activities drive biodiversity loss through a small number of major pathways.
  17. Extinction risk and the IUCN Red List: The IUCN Red List is the world's most comprehensive assessment of extinction risk.
  18. Why biodiversity matters: People value biodiversity for many reasons, from practical benefits to moral and cultural values.

School 7: Conservation Biology

18 lessons

Conservation Biology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is conservation biology?: Conservation biology is the science of protecting biodiversity.
  2. Conservation values and ethics: Why should we conserve nature?
  3. Conservation genetics: Small populations lose genetic variation, which can threaten their survival.
  4. Minimum viable populations: How many individuals does a species need to survive?
  5. Protected areas: Protected areas are the cornerstone of conservation.
  6. UK protected sites: SSSIs, SACs and SPAs: The UK protects its most important wildlife sites through national and international designations.
  7. Habitat management: Many valuable habitats depend on continuing management to stop them changing.
  8. Habitat restoration: Restoration aims to return degraded ecosystems towards a healthier state.
  9. Rewilding: Rewilding aims to restore natural processes rather than manage for particular species.
  10. Species reintroductions: Reintroductions return species to places where they were lost.
  11. Wildlife corridors and landscape connectivity: Fragmented habitats leave wildlife isolated.
  12. Zoos and captive breeding: Some species survive only because of captive breeding.
  13. Seed banks and botanic gardens: Plants can be conserved as seeds, living collections and tissue cultures.
  14. Conservation on farmland: Farmland covers about 70% of the UK's land, so how it is farmed shapes much of its wildlife.
  15. Marine conservation: The oceans face overfishing, pollution, habitat damage and climate change.
  16. International conservation agreements: Biodiversity crosses borders, so conservation needs international cooperation.
  17. Evidence-based conservation: Conservation resources are limited, so actions should be chosen on the basis of evidence of what works.
  18. Community-based conservation: Conservation succeeds or fails with the people who live alongside wildlife.

School 8: Climate Change

21 lessons

Climate Change: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Weather and climate: Weather is what happens in the atmosphere from day to day; climate is the long-term pattern of weather.
  2. The atmosphere: Earth's atmosphere is a thin layer of gases that regulates temperature and redistributes heat.
  3. Earth's energy balance: Earth's temperature depends on the balance between energy arriving from the Sun and energy leaving as infrared radiation.
  4. The greenhouse effect: Greenhouse gases keep Earth habitable by trapping outgoing infrared radiation.
  5. Greenhouse gases: Different greenhouse gases come from different sources, last for different times and trap different amounts of heat.
  6. Radiative forcing: Radiative forcing measures how much a change, such as more CO2 or more aerosols, alters Earth's energy balance.
  7. The instrumental record: Thermometers, tide gauges, satellites and ocean floats provide direct measurements of a changing climate.
  8. Past climates and proxies: Natural archives such as ice cores, tree rings and sediments record climate long before thermometers.
  9. Attribution of climate change: Attribution is the science of identifying the causes of observed climate change.
  10. Climate feedbacks: Feedbacks amplify or dampen the warming caused by a forcing.
  11. Tipping points: Some parts of the climate system may change abruptly and irreversibly once a threshold is crossed.
  12. Climate models and scenarios: Climate models use physics to simulate the climate system and project how it will respond to future emissions.
  13. Sea-level rise: Global sea level is rising and will keep rising for centuries.
  14. Ocean warming and acidification: The oceans have absorbed most of the extra heat and a quarter or more of the carbon dioxide released by humans.
  15. Phenology and mismatch: Phenology is the timing of seasonal events such as flowering, breeding and migration.
  16. Range shifts: As climate warms, many species are moving towards the poles, uphill and into deeper water.
  17. Coral reefs under climate change: Coral reefs are among the most diverse and most threatened ecosystems on Earth.
  18. Climate change and extinction risk: Climate change is becoming a major driver of extinction alongside habitat loss, exploitation and invasive species.
  19. Mitigation: Mitigation means reducing the causes of climate change by cutting greenhouse gas emissions and increasing removals.
  20. Adaptation: Adaptation means adjusting to actual or expected climate change to reduce harm or exploit opportunities.
  21. Nature-based solutions: Nature-based solutions use the protection, management and restoration of ecosystems to tackle societal challenges such as climate change.

School 9: Field Methods and Statistics

23 lessons

Field Methods and Statistics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The scientific method in ecology: Ecologists answer questions about nature by observing, forming hypotheses, testing them and revising their ideas.
  2. Hypotheses and null hypotheses: Statistical tests in ecology start with a null hypothesis of no effect.
  3. Experimental and observational studies: Ecologists can either manipulate the system or observe natural variation.
  4. Replication and pseudoreplication: Replication lets ecologists distinguish real effects from natural variation.
  5. Fieldwork ethics and safety: Fieldwork must be safe for people and responsible towards wildlife, habitats and landowners.
  6. Random, systematic and stratified sampling: Ecologists rarely count every organism, so they take samples that should represent the whole area.
  7. Quadrats: Quadrats are frames used to sample plants and slow-moving animals in a defined area.
  8. Transects: Transects are lines along which samples are taken to show how organisms change across an environmental gradient.
  9. Measuring abundance: frequency, cover and ACFOR: Ecologists use several measures of abundance, from precise counts to quick abundance scales.
  10. Sampling animals: Mobile and hidden animals need special sampling methods.
  11. Freshwater sampling and biotic indices: Freshwater invertebrates are sampled to assess water quality, because different groups tolerate pollution differently.
  12. Measuring abiotic factors: Abiotic factors help explain where organisms live.
  13. Types of data and variables: The type of data collected determines how it can be presented and analysed.
  14. Descriptive statistics: Descriptive statistics summarise a set of data.
  15. Standard deviation and standard error: Standard deviation describes how spread out data are, and standard error describes how precisely a sample mean estimates the population mean.
  16. Choosing a statistical test: The right statistical test depends on the question, the type of data and its distribution.
  17. The chi-squared test: The chi-squared test compares observed frequencies with those expected under a null hypothesis.
  18. Spearman's rank correlation: Spearman's rank correlation tests whether two variables measured on the same samples tend to increase or decrease together.
  19. The t-test: The t-test compares the means of two groups of normally distributed data.
  20. The Mann–Whitney U test: The Mann–Whitney U test compares two independent samples without assuming normal distributions.
  21. Remote sensing and GIS: Satellites, aircraft and drones collect data on ecosystems across whole landscapes, and geographic information systems (GIS) analyse them.
  22. Environmental DNA: Organisms shed DNA into water, soil and air, and ecologists can detect species from these traces.
  23. Citizen science: Members of the public collect large amounts of ecological data through citizen science projects.

School 10: Resources and Pollution

20 lessons

Resources and Pollution: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Energy resources: Societies depend on energy for heat, transport, industry and electricity.
  2. Fossil fuels: Coal, oil and natural gas powered industrialisation but are the main cause of climate change.
  3. Nuclear power: Nuclear power provides large amounts of low-carbon electricity, but raises concerns about safety, waste and cost.
  4. Renewable energy: Renewable energy sources are expanding rapidly and now supply a large share of UK electricity.
  5. Energy efficiency and storage: Using less energy and storing it for when it is needed are as important as generating it cleanly.
  6. Soil degradation and conservation: Soil supports food production, stores carbon and hosts much of the world's biodiversity, yet it forms very slowly and is easily degraded.
  7. Water resources: Fresh water is essential for people, farming, industry and ecosystems, yet it is unevenly distributed and under growing pressure.
  8. Agriculture and food security: Feeding a growing world population without destroying the environment is one of the great challenges of this century.
  9. Fisheries: Fish are a vital source of food and livelihoods, but many stocks are overexploited.
  10. Forestry: Forests provide timber, fuel and many ecosystem services, but deforestation continues at a high rate.
  11. Mineral resources: Modern technology depends on minerals, from iron and copper to lithium and rare earth elements.
  12. Principles of pollution: Pollution is the addition of substances or energy to the environment at harmful levels.
  13. Air pollution: Air pollution is the largest environmental risk to human health and also harms ecosystems.
  14. Water pollution: Rivers, lakes and seas receive pollution from sewage, farming, industry and roads.
  15. Plastics and persistent pollutants: Plastics and some chemicals persist in the environment for decades or longer.
  16. Pesticides and bioaccumulation: Pesticides protect crops but can harm wildlife and people.
  17. Noise and light pollution: Not all pollution is chemical.
  18. Waste management: How we manage waste affects land, water, air and climate.
  19. Life-cycle assessment: To compare the environmental impacts of products fairly, we need to consider their whole lives.
  20. The circular economy: The circular economy aims to keep products and materials in use and design out waste.

School 11: Sustainability and Environmental Policy

16 lessons

Sustainability and Environmental Policy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is sustainability?: Sustainability is about meeting human needs without undermining the natural systems that future generations will depend on.
  2. Sustainable development and the SDGs: The Sustainable Development Goals are the world's shared framework for development to 2030.
  3. Ecological footprints: The ecological footprint estimates how much biologically productive land and water a population needs to supply its resources and absorb its carbon emissions.
  4. Environmental ethics: Environmental ethics asks what we owe to nature and to future generations.
  5. Market failure and externalities: Markets often fail to protect the environment because the costs of pollution and resource depletion fall on people who are not part of the transaction.
  6. Valuing nature: Many benefits of nature have no market price, so they are often ignored in decisions.
  7. Policy instruments: regulation, taxes and markets: Governments use a range of tools to correct environmental market failures.
  8. International environmental agreements: Many environmental problems cross borders and need international cooperation.
  9. UK environmental law: UK environmental protection rests on a body of Acts of Parliament, regulations and regulators.
  10. Environmental impact assessment: Environmental impact assessment is the process of predicting and evaluating the environmental effects of a proposed development before a decision is made.
  11. Biodiversity net gain: Biodiversity net gain requires most new developments in England to leave habitats in a measurably better state than before.
  12. The precautionary principle: The precautionary principle guides decisions when an activity may cause serious harm but the science is uncertain.
  13. Environmental justice: Environmental harms and benefits are not shared equally across society.
  14. Indigenous and local knowledge: Indigenous peoples and local communities hold detailed knowledge of ecosystems built up over generations.
  15. Behaviour change and the environment: Many environmental problems depend on everyday choices about energy, travel, food and waste.
  16. Monitoring progress: environmental indicators: Indicators turn complex environmental change into measurable signals that show whether policies are working.
