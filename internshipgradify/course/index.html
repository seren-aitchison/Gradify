Free Careers and Employability course: revision notes, flashcards and questions

InternshipGradify's free Careers and Employability course runs from Knowing Yourself to Career Planning and Resilience: 145 lessons in 11 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for students looking for work experience.

General careers guidance, not personal advice. InternshipGradify teaches careers and employability for UK students and graduates. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a qualified careers professional, so they may contain errors, and pay figures, dates and employer practices change; check them against Prospects, the National Careers Service, gov.uk and ACAS. Nothing here is legal, financial or immigration advice. For guidance on your own situation, book a session with your university or college careers service or the National Careers Service (0800 100 900, England).

Course overview

  • Study paths: Landing a graduate job, Winning an internship or placement, Starting work well, Working for yourself

Schools and lessons

School 1: Knowing Yourself

12 lessons

Knowing Yourself: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What career development is: Career development is the lifelong process of managing learning, work and life.
  2. Career theories: matching, development and chance: Career theories explain how people come to do the work they do.
  3. The Gatsby Benchmarks and careers support: The Gatsby Benchmarks define good careers guidance in English schools and colleges, and universities and the National Careers Service extend that support into adulthood.
  4. Identifying your skills: Employers recruit for skills as much as for qualifications.
  5. Transferable skills and graduate attributes: Universities describe the qualities they aim to develop in every student as graduate attributes, and employers publish the skills they look for.
  6. Values and what matters to you at work: Work values are the things you most want work to give you, such as security, creativity, helping others or high pay.
  7. Strengths and how to use them: A strength is something you are good at and energised by, not just something you can do.
  8. Interests and personality tools: uses and limits: Career quizzes, interest inventories and personality questionnaires are everywhere.
  9. Reflective practice and the skills audit: Reflection turns experience into learning you can talk about.
  10. Motivations and work-life priorities: Why you want to work shapes which jobs will satisfy you.
  11. Your career story and personal brand: Recruiters remember a clear, credible story better than a list of facts.
  12. Setting career goals: Goals turn self-knowledge into action.

School 2: Exploring Sectors and Roles

12 lessons

Exploring Sectors and Roles: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What labour market information is: Labour market information (LMI) is data and intelligence about jobs, pay, skills and trends.
  2. Using ONS and official labour market data: The Office for National Statistics publishes the UK's headline figures on employment, unemployment, vacancies and pay.
  3. Reading job profiles: Prospects and the National Careers Service: Job profiles summarise what a role involves, what it pays, how to get in and where it can lead.
  4. Skills shortages and growth areas: Some occupations can't find enough skilled people while others shrink.
  5. How sectors and industries are organised: Jobs sit inside industries and occupations, which are classified in official systems.
  6. Public, private and third sector employers: Employers fall into three broad sectors with different purposes, funding and cultures.
  7. Large employers, SMEs and start-ups: Most UK businesses are small, yet graduate recruitment advertising is dominated by large employers.
  8. Graduate destinations: what degrees lead to: What do graduates actually do after their degrees?
  9. Job titles, job families and role descriptions: Job titles are inconsistent: the same title can mean very different jobs and different titles can mean the same one.
  10. Researching an employer: Employers expect candidates to know who they are, what they do and why the candidate wants to join.
  11. Informational interviews: An informational interview is a short conversation with someone in a job or sector you're curious about, to learn rather than to ask for a job.
  12. Weighing options and making a career decision: Career decisions involve uncertainty, trade-offs and emotion.

School 3: Work Experience, Internships and Placements

12 lessons

Work Experience, Internships and Placements: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Work experience and work shadowing: Short work experience and work shadowing let you see a job from the inside before committing to it.
  2. Internships: An internship is a fixed period of work experience, usually during the summer, designed to give students or graduates real work and employers a chance to assess them.
  3. Placement years and sandwich degrees: A placement year, or sandwich year, is a year of paid work taken as part of a degree.
  4. Insight days and spring weeks: Insight days and spring weeks are short programmes for students early in their degrees, run mainly by large employers.
  5. Volunteering: Volunteering means giving time unpaid to help a cause or organisation.
  6. Part-time work and its value: Most students now have part-time jobs, often in retail, hospitality or care.
  7. Finding opportunities: advertised and speculative: Some opportunities are advertised on job boards; many others are never advertised and are filled through contacts and direct approaches.
  8. Unpaid internships and the National Minimum Wage: Whether an intern must be paid depends on their employment status, not the label on the role.
  9. Making the most of a placement: Getting a placement or internship is only the start: what you learn and achieve there determines its value.
  10. Virtual and remote experience: Since 2020, virtual work experience, remote internships and online job simulations have become a common part of early careers programmes.
  11. Reflecting on and recording experience: Experience only becomes useful evidence when you can remember and explain it.
  12. Turning experience into evidence: Recruiters assess evidence, not experience itself.

School 4: Networking and Your Professional Presence

12 lessons

Networking and Your Professional Presence: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What networking is and why it works: Networking means building and maintaining professional relationships that share information, advice and opportunities.
  2. Building a network from where you are: Everyone already has a network, even if it doesn't feel professional.
  3. Careers fairs and networking events: Careers fairs, employer presentations and networking events put you in the same room as recruiters and professionals.
  4. Speculative approaches and follow-up: Reaching out to someone you don't know, whether for advice, an introduction or an opportunity, is a core networking skill.
  5. Mentors and sponsors: A mentor gives you advice and support; a sponsor uses their influence to open doors for you.
  6. Alumni networks: Your university's alumni, its former students, are one of the largest and most willing networks you can join.
  7. Building a LinkedIn profile: LinkedIn is the most widely used professional networking platform, and recruiters use it to find and check candidates.
  8. Using LinkedIn to research and connect: LinkedIn is as useful for research as for being found.
  9. Content, engagement and visibility: Posting and commenting on LinkedIn and similar platforms can make you visible to people in your field.
  10. Managing your digital footprint: Your digital footprint is everything about you that can be found online.
  11. Professional email communication: Emails to employers, recruiters and contacts form part of how you are judged.
  12. Job scams and staying safe online: Fake job adverts and recruitment scams target students and graduates, aiming to steal money or personal data or to recruit "money mules".

School 5: CVs and Cover Letters

12 lessons

CVs and Cover Letters: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What a CV is for: A CV is a short marketing document that persuades an employer to invite you to interview.
  2. CV structure and sections: A well-structured CV makes information easy to find.
  3. Writing achievement statements: Strong CVs describe achievements, not just duties.
  4. Tailoring a CV to a role: A tailored CV is far more effective than one sent unchanged to every employer.
  5. CV formats: chronological and skills-based: Most CVs use a chronological format, but a skills-based format can work better in some situations.
  6. Applicant tracking systems and keywords: Many employers use software to manage and filter applications.
  7. Common CV mistakes: Small errors can stop an otherwise strong CV from being shortlisted.
  8. Academic and international CVs: Academic posts and jobs abroad often need a different kind of CV.
  9. The purpose of a cover letter: A cover letter introduces your CV and explains why you want this role and why you are a good fit.
  10. Structuring a cover letter: A clear structure makes a cover letter easy to read and persuasive.
  11. Showing motivation and fit: Employers want candidates who genuinely want the role and fit the organisation.
  12. Speculative letters: Many jobs are never advertised.

School 6: Applications and Psychometric Tests

15 lessons

Applications and Psychometric Tests: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. How application forms work: Most large graduate employers, the public sector and the NHS use online application forms rather than CVs.
  2. Competency-based questions: Competency-based questions ask for a specific example of when you showed a skill or behaviour.
  3. The STAR method: STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard way to structure answers to competency questions.
  4. Strengths-based questions: Strengths-based questions explore what you enjoy and do naturally, rather than what you've done.
  5. Motivational questions: "Why do you want this job?" and "Why us?" appear on almost every application.
  6. Supporting and personal statements: Many public sector, NHS, education and charity applications ask for a supporting statement that shows how you meet the person specification.
  7. Civil Service Success Profiles: The UK Civil Service assesses candidates using Success Profiles, a framework of five elements including nine behaviours.
  8. What psychometric tests measure: Psychometric tests are standardised assessments of ability, aptitude or personality used in selection.
  9. Numerical reasoning tests: Numerical reasoning tests check how well you interpret data in tables and charts and calculate under time pressure.
  10. Verbal reasoning tests: Verbal reasoning tests check how accurately you understand and draw conclusions from written passages.
  11. Logical and inductive reasoning tests: Logical, inductive and diagrammatic reasoning tests assess how you spot patterns and rules in abstract shapes or sequences.
  12. Situational judgement tests: Situational judgement tests (SJTs) present realistic work scenarios and ask how you would or should respond.
  13. Personality questionnaires: Many employers use personality questionnaires to understand candidates' preferred ways of working.
  14. Game-based assessments: Some employers now replace traditional tests with short online games that measure cognitive abilities and behavioural traits.
  15. Reasonable adjustments in assessment: Disabled candidates have a legal right to reasonable adjustments in recruitment, including tests and interviews.

School 7: Interviews and Assessment Centres

15 lessons

Interviews and Assessment Centres: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Interview formats: Interviews range from short phone screens to panel interviews and multiple mini interviews.
  2. Preparing for an interview: Good interview performance rests on preparation.
  3. Answering competency and strengths questions: In interviews, competency and strengths questions are spoken, probed and followed up, which changes how you answer them.
  4. Telephone and live video interviews: Many first-round and some final interviews now happen by phone or video call.
  5. Pre-recorded video interviews: Pre-recorded or asynchronous video interviews ask you to record answers to on-screen questions with no live interviewer.
  6. Case interviews: Case interviews ask you to work through a business problem out loud with the interviewer.
  7. Technical interviews: Technical interviews test the specialist knowledge and problem-solving a role needs, from coding to engineering principles and finance.
  8. Questions to ask the interviewer: Most interviews end with "Do you have any questions for us?" This lesson explains why the question matters, what makes a good question and which questions to avoid.
  9. Handling nerves and difficult questions: Some nervousness before interviews is normal and can even help.
  10. What happens at an assessment centre: An assessment centre brings several selection exercises together over half a day or more, in person or online.
  11. Group exercises: Group exercises ask several candidates to discuss a problem or complete a task together while assessors watch.
  12. Presentations: Many assessment centres and interviews include a short presentation, either prepared in advance or on the day.
  13. In-tray and e-tray exercises: In-tray and e-tray exercises simulate a busy inbox of emails, documents and requests.
  14. Role-plays: Role-play exercises ask you to handle a realistic work conversation with an actor or assessor, such as a customer complaint or a difficult colleague.
  15. Feedback and learning from rejection: Rejection is a normal part of applying for jobs and courses, even for strong candidates.

School 8: Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes

12 lessons

Graduate Schemes, Apprenticeships and Other Routes: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The graduate recruitment market: The graduate recruitment market is the set of employers, jobs and routes open to people finishing degrees.
  2. Graduate schemes: Graduate schemes are structured training programmes for new graduates, mostly at large employers.
  3. Graduate jobs outside schemes: Most graduates start in direct-entry jobs rather than formal schemes.
  4. The recruitment calendar: Many UK opportunities follow an annual cycle, with deadlines months before the start date.
  5. Offers, negotiation and accepting a job: Receiving an offer is the start of a new set of decisions.
  6. Degree and higher apprenticeships: Degree and higher apprenticeships combine paid work with study for a higher-level qualification.
  7. School leaver programmes: School leaver programmes offer paid training with large employers straight after A levels or equivalent.
  8. Postgraduate study as a career step: Postgraduate study can open doors, but it is a significant investment of time and money.
  9. Working abroad and visas: Working abroad can build skills and broaden your career, but it depends on having the right permission to work.
  10. Gap years and career breaks: A planned gap year or career break can build skills, confidence and clarity.
  11. Creative and portfolio careers: Many people in the creative industries and beyond build careers from freelance work, projects and several part-time roles.
  12. Public service and fast-track routes: The public and third sectors offer structured fast-track programmes for graduates who want to make a difference.

School 9: Starting Work: Contracts and Rights

13 lessons

Starting Work: Contracts and Rights: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Employment status: employee, worker and self-employed: Your employment rights in the UK depend on your employment status.
  2. The written statement of employment particulars: Employees and workers in the UK are entitled to a written statement of their main terms of employment from day one.
  3. Pay, the minimum wage and payslips: Knowing how your pay is set and recorded helps you spot mistakes and protect your rights.
  4. Tax, National Insurance and student loan repayments: Your first full-time job usually brings Income Tax, National Insurance and, for graduates, student loan repayments.
  5. Pensions and auto-enrolment: Most employees are automatically enrolled into a workplace pension.
  6. Working hours and holiday: The Working Time Regulations 1998 set limits on working hours and give workers rights to rest breaks and paid holiday.
  7. Probation periods: Many jobs begin with a probation period during which your employer assesses whether the role is working out.
  8. Equality and discrimination at work: The Equality Act 2010 protects workers from discrimination, harassment and victimisation.
  9. Disability, disclosure and reasonable adjustments: Disabled employees have a right to reasonable adjustments at work, and choosing whether and how to disclose a condition is a personal decision.
  10. Flexible working: Employees in the UK can request changes to when, where and how much they work.
  11. Raising a grievance: A grievance is a concern or complaint raised with your employer.
  12. Your first 90 days: The first three months in a new job shape how colleagues see you and how quickly you become effective.
  13. Workplace wellbeing: Looking after your wellbeing helps you perform well and enjoy work.

School 10: Marketing

18 lessons

Marketing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is marketing?: Marketing is the process of understanding customers and creating value for them profitably.
  2. Market research: Market research gives businesses the information they need to understand customers and markets.
  3. Segmentation, targeting and positioning: Few products appeal to everyone.
  4. Consumer behaviour: Understanding why and how people buy helps businesses design and market their products.
  5. Business-to-business marketing: Business-to-business (B2B) marketing sells to organisations rather than individual consumers.
  6. Product and the product life cycle: The product is the core of the marketing mix.
  7. Pricing strategies: Price is the only element of the marketing mix that directly generates revenue.
  8. Promotion and marketing communications: Promotion communicates with customers to inform, persuade and remind them.
  9. Place and distribution channels: Place is about getting products to customers where and when they want them.
  10. The extended marketing mix: Services and customer experience need more than the four Ps.
  11. Branding and brand equity: Brands identify products and carry meaning that adds value.
  12. Customer relationship management: Keeping customers is usually cheaper than winning new ones.
  13. Services marketing: Services differ from physical goods in ways that shape how they are marketed.
  14. Digital and social media marketing: Digital channels have transformed how businesses reach and engage customers.
  15. Marketing metrics: Marketers must show that their spending creates value.
  16. Sustainable and ethical marketing: Marketing shapes consumption, so it carries responsibilities to society and the environment.
  17. International marketing: Selling across borders brings opportunities and challenges.
  18. The marketing plan: A marketing plan turns analysis into action.

School 11: Career Planning and Resilience

12 lessons

Career Planning and Resilience: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Career planning models: Career planning models give a structure for thinking about where you are and where you want to go.
  2. Career management skills: Career management skills are the abilities that let you manage your own learning and work throughout life.
  3. Action planning: An action plan turns career goals into steps you actually take.
  4. Lifelong learning and CPD: Learning continues long after education ends.
  5. Changing career: Many people change career at least once.
  6. The future of work and automation: Automation and artificial intelligence are changing which tasks people do at work.
  7. Career resilience: Career resilience is the capacity to adapt to change and recover from setbacks at work.
  8. Dealing with rejection: Rejection hurts, and how you interpret it affects your motivation to keep going.
  9. Imposter feelings: Many capable people feel like frauds who will be found out, especially in new roles.
  10. Uncertainty and planned happenstance: Careers are shaped by chance as well as planning.
  11. Career wellbeing and burnout: Burnout is a response to chronic workplace stress that has not been managed.
  12. Getting careers guidance: Professional careers guidance is available throughout life, often for free.
