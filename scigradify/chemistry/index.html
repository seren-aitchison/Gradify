Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Chemistry

25 lessons

Foundations of Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is chemistry?: Chemistry is the study of matter: what substances are made of, how their particles are arranged and bonded, and how substances change into new ones in chemical reactions.
  2. Matter and its classification: All matter can be classified as a pure substance (an element or a compound) or a mixture.
  3. Elements and compounds: There are 118 known elements, but millions of compounds.
  4. Mixtures: Mixtures can be separated by physical methods that exploit differences in the properties of their parts: particle size, solubility, boiling point or how strongly they stick to a surface.
  5. Physical and chemical changes: In a physical change the same substance remains, perhaps in a different state or shape; in a chemical change new substances form.
  6. States of matter: Substances exist as solids, liquids or gases depending on the balance between the energy of their particles and the forces between them.
  7. Atomic theory: Everything is made of atoms.
  8. Chemical symbols: Each element has a one- or two-letter symbol, agreed internationally by IUPAC.
  9. Chemical formulae: A chemical formula shows which elements are in a substance and in what ratio.
  10. Chemical equations: A chemical equation summarises a reaction: reactants on the left, products on the right, with formulae, state symbols and the numbers of each particle.
  11. Balancing equations: Balancing makes the number of atoms of each element the same on both sides of an equation, reflecting the conservation of mass.
  12. Relative atomic mass: Atoms are far too small to weigh individually in grams, so chemists compare their masses with carbon-12.
  13. Relative molecular mass: The relative molecular (or formula) mass of a substance is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in its formula.
  14. The mole: The mole is the chemist's counting unit.
  15. Avogadro's constant: The Avogadro constant, 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, connects the atomic scale with the laboratory scale.
  16. Molar mass: Molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol.
  17. Stoichiometry: Stoichiometry is the calculation of the amounts of substances that react and form, using the mole ratios in a balanced equation.
  18. Limiting reagents: In most reactions one reactant runs out first: the limiting reagent.
  19. Percentage yield: The actual amount of product is almost always less than the theoretical maximum.
  20. Atom economy: Atom economy measures how much of the mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.
  21. Concentration: The concentration of a solution tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume.
  22. Dilutions: Diluting a solution adds solvent but doesn't change the amount of solute.
  23. Significant figures: The number of significant figures in a result shows how precisely it is known.
  24. Scientific notation: Chemistry spans numbers from 10²³ particles in a mole to 10⁻¹⁰ m across an atom.
  25. Units and measurements: Every measurement in chemistry needs a number, a unit and an uncertainty.

School 2: Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

20 lessons

Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Structure of the atom: An atom is a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by a much larger cloud of electrons.
  2. Protons, neutrons and electrons: Atoms are built from three particles with very different masses and charges.
  3. Isotopes: Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
  4. Atomic number and mass number: Two numbers identify any nucleus: the atomic number, which fixes the element, and the mass number, which identifies the isotope.
  5. Electron shells: Electrons in atoms occupy shells at increasing distances and energies from the nucleus.
  6. Atomic orbitals: Within each shell, electrons occupy orbitals: regions where an electron is likely to be found.
  7. Quantum numbers: Four quantum numbers describe each electron in an atom: which shell, which type of orbital, which orientation and which spin.
  8. Electron configurations: An electron configuration lists the subshells an atom's electrons occupy and how many are in each, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ for sodium.
  9. Aufbau principle: The aufbau ("building up") principle says electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals available first.
  10. Pauli exclusion principle: The Pauli exclusion principle says no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers.
  11. Hund's rule: Hund's rule says that when electrons fill a set of orbitals of equal energy, they occupy them singly first, with parallel spins, before pairing up.
  12. Ionisation energy: Ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove electrons from atoms.
  13. Electron affinity: Electron affinity describes the energy change when atoms gain electrons.
  14. Atomic spectra: When atoms are given energy, they emit light at only certain wavelengths, producing a line spectrum unique to each element.
  15. Emission and absorption: Atoms emit light at particular wavelengths when excited electrons fall back, and absorb the same wavelengths when electrons are lifted up.
  16. Wave-particle duality: Light and electrons both behave sometimes like waves and sometimes like particles.
  17. Quantum mechanical model: The quantum mechanical model treats the electron in an atom as a wave described by the Schrödinger equation.
  18. Atomic orbitals and shapes: Each type of orbital has a characteristic shape: s orbitals are spherical, p orbitals have two lobes, and d orbitals mostly have four.
  19. Periodic trends: Atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity change in regular ways across periods and down groups.
  20. Electronic structure and chemical properties: An element's chemistry follows from its electron structure.

School 3: Periodic Table and Periodicity

25 lessons

Periodic Table and Periodicity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Development of the periodic table: The periodic table grew from attempts to organise the elements by their properties.
  2. Modern periodic law: The modern periodic table is ordered by atomic number.
  3. Periods and groups: The periodic table is arranged in rows called periods and columns called groups.
  4. s-block elements: The s-block contains the reactive metals of groups 1 and 2.
  5. p-block elements: The p-block contains metals, metalloids and non-metals.
  6. d-block elements: The d-block contains the transition elements of groups 3–12.
  7. f-block elements: The f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides.
  8. Group 1: Group 1, the alkali metals, are soft and very reactive.
  9. Group 2: Group 2, the alkaline earth metals, are reactive metals forming 2+ ions.
  10. Group 13: Group 13 runs from the metalloid boron to metals such as aluminium.
  11. Group 14: Group 14 shows the clearest change from non-metal to metal.
  12. Group 15: Group 15 includes nitrogen and phosphorus, essential for life.
  13. Group 16: Group 16, the chalcogens, includes oxygen and sulfur.
  14. Group 17: Group 17, the halogens, are reactive non-metals.
  15. Group 18: Group 18, the noble gases, are very unreactive.
  16. Transition metals: Transition metals show characteristic properties linked to their d electrons.
  17. Lanthanides: The lanthanides are fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium.
  18. Actinides: The actinides are radioactive elements from actinium to lawrencium.
  19. Atomic radius: Atomic radius describes the size of atoms.
  20. Ionic radius: Ions differ in size from their atoms.
  21. Ionisation energy trends: Ionisation energies reveal electron structure and follow clear patterns.
  22. Electronegativity: Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts bonding electrons.
  23. Reactivity trends: Reactivity varies predictably across the periodic table.
  24. Oxidation states: Oxidation states track electrons in compounds.
  25. Periodic relationships: The periodic table reveals relationships beyond simple group and period trends.

School 4: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

26 lessons

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to chemical bonding: Chemical bonds hold atoms together in elements and compounds.
  2. Ionic bonding: Ionic bonds form when electrons transfer from metal to non-metal atoms.
  3. Covalent bonding: Covalent bonds form when atoms share pairs of electrons.
  4. Coordinate bonding: In a coordinate bond, both shared electrons come from one atom.
  5. Metallic bonding: Metallic bonding holds metal atoms together with a sea of delocalised electrons.
  6. Lewis structures: Lewis structures show how valence electrons are arranged in molecules.
  7. Formal charge: Formal charge helps decide which Lewis structure best represents a molecule.
  8. Resonance: Some molecules cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure.
  9. VSEPR theory: VSEPR theory predicts molecular shapes from electron pair repulsion.
  10. Molecular geometry: Molecular shapes follow from the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  11. Bond polarity: Bonds between atoms of different electronegativity are polar.
  12. Molecular polarity: A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel.
  13. Electronegativity: This lesson applies electronegativity to bonding: predicting bond character, comparing scales and explaining the bonding continuum.
  14. Intermolecular forces: Intermolecular forces act between molecules and determine many physical properties.
  15. London dispersion forces: London dispersion forces arise from temporary dipoles in all molecules.
  16. Dipole-dipole interactions: Polar molecules attract each other through permanent dipoles.
  17. Hydrogen bonding: Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces in many substances.
  18. Bond enthalpy: Bond enthalpy measures the energy needed to break a bond.
  19. Lattice enthalpy: Lattice enthalpy measures the strength of ionic bonding.
  20. Born-Haber cycles: Born-Haber cycles use Hess's law to find lattice enthalpies.
  21. Molecular orbital theory: Molecular orbital theory describes electrons spread over whole molecules.
  22. Hybridisation: Hybridisation explains bonding geometry by mixing atomic orbitals.
  23. Sigma and pi bonding: Covalent bonds can be sigma or pi bonds, formed by different orbital overlaps.
  24. Delocalisation: Delocalised electrons spread over several atoms, changing structure and properties.
  25. Crystal structures: Solids are built from repeating arrangements of particles.
  26. Solid-state bonding: Solids are classified by the bonding between their particles.

School 5: States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

22 lessons

States of Matter and Physical Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Gases: Gases have properties that differ sharply from liquids and solids.
  2. Gas laws: The gas laws describe how pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are related.
  3. Ideal gas equation: The ideal gas equation links pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.
  4. Real gases: Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour under some conditions.
  5. Kinetic molecular theory: Kinetic molecular theory explains gas behaviour from the motion of particles.
  6. Liquids: Liquids have particles close together but free to move.
  7. Vapour pressure: Vapour pressure is the pressure of a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid.
  8. Boiling and melting: Melting and boiling are changes of state that require energy.
  9. Phase diagrams: Phase diagrams show which state a substance is in at any temperature and pressure.
  10. Solids: Solids have fixed shapes because their particles are held in place.
  11. Crystalline solids: Crystalline solids have long-range order that gives them sharp melting points and flat faces.
  12. Amorphous solids: Amorphous solids lack long-range order.
  13. Solutions: A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute in a solvent.
  14. Solubility: Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent.
  15. Concentration: This lesson extends concentration beyond mol dm⁻³ to other units used in physical chemistry and everyday life.
  16. Colligative properties: Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their identity.
  17. Osmosis: Osmosis is the movement of solvent through a semipermeable membrane.
  18. Diffusion: Diffusion is the spreading of particles from high to low concentration.
  19. Surface tension: Surface tension makes liquid surfaces behave like a stretched skin.
  20. Viscosity: Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flow.
  21. Intermolecular interactions: This lesson compares all the interactions between particles and uses them to predict and explain physical properties.
  22. Materials properties: The properties of materials come from their structure and bonding.

School 6: Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

20 lessons

Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Energy in chemical systems: Chemical reactions involve energy changes between a system and its surroundings.
  2. Exothermic reactions: Exothermic reactions release energy to their surroundings.
  3. Endothermic reactions: Endothermic reactions take in energy from their surroundings.
  4. Enthalpy: Enthalpy is the heat content of a system at constant pressure.
  5. Standard enthalpy changes: Standard conditions allow enthalpy changes to be compared.
  6. Enthalpy of formation: The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change for making a compound from its elements.
  7. Enthalpy of combustion: Enthalpy of combustion measures the energy released when a substance burns completely.
  8. Enthalpy of neutralisation: Neutralisation releases energy as acid and alkali react.
  9. Hess's law: Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route.
  10. Bond enthalpies: This lesson compares enthalpy changes calculated from bond enthalpies with those from Hess's law and explains the differences.
  11. Calorimetry: Calorimetry measures energy changes by recording temperature changes.
  12. Entropy: Entropy measures the dispersal of energy and matter in a system.
  13. Gibbs free energy: Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy to predict whether reactions are feasible.
  14. Spontaneity: A spontaneous process occurs without continuous outside help.
  15. Thermodynamic equilibrium: At equilibrium, ΔG is zero and there is no driving force for change.
  16. First law of thermodynamics: The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved.
  17. Second law of thermodynamics: The second law states that the total entropy of the universe increases in spontaneous processes.
  18. Chemical potential: Chemical potential describes how Gibbs free energy changes when a substance is added.
  19. Temperature dependence: Temperature changes the balance between enthalpy and entropy.
  20. Thermodynamic cycles: Thermodynamic cycles combine energy terms to find quantities that cannot be measured directly.

School 7: Chemical Kinetics

22 lessons

Chemical Kinetics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to reaction rates: Reaction rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products form.
  2. Collision theory: Collision theory explains why reactions happen and what changes their rates.
  3. Activation energy: Activation energy is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur.
  4. Reaction profiles: Reaction profiles show how energy changes as a reaction proceeds.
  5. Catalysts: Catalysts speed up reactions without being used up.
  6. Homogeneous catalysis: Homogeneous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants.
  7. Heterogeneous catalysis: Heterogeneous catalysts are in a different phase from the reactants.
  8. Enzyme catalysis: Enzymes are biological catalysts with remarkable speed and selectivity.
  9. Rate equations: Rate equations show how rate depends on the concentrations of reactants.
  10. Reaction order: Reaction orders can be found from initial rates or from graphs.
  11. Zero-order reactions: In a zero-order reaction, rate does not depend on concentration.
  12. First-order reactions: In a first-order reaction, rate is proportional to concentration.
  13. Second-order reactions: In a second-order reaction, rate depends on the square of a concentration or on two concentrations.
  14. Rate constants: The rate constant links rate to concentration and depends on temperature.
  15. Half-life: Half-life is the time for a concentration to fall to half its value.
  16. Integrated rate laws: Integrated rate laws give concentration as a function of time.
  17. Arrhenius equation: The Arrhenius equation links the rate constant to temperature and activation energy.
  18. Temperature effects: Temperature has a strong effect on reaction rates.
  19. Reaction mechanisms: A reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps by which reactants become products.
  20. Rate-determining steps: The slowest step in a mechanism controls the overall rate.
  21. Catalytic cycles: Catalytic cycles show how catalysts are used and regenerated in repeating loops.
  22. Experimental kinetics: Kinetics experiments require careful planning and analysis.

School 8: Chemical Equilibrium

15 lessons

Chemical Equilibrium: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Reversible reactions: Many reactions can go in both directions.
  2. Dynamic equilibrium: At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates.
  3. Equilibrium constants: The equilibrium constant expresses the position of equilibrium as a number.
  4. Kc: Kc is the equilibrium constant in terms of concentrations.
  5. Kp: Kp is the equilibrium constant for gases in terms of partial pressures.
  6. Reaction quotients: The reaction quotient Q describes a mixture that may not be at equilibrium.
  7. Le Chatelier's principle: Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to change.
  8. Temperature and equilibrium: Temperature is the only factor that changes the equilibrium constant.
  9. Pressure and equilibrium: Pressure changes affect equilibria involving gases.
  10. Concentration and equilibrium: Changing concentrations shifts the position of equilibrium.
  11. Heterogeneous equilibria: Heterogeneous equilibria involve more than one phase.
  12. Equilibrium calculations: Equilibrium calculations find unknown concentrations or constants.
  13. Industrial equilibrium: Industry chooses conditions to make equilibrium reactions economical.
  14. Coupled equilibria: Coupled equilibria share species, so a change in one affects the other.
  15. Thermodynamic interpretation: Thermodynamics explains why equilibrium positions are where they are.

School 9: Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

22 lessons

Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to acids and bases: Acids and bases are among the most important classes of substance in chemistry.
  2. Arrhenius theory: The Arrhenius theory defined acids and bases by the ions they produce in water.
  3. Brønsted-Lowry theory: The Brønsted-Lowry theory defines acids and bases by proton transfer.
  4. Lewis acid-base theory: Lewis theory defines acids and bases by electron pairs, covering even more reactions.
  5. Strong acids and bases: Strong acids and bases ionise completely in water.
  6. Weak acids and bases: Weak acids and bases only partially ionise in water.
  7. pH: pH measures the acidity of a solution on a logarithmic scale.
  8. pOH: pOH and the ionic product of water connect acidity and basicity.
  9. Acid dissociation constants: The acid dissociation constant Ka measures the strength of a weak acid.
  10. Base dissociation constants: The base dissociation constant Kb measures the strength of a weak base.
  11. Ka and Kb: Ka and Kb of a conjugate pair are linked through Kw.
  12. Buffers: Buffers resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.
  13. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation calculates buffer pH.
  14. Acid-base titrations: Titrations measure the concentration of an acid or base accurately.
  15. Indicators: Indicators change colour over a pH range.
  16. Equivalence points: The equivalence point is reached when reactants have reacted in exact stoichiometric amounts.
  17. Solubility equilibria: Sparingly soluble salts set up equilibria between solid and dissolved ions.
  18. Ksp: The solubility product Ksp quantifies the solubility of sparingly soluble salts.
  19. Common-ion effect: Adding an ion already present in an equilibrium reduces solubility or ionisation.
  20. Complex ion equilibria: Metal ions form complexes with ligands in equilibrium.
  21. Amphoteric substances: Amphoteric substances can act as acids or bases.
  22. Polyprotic acids: Polyprotic acids can donate more than one proton.

Redox and Electrochemistry: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Oxidation and reduction: Oxidation and reduction always happen together in redox reactions.
  2. Oxidation states: This lesson uses oxidation states to analyse redox reactions, including disproportionation and complex compounds.
  3. Redox equations: Redox reactions can be split into half-equations showing electron transfer.
  4. Balancing redox reactions: Redox reactions in acidic or alkaline solution need water and H⁺ or OH⁻ to balance.

  6. Reducing agents: Reducing agents cause reduction by donating electrons.
  7. Electrochemical cells: Electrochemical cells interconvert chemical and electrical energy.
  8. Galvanic cells: Galvanic cells generate electricity from spontaneous redox reactions.
  9. Electrolytic cells: Electrolytic cells use electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions.
  10. Standard electrode potentials: Standard electrode potentials measure the tendency of half-cells to be reduced.
  11. Cell potentials: The cell potential shows how strongly a cell drives electrons.
  12. Electrochemical series: The electrochemical series ranks half-cells by their standard electrode potentials.
  13. Nernst equation: The Nernst equation shows how electrode potentials change with concentration.
  14. Electrolysis: Electrolysis uses electricity to decompose compounds.
  15. Faraday's laws: Faraday's laws relate the amount of product in electrolysis to the charge passed.
  16. Batteries: Batteries are galvanic cells that store chemical energy for later use.
  17. Fuel cells: Fuel cells generate electricity continuously from a fuel and oxygen.
  18. Corrosion: Corrosion is the electrochemical deterioration of metals.
  19. Corrosion prevention: Corrosion can be slowed or prevented by several methods.
  20. Industrial electrochemistry: Electrochemistry underpins many large-scale industries.
