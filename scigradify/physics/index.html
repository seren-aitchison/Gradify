Units and lessons

Unit 1: Foundations of Physics

19 lessons

Foundations of Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Scope and aims of physics: Physics is the study of matter, energy, space and time, and of the laws that connect them.
  2. Physical quantities and observables: A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number times a unit.
  3. SI base and derived units: The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units by fixing exact values of seven constants of nature.
  4. Dimensional analysis: Every physical quantity has dimensions: powers of the base dimensions mass [M], length [L], time [T], current [I], temperature [Θ], amount [N] and luminous intensity [J].
  5. Unit conversions and prefixes: SI prefixes scale units by powers of ten; conversions multiply by factors equal to one.
  6. Scalar and vector quantities: A scalar has magnitude only; a vector has magnitude and direction and adds by the parallelogram rule.
  7. Significant figures and scientific notation: Significant figures carry the precision of a measurement; scientific notation (a × 10ⁿ, 1 ≤ a < 10) makes size and precision explicit.
  8. Estimation and order-of-magnitude calculations: An order-of-magnitude estimate gets the answer to within a factor of about 10 using rough inputs.
  9. Physical constants: Physical constants are quantities that appear in the laws of nature and have the same value everywhere.
  10. Mathematical modelling: A mathematical model represents a physical system with variables and equations so its behaviour can be predicted.
  11. Idealisation and simplifying assumptions: Idealisation replaces a messy real system with a simpler one that keeps the essential physics: point masses, frictionless surfaces, ideal gases, thin lenses.
  12. Deterministic and probabilistic models: A deterministic model gives one future from a given present; a probabilistic model gives a distribution of possible outcomes.
  13. Conservation laws: A conservation law states that some quantity of an isolated system stays constant however the system changes.
  14. Symmetry and invariance: A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the laws of physics unchanged.
  15. Physical laws, principles and theories: Physicists use these words for different things.
  16. Classical and modern physics: Classical physics (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism before 1900) works superbly for everyday scales.
  17. Microscopic and macroscopic descriptions: A macroscopic description uses a few bulk variables (pressure, temperature, volume); a microscopic one tracks the constituents (molecules, electrons).
  18. Uncertainty and measurement limitations: No measurement is exact.
  19. Scientific reasoning and falsifiability: Science advances by proposing hypotheses that make risky predictions and testing them against observation.

Unit 2: Mathematics for Physics

34 lessons

Mathematics for Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Algebraic manipulation: Rearranging equations is the most-used skill in physics.
  2. Equations and inequalities: An equation states that two expressions are equal; an inequality states that one is bigger.
  3. Simultaneous equations: When several unknowns are linked by several equations, solve them together.
  4. Trigonometry and circular functions: Sine, cosine and tangent relate angles to ratios of sides, and as functions of a continuous angle they describe every oscillation and wave.
  5. Exponential and logarithmic functions: Exponentials describe anything whose rate of change is proportional to its size: radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, population growth, absorption.
  6. Complex numbers: A complex number z = x + iy, with i² = −1, is a point in a plane.
  7. Vectors and vector decomposition: Vectors are added, scaled and multiplied using components.
  8. Matrices and linear algebra: A matrix is a rectangular array that represents a linear map: rotation, scaling, a system of equations, a set of coupled oscillators, a quantum operator.
  9. Coordinate systems: A coordinate system assigns numbers to points in space.
  10. Cartesian, polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates: Cartesian coordinates suit boxes and straight-line motion; polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates suit circles, wires and spheres.
  11. Differentiation: The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change: the gradient of a graph at a point.
  12. Integration: Integration adds up infinitely many small pieces and reverses differentiation.
  13. Partial derivatives: When a quantity depends on several variables, a partial derivative measures how it changes with one while the others are held fixed.
  14. Ordinary differential equations: An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates a function of one variable to its derivatives.
  15. Partial differential equations: A partial differential equation (PDE) involves partial derivatives in several variables, such as space and time.
  16. First- and second-order differential equations: Most of physics reduces to first- and second-order linear ODEs.
  17. Boundary and initial conditions: Differential equations have families of solutions; conditions on the boundaries of the region, or at the starting time, pick out the one that describes the actual system.
  18. Taylor and Fourier series: A Taylor series approximates a smooth function near a point by a polynomial; a Fourier series represents a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines.
  19. Fourier transforms: The Fourier transform breaks a (not necessarily periodic) signal into a continuous spectrum of frequencies.
  20. Laplace transforms: The Laplace transform turns linear ODEs with initial conditions into algebra.
  21. Multivariable calculus: Multivariable calculus extends derivatives and integrals to functions of several variables: fields in 3D space, thermodynamic states, probability distributions.
  22. Gradient, divergence and curl: The operator ∇ (del) builds three derivatives of fields: the gradient of a scalar field, and the divergence and curl of a vector field.
  23. Line, surface and volume integrals: Integrals along curves, over surfaces and through volumes add up field quantities: work along a path, flux through a surface, total charge in a region.
  24. Vector calculus theorems: The divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem connect what happens inside a region to what happens on its boundary.
  25. Linear algebra eigenvalues and eigenvectors: An eigenvector of a matrix keeps its direction when the matrix acts on it, only scaling by the eigenvalue.
  26. Tensor algebra: A tensor is a quantity whose components transform in a definite way when the coordinates change.
  27. Probability distributions: A probability distribution describes how likely each outcome is.
  28. Statistics and error propagation: Statistics turns repeated measurements into a best estimate and an honest uncertainty, and error propagation carries those uncertainties through calculations into the final result.
  29. Numerical methods: Most real problems have no closed-form solution.
  30. Optimisation: Optimisation finds the maximum or minimum of a function, often under constraints.
  31. Dimensional analysis: In mathematical physics, dimensional analysis is a tool for checking work and finding scaling laws.
  32. Complex analysis: Complex analysis studies functions of a complex variable.
  33. Group theory and mathematical symmetry: A group is the mathematics of symmetry: a set of transformations that can be combined and undone.
  34. Variational calculus: Variational calculus finds the function, rather than the number, that makes a quantity stationary.

Unit 3: Measurement, Experimental Physics and Uncertainty

31 lessons

Measurement, Experimental Physics and Uncertainty: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Measurement instruments and calibration: An instrument converts a physical quantity into a reading.
  2. Accuracy, precision and resolution: Accuracy is how close a result is to the true value; precision is how closely repeated results agree; resolution is the smallest change an instrument can register.
  3. Random and systematic errors: Random errors scatter readings unpredictably either side of the true value; systematic errors shift all readings the same way.
  4. Measurement uncertainty: Uncertainty is the range within which the true value is expected to lie, at a stated confidence.
  5. Absolute and relative uncertainty: Absolute uncertainty has the units of the quantity (± 0.2 cm); relative (fractional or percentage) uncertainty is the absolute uncertainty divided by the value.
  6. Significant figures in experimental results: In experimental work significant figures follow the uncertainty, not the calculator.
  7. Propagation of uncertainty: When a result is calculated from measured quantities, their uncertainties propagate into the result.
  8. Error bars and confidence intervals: Error bars show the uncertainty of each data point on a graph; confidence intervals state the range within which a parameter lies with a given probability.
  9. Repeatability and reproducibility: Repeatability is agreement between results under the same conditions (same person, apparatus, lab, short time); reproducibility is agreement under changed conditions (different people, methods, labs).
  10. Experimental controls: A control removes alternative explanations for a result.
  11. Independent, dependent and controlled variables: In an experiment you change the independent variable, measure the dependent variable, and keep the controlled variables constant.
  12. Experimental design: Experimental design plans what to measure, how, and how many times so that the result answers the question with the smallest uncertainty for the effort.
  13. Calibration curves: A calibration curve maps an instrument's raw output to the true quantity using measurements of known standards.
  14. Linearisation of experimental relationships: Straight lines are easy to test and fit.
  15. Least-squares fitting: Least squares finds the line (or curve) that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances between data and model.
  16. Regression analysis: Regression models how a dependent variable depends on one or more predictors, estimates the model's parameters, and quantifies how well it fits.
  17. Residual analysis: Residuals are the differences between data and the fitted model.
  18. Hypothesis testing: Hypothesis testing asks whether data are consistent with a null hypothesis (no effect, or the standard model).
  19. Statistical significance: A result is statistically significant when chance alone is an unlikely explanation.
  20. Instrument response and sensitivity: An instrument's response describes how its output follows its input: how much (sensitivity), how linearly, how fast (time response) and over which frequencies (bandwidth).
  21. Detection limits: The detection limit is the smallest signal an instrument or method can reliably distinguish from noise and background.
  22. Signal-to-noise ratio: The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the strength of a wanted signal with the random fluctuations hiding it.
  23. Data acquisition systems: A data acquisition (DAQ) system converts physical signals into digital numbers a computer can store: sensor, signal conditioning, sampling and analogue-to-digital conversion, then software for logging and analysis.
  24. Sensors and transducers: A transducer converts energy from one form into another; a sensor is a transducer that produces a measurable signal (usually electrical) from a physical quantity.
  25. Oscilloscopes and signal generators: An oscilloscope displays voltage against time, so you can see waveforms, measure amplitudes, periods and phase, and catch glitches.
  26. Lock-in amplifiers: A lock-in amplifier extracts a tiny signal buried in noise by modulating it at a known frequency and detecting only what varies at exactly that frequency and phase.
  27. Vacuum systems: Many experiments need low pressure: to let particles travel without collisions, to keep surfaces clean, or to insulate thermally.
  28. Cryogenic measurement: Cryogenics studies matter at very low temperatures, where superconductivity, superfluidity and quantum effects appear and thermal noise falls.
  29. High-voltage and high-current safety: Electricity becomes dangerous through current passing through the body, burns, arcs, fire and stored energy.
  30. Laboratory notebooks and reproducibility: A lab notebook is the permanent, dated record of what you did, saw and thought.
  31. Experimental validation of physical models: A model is validated when its predictions agree with independent measurements within uncertainties across its claimed range.

Unit 4: Classical Mechanics

33 lessons

Classical Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Position, displacement, distance and time: Position locates an object relative to an origin; displacement is the change in position (a vector); distance is the total path length travelled (a scalar).
  2. Speed and velocity: Speed is the rate of change of distance; velocity is the rate of change of displacement, with direction.
  3. Acceleration: Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.
  4. Motion in one dimension: Straight-line motion is described by signed position, velocity and acceleration along one axis.
  5. Motion in two and three dimensions: In two and three dimensions position, velocity and acceleration are vectors.
  6. Kinematic equations: For constant acceleration, five quantities (s, u, v, a, t) are linked by the suvat equations.
  7. Projectile motion: A projectile moves under gravity alone: constant velocity horizontally and constant acceleration g vertically.
  8. Relative motion: Velocities are measured relative to a frame.
  9. Reference frames: A reference frame is a viewpoint with its own clocks and rulers from which motion is measured.
  10. Newton's laws of motion: Newton's three laws connect force and motion.
  11. Inertial and non-inertial frames: Newton's laws hold as written only in inertial frames.
  12. Free-body diagrams: A free-body diagram shows one object, isolated, with every external force acting on it drawn as an arrow from its point of action.
  13. Normal force and tension: The normal force is the perpendicular push a surface exerts to stop objects passing through it; tension is the pull transmitted along a rope or string.
  14. Friction and drag: Friction opposes relative sliding between surfaces; drag opposes motion through a fluid.
  15. Inclined planes: On a slope, resolve forces parallel and perpendicular to the surface.
  16. Circular motion: An object moving in a circle constantly changes direction, so it accelerates towards the centre even at constant speed.
  17. Centripetal acceleration and force: Centripetal means "centre-seeking": the acceleration v²/r towards the centre of a circular path, and the net force mv²/r that causes it.
  18. Work and energy: Work is energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement.
  19. Kinetic and potential energy: Kinetic energy is energy of motion; potential energy is energy stored by position or configuration in a field or a deformed body.
  20. Conservative and non-conservative forces: A conservative force does work that depends only on start and end points, so a potential energy can be defined for it.
  21. Power and efficiency: Power is the rate of transferring energy or doing work.
  22. Momentum and impulse: Momentum p = mv measures the quantity of motion.
  23. Conservation of momentum: If no net external force acts on a system, its total momentum is constant.
  24. Collisions and explosions: In collisions and explosions, large internal forces act for a short time, so momentum is conserved.
  25. Elastic and inelastic collisions: The coefficient of restitution e measures how bouncy a collision is: e = 1 for elastic, 0 for perfectly inelastic.
  26. Centre of mass: The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system.
  27. Systems of particles: Treating many particles as a system, internal forces cancel in pairs, so the system's total momentum responds only to external forces.
  28. Variable-mass systems: When a system gains or loses mass, as rockets, conveyor belts and raindrops do, F = ma isn't enough.
  29. Motion under resistive forces: Real objects move against drag and friction, which depend on speed.
  30. Terminal velocity: A falling object accelerates until drag equals its weight; then the net force is zero and it falls at constant terminal velocity.
  31. Equilibrium and statics: A body is in static equilibrium when it neither translates nor rotates: the net force and net torque on it are both zero.
  32. Mechanical advantage: Mechanical advantage (MA) is the ratio of the output force a machine delivers to the input force you apply.
  33. Simple machines: The six classical simple machines (lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw) all trade force against distance.

Unit 5: Rotational Mechanics

20 lessons

Rotational Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration: Rotation is described by the angle turned (angular displacement θ), its rate (angular velocity ω) and the rate of change of that (angular acceleration α).
  2. Angular and linear motion relationships: Each point on a rotating rigid body moves in a circle, with linear quantities equal to the angular ones times the radius.
  3. Torque and moment of force: Torque is the turning effect of a force: force times its perpendicular distance from the axis.
  4. Moment of inertia: Moment of inertia measures how hard it is to change an object's rotation.
  5. Rotational kinetic energy: A spinning body stores kinetic energy because each part of it moves.
  6. Angular momentum: Angular momentum is the rotational counterpart of linear momentum.
  7. Conservation of angular momentum: With no net external torque, a system's total angular momentum stays constant.
  8. Rotational dynamics: Rotational dynamics applies τ = Iα (for a fixed axis) to find how torques change rotation.
  9. Rolling without slipping: When a wheel rolls without slipping, its contact point is momentarily at rest, linking translation and rotation by v = ωR.
  10. Rotational equilibrium: A body is in rotational equilibrium when the net torque on it is zero about every point.
  11. Parallel-axis theorem: The parallel-axis theorem gives the moment of inertia about any axis from the moment about a parallel axis through the centre of mass.
  12. Perpendicular-axis theorem: For a flat (planar) object, the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to its plane equals the sum of the moments about two perpendicular axes lying in the plane and crossing at the same point.
  13. Gyroscopic motion: A spinning gyroscope resists changes in the direction of its axis.
  14. Precession and nutation: Precession is the slow circling of a spinning body's axis under a torque; nutation is a nodding wobble superimposed on it.
  15. Rigid-body dynamics: A rigid body's motion is the translation of its centre of mass plus rotation about it.
  16. Euler's equations of rotation: Euler's equations give the rotational dynamics of a rigid body in its own rotating principal-axis frame.
  17. Rotating reference frames: In a frame rotating at angular velocity ω, Newton's laws need fictitious forces: centrifugal, Coriolis and (if ω changes) Euler forces.
  18. Centrifugal and Coriolis forces: Centrifugal and Coriolis forces are fictitious forces seen in rotating frames.
  19. Top motion and stability: A spinning top on a table combines spin, precession and nutation.
  20. Coupled translational and rotational motion: Many objects translate and rotate at once, with friction or constraints coupling the two.

Unit 6: Oscillations and Vibrations

20 lessons

Oscillations and Vibrations: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Periodic motion: Periodic motion repeats itself after a fixed time, the period.
  2. Simple harmonic motion: Simple harmonic motion (SHM) is oscillation in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and directed towards equilibrium.
  3. Amplitude, frequency, period and phase: An oscillation x = A cos(ωt + φ) is fully specified by its amplitude A, angular frequency ω and phase constant φ.
  4. Mass-spring systems: A mass on a spring is the model simple harmonic oscillator.
  5. Simple and physical pendulums: A simple pendulum is a point mass on a light string; a physical (compound) pendulum is any rigid body swinging about a pivot.
  6. Energy in oscillatory systems: An undamped oscillator continually exchanges kinetic and potential energy while the total stays constant.
  7. Damped oscillations: Real oscillators lose energy to friction, air resistance or electrical resistance, so their amplitude decays.
  8. Underdamping, critical damping and overdamping: How strongly an oscillator is damped decides whether it oscillates as it settles.
  9. Driven oscillations: A periodic driving force makes an oscillator vibrate at the driving frequency, not its natural one.
  10. Resonance: Resonance is the large response of a system driven at (or near) its natural frequency.
  11. Quality factor: The quality factor Q measures how lightly damped an oscillator is: how many cycles it rings for, and how sharp its resonance peak is.
  12. Coupled oscillators: When oscillators are linked, energy flows between them.
  13. Normal modes: A normal mode is a pattern of motion in which every part of a system oscillates at the same frequency with fixed relative amplitudes.
  14. Beat frequencies: When two oscillations of slightly different frequency add, the result swells and fades at the difference frequency: beats.
  15. Anharmonic oscillations: An anharmonic oscillator has a restoring force that isn't exactly proportional to displacement.
  16. Nonlinear oscillators: Nonlinear oscillators obey equations where superposition fails.
  17. Parametric resonance: Parametric resonance grows oscillations by periodically varying a parameter of the system (length, stiffness) rather than pushing it.
  18. Mechanical vibration analysis: Vibration analysis measures and models how machines and structures vibrate, to prevent fatigue, noise and failure and to diagnose faults early.
  19. Frequency response: A system's frequency response describes how it amplifies (or attenuates) and phase-shifts sinusoidal inputs at each frequency.
  20. Fourier analysis of oscillations: Fourier analysis breaks any oscillation into a sum of pure sinusoids.

Unit 7: Gravitation and Orbital Mechanics

22 lessons

Gravitation and Orbital Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Newton's law of universal gravitation: Every pair of masses attracts with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
  2. Gravitational fields: A gravitational field is the region around a mass where other masses feel a force.
  3. Gravitational field strength: Gravitational field strength g is the force per unit mass at a point.
  4. Gravitational potential and potential energy: Gravitational potential is the work done per unit mass to bring a mass from infinity to a point.
  5. Escape velocity: Escape velocity is the minimum launch speed for an unpowered object to escape a body's gravity completely.
  6. Orbital velocity: A satellite in a circular orbit falls continuously around the planet: gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force needed at its speed.
  7. Circular and elliptical orbits: Bound orbits under an inverse-square force are ellipses with the central body at one focus; a circle is the special case of zero eccentricity.
  8. Kepler's laws: Kepler found three laws in Tycho Brahe's planetary data; Newton showed they follow from an inverse-square gravitational force.
  9. Satellite motion: Satellites are placed in orbits chosen for their purpose: low orbits for imaging and the ISS, sun-synchronous polar orbits for Earth observation, medium orbits for navigation, and geostationary…
  10. Orbital energy and angular momentum: An orbit's total energy fixes its size (semi-major axis); its angular momentum fixes its shape.
  11. Two-body gravitational problem: Two bodies orbiting under mutual gravity both circle their common centre of mass.
  12. Tidal forces: Tidal forces arise because gravity varies across an extended body: the near side is pulled more strongly than the far side.
  13. Roche limits: The Roche limit is the distance within which a body held together only by its own gravity is torn apart by a planet's tides.
  14. Lagrange points: In a system of two orbiting masses there are five points where a small third body can orbit in step with them, the gravitational and centrifugal forces balancing in the rotating frame.
  15. Restricted three-body problem: The general three-body problem has no closed-form solution and can be chaotic.
  16. Orbital transfers: Changing orbit means changing velocity with rocket burns (Δv).
  17. Hohmann transfers: A Hohmann transfer moves a spacecraft between two circular orbits using two burns and half an elliptical orbit that touches both.
  18. Gravitational perturbations: Real orbits are perturbed by other bodies, by the non-spherical shapes of planets, by drag and by radiation pressure.
  19. Precession of orbits: Under a pure inverse-square force around a point mass, an orbit's ellipse stays fixed.
  20. General relativistic corrections: General relativity treats gravity as curved spacetime.
  21. Gravitational lensing: Mass bends light, so a galaxy or cluster in front of a distant source acts as a lens, distorting, magnifying and multiplying its images.
  22. Gravitational waves: Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime that travel at the speed of light, produced by accelerating masses.

Unit 8: Fluid Mechanics

25 lessons

Fluid Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Density and pressure: Density is mass per unit volume; pressure is force per unit area acting perpendicular to a surface.
  2. Hydrostatic equilibrium: In a fluid at rest, pressure increases with depth because each layer supports the weight of the fluid above it.
  3. Pascal's principle: Pascal's principle states that a pressure change applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and its container walls.
  4. Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle: A body immersed in a fluid feels an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid it displaces.
  5. Fluid statics: Fluid statics covers fluids at rest: pressure distributions, forces on submerged surfaces, manometers and the stability of floating bodies.
  6. Fluid dynamics: Fluid dynamics studies fluids in motion: rivers, blood, weather, aircraft, stars.
  7. Continuity equation: The continuity equation expresses conservation of mass in a flow.
  8. Conservation of mass in fluid flow: Mass can't be created or destroyed in a flow; it can only move.
  9. Bernoulli's equation: Along a streamline in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, pressure energy, kinetic energy and potential energy per unit volume add to a constant.
  10. Viscosity: Viscosity is a fluid's internal friction: its resistance to layers sliding past each other.
  11. Laminar and turbulent flow: In laminar flow, fluid moves in smooth parallel layers; in turbulent flow, it churns in irregular eddies at many scales.
  12. Reynolds number: The Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in a flow.
  13. Poiseuille flow: Poiseuille flow is steady laminar flow through a pipe.
  14. Drag and lift: A body moving through a fluid feels a force with two components: drag, opposing the relative motion, and lift, perpendicular to it.
  15. Boundary layers: At a solid surface a real fluid sticks (no-slip), so a thin boundary layer forms in which the velocity rises from zero to the free-stream value.
  16. Surface tension: Molecules at a liquid's surface are pulled inward by their neighbours, so the surface behaves like a stretched membrane.
  17. Capillary action: Capillary action is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube because of the balance between adhesion to the walls, cohesion within the liquid and surface tension.
  18. Compressible and incompressible fluids: Liquids barely change volume under pressure; gases compress easily.
  19. Navier–Stokes equations: The Navier–Stokes equations are Newton's second law for a viscous fluid.
  20. Euler equations for fluids: The Euler equations describe an inviscid fluid: Navier–Stokes without viscosity.
  21. Vorticity and circulation: Vorticity measures local spinning of a fluid; circulation measures the total rotation around a loop.
  22. Flow instabilities: A steady flow is unstable if small disturbances grow instead of dying out.
  23. Shock waves: When a disturbance moves through a fluid faster than sound, pressure waves can't get ahead of it and pile up into a shock: an extremely thin jump in pressure, density, temperature and velocity.
  24. Turbulence modelling: Turbulence spans a huge range of scales, so simulating every eddy is usually impossible.
  25. Computational fluid dynamics: Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solves the equations of fluid flow numerically.

Unit 9: Elasticity and Solid Mechanics

19 lessons

Elasticity and Solid Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Stress and strain: Stress is the internal force per unit area in a material; strain is the fractional deformation it produces.
  2. Young's modulus: Young's modulus is the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear elastic region.
  3. Shear modulus: The shear (rigidity) modulus relates shear stress to shear strain: how a material resists being skewed.
  4. Bulk modulus: The bulk modulus measures resistance to uniform compression: the pressure needed per fractional change in volume.
  5. Hooke's law: Hooke's law states that extension is proportional to the applied force, up to a limit.
  6. Elastic and plastic deformation: Elastic deformation is reversible: atoms are displaced but return when the load is removed.
  7. Poisson's ratio: When you stretch a material it usually gets thinner.
  8. Elastic energy: Work done deforming a material elastically is stored as elastic potential energy, released when it springs back.
  9. Bending and torsion: When a beam bends, one side stretches and the other compresses, with a neutral axis between.
  10. Beam deflection: Engineers predict how much beams sag under loads using the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation.
  11. Pressure and material deformation: Materials under pressure compress, and their structure can change: pressure vessels must contain it safely, rocks deep in the Earth deform plastically, and high-pressure physics creates new phases like metallic hydrogen.
  12. Fracture and failure: Materials fail by yielding (permanent deformation), fracture (cracking apart), buckling (sudden instability under compression) or fatigue.
  13. Viscoelasticity: Viscoelastic materials behave partly like elastic solids and partly like viscous fluids: their response depends on how fast and how long you load them.
  14. Crystal elasticity: In crystals, elastic properties depend on direction because atoms are arranged in regular lattices.
  15. Mechanical waves in solids: Solids carry several kinds of elastic wave: compressional (P), shear (S) and surface waves.
  16. Stress tensors: Stress inside a material can't be described by a single number: the force on a small surface depends on the surface's orientation.
  17. Continuum mechanics: Continuum mechanics treats solids and fluids as continuous media described by fields (density, velocity, stress, strain) instead of individual atoms.
  18. Material stability: A structure or material is stable if small disturbances die away and unstable if they grow.
  19. Fatigue and fracture mechanics: Repeated loading well below a material's strength can grow microscopic cracks until a part fails: fatigue causes most mechanical failures.

Unit 10: Waves and Wave Motion

25 lessons

Waves and Wave Motion: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Wave properties: A wave is a travelling disturbance that carries energy and momentum from place to place without carrying the medium along with it.
  2. Transverse and longitudinal waves: In a transverse wave the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of travel; in a longitudinal wave it's along it, forming compressions and rarefactions.
  3. Wavelength, frequency, period and speed: For any periodic wave, the speed equals frequency times wavelength.
  4. Wave equations: The one-dimensional wave equation says the acceleration of each bit of medium is proportional to the curvature of the wave there.
  5. Wave propagation: As waves travel they spread out, lose energy to the medium, and may change speed and shape.
  6. Phase and group velocity: The phase velocity is how fast individual wave crests move; the group velocity is how fast the envelope of a wave packet, and hence energy and information, moves.
  7. Superposition: When waves overlap in a linear medium, the total displacement at each point is the sum of the individual displacements.
  8. Interference: Interference is the pattern of reinforcement and cancellation when coherent waves overlap.
  9. Constructive and destructive interference: Waves interfere constructively where they arrive in phase (path difference a whole number of wavelengths) and destructively where they arrive in antiphase (an odd number of half wavelengths).
  10. Standing waves: A standing wave forms when two identical waves travel in opposite directions, usually a wave and its reflection.
  11. Reflection and transmission: When a wave meets a boundary between two media, part is reflected and part transmitted.
  12. Refraction: Refraction is the change in direction of a wave when it enters a medium where its speed is different, at an angle.
  13. Diffraction: Diffraction is the spreading of waves when they pass through gaps or around obstacles.
  14. Dispersion: Dispersion is the dependence of wave speed on frequency.
  15. Polarisation: Polarisation describes the direction of oscillation of a transverse wave.
  16. Wave packets: A wave packet is a localised wave made by superposing many waves with a range of wavelengths.
  17. Beats: Beats are the slow periodic swelling and fading of loudness when two waves of slightly different frequencies combine.
  18. Boundary conditions: Boundary conditions specify what a wave must do at the edges of its medium: fixed, free, or matched to another medium.
  19. Resonant modes: A bounded system can only sustain standing waves whose shapes fit its boundary conditions: its resonant modes.
  20. Coupled waves: When two wave systems are linked, energy can pass between them.
  21. Nonlinear waves: In nonlinear media, a wave's speed depends on its amplitude, so waves steepen, break, generate harmonics and interact with each other.
  22. Solitons: A soliton is a solitary wave that keeps its shape while travelling at constant speed, because nonlinear steepening exactly balances dispersive spreading.
  23. Wave energy and intensity: Waves carry energy at a rate set by their amplitude, frequency and medium.
  24. Huygens' principle: Huygens' principle says every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is the envelope of these wavelets.
  25. Fourier decomposition of waves: Any wave can be written as a sum (or integral) of sinusoidal waves of different frequencies, amplitudes and phases.

Acoustics and Sound: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

Unit 12: Thermodynamics

31 lessons

Thermodynamics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Temperature and thermal equilibrium: Temperature is the property that decides which way heat flows: from hot to cold, until the two are in thermal equilibrium at the same temperature.
  2. Zeroth law of thermodynamics: The zeroth law says that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they're in equilibrium with each other.
  3. Heat and internal energy: Internal energy is the total microscopic energy of a system's particles.
  4. Specific heat capacity: Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K. Water's unusually high value moderates climates and makes it an excellent coolant.
  5. Latent heat: Latent heat is the energy absorbed or released during a change of phase at constant temperature.
  6. Thermal expansion: Most materials expand when heated because their atoms vibrate with larger amplitude in an asymmetric potential, increasing the average spacing.
  7. First law of thermodynamics: The first law is energy conservation for thermodynamic systems: the change in internal energy equals the heat supplied to the system minus the work it does.
  8. Work done by thermodynamic systems: A gas does work when it expands against an external pressure.
  9. Pressure-volume diagrams: A p–V diagram plots a gas's state as it changes.
  10. Isothermal processes: In an isothermal process the temperature stays constant.
  11. Adiabatic processes: In an adiabatic process no heat enters or leaves.
  12. Isobaric and isochoric processes: In an isobaric process the pressure is constant; in an isochoric one the volume is constant.
  13. Heat engines: A heat engine takes heat from a hot source, converts part of it to work, and rejects the rest to a cold sink.
  14. Refrigerators and heat pumps: Refrigerators and heat pumps run a heat engine backwards: work drives heat from a cold place to a hot one.
  15. Thermal efficiency: Thermal efficiency measures how much of the heat put into an engine becomes useful work.
  16. Second law of thermodynamics: The second law says heat flows spontaneously only from hot to cold, and no cyclic process can turn heat entirely into work.
  17. Entropy: Entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements correspond to a system's macroscopic state, or equivalently how much energy is dispersed and unavailable for work.
  18. Reversible and irreversible processes: A reversible process can be undone exactly, leaving no change anywhere; it happens through a sequence of equilibrium states and creates no entropy.
  19. Carnot cycle: The Carnot cycle is the ideal reversible engine: two isothermal and two adiabatic steps between a hot and a cold reservoir.
  20. Thermodynamic potentials: Thermodynamic potentials (U, H, F, G) are energy-like state functions, each natural for different controlled conditions.
  21. Enthalpy: Enthalpy H = U + pV is the energy-like quantity whose change equals the heat absorbed at constant pressure.
  22. Helmholtz free energy: The Helmholtz free energy F = U − TS is the maximum work a system can do at constant temperature.
  23. Gibbs free energy: The Gibbs free energy G = H − TS decides whether a process happens spontaneously at constant temperature and pressure, the conditions of most chemistry and biology.
  24. Chemical and phase equilibrium: At equilibrium, matter has no tendency to move between phases or react further: each species has the same chemical potential everywhere it can go.
  25. Maxwell relations: Maxwell relations are equalities between partial derivatives of thermodynamic variables, following from the potentials being state functions.
  26. Third law of thermodynamics: The third law says the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero, and that absolute zero can't be reached in a finite number of steps.
  27. Thermodynamic cycles: Practical engines and refrigerators follow particular cycles chosen for their working fluid and hardware.
  28. Equations of state: An equation of state relates pressure, volume and temperature (and amount) for a substance.
  29. Critical points: A critical point is where the distinction between two phases disappears: above the critical temperature, liquid and gas merge into one supercritical fluid.
  30. Phase transitions: A phase transition is an abrupt change in a system's properties as a control parameter (temperature, pressure, field) crosses a critical value: melting, boiling, magnetisation, superconductivity.
  31. Non-equilibrium thermodynamics: Most of the world is out of equilibrium: heat flows, currents run, chemical reactions proceed, organisms live.

Unit 13: Statistical Mechanics

26 lessons

Statistical Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Microstates and macrostates: A microstate specifies the exact state of every particle; a macrostate specifies only bulk properties like energy, volume and particle number.
  2. Statistical ensembles: An ensemble is an imagined collection of many copies of a system, each in a different microstate consistent with the macroscopic constraints.
  3. Microcanonical, canonical and grand canonical ensembles: The three main ensembles each have their own probability rule and their own link to thermodynamics.
  4. Boltzmann distribution: In thermal equilibrium at temperature T, the probability of finding a system in a state of energy E is proportional to e^(−E/k_BT).
  5. Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution: The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution gives the spread of molecular speeds in a gas at temperature T. Most molecules move near a typical speed, but a long tail of fast molecules drives evaporation,…
  6. Partition functions: The partition function Z is a weighted sum over all states.
  7. Entropy and probability: Entropy measures how probable a macrostate is: the logarithm of its number of microstates.
  8. Multiplicity and state counting: Multiplicity is the number of microstates in a macrostate.
  9. Equipartition theorem: In classical thermal equilibrium, every quadratic term in a system's energy has an average value of ½k_BT.
  10. Classical ideal gases: An ideal gas is a collection of non-interacting point particles.
  11. Mean free path: The mean free path is the average distance a molecule travels between collisions.
  12. Molecular speed distributions: Molecular speeds in a gas follow the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, but what a measurement sees depends on how it samples them: molecules effusing through a hole, hitting a wall, or colliding are…
  13. Chemical potential: The chemical potential is the energy cost of adding one particle to a system.
  14. Fermi–Dirac statistics: Fermions (electrons, protons, neutrons, ³He) obey the Pauli exclusion principle: no two can share a quantum state.
  15. Bose–Einstein statistics: Bosons (photons, phonons, ⁴He atoms, many other atoms) can pile into the same quantum state.
  16. Quantum gases: When particles are close compared with their thermal de Broglie wavelength, quantum statistics take over and gases behave very differently from classical ones.
  17. Degenerate matter: Degenerate matter is so dense that the Pauli exclusion principle, not temperature, provides most of its pressure.
  18. Blackbody radiation: A blackbody absorbs all radiation and emits a spectrum fixed only by its temperature.
  19. Fluctuations and correlations: Thermodynamic quantities fluctuate about their averages.
  20. Phase transitions: Statistical mechanics explains phase transitions as collective behaviour: interactions between many particles produce sudden changes in macroscopic order.
  21. Critical phenomena: Near a continuous phase transition, physical quantities follow power laws in the distance from the critical point, with exponents that are the same for whole classes of different systems.
  22. Ising model: The Ising model places spins that can point up or down on a lattice, with neighbouring spins preferring to align.
  23. Renormalisation group: The renormalisation group (RG) studies how a system's description changes as you zoom out, averaging over small-scale details.
  24. Statistical field theory: Statistical field theory describes systems near critical points by continuous fields instead of individual particles.
  25. Monte Carlo methods: Monte Carlo methods use random sampling to estimate averages in systems with too many states to enumerate.
  26. Nonequilibrium statistical mechanics: Nonequilibrium statistical mechanics describes systems driven away from equilibrium: how they relax, transport heat and particles, and fluctuate.

Unit 14: Heat Transfer and Thermal Physics

20 lessons

Heat Transfer and Thermal Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Conduction: Conduction transfers heat through a material without bulk motion, by collisions between neighbouring particles and, in metals, by free electrons.
  2. Convection: Convection transfers heat by bulk movement of a fluid.
  3. Thermal radiation: Every object above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation because of the thermal motion of its charges.
  4. Fourier's law of heat conduction: Fourier's law states that heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient and flows down it.
  5. Newton's law of cooling: Newton's law of cooling says an object's rate of heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference between it and its surroundings.
  6. Stefan–Boltzmann law: The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives the total power radiated by a blackbody: proportional to its surface area and the fourth power of its absolute temperature.
  7. Wien's displacement law: Wien's law says the wavelength at which a blackbody's emission peaks is inversely proportional to its temperature.
  8. Thermal diffusivity: Thermal diffusivity measures how quickly temperature changes spread through a material.
  9. Heat exchangers: Heat exchangers move heat from one fluid to another without mixing them.
  10. Insulation and thermal resistance: Insulation slows heat flow.
  11. Blackbody and greybody radiation: A blackbody absorbs and emits perfectly at every wavelength; real surfaces don't.
  12. Emissivity and absorptivity: Emissivity is how well a surface emits thermal radiation compared with a blackbody; absorptivity is the fraction of incoming radiation it absorbs.
  13. Greenhouse effect: Greenhouse gases are transparent to most incoming sunlight but absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared.
  14. Thermoelectric effects: Thermoelectric effects convert directly between heat and electricity in conducting materials.
  15. Peltier and Seebeck effects: Seebeck and Peltier effects are two faces of thermoelectricity.
  16. Thermal contact resistance: When two solid surfaces touch, they really meet only at tiny high points, so heat flow across the interface is impeded.
  17. Cryogenics: Cryogenics is the production and use of very low temperatures, typically below about 120 K. It liquefies gases, enables superconducting magnets and quantum computers, and preserves biological samples.
  18. Low-temperature physics: Cooling matter towards absolute zero reveals quantum behaviour on a macroscopic scale: superconductivity, superfluidity, quantum Hall effects and Bose–Einstein condensates.
  19. Heat transport in solids and fluids: Heat moves through matter by different carriers: phonons and electrons in solids, molecules in gases, and both conduction and convection in liquids.
  20. Thermal management in engineering: Thermal management keeps devices within safe temperatures: removing heat from chips, batteries, engines and spacecraft, or keeping things warm.

Unit 15: Electricity and Electrostatics

24 lessons

Electricity and Electrostatics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Electric charge: Electric charge is a basic property of matter that makes particles exert electric and magnetic forces on each other.
  2. Conservation and quantisation of charge: Electric charge is conserved: the total in an isolated system never changes.
  3. Conductors, insulators and dielectrics: In conductors, charges move freely; in insulators, they're bound.
  4. Coulomb's law: Coulomb's law gives the force between two point charges: proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.
  5. Electric fields: An electric field is the force per unit charge that a test charge would feel at a point.
  6. Electric field lines: Field lines map an electric field: their direction gives the field's direction, and their density gives its strength.
  7. Electric potential: Electric potential is the potential energy per unit charge at a point, measured relative to a reference (often infinity or earth).
  8. Electric potential energy: Electric potential energy is the energy stored in a configuration of charges because of their positions.
  9. Voltage and potential difference: Voltage, or potential difference, is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points.
  10. Electric dipoles: An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.
  11. Electric flux: Electric flux measures how much electric field passes through a surface: field strength times area, counting only the component perpendicular to the surface.
  12. Gauss's law: Gauss's law states that the electric flux out of any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀.
  13. Charge distributions: Real charges are spread over lines, surfaces and volumes.
  14. Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium: In electrostatic equilibrium, charges in a conductor have stopped moving.
  15. Capacitance: Capacitance is the ability of a conductor arrangement to store charge per volt.
  16. Parallel-plate and spherical capacitors: Simple geometries give simple formulas for capacitance.
  17. Capacitors in series and parallel: Capacitors combine opposite to resistors: in parallel their capacitances add; in series their reciprocals add.
  18. Energy stored in electric fields: Charging a capacitor stores energy, and that energy lives in the electric field between the plates.
  19. Dielectric polarisation: In an electric field, a dielectric's bound charges shift, creating tiny dipoles throughout the material.
  20. Boundary conditions for electric fields: At the boundary between two materials, electric fields obey simple matching rules derived from Maxwell's equations.
  21. Electrostatic shielding: A closed conducting shell blocks external static electric fields from its interior: a Faraday cage.
  22. Method of images: The method of images solves electrostatics problems near conductors by replacing the conductor with imaginary charges that reproduce the same boundary conditions.
  23. Poisson's equation: Poisson's equation relates the electric potential to the charge density producing it.
  24. Laplace's equation: In regions without charge, the electric potential obeys Laplace's equation.

Unit 16: Electric Circuits

28 lessons

Electric Circuits: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Electric current: Electric current is the rate of flow of charge.
  2. Resistance and resistivity: Resistance measures how much a component opposes current: the voltage needed per ampere.
  3. Ohm's law: Ohm's law states that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it, at constant temperature.
  4. Conductivity: Conductivity σ is the reciprocal of resistivity: how readily a material carries current.
  5. Series and parallel circuits: Components in series share the same current; in parallel they share the same voltage.
  6. Kirchhoff's laws: Kirchhoff's laws express conservation of charge and energy in circuits.
  7. Electromotive force: Electromotive force (EMF) is the energy a source gives each coulomb of charge passing through it.
  8. Internal resistance: Every real source has internal resistance, so its terminal voltage drops when it supplies current.
  9. Electrical power and energy: Electrical power is the rate at which energy is transferred by a current.
  10. Resistor networks: Complex resistor networks can often be simplified by combining series and parallel parts, using symmetry, or converting between star and delta shapes.
  11. Capacitor charging and discharging: When a capacitor charges or discharges through a resistor, the current doesn't change instantly: voltage and charge follow exponential curves with time constant RC.
  12. RC circuits: RC circuits combine resistors and capacitors to shape signals: they set delays, smooth ripple, block DC, and filter frequencies.
  13. RL circuits: An inductor opposes changes in current by generating a back EMF.
  14. RLC circuits: An RLC circuit combines resistance, inductance and capacitance.
  15. Transient responses: A transient is a circuit's temporary response when something changes suddenly, like a switch closing.
  16. Alternating current: Alternating current (AC) reverses direction periodically, usually sinusoidally.
  17. Root-mean-square values: The root-mean-square (rms) value of an alternating quantity is the steady DC value that would deliver the same average power to a resistor.
  18. Impedance and reactance: Impedance generalises resistance to AC: it includes the resistance and the reactances of capacitors and inductors, which depend on frequency and shift the phase between voltage and current.
  19. Phasor diagrams: Phasors represent sinusoidal quantities as rotating arrows: length for amplitude, angle for phase.
  20. Resonance in AC circuits: A series RLC circuit resonates when the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.
  21. Transformers: A transformer changes AC voltage using electromagnetic induction between two coils on an iron core.
  22. Rectification: Rectification converts AC into DC using diodes, which conduct one way only.
  23. Filters and frequency response: Filters pass some frequencies and block others.
  24. Electrical measurement: Measuring voltage, current and resistance accurately means choosing the right instrument and connecting it so it disturbs the circuit as little as possible.
  25. Circuit analysis methods: Beyond series and parallel rules, systematic methods solve any linear circuit: nodal and mesh analysis, superposition, and source transformations.
  26. Thevenin and Norton equivalents: Any linear network seen from two terminals behaves like a single voltage source with a series resistance (Thevenin) or a current source with a parallel resistance (Norton).
  27. Operational amplifiers: An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain differential amplifier on a chip.
  28. Semiconductor circuit fundamentals: Semiconductor devices, chiefly diodes and transistors, control current in ways resistors can't: one-way flow, amplification and switching.

Unit 17: Magnetism and Electromagnetism

29 lessons

Magnetism and Electromagnetism: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Magnetic fields: A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or moving charge where other moving charges and magnets feel a force.
  2. Magnetic forces on charges: A magnetic field exerts a force on a moving charge, perpendicular to both its velocity and the field.
  3. Lorentz force: The Lorentz force is the total electromagnetic force on a charged particle: the electric force plus the magnetic force.
  4. Magnetic forces on current-carrying wires: A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force because the moving charges in it do.
  5. Motion of charged particles in magnetic fields: In a uniform magnetic field a charged particle moves in a circle (or helix), with a radius set by its momentum and a frequency independent of its speed.
  6. Biot–Savart law: The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field produced by a small element of current.
  7. Ampère's law: Ampère's law relates the magnetic field circulating around a closed loop to the current passing through it.
  8. Magnetic dipoles: A current loop, a bar magnet, and an electron all behave as magnetic dipoles: they produce a characteristic field and twist to align with external fields.
  9. Magnetic flux: Magnetic flux measures the total magnetic field passing through a surface.
  10. Electromagnetic induction: Electromagnetic induction is the production of an EMF by a changing magnetic flux.
  11. Faraday's law: Faraday's law states that the EMF induced in a circuit equals the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage through it.
  12. Lenz's law: Lenz's law says an induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that caused it.
  13. Inductance: Inductance is the property of a circuit that opposes changes in current, by producing an EMF proportional to the rate of change.
  14. Self-induction and mutual induction: Self-induction is a changing current inducing an EMF in its own circuit; mutual induction is a changing current in one circuit inducing an EMF in another nearby.
  15. Energy stored in magnetic fields: Building up a current in an inductor stores energy in its magnetic field.
  16. Magnetic materials: Materials respond to magnetic fields because their atoms carry magnetic moments from electron orbits and spins.
  17. Diamagnetism: Diamagnetism is a weak repulsion from magnetic fields shown by all materials.
  18. Paramagnetism: Paramagnetic materials have atoms with unpaired electrons whose moments align partly with an applied field, giving weak attraction.
  19. Ferromagnetism: In ferromagnets, a quantum-mechanical exchange interaction makes neighbouring spins align, producing strong spontaneous magnetisation in regions called domains.
  20. Hysteresis: Hysteresis is the lagging of a ferromagnet's magnetisation behind the applied field.
  21. Electromagnets: An electromagnet is a coil of wire, usually around an iron core, that becomes magnetic when current flows.
  22. Solenoids and toroids: A solenoid is a long helical coil; a toroid is a coil wound around a ring.
  23. Electric motors and generators: Motors turn electrical energy into motion using the force on currents in magnetic fields; generators do the reverse using induction.
  24. Maxwell's equations: Maxwell's four equations describe all classical electric and magnetic phenomena: how charges and currents create fields, how changing fields create each other, and therefore how light propagates.
  25. Displacement current: Displacement current is Maxwell's addition to Ampère's law: a changing electric field produces a magnetic field just as a real current does.
  26. Electromagnetic potentials: Instead of working with E and B directly, electromagnetism can be written in terms of a scalar potential V and a vector potential A. The potentials simplify calculations, are essential in quantum…
  27. Gauge transformations: The electromagnetic potentials can be changed by a gauge transformation without altering the physical fields.
  28. Electromagnetic boundary conditions: At the boundary between two materials, Maxwell's equations require particular components of E, D, B and H to match.
  29. Electromagnetic momentum and energy: Electromagnetic fields carry energy and momentum.

Unit 18: Electromagnetic Waves

27 lessons

Electromagnetic Waves: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Electromagnetic wave propagation: Electromagnetic waves are self-sustaining oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that travel through space at the speed of light.
  2. Speed of light: The speed of light in vacuum, c = 299 792 458 m s⁻¹, is a universal constant: the same for all observers and the maximum speed for any signal.
  3. Electric and magnetic field relationships: In an electromagnetic wave, the electric and magnetic fields are locked together: perpendicular to each other and the direction of travel, in phase, and with sizes in the fixed ratio E/B = c.
  4. Electromagnetic wave equation: Combining Maxwell's equations in empty space gives a wave equation for E and B, with wave speed 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = c. Its plane-wave solutions describe all electromagnetic radiation.
  5. Electromagnetic spectrum: Electromagnetic waves range from radio waves kilometres long to gamma rays smaller than nuclei.
  6. Radio waves: Radio waves have the longest wavelengths in the EM spectrum.
  7. Microwaves: Microwaves span wavelengths from about 1 mm to 1 m.
  8. Infrared radiation: Infrared radiation lies between visible light and microwaves.
  9. Visible light: Visible light is the narrow band of EM wavelengths, about 400–700 nm, that human eyes detect.
  10. Ultraviolet radiation: Ultraviolet radiation has wavelengths shorter than violet light, 10–400 nm.
  11. X-rays: X-rays are high-energy photons (about 100 eV to 100 keV) produced by decelerating electrons and by inner-shell electron transitions.
  12. Gamma rays: Gamma rays are the most energetic photons, emitted by atomic nuclei, particle annihilations and the most violent events in the universe.
  13. Polarisation: The polarisation of an electromagnetic wave is the direction in which its electric field oscillates.
  14. Reflection and transmission: When an EM wave meets a boundary, part reflects and part transmits.
  15. Refraction: Refraction bends light as it crosses into a medium of different refractive index, because its speed changes.
  16. Dispersion: Dispersion is the variation of a medium's refractive index with frequency.
  17. Absorption: Matter absorbs electromagnetic waves when their frequency matches a way for the material to take up energy: electronic transitions, molecular vibrations and rotations, or free charges.
  18. Electromagnetic wave intensity: The intensity of an electromagnetic wave is the average power it carries per unit area.
  19. Poynting vector: The Poynting vector S = E × B/μ₀ describes the direction and rate of electromagnetic energy flow.
  20. Radiation pressure: Electromagnetic waves carry momentum, so they exert pressure on surfaces that absorb or reflect them.
  21. Waveguides: A waveguide is a structure that confines and guides electromagnetic waves: hollow metal pipes for microwaves, optical fibres for light.
  22. Antennas and radiation patterns: An antenna converts electrical signals into electromagnetic waves and back.
  23. Near-field and far-field behaviour: Close to an antenna or source, electromagnetic fields store energy and fall off rapidly; far away, they form radiating waves that fall as 1/r.
  24. Electromagnetic interference: Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is unwanted electromagnetic energy disrupting electronic devices.
  25. Electromagnetic shielding: Shielding blocks electromagnetic fields from entering or leaving a region.
  26. Microwave engineering: Microwave engineering designs circuits and systems at frequencies (~300 MHz–300 GHz) where wavelengths are comparable to component sizes.
  27. Radio-frequency physics: Radio-frequency (RF) physics covers electromagnetic phenomena from kHz to GHz: how RF fields interact with matter, plasmas and biological tissue, and how they're generated, transmitted and detected.

Optics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Geometrical optics: Geometrical (ray) optics treats light as rays travelling in straight lines, bending at interfaces.
  2. Reflection and refraction: At a boundary, light partly reflects and partly refracts.

  18. Michelson interferometer: A Michelson interferometer splits a beam, sends the halves along two arms, and recombines them.

  20. Polarisation: Polarisation describes the orientation of light's electric field oscillation.
  21. Brewster's angle: At Brewster's angle, light polarised in the plane of incidence (p) isn't reflected at all, so reflected light is perfectly polarised.
  22. Birefringence: Birefringent crystals have two refractive indices depending on the polarisation of light.
  23. Optical activity: Optically active materials rotate the plane of polarised light as it passes through.
  24. Lasers: A laser produces intense, coherent, highly directional, nearly monochromatic light by stimulated emission in a gain medium inside an optical cavity.
  25. Optical cavities: An optical cavity (resonator) traps light between mirrors so it circulates many times.
  26. Fibre optics: Optical fibres guide light along thin glass strands by total internal reflection.
  27. Nonlinear optics: At high intensities, a material's response to light is no longer proportional to the field.
  28. Ultrafast optics: Ultrafast optics creates and uses light pulses lasting femtoseconds to attoseconds.
  29. Integrated photonics: Integrated photonics puts optical components (waveguides, modulators, detectors, lasers) on chips, as electronics did for circuits.
  30. Adaptive optics: Adaptive optics corrects in real time for distortions in light, mainly from atmospheric turbulence, using a deformable mirror controlled by a wavefront sensor.

Unit 20: Quantum Mechanics

42 lessons

Quantum Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Origins of quantum theory: Quantum theory began around 1900 when classical physics failed to explain how hot objects glow, how light ejects electrons, and why atoms are stable with sharp spectral lines.
  2. Blackbody radiation: Classical physics predicted a hot cavity would radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths.
  3. Photoelectric effect: When light shines on a metal, electrons are emitted only if the light's frequency exceeds a threshold, however intense it is.

  5. Wave-particle duality: Light and matter both show wave and particle behaviour.
  6. de Broglie wavelength: De Broglie proposed that every particle has a wavelength inversely proportional to its momentum.
  7. Double-slit experiments: Sending particles one at a time through two slits builds up an interference pattern dot by dot.
  8. Heisenberg uncertainty principle: The uncertainty principle states that certain pairs of quantities, like position and momentum, can't both have precisely defined values.
  9. Wavefunctions: The wavefunction ψ is the mathematical object describing a quantum system's state.
  10. Probability amplitudes: In quantum mechanics, each way an event can happen has a complex probability amplitude.
  11. Born rule: The Born rule connects quantum theory to experiment: the probability of a measurement outcome is the squared magnitude of the corresponding amplitude.
  12. Schrödinger equation: The Schrödinger equation governs how quantum states evolve in time.
  13. Time-dependent and time-independent Schrödinger equations: For potentials that don't change in time, the Schrödinger equation separates.
  14. Quantum states and observables: A quantum state contains everything that can be predicted about a system.
  15. Operators and eigenvalues: Operators act on quantum states to produce new states.
  16. Expectation values: The expectation value of an observable is the average of many measurements on identically prepared systems.
  17. Hilbert spaces: A Hilbert space is the complete vector space with an inner product in which quantum states live.
  18. Superposition: The superposition principle says that if two states are possible, any normalised complex combination of them is also a possible state.
  19. Quantum measurement: Measurement in quantum mechanics gives a definite outcome with Born-rule probabilities and leaves the system in a corresponding state.
  20. Commutators: The commutator of two operators measures whether the order of applying them matters.
  21. Compatible and incompatible observables: Compatible observables can have definite values at the same time and share a set of eigenstates; incompatible observables can't.
  22. Infinite and finite potential wells: A particle confined in a potential well can only have certain energies.
  23. Quantum tunnelling: A quantum particle can pass through a barrier higher than its energy, because its wavefunction decays inside the barrier but doesn't vanish.
  24. Quantum harmonic oscillator: The quantum harmonic oscillator has evenly spaced energy levels and a nonzero ground-state energy.
  25. Quantum barriers and scattering: Quantum scattering describes how particles bounce off or pass through potentials.
  26. Angular momentum: In quantum mechanics angular momentum is quantised in both magnitude and direction.
  27. Spin: Spin is an intrinsic angular momentum carried by particles, with no classical analogue as literal rotation.
  28. Addition of angular momentum: When two angular momenta combine (orbital and spin, or two particles' spins), the total takes a range of quantum numbers.
  29. Identical particles: Identical quantum particles are truly indistinguishable: swapping two can't change any measurable result.
  30. Bosons and fermions: All particles are either bosons (integer spin, sociable: any number can share a state) or fermions (half-integer spin, antisocial: one per state).
  31. Pauli exclusion principle: No two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state.
  32. Hydrogen atom: Hydrogen, one electron bound to a proton, is the only atom the Schrödinger equation solves exactly.
  33. Orbital angular momentum: An electron moving around a nucleus has orbital angular momentum, quantised by l and m.
  34. Approximation methods: Most quantum problems can't be solved exactly.
  35. Perturbation theory: Perturbation theory finds approximate energies and states when a small extra term is added to a Hamiltonian we can solve.
  36. Variational method: The variational method estimates a ground-state energy by guessing a trial wavefunction with adjustable parameters and minimising its energy.
  37. WKB approximation: The WKB approximation solves the Schrödinger equation when the potential varies slowly compared with the wavelength.
  38. Density matrices: The density matrix describes quantum states including classical uncertainty about which state a system is in.
  39. Mixed and pure states: A pure state is a single quantum state vector; a mixed state is a statistical mixture, arising from classical ignorance or from entanglement with something else.
  40. Quantum entanglement: Entangled particles share a joint quantum state that can't be written as separate states for each.
  41. Decoherence: Decoherence is the loss of quantum coherence when a system interacts with its environment, which effectively measures it.
  42. Quantum information fundamentals: Quantum information theory studies how information is stored, processed and transmitted using quantum systems.

Nuclear Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Nuclear structure: The nucleus is a tightly bound cluster of protons and neutrons held together by the strong nuclear force.
  2. Protons, neutrons and nucleons: Protons and neutrons, together called nucleons, are the building blocks of nuclei.

  7. Nuclear stability: Only certain combinations of protons and neutrons are stable.
  8. Radioactive decay: Unstable nuclei spontaneously transform into more stable ones, emitting radiation.
  9. Alpha, beta and gamma decay: The three classic types of radioactive decay emit helium nuclei (alpha), electrons or positrons (beta) or high-energy photons (gamma).
  10. Decay chains and half-life: Many radioactive nuclei decay into products that are themselves radioactive, forming decay chains that end at a stable isotope.
  11. Nuclear reaction equations: Nuclear reactions are written like chemical equations, but must conserve mass number, charge, energy and momentum.
  12. Nuclear cross-sections: A cross-section measures how likely a nuclear reaction is, expressed as an effective target area.

  17. Nuclear reactors: A nuclear reactor sustains a controlled fission chain reaction, using the heat to make steam and generate electricity.
  18. Neutron moderation: Fission neutrons are born fast (~2 MeV) but U-235 fissions far more readily with slow (thermal, ~0.025 eV) neutrons.
  19. Nuclear energy and power: Nuclear power generates about 10% of the world's electricity with very low carbon emissions.
  20. Radiation detection: Radiation detectors turn invisible ionising radiation into measurable signals by collecting the charge or light it produces.
  21. Radiation shielding: Shielding reduces radiation exposure by placing material between the source and people.
  22. Nuclear spectroscopy: Nuclear spectroscopy measures the energies of radiation emitted by nuclei to map their energy levels, spins and shapes.
  23. Nuclear magnetic resonance: Nuclei with spin behave like tiny magnets that precess in a magnetic field.
  24. Nuclear astrophysics: Nuclear astrophysics explains how stars shine and how the chemical elements were made.
  25. Nuclear waste and radiation safety: Nuclear technology produces radioactive waste and exposes people to radiation, so it's governed by strict safety principles.

Unit 24: Particle Physics

Particle Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Fundamental particles: Fundamental particles have no known internal structure.
  2. Quarks and leptons: Quarks feel the strong force and are always confined inside hadrons; leptons don't feel it and can exist freely.
  3. Fermions and bosons: Particles are either fermions (half-integer spin, obeying Pauli exclusion) or bosons (integer spin, able to share states).
  4. Fundamental interactions: All known physics arises from four interactions: gravity, electromagnetism, the weak force and the strong force.
  5. Electromagnetic interaction: The electromagnetic interaction acts between electrically charged particles through the exchange of photons.
  6. Weak interaction: The weak interaction changes one type of quark or lepton into another, causing beta decay and powering the Sun's first fusion step.
  7. Strong interaction: The strong interaction binds quarks into protons, neutrons and other hadrons, and indirectly binds nuclei.
  8. Gravitational interaction: Gravity is the weakest fundamental interaction but dominates on large scales because it's always attractive and acts on all energy.
  9. Standard Model: The Standard Model is the quantum field theory of all known particles and three of the four forces.
  10. Particle generations: Quarks and leptons come in three generations, each a heavier copy of the first with the same charges and interactions.

  16. Feynman diagrams: Feynman diagrams are pictures of particle interactions that stand for precise mathematical terms in quantum field theory calculations.
  17. Quantum electrodynamics: Quantum electrodynamics (QED) is the quantum field theory of electrons, positrons and photons.
  18. Quantum chromodynamics: Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the theory of the strong interaction between quarks and gluons.
  19. Electroweak theory: Electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak force into a single interaction, broken at low energies by the Higgs field.
  20. Higgs mechanism: The Higgs mechanism explains how the W and Z bosons, quarks and charged leptons acquire mass without breaking the underlying gauge symmetry of the theory.
  21. Higgs boson: The Higgs boson is the particle excitation of the Higgs field.
  22. Neutrino oscillations: Neutrinos change flavour as they travel: an electron neutrino can later be detected as a muon or tau neutrino.
  23. CP violation: CP symmetry combines swapping particles with antiparticles (C) and mirror reflection (P).
  24. Symmetry breaking: Symmetry breaking occurs when a system's lowest-energy state lacks a symmetry that its laws possess.
  25. Particle accelerators: Particle accelerators use electric fields to speed up charged particles and magnetic fields to steer and focus them.
  26. Collider experiments: Colliders smash particle beams head-on so all their energy is available to create new particles.
  27. Particle detectors: Particle detectors reconstruct what happened in a collision by measuring the tracks, energies and identities of the particles produced.
  28. Scattering experiments: Scattering experiments fire particles at targets and measure how they deflect, lose energy or create new particles.
  29. Beyond the Standard Model: Physics beyond the Standard Model seeks to explain what it leaves out: dark matter, neutrino masses, the matter–antimatter asymmetry, gravity, and the puzzling values of its parameters.
  30. Supersymmetry: Supersymmetry (SUSY) proposes that every boson has a fermion partner and vice versa.
  31. Grand unified theories: Grand unified theories (GUTs) propose that the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces merge into one at extremely high energies (~10¹⁶ GeV).
  32. Dark matter candidates: Dark matter makes up about 85% of the universe's matter but emits no light.
  33. Open questions in particle physics: Despite the Standard Model's success, deep questions remain unanswered: what dark matter is, why matter beat antimatter, what neutrino masses are, why particles have the masses they do, and how…

Unit 25: Special Relativity

24 lessons

Special Relativity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Historical foundations of relativity: By 1900, Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism clashed: Maxwell's equations gave light a fixed speed, but in which frame?
  2. Galilean transformations: Galilean transformations convert positions and times between frames moving at constant velocity, assuming time is absolute.
  3. Inertial reference frames: An inertial frame is one in which a free object moves in a straight line at constant speed.
  4. Postulates of special relativity: Special relativity rests on two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer.
  5. Speed of light invariance: The speed of light in vacuum is the same for every inertial observer, whatever their motion or the source's motion.
  6. Lorentz transformations: The Lorentz transformations convert space and time coordinates between inertial frames moving relative to each other, keeping the speed of light the same in both.
  7. Relativity of simultaneity: Events that happen at the same time in one inertial frame generally happen at different times in another.
  8. Time dilation: A clock moving relative to you ticks more slowly than one at rest beside you, by the factor γ.
  9. Length contraction: An object moving relative to you is shorter along its direction of motion than when measured at rest, by the factor γ.
  10. Relativistic velocity addition: Velocities don't simply add in relativity.
  11. Spacetime diagrams: Spacetime diagrams plot time against position, showing events as points and objects' histories as worldlines.
  12. Minkowski spacetime: Minkowski spacetime is the four-dimensional geometry of special relativity, combining three dimensions of space and one of time.
  13. Proper time: Proper time is the time measured by a clock carried along a worldline.
  14. Four-vectors: Four-vectors combine a time-like component and three space components into objects that transform by Lorentz transformations.
  15. Relativistic momentum: Momentum in relativity is p = γmv, which grows without limit as an object approaches c. With this definition, momentum is conserved in all inertial frames.
  16. Mass-energy equivalence: Mass and energy are equivalent: any system's rest mass corresponds to an energy E = mc².
  17. Relativistic energy: A moving object's total energy is its rest energy multiplied by γ.
  18. Energy-momentum relation: The energy–momentum relation links a particle's total energy, momentum and mass, valid at any speed.
  19. Relativistic collisions: In high-energy collisions, energy and momentum are conserved as four-vectors, but rest mass isn't: kinetic energy can turn into new particles.
  20. Relativistic Doppler effect: The relativistic Doppler effect is the change in frequency of light from a moving source, including time dilation.
  21. Relativistic electromagnetism: Electricity and magnetism are two aspects of one electromagnetic field that transforms between frames.
  22. Causality and light cones: The light cone of an event divides spacetime into its causal future, causal past and the elsewhere region it can never influence.
  23. Twin paradox: One twin travels to a distant star at high speed and returns younger than the twin who stayed home.
  24. Relativistic particle dynamics: Relativistic dynamics describes how forces change momentum and energy at any speed.

Unit 26: General Relativity

23 lessons

General Relativity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Equivalence principle: The equivalence principle says that, locally, the effects of gravity are indistinguishable from those of acceleration.
  2. Accelerated reference frames: Accelerated frames experience inertial forces that mimic gravity.
  3. Gravity as spacetime curvature: General relativity describes gravity not as a force but as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
  4. Metric tensors: The metric tensor defines distances and times in spacetime, determining its geometry.
  5. Geodesics: A geodesic is the straightest possible path in curved spacetime.
  6. Curvature and the Riemann tensor: The Riemann curvature tensor measures how spacetime is curved: how much a vector changes when carried around a small loop, and how nearby geodesics converge or diverge.
  7. Einstein field equations: Einstein's field equations relate spacetime curvature to the energy and momentum within it.
  8. Stress-energy tensor: The stress–energy tensor describes the density and flow of energy and momentum in spacetime.
  9. Schwarzschild solution: The Schwarzschild solution describes spacetime outside a spherical, non-rotating, uncharged mass.
  10. Kerr black holes: The Kerr solution describes a rotating black hole, defined by just mass and spin.
  11. Gravitational time dilation: Clocks deeper in a gravitational potential run slower than clocks higher up.
  12. Gravitational redshift: Light climbing out of a gravitational well loses energy and is shifted to longer wavelengths.
  13. Perihelion precession: General relativity predicts that orbits around a massive body aren't closed ellipses: the point of closest approach slowly advances.
  14. Light bending and gravitational lensing: Light follows curved paths near massive objects.
  15. Black holes and event horizons: A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.
  16. Singularities: Singularities are places where general relativity predicts infinite curvature and the theory breaks down.
  17. Gravitational waves: Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime curvature that travel at the speed of light, produced by accelerating masses.
  18. Binary systems and gravitational radiation: Two compact objects orbiting each other emit gravitational waves, losing energy and spiralling together.
  19. Cosmological solutions: Applying general relativity to the whole universe, assuming it's uniform on large scales, gives expanding or contracting models.
  20. Friedmann equations: The Friedmann equations govern how the universe's expansion rate changes over time, given its contents: matter, radiation, dark energy and curvature.
  21. Tests of general relativity: General relativity has passed every experimental test for over a century, from the solar system to merging black holes.
  22. Numerical relativity: Numerical relativity solves Einstein's equations on supercomputers when no exact solution exists, most famously for merging black holes and neutron stars.
  23. Quantum gravity challenges: Quantum mechanics and general relativity are both spectacularly successful but don't fit together.

Unit 27: Condensed Matter Physics

35 lessons

Condensed Matter Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Crystalline and amorphous solids: In crystalline solids, atoms sit in a regular repeating pattern extending over long distances; in amorphous solids like glass, they have only short-range order.
  2. Crystal lattices and unit cells: A crystal is a lattice (an infinite array of points with identical surroundings) plus a basis (the group of atoms attached to each point).
  3. Bravais lattices: In three dimensions there are exactly 14 distinct ways to arrange points so every point has identical surroundings: the Bravais lattices.

  5. Crystal symmetry: Crystal symmetry describes the operations (rotations, reflections, inversions, translations, screw axes and glide planes) that map a crystal onto itself.
  6. Reciprocal lattices: The reciprocal lattice is the Fourier transform of the crystal lattice: a lattice in wavevector space.
  7. X-ray diffraction: X-rays have wavelengths similar to atomic spacings, so crystals diffract them into sharp beams.
  8. Phonons and lattice vibrations: Atoms in a crystal vibrate collectively in waves.
  9. Specific heat of solids: The heat capacity of solids is constant at high temperatures but falls towards zero as temperature drops.
  10. Thermal conductivity: Heat in solids is carried by phonons and, in metals, mostly by electrons.
  11. Electronic band structure: In a crystal, electrons' allowed energies form bands separated by gaps, because their waves interact with the periodic lattice.
  12. Metals, insulators and semiconductors: Whether a material conducts depends on how electrons fill its bands.
  13. Fermi energy and Fermi surfaces: The Fermi energy is the highest occupied electron energy at absolute zero; the Fermi surface is the surface in momentum space separating filled from empty states.
  14. Density of states: The density of states counts how many electron (or phonon) states there are per unit energy.
  15. Electron transport: Electron transport describes how electrons carry current and heat through materials under fields and temperature gradients.
  16. Electrical resistivity: Resistivity measures how strongly a material opposes current.
  17. Hall effect: When current flows through a conductor in a perpendicular magnetic field, a voltage appears across it.
  18. Magnetic ordering: In many materials, electron spins order spontaneously below a critical temperature into patterns: all parallel (ferromagnets), alternating (antiferromagnets), unequal antiparallel (ferrimagnets) or…

  20. Dielectric properties: Dielectrics are insulators that polarise in electric fields, storing energy and slowing light.
  21. Piezoelectricity: Piezoelectric materials produce a voltage when squeezed and change shape when a voltage is applied.
  22. Ferroelectricity: Ferroelectrics have a spontaneous electric polarisation that can be reversed by an applied field.
  23. Superconductivity: Below a critical temperature, superconductors carry current with exactly zero resistance and expel magnetic fields.
  24. Conventional and unconventional superconductors: Conventional superconductors are explained by BCS theory, with electrons paired by lattice vibrations.
  25. Cooper pairs: Cooper pairs are bound pairs of electrons with opposite momenta and spins, formed by a weak effective attraction in a metal.
  26. Josephson effect: When two superconductors are separated by a thin barrier, Cooper pairs tunnel through it, creating a supercurrent with no voltage.
  27. Semiconductor heterostructures: Heterostructures stack different semiconductors in nanometre-thin layers, engineering band edges to confine electrons.
  28. Quantum Hall effect: In a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field, the Hall resistance takes exactly quantised values h/(νe²).
  29. Topological insulators: Topological insulators are insulating inside but conduct along their surfaces or edges through special states protected by symmetry and topology.
  30. Strongly correlated electron systems: In strongly correlated materials, electron–electron interactions dominate over kinetic energy, so simple band theory fails.
  31. Low-dimensional materials: When electrons or atoms are confined to two, one or zero dimensions, new quantum effects emerge.
  32. Graphene and two-dimensional materials: Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb lattice: the first true 2D material, isolated in 2004 with sticky tape (Geim and Novoselov, Nobel 2010).
  33. Nanomaterials: Nanomaterials have structures between about 1 and 100 nm.
  34. Soft condensed matter: Soft condensed matter covers materials easily deformed by thermal fluctuations or small forces: polymers, colloids, gels, foams, liquid crystals and biological matter.
  35. Polymers, colloids and liquid crystals: Polymers are long chain molecules; colloids are particles suspended in a fluid; liquid crystals flow like liquids but have orientational order like crystals.

Unit 28: Semiconductor Physics and Electronics

25 lessons

Semiconductor Physics and Electronics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Energy bands in solids: When atoms join into a solid, their discrete energy levels broaden into bands separated by gaps.
  2. Intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors: An intrinsic semiconductor is pure: its few carriers come from electrons thermally excited across the gap.
  3. Doping: Doping adds tiny amounts of impurity atoms to a semiconductor to control its conductivity and carrier type.
  4. Electrons and holes: In semiconductors current is carried by electrons in the conduction band and by holes in the valence band.
  5. Carrier concentration: The number of free electrons and holes in a semiconductor depends on temperature, band gap and doping.
  6. Fermi level: The Fermi level is the energy at which the probability of finding an electron is one half; it's the electrochemical potential of electrons.
  7. Drift and diffusion: Charge carriers move in two ways: drift, pushed by an electric field, and diffusion, spreading from high to low concentration.
  8. Mobility and conductivity: Mobility measures how quickly carriers move in an electric field; with carrier density, it sets conductivity.
  9. p-n junctions: A p–n junction forms where p-type and n-type semiconductor meet.
  10. Diodes: A diode lets current flow easily in one direction and blocks it in the other.
  11. Transistors: A transistor uses a small signal to control a larger current, acting as an amplifier or a switch.
  12. Bipolar junction transistors: A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three regions, emitter, base and collector, forming two p–n junctions.
  13. Field-effect transistors: A field-effect transistor controls current through a channel using the electric field from a gate voltage.
  14. MOSFETs: The metal–oxide–semiconductor FET is the most manufactured device in history.
  15. Semiconductor heterojunctions: A heterojunction joins two different semiconductors with different band gaps.
  16. Photodiodes: Photodiodes convert light into current: photons absorbed in or near the depletion region create electron–hole pairs, which the built-in field separates.
  17. Solar cells: Solar cells are large-area p–n junctions that turn sunlight directly into electricity.
  18. LEDs: Light-emitting diodes produce light when electrons and holes recombine across a direct band gap in a forward-biased junction.
  19. Semiconductor lasers: Semiconductor (diode) lasers create coherent light from a forward-biased p–n junction with an optical cavity.
  20. Integrated circuits: An integrated circuit puts billions of transistors, resistors and interconnects on one chip of silicon.
  21. Quantum wells, wires and dots: Shrinking semiconductors to nanometre sizes in one, two or three dimensions confines electrons, quantising their energies.
  22. Tunnel diodes: A tunnel diode is a very heavily doped p–n junction so thin that electrons tunnel directly through it.
  23. Spintronics: Spintronics uses the electron's spin as well as its charge to store and process information.
  24. Semiconductor fabrication principles: Chips are made by repeatedly depositing, patterning and modifying thin layers on silicon wafers in ultra-clean factories.
  25. Nanoscale electronic devices: As electronic devices shrink to nanometre sizes, quantum effects, single electrons and individual atoms matter.

Unit 29: Plasma Physics

20 lessons

Plasma Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Plasma as a state of matter: A plasma is an ionised gas of free electrons and ions that behaves collectively through long-range electric and magnetic forces.
  2. Ionisation and recombination: Plasmas form when atoms lose electrons through collisions, radiation or strong fields, and they're maintained by a balance between ionisation and recombination.
  3. Debye shielding: In a plasma, free charges rearrange to screen out any electric potential, so the field of a charge falls off exponentially beyond a short distance.
  4. Debye length: The Debye length is the distance over which charges in a plasma screen each other.
  5. Plasma frequency: The plasma frequency is the natural oscillation frequency of electrons displaced in a plasma.
  6. Charged-particle motion: Understanding how individual charged particles move in electric and magnetic fields is the starting point of plasma physics.
  7. Plasma oscillations: Displace electrons slightly in a plasma and the resulting charge separation pulls them back, so they oscillate at the plasma frequency.
  8. Magnetised plasmas: In a magnetic field, plasma behaviour becomes strongly directional: particles move freely along field lines but are tied to them across.
  9. Magnetohydrodynamics: Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) treats a plasma as an electrically conducting fluid interacting with magnetic fields.
  10. Plasma waves: Plasmas support a rich variety of waves: electrostatic and electromagnetic, involving electrons, ions or both, with magnetic fields adding more modes.
  11. Alfvén waves: Alfvén waves are transverse oscillations of magnetic field lines and plasma, like waves on a plucked string, with the field's tension providing the restoring force.
  12. Plasma instabilities: Plasmas store energy in gradients of density, temperature, current and velocity, and often release it through instabilities that grow rapidly.
  13. Magnetic confinement: Magnetic confinement holds a hot plasma away from material walls using magnetic fields, since charged particles spiral along field lines.
  14. Tokamaks and stellarators: Tokamaks and stellarators are the two main toroidal magnetic confinement designs for fusion.
  15. Inertial confinement fusion: Inertial confinement fusion compresses a tiny fuel capsule so fast and hard that it fuses before it can fly apart.
  16. Fusion energy: Fusion energy aims to generate electricity from the same reactions that power the Sun, using abundant fuels and producing no carbon emissions or long-lived high-level waste.
  17. Solar and astrophysical plasmas: Almost all visible matter in the universe is plasma.
  18. Space plasma physics: Space plasma physics studies plasmas in the solar system: the solar wind, planetary magnetospheres and ionospheres.
  19. Plasma diagnostics: Plasma diagnostics measure temperatures, densities, fields and flows in plasmas that are too hot or remote for ordinary instruments.
  20. Industrial and technological plasmas: Low-temperature plasmas are essential industrial tools: they etch and deposit layers on every computer chip, light our streets, cut and weld metals, sterilise medical equipment and treat surfaces.

Unit 31: Cosmology

24 lessons

Cosmology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The observable universe: The observable universe is the region from which light has had time to reach us since the Big Bang.
  2. Cosmological principles: The cosmological principle states that, on large enough scales, the universe looks the same everywhere (homogeneous) and in every direction (isotropic).
  3. Expansion of the universe: The universe is expanding: distant galaxies are moving apart, not through space but because space itself stretches.
  4. Hubble's law: Hubble's law states that galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance.
  5. Redshift and distance measurements: Redshift measures how much the universe has expanded since light left a source; turning redshift into distance requires a cosmological model.
  6. Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker models: FLRW models are the family of general-relativistic universes that are homogeneous and isotropic.
  7. Big Bang cosmology: The Big Bang theory states that the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  8. Cosmic microwave background: The cosmic microwave background is the afterglow of the Big Bang: light released when the universe became transparent 380 000 years after it began.
  9. Primordial nucleosynthesis: In the first few minutes after the Big Bang, protons and neutrons fused into deuterium, helium and a little lithium.
  10. Structure formation: Galaxies, clusters and the cosmic web grew from tiny density fluctuations in the early universe, amplified by gravity over billions of years.
  11. Dark matter: Dark matter is invisible matter making up about 27% of the universe's energy (85% of its matter).
  12. Dark energy: Dark energy is the mysterious component causing the universe's expansion to accelerate.
  13. Cosmological constant: The cosmological constant Λ is a term in Einstein's equations representing a constant energy density of empty space.
  14. Inflationary cosmology: Inflation is a proposed period of extremely rapid exponential expansion in the first ~10⁻³² s.
  15. Baryon acoustic oscillations: Before recombination, sound waves rippled through the hot plasma of photons and baryons.
  16. Large-scale structure: On scales of millions of light-years, galaxies form a cosmic web: dense clusters linked by filaments and sheets surrounding vast empty voids.
  17. Galaxy surveys: Galaxy surveys map the positions, distances and properties of millions to billions of galaxies.
  18. Cosmic distance ladder: The cosmic distance ladder measures distances step by step: each method calibrates the next, reaching from the Solar System to the edge of the observable universe.
  19. Age and geometry of the universe: Combining the expansion rate and the universe's contents gives its age, 13.8 billion years, and its geometry, flat to within 0.2%.
  20. Open, flat and closed cosmologies: The overall geometry of the universe depends on its total density compared with the critical density.
  21. Matter-energy density parameters: The universe's contents are summarised by density parameters: each component's density as a fraction of the critical density.
  22. Modified gravity: Modified gravity theories change general relativity on large scales or at low accelerations, aiming to explain cosmic acceleration or galaxy dynamics without dark energy or dark matter.
  23. Cyclic and alternative cosmological models: Besides the standard inflationary Big Bang, physicists explore alternatives: cyclic universes, bouncing cosmologies and steady-state theories.
  24. Observational constraints on cosmology: Modern cosmology combines many independent observations, each constraining different parameters.

Quantum Information and Quantum Computing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Qubits and quantum states: A qubit is the quantum version of a bit: a two-level system that can be in 0, 1 or any superposition of both.
  2. Bloch sphere: The Bloch sphere represents any pure single-qubit state as a point on a unit sphere.
  3. Quantum gates: Quantum gates are unitary operations that transform qubit states reversibly.

  5. Quantum measurement: Measurement is how quantum information becomes classical.
  6. Entanglement: Entanglement is a correlation between quantum systems so strong that the state of the whole cannot be described by states of the parts.
  7. Bell inequalities: Bell inequalities test whether nature can be explained by local hidden variables, pre-existing values with no faster-than-light influences.
  8. Quantum teleportation: Quantum teleportation transfers an unknown quantum state from one place to another using a shared entangled pair and two bits of classical communication.
  9. Quantum cryptography: Quantum cryptography uses the laws of physics, rather than the difficulty of maths problems, to secure communication.
  10. Quantum key distribution: Quantum key distribution (QKD) lets two parties share a secret random key over an insecure channel, with any eavesdropping revealed by errors.
  11. Quantum algorithms: Quantum algorithms use superposition, interference and entanglement to solve certain problems faster than any known classical method.
  12. Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm: The Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm decides with a single query whether a function is constant or balanced, where a deterministic classical algorithm may need exponentially many queries.

  14. Shor's algorithm: Shor's algorithm factors large integers in polynomial time on a quantum computer, exponentially faster than the best known classical algorithms.
  15. Quantum error correction: Quantum error correction protects fragile quantum information by spreading one logical qubit across many physical qubits.
  16. Quantum noise and decoherence: Decoherence is the loss of quantum behaviour as a qubit becomes entangled with its environment.
  17. Fault-tolerant quantum computing: Fault-tolerant quantum computing performs arbitrarily long computations reliably using error-corrected logical qubits, even though every physical component is imperfect.
  18. Quantum complexity: Quantum complexity theory classifies problems by the resources a quantum computer needs to solve them.
  19. Quantum simulation: Quantum simulation uses controllable quantum systems to model other quantum systems, such as molecules, materials and field theories, that are exponentially hard for classical computers.
  20. Quantum communication: Quantum communication sends quantum states between distant parties, enabling secure key distribution, teleportation and distributed quantum computing.
  21. Quantum networks: Quantum networks connect quantum processors and sensors by distributing entanglement between nodes.
  22. Physical implementations of qubits: Many physical systems can act as qubits, each with distinct strengths in coherence, speed, connectivity and scalability.
  23. Superconducting qubits: Superconducting qubits are tiny electrical circuits cooled near absolute zero that behave like artificial atoms.
  24. Trapped-ion systems: Trapped-ion quantum computers store qubits in the internal states of individual ions held by electromagnetic fields in vacuum.
  25. Neutral-atom quantum computing: Neutral-atom quantum computers hold individual atoms in arrays of tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers.
  26. Photonic quantum computing: Photonic quantum computing encodes qubits in single photons or squeezed light, which travel at the speed of light, barely decohere and work at room temperature.

Unit 38: Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos

21 lessons

Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Linear and nonlinear systems: A linear system's response is proportional to its input, and solutions can be added together.
  2. Nonlinear differential equations: Nonlinear differential equations describe how systems change when rates depend nonlinearly on the state.
  3. Fixed points and stability: Fixed points are states where a system stays put.
  4. Phase-space analysis: Phase-space analysis pictures every possible state of a system as a point and its evolution as a trajectory.
  5. Bifurcation theory: A bifurcation is a qualitative change in a system's behaviour as a parameter crosses a critical value: equilibria appear, disappear or swap stability, or oscillations begin.
  6. Period-doubling: Period-doubling is a route to chaos in which an oscillation repeatedly doubles its period as a parameter increases: period 2, 4, 8 and so on, faster and faster, until chaos appears.
  7. Deterministic chaos: Deterministic chaos is complex, unpredictable-looking behaviour arising from simple rules with no randomness.
  8. Sensitivity to initial conditions: Sensitivity to initial conditions, the butterfly effect, means that two almost identical starting states end up completely different.
  9. Lyapunov exponents: Lyapunov exponents measure the average exponential rate at which nearby trajectories separate or converge.
  10. Strange attractors: A strange attractor is the set a chaotic dissipative system settles onto: bounded, with a fractal structure and chaotic motion on it.
  11. Logistic map: The logistic map xₙ₊₁ = rxₙ(1 − xₙ) is a one-line model of population growth that shows the whole route from steady behaviour to chaos.
  12. Lorenz system: The Lorenz system is three simple equations derived from atmospheric convection that became the icon of chaos.
  13. Fractals: Fractals are shapes with detail at every scale, often self-similar, and with non-integer dimensions.
  14. Self-organisation: Self-organisation is the spontaneous emergence of order from local interactions, without a designer or external template.
  15. Synchronisation: Synchronisation is the tendency of coupled oscillators to fall into step.
  16. Coupled nonlinear oscillators: Coupled nonlinear oscillators exchange energy and phase in rich ways: normal modes, beats, localisation, resonance and chaos.
  17. Pattern formation: Pattern formation is how uniform states break into spatial structures such as stripes, spots, spirals and hexagons.
  18. Reaction-diffusion systems: Reaction-diffusion systems combine local chemical reactions with diffusion, producing waves, spirals and stationary Turing patterns.
  19. Solitons and nonlinear waves: Solitons are solitary waves that keep their shape while travelling and survive collisions intact, because nonlinearity exactly balances dispersion.
  20. Chaos in mechanical and electrical systems: Chaos is not just theory: it appears in pendulums, rotating machines, electronic circuits, lasers and orbits.
  21. Complex systems and emergent behaviour: Complex systems consist of many interacting parts whose collective behaviour is not obvious from the parts alone.

Computational Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  12. N-body simulations: N-body simulations follow many particles interacting through long-range forces, mainly gravity.
  13. Computational fluid dynamics: Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solves the equations of fluid flow numerically, predicting forces, heat transfer and mixing.
  14. Numerical linear algebra: Numerical linear algebra solves systems of equations, least-squares problems and matrix factorisations, the core of almost every simulation.
  15. Eigenvalue algorithms: Eigenvalue problems give normal modes, energy levels, stability and principal components.
  16. Fourier transforms and spectral methods: Fourier transforms decompose signals and fields into frequencies, and the fast Fourier transform computes them in N log N operations.
  17. Optimisation and parameter estimation: Optimisation finds the best parameters for a model or design by minimising a cost function.
  18. Random number generation: Simulations need vast numbers of random-looking numbers.
  19. Error and stability analysis: Every numerical result carries errors from modelling, discretisation, iteration and round-off.
  20. Parallel computing: Parallel computing splits a problem across many processors that work simultaneously.
  21. High-performance computing: High-performance computing (HPC) uses supercomputers with hundreds of thousands of processors and accelerators to tackle the largest scientific problems: climate projections, fusion plasmas,…
  22. GPU computing: Graphics processing units contain thousands of simple cores that run the same instructions on many data at once.
  23. Scientific visualisation: Scientific visualisation turns numbers into images that reveal structure, trends and errors.
  24. Data analysis and fitting: Data analysis extracts physical meaning from measurements and simulations: estimating parameters, testing hypotheses and quantifying uncertainty.
  25. Reproducible computational experiments: A computational result is reproducible if others (or you, later) can rerun the code and get the same answer.
  26. Verification and validation of simulations: Verification asks whether a code solves its equations correctly; validation asks whether those equations describe reality well enough.
  27. Machine learning in physics: Machine learning finds patterns in data and builds fast models from examples.
  28. Physics-informed neural networks: Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) train a neural network to satisfy both data and the governing equations, by putting the PDE residual into the loss function.
  29. Digital twins and surrogate models: A surrogate model is a fast approximation of an expensive simulation; a digital twin is a living virtual model of a real system, continuously updated with sensor data.

  3. Principle of least action: The principle of least (stationary) action says a system follows the path that makes the action S = ∫L dt stationary.

  5. Canonical transformations: Canonical transformations change phase-space variables (q, p) to new ones (Q, P) while preserving the form of Hamilton's equations.
  6. Hamilton–Jacobi theory: Hamilton–Jacobi theory seeks a canonical transformation that makes the new Hamiltonian zero, so all new variables are constant.
  7. Symplectic geometry: Symplectic geometry is the mathematical language of Hamiltonian mechanics.
  8. Noether's theorem: Noether's theorem states that every continuous symmetry of the action gives a conserved quantity.
  9. Continuous and discrete symmetries: Symmetries are transformations that leave physical laws unchanged.
  10. Lie groups and Lie algebras: Lie groups are continuous symmetry groups, like rotations, whose elements depend smoothly on parameters.
  11. Representation theory: A representation assigns matrices to group elements so that the group's multiplication is preserved.
  12. Tensor calculus: Tensors are quantities that transform in a definite way under coordinate changes, so equations written with them hold in every coordinate system.
  13. Differential geometry: Differential geometry studies curves, surfaces and higher-dimensional spaces using calculus.
  14. Manifolds and curvature: A manifold is a space that looks flat locally but may be curved or twisted globally, like the Earth's surface.
  15. Classical field theory: Classical field theory treats physical quantities as continuous fields spread through space and time, such as electromagnetic, gravitational and fluid fields.
  16. Quantum field theory: Quantum field theory combines quantum mechanics and special relativity by treating particles as excitations of underlying fields.
  17. Path integrals: Feynman's path integral says a quantum amplitude is the sum over every possible path, each weighted by e^(iS/ħ).
  18. Feynman diagrams: Feynman diagrams are pictorial shorthand for terms in perturbative calculations of particle interactions.
  19. Renormalisation: Renormalisation handles infinities that appear in quantum field theory loop calculations by absorbing them into measurable parameters like mass and charge.
  20. Gauge theories: Gauge theories describe forces arising from local symmetries: demanding that a phase or internal rotation can vary from point to point forces the existence of gauge fields.
  21. Effective field theories: Effective field theories describe physics at a given energy scale using only the relevant degrees of freedom, with unknown high-energy effects packaged into a few parameters.
  22. Scattering theory: Scattering theory describes what happens when particles collide and fly apart.
  23. Green's functions: A Green's function is the response of a linear system to a point source.
  24. Perturbative and non-perturbative methods: Perturbation theory expands answers in powers of a small parameter, giving most of physics' precise predictions.
  25. Solvable models: Exactly solvable models are idealised systems whose behaviour can be computed completely.
  26. Integrable systems: Integrable systems have as many independent conserved quantities as degrees of freedom, so their motion is regular and solvable rather than chaotic.
  27. Mathematical methods for boundary-value problems: Boundary-value problems ask for solutions of differential equations that satisfy conditions on the edges of a region.

Unit 41: Classical Field Theory

15 lessons

Classical Field Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Scalar, vector and tensor fields: A field assigns a quantity to every point in space and time: a number for a scalar field, an arrow for a vector field, or a matrix-like object for a tensor field.
  2. Field equations: Field equations are partial differential equations that determine how fields vary in space and time, given their sources.
  3. Lagrangian field theory: Lagrangian field theory derives field equations from a Lagrangian density integrated over spacetime.
  4. Hamiltonian field theory: Hamiltonian field theory treats a field and its conjugate momentum density as canonical variables at every point.
  5. Field symmetries: Symmetries of a field theory are transformations of the fields or spacetime that leave the action unchanged.
  6. Noether currents: Each continuous symmetry of a field theory gives a Noether current, a four-vector j^μ whose divergence vanishes.
  7. Conservation laws: Conservation laws state that certain quantities, such as energy, momentum, angular momentum and charge, stay constant in isolated systems.
  8. Electromagnetic field theory: Electromagnetism is the prototype classical field theory: a gauge field A_μ, a Lagrangian, Maxwell's equations as field equations, and conservation of energy, momentum and charge.
  9. Stress-energy tensors: The stress-energy tensor T^(μν) collects energy density, momentum density, energy flux and stress (pressure and shear) into one symmetric object.
  10. Classical wave fields: Wave fields carry energy and information through space as oscillations of a field.
  11. Gauge invariance: Gauge invariance means that certain changes to the potentials leave all physical fields and predictions unchanged.
  12. Green's functions for field equations: Green's functions solve field equations for point sources, so any source distribution can be handled by superposition.
  13. Boundary conditions: Boundary conditions specify how fields behave at edges and interfaces, completing a field problem.
  14. Radiation from sources: Accelerating charges radiate electromagnetic waves, and oscillating masses radiate gravitational waves.
  15. Continuum field models: Continuum models treat matter as smooth fields rather than discrete atoms: density, velocity, displacement and order parameters.

Unit 42: Quantum Field Theory

22 lessons

Quantum Field Theory: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Quantum fields and particles: In quantum field theory, the fundamental objects are fields filling all of space, and particles are their quantised excitations.
  2. Creation and annihilation operators: Creation and annihilation operators add or remove a quantum of a field mode.
  3. Canonical quantisation: Canonical quantisation turns a classical field theory into a quantum one by promoting fields and conjugate momenta to operators with equal-time commutation relations.
  4. Path-integral quantisation: Path-integral quantisation computes quantum amplitudes by integrating over all possible field configurations, each weighted by e^(iS/ħ).
  5. Propagators: A propagator gives the amplitude for a particle to travel from one spacetime point to another.
  6. Feynman rules: Feynman rules translate diagrams into mathematical expressions for scattering amplitudes.
  7. Scattering amplitudes: Scattering amplitudes are the central predictions of QFT: complex numbers whose squared magnitudes give probabilities of particles scattering into particular final states.
  8. Perturbation theory: Perturbation theory in QFT expands amplitudes in powers of small couplings, with each order corresponding to Feynman diagrams with more vertices and loops.
  9. Divergences and regularisation: Loop diagrams in QFT often give infinite integrals because virtual particles can have arbitrarily large momenta.
  10. Renormalisation: Renormalisation turns divergent loop calculations into finite predictions by expressing results in terms of measured quantities.
  11. Gauge invariance: In quantum field theory, gauge invariance is the principle that fixes how matter couples to force carriers.
  12. Quantum electrodynamics: Quantum electrodynamics is the quantum field theory of electrons, positrons and photons.
  13. Quantum chromodynamics: Quantum chromodynamics is the SU(3) gauge theory of quarks and gluons, describing the strong force that binds quarks into protons, neutrons and other hadrons.
  14. Electroweak theory: Electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak force in an SU(2) × U(1) gauge theory.
  15. Spontaneous symmetry breaking: Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the laws are symmetric but the lowest-energy state is not.
  16. Higgs mechanism: The Higgs mechanism gives mass to the W and Z bosons through spontaneous breaking of electroweak gauge symmetry by the Higgs field.
  17. Effective field theory: Effective field theory describes physics at a chosen energy scale using only the relevant fields, with heavier physics summarised by higher-dimension operators.
  18. Renormalisation group: The renormalisation group describes how a theory's couplings and effective description change with the scale at which it is probed.
  19. Anomalies: An anomaly is a classical symmetry broken by quantum effects.
  20. Non-Abelian gauge theories: Non-Abelian gauge theories, or Yang–Mills theories, use symmetry groups whose transformations don't commute.
  21. Lattice field theory: Lattice field theory discretises spacetime into a grid so quantum field theories can be computed numerically from first principles.
  22. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime: Quantum field theory in curved spacetime studies quantum fields on a classical, curved background geometry.

Unit 43: Nuclear and Particle Detection Methods

19 lessons

Nuclear and Particle Detection Methods: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Ionisation chambers: An ionisation chamber measures radiation by collecting the electrons and ions it creates in a gas.
  2. Geiger–Müller counters: A Geiger–Müller tube produces a large, uniform pulse each time radiation triggers an avalanche in its gas.
  3. Scintillation detectors: Scintillation detectors convert radiation energy into flashes of visible light, which photodetectors turn into electrical pulses.
  4. Semiconductor radiation detectors: Semiconductor detectors collect electron–hole pairs created by radiation in a reverse-biased diode.
  5. Photomultiplier tubes: A photomultiplier tube converts a faint flash of light into a measurable electrical pulse by turning photons into electrons and multiplying them in a chain of dynodes.
  6. Silicon tracking detectors: Silicon tracking detectors measure where charged particles pass with micrometre precision.
  7. Time projection chambers: A time projection chamber is a large gas or liquid volume where charged particles leave ionisation trails that drift to a readout plane.
  8. Calorimeters: Calorimeters measure particle energy by absorbing it completely and sampling the resulting shower.
  9. Cherenkov detectors: A charged particle moving faster than light in a medium emits Cherenkov radiation, a cone of blue light like an optical sonic boom.
  10. Time-of-flight measurements: Time-of-flight systems measure how long a particle takes to travel a known distance.
  11. Particle identification: Particle identification (PID) determines what kind of particle left a signal: electron, muon, pion, kaon, proton, photon, neutron or neutrino.
  12. Trigger systems: Trigger systems decide in microseconds which collisions to keep, since detectors produce far more data than can be stored.
  13. Event reconstruction: Event reconstruction turns millions of raw detector signals into physics objects: tracks, vertices, electrons, photons, muons, jets and missing energy.
  14. Background suppression: Background suppression removes ordinary events that mimic the signal being searched for.
  15. Detector calibration: Calibration translates raw detector signals into physical quantities such as energy, position and time.
  16. Radiation spectroscopy: Radiation spectroscopy measures the energy distribution of emitted gamma rays, X-rays, alpha and beta particles to identify isotopes and study nuclear structure.
  17. Accelerator instrumentation: Accelerator instrumentation measures the beam's position, size, intensity, energy and losses so operators can steer, focus and protect a machine.
  18. Collider data analysis: Collider data analysis turns petabytes of recorded collisions into physics measurements: cross-sections, masses, couplings and searches for new particles.
  19. Statistical significance of discoveries: Particle physicists claim a discovery when the chance that background alone produced the observed excess is below about 1 in 3.5 million: five sigma.

Unit 49: Historical Development of Physics

Historical Development of Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Ancient Greek natural philosophy: Ancient Greek thinkers sought natural, rational explanations for the world instead of myths.

  7. Development of thermodynamics: Thermodynamics grew from efforts to improve steam engines into a universal science of energy, heat and entropy.
  8. Electromagnetism and Maxwell: In the 19th century electricity and magnetism were unified into one theory, culminating in Maxwell's equations, which predicted electromagnetic waves travelling at the speed of light.
  9. Discovery of radioactivity: In just a few years around 1900, the discovery of X-rays and radioactivity revealed that atoms have internal structure and can transform.
  10. Origins of quantum theory: Quantum theory began with puzzles classical physics couldn't solve: blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect and atomic spectra.
  11. Development of special and general relativity: Einstein's relativity overturned absolute space and time.
  12. Nuclear physics and the atomic age: Nuclear physics advanced rapidly from the neutron's discovery in 1932 to fission in 1938 and the first reactor and atomic bombs in the 1940s.
  13. Development of quantum mechanics: In the mid-1920s, a new quantum mechanics replaced the old quantum theory.
  14. Particle physics and the Standard Model: Particle physics grew from cosmic-ray discoveries in the 1930s to the Standard Model of the 1970s and the Higgs boson in 2012.
  15. Space exploration and modern astrophysics: The 20th century transformed astronomy: galaxies, the expanding universe, the cosmic microwave background, pulsars, black holes and exoplanets were discovered, while spacecraft visited every planet.
  16. History of computing in physics: Physics and computing grew together: physicists built early computers, invented simulation methods and created the World Wide Web.
  17. Women and underrepresented groups in physics: People of many backgrounds have shaped physics, though women and other underrepresented groups often faced exclusion and saw their work overlooked.
  18. Scientific institutions and funding: Physics depends on institutions, from universities and national laboratories to international collaborations and funding agencies.
  19. Physics, technology and society: Physics drives technology, and technology changes society: steam engines, electricity, radio, nuclear power, semiconductors, lasers, GPS and the internet.
  20. Philosophy and history of scientific revolutions: Philosophers and historians of science ask how scientific knowledge grows and changes.

Unit 50: Philosophy and Foundations of Physics

23 lessons

Philosophy and Foundations of Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Scientific realism and instrumentalism: Scientific realism holds that our best theories describe a real world, including unobservable things like electrons and quarks.
  2. Explanation and prediction in physics: Physics both predicts what will happen and explains why it happens.
  3. Laws of nature: Laws of nature, like Newton's gravitation or the conservation of energy, seem to express how the universe must behave.
  4. Causality and determinism: Causality concerns how events bring about others; determinism holds that the present state fixes the entire future.
  5. Probability and chance: Probability is everywhere in physics, from statistical mechanics to quantum measurement.
  6. Measurement and observation: Measurement connects theory to the world, but it is never simple.
  7. The problem of induction: Physics generalises from finite observations to universal laws, but logic alone cannot justify this leap.
  8. Reductionism and emergence: Reductionism holds that complex phenomena can be explained by simpler underlying parts and laws.
  9. Space and time: What are space and time?
  10. The nature of spacetime: General relativity treats spacetime as a dynamical, curved entity that carries energy and waves.
  11. Interpretations of quantum mechanics: Quantum mechanics predicts experimental results with extraordinary accuracy, yet physicists disagree about what it says reality is like.
  12. Copenhagen interpretation: The Copenhagen interpretation, developed by Bohr, Heisenberg and others in the 1920s, became the standard view taught in textbooks.
  13. Many-worlds interpretation: The many-worlds interpretation, proposed by Hugh Everett in 1957, takes the quantum wavefunction literally and denies collapse.
  14. Pilot-wave theory: Pilot-wave theory, developed by de Broglie (1927) and revived by David Bohm (1952), says particles always have definite positions, guided by the wavefunction.
  15. Objective collapse theories: Objective collapse theories modify quantum mechanics so that wavefunctions collapse spontaneously and physically, rarely for single particles but rapidly for large objects.
  16. Quantum measurement problem: The quantum measurement problem asks how definite outcomes arise from quantum superpositions, given that the Schrödinger equation never produces a single result.
  17. Locality and nonlocality: Locality means things are influenced only by their immediate surroundings, with influences spreading no faster than light.
  18. Bell's theorem: Bell's theorem (1964) proves that no theory based on local hidden variables can reproduce all predictions of quantum mechanics.
  19. Scientific explanation and unification: Unification, explaining diverse phenomena with fewer principles, has driven physics from Newton's gravity to Maxwell's electromagnetism and the electroweak theory.
  20. Symmetry and physical law: Symmetry is one of physics's deepest principles: laws stay the same under certain transformations.
  21. Model dependence and idealisation: Physics relies on models that simplify reality: frictionless planes, point masses, ideal gases, perfect crystals.
  22. Underdetermination of theory by evidence: Underdetermination says that evidence alone may not decide between rival theories: different theories can fit the same data.
  23. The limits of scientific knowledge: Physics has explained a vast range of phenomena, but are there limits to what it can know?

  11. Peer review and research critique: Peer review is how scientists evaluate each other's work before publication, checking methods, analysis and conclusions.
  12. Communicating uncertainty: Every measurement and prediction has uncertainty, and communicating it well is essential for honest science and good decisions.
  13. Public engagement with physics: Public engagement shares physics with society through talks, festivals, museums, media and citizen science, and listens to public concerns.
  14. Science communication and misinformation: Misinformation about physics, such as claims about radiation, 5G, climate or free energy, spreads quickly online.
  15. Teaching and assessing conceptual understanding: Understanding physics means more than plugging numbers into formulas: it means grasping concepts deeply enough to explain and apply them in new situations.
  16. Identifying misconceptions: Learners bring intuitive ideas about the physical world, many of them reasonable but incorrect.
  17. Interdisciplinary communication: Physicists increasingly work with biologists, engineers, clinicians, economists, data scientists and policymakers.

Unit 52: Advanced Problem-Solving Skills

17 lessons

Advanced Problem-Solving Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Identifying relevant physical principles: The hardest step in many physics problems is deciding which principles apply.
  2. Defining systems and boundaries: Choosing the system, what's included and what's outside, determines which forces are internal or external, which energies are counted and which quantities are conserved.
  3. Choosing coordinate systems: The right coordinate system can make a problem simple: aligning axes with motion, using polar coordinates for circles and spherical coordinates for spheres.
  4. Drawing free-body and field diagrams: Free-body diagrams isolate one object and show every force on it, while field diagrams map electric, magnetic or gravitational fields.
  5. Applying conservation laws: Conservation laws, of energy, momentum, angular momentum and charge, are among the most powerful problem-solving tools because they connect initial and final states without needing details in between.
  6. Deriving equations from fundamental laws: Many problems require building your own equations from fundamental laws rather than using memorised formulas.
  7. Checking units and dimensions: Dimensional analysis checks whether equations make sense and can even predict how quantities depend on each other.
  8. Evaluating limiting cases: Checking what an answer does in extreme or special cases, like zero mass, infinite time or a vanishing angle, is a powerful test.
  9. Estimating numerical answers: Before calculating, estimate roughly what the answer should be.
  10. Analysing approximations: Approximations make problems tractable: small angles, weak fields, low speeds, thin films, ideal gases.
  11. Solving coupled equations: Many physical systems involve several interacting quantities described by coupled equations: masses on springs, circuits with multiple loops, predator–prey populations and simultaneous force balances.
  12. Selecting appropriate mathematical techniques: Physics problems use a toolkit of mathematical techniques: calculus, vectors, differential equations, series, complex numbers, linear algebra and Fourier analysis.
  13. Comparing analytical and numerical solutions: Analytical solutions give exact formulas and insight; numerical solutions handle problems no formula can.
  14. Sensitivity analysis: Sensitivity analysis asks how much a result changes when inputs change.
  15. Physical interpretation of mathematical results: A mathematical answer is only half the job: you must understand what it means physically.
  16. Error checking and independent verification: Even experts make mistakes, so good physicists check their work systematically and seek independent verification.
  17. Communicating assumptions and limitations: Every physics result rests on assumptions and has limitations.

Unit 53: Interdisciplinary Physics

22 lessons

Interdisciplinary Physics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Physics and chemistry: Physics and chemistry share foundations: quantum mechanics explains chemical bonds, thermodynamics governs reactions and spectroscopy reveals molecular structure.
  2. Physical chemistry: Physical chemistry applies physics to chemical systems: thermodynamics, kinetics, quantum chemistry and spectroscopy.
  3. Chemical physics: Chemical physics studies chemical processes with the tools and concepts of physics: molecular dynamics, quantum scattering, laser spectroscopy and statistical mechanics.
  4. Physics and biology: Physics brings quantitative tools and principles to biology: from the mechanics of cells and the thermodynamics of metabolism to imaging and the information processing of neurons.
  5. Biomechanics: Biomechanics applies mechanics to living bodies: how bones bear loads, muscles generate force, joints move and organisms walk, swim and fly.
  6. Physics and medicine: Physics transforms medicine through imaging, radiation therapy, diagnostics and devices.
  7. Physics and neuroscience: The brain is an electrical, chemical and computational system that physics helps measure and model.
  8. Physics and materials science: Materials science and physics together explain and design materials: metals, semiconductors, polymers, ceramics and composites.
  9. Physics and computer science: Physics and computer science are deeply linked: physics provides the hardware (transistors, lasers, magnetic storage), computation powers simulation and data analysis, and ideas flow both ways in…
  10. Physics and mathematics: Mathematics is the language of physics, and physics has inspired some of the deepest mathematics.
  11. Physics and engineering: Engineering applies physics to design and build: bridges, engines, circuits, aircraft, power grids and communication systems.
  12. Physics and environmental science: Environmental science relies on physics to understand the atmosphere, oceans, pollution, energy flows and climate.
  13. Physics and Earth science: Geophysics applies physics to the Earth: its interior, gravity, magnetism, earthquakes, tectonics and resources.
  14. Physics and economics: Econophysics applies tools from statistical physics to economic and financial systems: random walks, power laws, network models and agent-based simulations.
  15. Complex systems: Complex systems contain many interacting parts whose collective behaviour can't be predicted easily from the parts alone.
  16. Network science: Network science studies systems as nodes connected by links: social networks, the internet, power grids, food webs, neurons and proteins.
  17. Statistical modelling of social systems: Statistical physics models help understand social phenomena: opinion formation, segregation, crowd motion, voting and cultural spread.
  18. Climate and energy systems: Climate and energy are tightly linked: burning fossil fuels emits greenhouse gases, while low-carbon energy systems need careful design to keep power reliable.
  19. Nanotechnology: Nanotechnology manipulates matter at scales of 1–100 nm, where quantum effects and surfaces dominate.
  20. Quantum technologies: Quantum technologies exploit superposition, entanglement and quantum measurement for practical uses: computing, secure communication, ultra-precise sensing and timing.
  21. Scientific machine learning: Scientific machine learning combines data-driven methods with physical knowledge.
  22. Cross-disciplinary research design: Cross-disciplinary research tackles problems no single field can solve alone, such as climate change, pandemics, energy and brain science.

Unit 54: Research and Professional Physics Skills

20 lessons

Research and Professional Physics Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Reading and evaluating scientific papers: Reading research papers efficiently and critically is a core professional skill.
  2. Literature searches and systematic reviews: A literature search finds what is already known before starting research, avoiding duplication and building on others' work.
  3. Formulating research questions: A good research question is specific, answerable, significant and feasible.
  4. Developing theoretical models: Theoretical models explain observations and make predictions using physical principles and mathematics.
  5. Designing experiments: Experimental design determines whether a study can answer its question convincingly.
  6. Writing research proposals: A research proposal persuades others that a project is worth doing, feasible and well planned.
  7. Grant applications and project planning: Research is funded through competitive grants from government agencies, charities and industry.
  8. Laboratory management: Running a laboratory involves more than doing experiments: managing equipment, budgets, safety, people, data and schedules.
  9. Research ethics and integrity: Research integrity means honesty, rigour, transparency and accountability in how research is done and reported.
  10. Data management and documentation: Research data management keeps data organised, safe, documented and shareable throughout and after a project.
  11. Version control and reproducible workflows: Version control systems like git track every change to code and documents, enabling collaboration, rollback and reproducibility.
  12. Collaboration across disciplines: Modern physics research is collaborative, from small teams to collaborations of thousands.
  13. Research presentations and posters: Presenting research at group meetings, conferences and seminars builds your reputation, invites feedback and spreads ideas.
  14. Scientific writing and publishing: Scientific writing communicates research precisely and clearly so others can understand, evaluate and build on it.
  15. Peer review: Peer review is how experts evaluate research for publication, funding and facility time.
  16. Intellectual property and technology transfer: Physics research can produce inventions with commercial value.
  17. Project management: Project management keeps research on track: defining goals, planning tasks, managing time, resources and risks, and communicating progress.
  18. Research impact and public engagement: Research impact is the difference research makes beyond academia: to the economy, society, health, policy, culture and the environment.
  19. Communicating with industry and policymakers: Physicists increasingly advise industry and government.
  20. Careers in academia, industry and scientific computing: A physics education opens many careers: academic research, industry R&D, engineering, data science, finance, medicine, teaching, policy and entrepreneurship.
