University Study Skills: a free course on UnderGradify

UnderGradify's free University Study Skills course runs from Starting University and Independent Learning to Knowing Yourself: 189 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for undergraduates at university.

General study skills, not your university's rules. UnderGradify teaches study skills that apply across UK universities and subjects. Universities set their own regulations on assessment, extensions, referencing styles, academic misconduct and the use of generative AI, and these differ between institutions, departments and even modules: always follow your own university's regulations, your module handbook and your tutors' instructions where they differ from anything here. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by an academic skills specialist, so they may contain errors. Nothing here is medical, financial or legal advice; for wellbeing, disability or money matters speak to your university's support services or your GP, and in an emergency call 999 or Samaritans on 116 123 (UK).

Schools and lessons

School 1: Starting University and Independent Learning

19 lessons

Starting University and Independent Learning: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is different about university study: University study differs from school or college less in difficulty than in who is responsible for learning.
  2. Independent learning: Independent learning doesn't mean learning alone or without help.
  3. How degrees are structured: credits, levels and modules: A UK degree is built from modules worth credits at defined levels.
  4. Learning outcomes and the QAA level descriptors: Every module states learning outcomes: what you should be able to do by the end.
  5. Assessment at university: types, criteria and marks: University assessment comes in many forms and is marked against published criteria.
  6. Academic regulations and the student handbook: Every university has regulations that decide how you progress, what happens if you fail or fall ill, and how to appeal.
  7. Teaching formats: lectures, seminars, labs and tutorials: Universities teach through several formats, each with a different purpose and a different job for you.
  8. Personal tutors and academic staff: Most UK universities give each student a personal or academic tutor.
  9. Writing to academic staff: Email is how most students contact lecturers, tutors and offices, and a clear, polite message gets a faster, more useful answer.
  10. The virtual learning environment and digital study: The virtual learning environment (VLE) is where most modules live: materials, recordings, announcements and submission.
  11. The university library: A university library is far more than a building with books: it is a gateway to databases, journals, e-books and specialist staff.
  12. Support services and reasonable adjustments: Universities offer a wide range of support, from academic skills advice to disability, wellbeing, money and careers services.
  13. Self-assessment of study skills: Knowing your strengths and gaps lets you spend effort where it matters.
  14. Setting goals for your degree: Clear goals direct effort and help you persist.
  15. Motivation and self-efficacy: Motivation isn't a fixed amount you either have or lack; it depends on what you believe about yourself and the task.
  16. Self-regulated learning: Self-regulated learners plan, monitor and reflect on their own learning, adjusting as they go.
  17. Building study habits: Much of what successful students do is habit, not willpower.
  18. Peer learning and study groups: Learning with and from other students is one of the most effective and least used resources at university.
  19. Belonging and the transition to university: Starting university is a major life transition, and doubts about fitting in are common and usually temporary.

School 2: How Pupils Learn

19 lessons

How Pupils Learn: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Learning as a change in long-term memory: The Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework starts from one idea: learning is a lasting change in what pupils know, understand or can do.
  2. Working memory and its limits: Working memory is where pupils hold and manipulate information while they think, and it is small.
  3. Long-term memory and schemas: Long-term memory is not a filing cabinet of separate facts but a web of connected knowledge called schemas.
  4. Prior knowledge and how it shapes learning: What pupils already know is the strongest single influence on what they learn next.
  5. Misconceptions and conceptual change: Pupils do not arrive as blank slates: they bring intuitive ideas that are often wrong and hard to shift.
  6. Cognitive load theory: Sweller 1988: Cognitive load theory, first set out by John Sweller in 1988, asks how instruction should be designed given the limits of working memory.
  7. Intrinsic and extraneous load: Cognitive load theory distinguishes the load that comes from the material itself from the load that comes from how it is taught.
  8. The worked example effect: Studying a worked example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem, is one of the most reliable ways for novices to learn a procedure.
  9. The expertise reversal effect: Guidance that helps novices can become a hindrance as pupils gain expertise.
  10. Split attention and redundancy: Two effects from cognitive load theory explain why some slides and worksheets make learning harder: split attention and redundancy.
  11. Dual coding: Dual coding means combining words with visuals such as diagrams, timelines and graphic organisers, so that pupils can process information through two connected channels.
  12. Retrieval practice: Retrieval practice means bringing information to mind from memory, for example through low-stakes quizzes, rather than re-reading it.
  13. Spaced practice: Spreading practice of the same material over time leads to better long-term retention than cramming it into one session.
  14. Interleaving: Interleaving means mixing different types of problem or topic in practice, rather than practising one type in a block.
  15. Elaboration and generative learning: Learning is deeper when pupils actively make sense of material: explaining why, connecting it to what they know, and transforming it into a new form.
  16. Forgetting and desirable difficulties: Forgetting is normal and, managed well, useful.
  17. Motivation and self-efficacy in learning: Pupils are more likely to persist and succeed when they believe they can, value the work and feel some control and belonging.
  18. Metacognition and self-regulated learning: Metacognition is thinking about thinking: how pupils plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning.
  19. Learning styles and other neuromyths: Some popular ideas about the brain and learning have little or no evidence behind them.

School 3: Time Management, Lectures and Note-making

14 lessons

Time Management, Lectures and Note-making: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. How students use their time: University gives students far more control over their time than school.
  2. Semester and weekly planning: Good planning turns a busy semester into manageable steps.
  3. Prioritising tasks: There is rarely time to do everything at once.
  4. Procrastination: Almost every student procrastinates sometimes.
  5. Focus and distraction: Deep concentration is increasingly rare and valuable.
  6. Balancing study, work and life: Many students balance study with paid work, caring, commuting and wellbeing.
  7. Preparing for lectures: A little preparation makes lectures much easier to follow.
  8. Active listening in lectures: Lectures are most useful when you listen actively rather than just copying.
  9. Note-making methods: linear, Cornell and maps: Notes are tools for understanding and revision, not transcripts.
  10. Handwriting or typing notes: Should students take notes by hand or on a laptop?
  11. Using lecture recordings and slides: Many universities record lectures and post slides online.
  12. Reviewing and consolidating notes: Notes are only useful if you return to them.
  13. Seminars and discussion: Seminars and tutorials are where students test and develop ideas through discussion.
  14. Practical classes and lab notebooks: Practical classes build hands-on skills, and lab notebooks record what was actually done.

School 4: Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking

16 lessons

Reading Academic Texts and Critical Thinking: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Types of academic text: University reading lists mix many kinds of text, from textbooks to journal articles, reports and grey literature.
  2. Reading strategically: purpose and selection: No student can read everything on a long reading list, so reading has to be strategic.
  3. Reading a journal article: Journal articles are the main way new research is reported, but their structure can be off-putting at first.
  4. Reading actively: SQ3R and questioning: Passive reading, where the eyes move but little is learned, is common.
  5. Making notes from reading: Notes turn reading into material you can use for seminars, essays and exams.
  6. Paraphrasing and summarising: Paraphrasing and summarising let you use other writers' ideas in your own words.
  7. Reading difficult texts: Some academic texts are hard because of dense language, unfamiliar theory or complex arguments.
  8. What critical thinking is: Critical thinking is one of the most valued outcomes of a degree, but it is often misunderstood as being negative.
  9. Arguments: claims, reasons and conclusions: In critical thinking, an argument is a set of reasons offered to support a conclusion, not a quarrel.
  10. Identifying assumptions: Arguments rest on assumptions that are not stated but must be true for the reasoning to work.
  11. Evaluating evidence: Arguments are only as strong as the evidence behind them.
  12. Flaws in reasoning: Some arguments go wrong not because the evidence is false but because the reasoning is faulty.
  13. Cognitive biases in reasoning: Even careful thinkers are affected by systematic biases in judgement.
  14. Comparing perspectives: Academic debates involve competing theories, schools of thought and interpretations.
  15. Moving from description to analysis: A common piece of feedback is "too descriptive".
  16. Building your own argument: At university you are expected not just to evaluate other people's arguments but to make your own.

School 5: Academic Writing

19 lessons

Academic Writing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What academic writing is: Academic writing has its own purposes, conventions and expectations, which differ from school, journalism and everyday writing.
  2. Analysing the question: Many low marks come from answering a different question from the one set.
  3. Planning and outlining: A plan turns research into a structure before you write.
  4. Drafting: Drafting is where a plan becomes continuous prose.
  5. Editing and proofreading: Good writing is usually rewritten writing.
  6. Essay structure: An essay's structure makes its argument easy to follow.
  7. Introductions and conclusions: Introductions and conclusions frame an essay and shape the marker's first and last impressions.
  8. Paragraphs and the PEEL model: The paragraph is the basic unit of an argument.
  9. Report structure: Many courses set reports as well as essays, and reports follow different conventions.
  10. Writing a literature review: A literature review surveys and evaluates research on a topic, either as an assignment in its own right or as part of a dissertation.
  11. Reflective writing: Many courses, especially in health, education and professional subjects, set reflective writing.
  12. Abstracts and summaries: Abstracts and summaries condense a longer piece of work into a few sentences.
  13. Academic style and register: Academic writing uses a particular register: formal, precise and objective in tone.
  14. Clarity and concision: Clear, concise writing helps markers follow your argument and saves words for analysis.
  15. Hedging and stance: Academic writers signal how confident they are in their claims.
  16. Using sources in your writing: Using sources well means integrating them into your own argument rather than letting them take over.
  17. Signposting and cohesion: Readers follow an argument more easily when writing is cohesive and clearly signposted.
  18. Grammar and punctuation for academic writing: Errors in grammar and punctuation can obscure meaning and lower marks.
  19. Writing in your discipline: Each discipline has its own expectations about evidence, structure and style.

School 6: Referencing and Academic Integrity

17 lessons

Referencing and Academic Integrity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why we reference: Referencing is more than a rule to avoid penalties: it is how scholars give credit, show their evidence and join an academic conversation.
  2. What needs a reference: Knowing when to reference is as important as knowing how.
  3. Referencing styles: an overview: Universities use several referencing styles, and different departments may require different ones.
  4. Harvard referencing: Harvard is the most widely used referencing style in UK universities.
  5. APA style: APA style, from the American Psychological Association, is used in psychology, education, nursing and many social sciences.
  6. Numeric styles: Vancouver and IEEE: Numeric styles are standard in medicine, health sciences, engineering and computing.
  7. Footnote styles: OSCOLA and Chicago: Law, history and some humanities use footnote styles, which put source details at the bottom of the page.
  8. Secondary referencing: Sometimes you read about one author's work in another author's book or article.
  9. Reference management software: Reference managers store your sources and format citations automatically.
  10. Academic integrity and its values: Academic integrity is the commitment to honest and responsible scholarship.
  11. Plagiarism and how to avoid it: Plagiarism is the most common form of academic misconduct, and much of it is unintentional.
  12. Collusion and group work boundaries: Working with others is encouraged at university, but collaboration has limits in individual assessments.
  13. Contract cheating and essay mills: Contract cheating means getting someone else to complete assessed work for you.
  14. Text-matching software: Most UK universities run submitted work through text-matching software such as Turnitin.
  15. Generative AI and academic integrity: Generative AI tools such as chatbots raise new questions for academic integrity.
  16. Using generative AI responsibly in study: Used well and within the rules, generative AI can support learning; used badly, it can replace the thinking that learning requires.
  17. Academic misconduct procedures: If a university suspects academic misconduct, it follows a formal procedure.

School 7: Research and Information Literacy

17 lessons

Research and Information Literacy: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Information literacy: Information literacy is the ability to find, evaluate and use information well.
  2. Defining a research question: A good research question gives focus to an assignment or project.
  3. Search strategies: keywords and Boolean operators: Effective searching saves time and finds better sources.
  4. Library catalogues and discovery tools: University libraries offer catalogues and discovery tools to find books, articles and other resources.
  5. Subject databases: Subject databases index research in specific disciplines and are essential for finding scholarly sources.
  6. Google Scholar and the open web: Google Scholar and the open web are useful starting points, but they have limits.
  7. Grey literature and official statistics: Not all useful research is published in journals.
  8. Evaluating sources: Judging the quality of sources is at the heart of academic work.
  9. Peer review and the publication process: Peer review is how academic research is checked before publication.
  10. Primary and secondary sources: Different disciplines use the terms primary and secondary sources in slightly different ways.
  11. Recognising misinformation: Misinformation spreads quickly online and can shape beliefs and behaviour.
  12. Open access: Open access makes research free to read online.
  13. Organising your research: Good organisation saves time and prevents accidental plagiarism.
  14. Research ethics for students: Any research involving people must be ethical.
  15. Designing a small project: Many students complete a small research project or dissertation.
  16. Collecting data: surveys and interviews: Surveys and interviews are the most common ways students collect data from people.
  17. Writing up a project: Writing up a project communicates what you did and found.

School 8: Numeracy and Data Skills

14 lessons

Numeracy and Data Skills: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Maths anxiety and number confidence: Many students feel anxious about numbers, which can hold them back.
  2. Percentages, ratios and proportions: Percentages, ratios and proportions appear in every subject, from survey results to recipes for lab solutions.
  3. Rounding, significant figures and estimation: Rounding and estimation help you present numbers clearly and check your work.
  4. Units and conversions: Using units correctly is essential in science, health and everyday life.
  5. Using formulae: Formulae express relationships between quantities.
  6. Types of data: Different types of data need different summaries and analyses.
  7. Descriptive statistics: Descriptive statistics summarise data with a few key numbers.
  8. Tables and charts: Good tables and charts make data easy to understand.
  9. Reading graphs critically: Graphs in the media and research can inform or mislead.
  10. Correlation and causation: Two things happening together does not mean one causes the other.
  11. Probability and risk: Probability and risk help us make decisions under uncertainty.
  12. Statistical significance in plain terms: Research papers often report "statistically significant" results.
  13. Spreadsheets for students: Spreadsheets help students organise, calculate and chart data.
  14. Presenting numbers in writing: Numbers in essays and reports must be accurate, clear and meaningful.

School 9: Professional Communication

14 lessons

Professional Communication: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Communication at work: purpose and audience: Every message at work exists to make something happen: a decision, an action, a change in understanding.
  2. Active listening: Listening is the communication skill people use most and are taught least.
  3. Questioning skills: Good questions uncover facts, test assumptions and help people think.
  4. Non-verbal communication: Tone of voice, facial expression, posture, gesture and timing all carry meaning alongside words.
  5. Plain English and clear writing: Clear writing gets read, understood and acted on.
  6. Structuring reports and briefings: A report or briefing exists to support a decision.
  7. Writing effective emails: Email remains the main written channel at work, and most professionals receive more than they can read carefully.
  8. Tone, etiquette and choosing the channel: The same message can land well or badly depending on its tone and the channel it travels through.
  9. Running effective meetings: Meetings take a large share of professional time and are often judged a poor use of it.
  10. Minutes, decisions and action points: The record of a meeting is what turns talk into action and accountability.
  11. Planning a presentation: Most presentations succeed or fail before they begin, in how clearly the message is defined and structured.
  12. Delivering a presentation: Delivery turns a well-planned talk into one people remember.
  13. Communicating in virtual and hybrid teams: Many teams now work partly or fully remotely.
  14. Giving and receiving feedback: Feedback helps people learn, but badly given feedback can reduce performance.

School 10: Exams and Revision

14 lessons

Exams and Revision: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Types of exam: University exams come in many forms, each testing different skills.
  2. Planning revision: Good revision starts with a realistic plan.
  3. Revision techniques that work: Some revision techniques are much more effective than others.
  4. Past papers and practice questions: Past papers are one of the most valuable revision tools.
  5. Revising for different subjects: Different subjects need different revision approaches.
  6. Exam technique: before and on the day: Good preparation in the final days and on exam day helps you perform at your best.
  7. Answering essay questions in exams: Writing essays under exam conditions needs quick planning and a clear argument.
  8. Multiple-choice and short-answer questions: Multiple-choice and short-answer questions test precise knowledge.
  9. Problem-solving and calculation exams: Problem-solving exams test the ability to apply methods under time pressure.
  10. Open-book and online exams: Open-book and online exams are increasingly common.
  11. Managing exam anxiety: Some anxiety before exams is normal, but too much can harm performance and wellbeing.
  12. Results, resits and progression: Understanding results and what happens next helps students plan.
  13. Extenuating circumstances: Serious problems can affect students' ability to study or sit exams.
  14. Learning from exam feedback: Exam results and feedback are a valuable source of learning.

School 11: Sleep, Stress and Rest

14 lessons

Sleep, Stress and Rest: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why we sleep: Sleep is essential for physical health, learning and emotional wellbeing.
  2. Sleep stages and cycles: Sleep isn't a single state but a cycle of distinct stages.
  3. Circadian rhythms: Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles in body processes, controlled by an internal clock and set by light.
  4. How much sleep we need: Sleep needs vary with age and between individuals.
  5. Sleep across the lifespan: Sleep changes dramatically from birth to old age.
  6. Insomnia: Insomnia is persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep that affects daytime functioning.
  7. Improving sleep: Many everyday habits affect sleep.
  8. Shift work and sleep: Shift work, especially at night, conflicts with the body clock.
  9. Sleep disorders beyond insomnia: Several sleep disorders other than insomnia are common and treatable.
  10. What stress is: Stress is the response to demands that challenge or exceed our ability to cope.
  11. The stress response: The body responds to stress through two linked systems: a fast nervous and hormonal response, and a slower hormonal response.
  12. Stress and health: Chronic stress affects both physical and mental health.
  13. Coping and stress management: Coping is what people do to manage stress.
  14. Work-related stress and burnout: Work is a major source of stress for many people.

School 12: Knowing Yourself

12 lessons

Knowing Yourself: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What career development is: Career development is the lifelong process of managing learning, work and life.
  2. Career theories: matching, development and chance: Career theories explain how people come to do the work they do.
  3. The Gatsby Benchmarks and careers support: The Gatsby Benchmarks define good careers guidance in English schools and colleges, and universities and the National Careers Service extend that support into adulthood.
  4. Identifying your skills: Employers recruit for skills as much as for qualifications.
  5. Transferable skills and graduate attributes: Universities describe the qualities they aim to develop in every student as graduate attributes, and employers publish the skills they look for.
  6. Values and what matters to you at work: Work values are the things you most want work to give you, such as security, creativity, helping others or high pay.
  7. Strengths and how to use them: A strength is something you are good at and energised by, not just something you can do.
  8. Interests and personality tools: uses and limits: Career quizzes, interest inventories and personality questionnaires are everywhere.
  9. Reflective practice and the skills audit: Reflection turns experience into learning you can talk about.
  10. Motivations and work-life priorities: Why you want to work shapes which jobs will satisfy you.
  11. Your career story and personal brand: Recruiters remember a clear, credible story better than a list of facts.
  12. Setting career goals: Goals turn self-knowledge into action.
