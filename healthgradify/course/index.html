Health and Wellbeing: a free course on HealthGradify

HealthGradify's free Health and Wellbeing course runs from Foundations of Health and Wellbeing to Health Across the Life Course: 176 lessons in 11 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for anyone improving their health.

Education, not medical advice. HealthGradify teaches health and wellbeing as an academic subject. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by health professionals, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials and official guidance (NHS, NICE, UKHSA). Nothing here can diagnose a condition, recommend a treatment or replace advice from someone who knows your health. For advice about your own health speak to your GP or a pharmacist, or call NHS 111; in an emergency call 999. If you are struggling with your mental health, Samaritans are on 116 123 (UK).

Schools and lessons

School 1: Foundations of Health and Wellbeing

14 lessons

Foundations of Health and Wellbeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What is health?: Health can be defined negatively, as the absence of disease, or positively, as complete wellbeing or the ability to adapt and cope.
  2. Models of health: biomedical and biopsychosocial: A model of health is a way of explaining what causes ill health and what should be done about it.
  3. Wellbeing and how it is measured: Wellbeing is how people feel and function, and how they judge their lives.
  4. Holistic health: physical, mental and social: A holistic approach treats a person as a whole, recognising that physical, intellectual, emotional and social aspects of health affect one another.
  5. The determinants of health: The determinants of health are the factors that make people healthy or unhealthy, from genes to government policy.
  6. Health inequalities: Health inequalities are systematic, avoidable and unfair differences in health between groups.
  7. Lifestyle factors and health: Smoking, diet, physical activity and alcohol are the four lifestyle factors with the largest effects on health in the UK.
  8. The wider determinants: housing, work and environment: The wider determinants are the social, economic and environmental conditions in which people live.
  9. Judging health information: Health information is everywhere: in news, adverts, social media and conversation.
  10. Levels of evidence: Not all evidence is equally trustworthy.
  11. Understanding risk: absolute and relative: Risks are often reported in ways that make them sound bigger or smaller than they are.
  12. Screening: benefits and harms: Screening tests apparently healthy people to find disease, or the risk of it, early.
  13. Measuring the nation's health: Governments and researchers describe a population's health with measures of death, illness and wellbeing.
  14. Leading causes of death and ill health in the UK: Knowing what kills people and what makes them ill shapes public health priorities.

School 2: The Human Body

17 lessons

The Human Body: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Cells, tissues and organs: The body is built in levels: cells form tissues, tissues form organs and organs work together in systems.
  2. Homeostasis: Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite changes outside and inside the body.
  3. Body temperature regulation: The body keeps its core temperature close to 37 °C by balancing heat production and heat loss.
  4. Blood glucose regulation: Blood glucose must stay within narrow limits because the brain depends on it and high levels damage blood vessels.
  5. The nervous system: The nervous system detects changes, processes information and coordinates fast responses.
  6. The endocrine system: The endocrine system controls the body with hormones carried in the blood.
  7. The heart: The heart is a muscular pump that drives blood through two circulations.
  8. Blood vessels and blood pressure: Arteries, capillaries and veins carry blood around the body, and blood pressure keeps it moving.
  9. Blood: Blood transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and wastes, defends against infection and seals wounds.
  10. The respiratory system: The respiratory system brings air into the lungs so oxygen can enter the blood and carbon dioxide can leave.
  11. Gas exchange and breathing: Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs; gas exchange moves oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out.
  12. The digestive system: The digestive system breaks food down into molecules small enough to be absorbed into the blood.
  13. The liver: The liver is the body's chemical processing centre, with hundreds of functions.
  14. The kidneys and fluid balance: The kidneys filter the blood, remove wastes and control the body's water and salt balance.
  15. The immune system: The immune system defends the body against pathogens.
  16. The musculoskeletal system: Bones, joints and muscles support the body, protect organs and make movement possible.
  17. The skin: The skin is the body's largest organ, forming a barrier between the body and the outside world.

School 3: Nutrition

19 lessons

Nutrition: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Carbohydrates and dietary fibre: Carbohydrates are the body's main energy source, and dietary fibre is the part of plant foods the small intestine can't digest.
  2. Protein: Proteins are made of amino acids and are needed for growth, repair, enzymes, hormones and immunity.
  3. Fats: Fats provide energy, fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids, but the type matters.
  4. Vitamins: Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small amounts for health.
  5. Minerals: Minerals are inorganic elements needed for structure, fluid balance, nerve function and enzyme activity.
  6. Water and hydration: Water makes up about 60% of adult body weight and is essential for nearly every body process.
  7. The Eatwell Guide: The Eatwell Guide is the UK government's model of a healthy, balanced diet.
  8. Energy balance: Body weight changes when energy intake and energy expenditure are out of balance.
  9. Reading food labels: Food labels give the information people need to compare products and make healthier choices.
  10. Salt, sugar and saturated fat: Salt, sugar and saturated fat are the three nutrients UK diets most often contain too much of.
  11. Ultra-processed food: Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) make up more than half of the energy in UK diets.
  12. Nutrition in pregnancy and infancy: Good nutrition before and during pregnancy and in the first years of life supports healthy growth and development.
  13. Nutrition in childhood and adolescence: Children and teenagers need energy and nutrients for growth and development, but many UK children eat too much sugar and too little fibre.
  14. Nutrition in older age: Older adults need less energy but as many or more nutrients, and are at risk of both undernutrition and dehydration.
  15. Vegetarian and vegan diets: Plant-based diets are increasingly common in the UK.
  16. Malnutrition: Malnutrition means an imbalance of energy or nutrients, including undernutrition and overnutrition.
  17. Diet and chronic disease: Diet is one of the leading modifiable causes of chronic disease.
  18. Food insecurity: Food insecurity is a lack of reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
  19. Eating disorders: Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions affecting eating, weight and body image.

School 4: Physical Activity

14 lessons

Physical Activity: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Physical activity, exercise and fitness: Physical activity, exercise and fitness are related but different ideas.
  2. The UK physical activity guidelines: The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 guidelines set out how much activity people of different ages need.
  3. Sedentary behaviour: Sedentary behaviour is sitting or lying with low energy use while awake, and it carries health risks separate from a lack of exercise.
  4. The benefits of physical activity: Regular physical activity reduces the risk of many diseases and improves mental health and quality of life.
  5. Short-term responses to exercise: When exercise starts, the body responds within seconds to deliver more oxygen to working muscles.
  6. Long-term adaptations to training: Regular training causes lasting changes in the body that improve health and performance.
  7. Energy systems: Muscles get energy from ATP, which is resupplied by three energy systems.
  8. Components of fitness: Fitness is made up of several components, some linked mainly to health and others to skill and performance.
  9. Measuring fitness: Fitness tests measure components of fitness so that progress can be tracked and training planned.
  10. Physical activity in children and young people: Physical activity supports children's growth, development, health and wellbeing, but fewer than half meet the guidelines.
  11. Physical activity in older adults: Staying active in later life helps maintain independence, prevent falls and protect mental health.
  12. Activity with long-term conditions: Physical activity helps prevent and manage many long-term conditions, but people may need tailored advice.
  13. Barriers to physical activity: Many people want to be more active but face barriers.
  14. Active environments and active travel: The places where people live, work and travel strongly affect how active they are.

School 5: Sleep, Stress and Rest

14 lessons

Sleep, Stress and Rest: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why we sleep: Sleep is essential for physical health, learning and emotional wellbeing.
  2. Sleep stages and cycles: Sleep isn't a single state but a cycle of distinct stages.
  3. Circadian rhythms: Circadian rhythms are roughly 24-hour cycles in body processes, controlled by an internal clock and set by light.
  4. How much sleep we need: Sleep needs vary with age and between individuals.
  5. Sleep across the lifespan: Sleep changes dramatically from birth to old age.
  6. Insomnia: Insomnia is persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep that affects daytime functioning.
  7. Improving sleep: Many everyday habits affect sleep.
  8. Shift work and sleep: Shift work, especially at night, conflicts with the body clock.
  9. Sleep disorders beyond insomnia: Several sleep disorders other than insomnia are common and treatable.
  10. What stress is: Stress is the response to demands that challenge or exceed our ability to cope.
  11. The stress response: The body responds to stress through two linked systems: a fast nervous and hormonal response, and a slower hormonal response.
  12. Stress and health: Chronic stress affects both physical and mental health.
  13. Coping and stress management: Coping is what people do to manage stress.
  14. Work-related stress and burnout: Work is a major source of stress for many people.

School 6: Mental Health and Wellbeing

15 lessons

Mental Health and Wellbeing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Mental health and mental illness: Mental health is more than the absence of mental illness.
  2. Mental health in the UK: Mental health problems are common in the UK, affect some groups more than others, and carry large social and economic costs.
  3. Risk and protective factors for mental health: Mental health is shaped by the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors across life.
  4. Stigma and discrimination: Stigma about mental illness discourages people from seeking help and harms their lives.
  5. Anxiety: Anxiety is a normal response to threat, but anxiety disorders are persistent and disabling.
  6. Depression: Depression is one of the most common causes of disability worldwide.
  7. Self-harm and suicide prevention: Suicide is a leading cause of death among young adults in the UK, and much is preventable.
  8. Severe mental illness: Severe mental illnesses such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder can profoundly affect people's lives.
  9. Mental health in children and young people: Many mental health problems begin in childhood or adolescence.
  10. Perinatal mental health: Pregnancy and the first year after birth bring a higher risk of mental health problems for parents.
  11. The Five Ways to Wellbeing: The Five Ways to Wellbeing are evidence-based actions that people can take to improve their mental wellbeing.
  12. Resilience: Resilience is the capacity to adapt well in the face of adversity.
  13. Loneliness and social connection: Social connection is vital for mental and physical health, and loneliness is a significant public health issue.
  14. Digital life and wellbeing: Digital technology shapes how people connect, learn and relax, with both benefits and risks for wellbeing.
  15. Getting help: NHS talking therapies and other support: Knowing how and where to get help is an essential part of mental health literacy.

School 7: Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change

17 lessons

Health Behaviour and Behaviour Change: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Why health behaviours matter: Everyday behaviours such as smoking, drinking, diet and activity have a huge effect on health.
  2. The health belief model: The health belief model explains health behaviour in terms of people's perceptions of threat and of the benefits and barriers to action.
  3. The theory of planned behaviour: The theory of planned behaviour explains behaviour through intentions shaped by attitudes, social norms and perceived control.
  4. The transtheoretical model: The transtheoretical model, or stages of change model, describes behaviour change as a process through stages.
  5. The COM-B model and the behaviour change wheel: The COM-B model explains behaviour as the product of capability, opportunity and motivation, and the behaviour change wheel links these to intervention types and policies.
  6. Habits: Many health behaviours are habits, triggered automatically by cues.
  7. Behaviour change techniques: Behaviour change techniques (BCTs) are the active ingredients of interventions.
  8. Motivational interviewing: Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation to change.
  9. Nudges and choice architecture: Nudges change the way choices are presented to make healthier options easier, without restricting freedom.
  10. Health literacy: Health literacy is the ability to find, understand and use health information.
  11. Making Every Contact Count: Making Every Contact Count (MECC) encourages health and care staff to use everyday interactions to support healthier behaviour.
  12. Alcohol: Alcohol is widely used in the UK but causes substantial harm.
  13. Smoking: Smoking is the leading preventable cause of illness and death in the UK.
  14. Vaping: E-cigarettes, or vapes, are an effective aid to stopping smoking but raise concerns about use by young people.
  15. Drugs and harm reduction: Drug use causes deaths, illness and social harm.
  16. Sexual health: Sexual health includes preventing sexually transmitted infections, unplanned pregnancy and HIV, and positive, respectful relationships.
  17. Gambling harms: Gambling harms affect individuals, families and communities, and are increasingly treated as a public health issue.

School 8: Disease and Long-term Conditions

17 lessons

Disease and Long-term Conditions: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Pathogens and how infections spread: Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens that pass from one host to another.
  2. Breaking the chain of infection: Infection spreads through a chain of linked steps.
  3. Vaccination and herd immunity: Vaccines are among the most effective public health interventions ever developed.
  4. Antimicrobial resistance: Antimicrobial resistance threatens to make common infections untreatable.
  5. Cardiovascular disease: Cardiovascular disease, affecting the heart and blood vessels, is a leading cause of death in the UK and worldwide.
  6. Hypertension: High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms but greatly increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
  7. Stroke: A stroke happens when the blood supply to part of the brain is cut off.
  8. Type 2 diabetes: Type 2 diabetes is one of the fastest-growing long-term conditions in the UK.
  9. Type 1 diabetes: Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the body cannot make insulin.
  10. Cancer: causes and prevention: About one in two people in the UK will develop cancer during their lifetime, yet many cancers are preventable.
  11. Chronic respiratory disease: asthma and COPD: Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are the most common long-term lung conditions.
  12. Obesity: More than a quarter of adults in England are living with obesity.
  13. Dementia: Nearly a million people in the UK live with dementia.
  14. What a long-term condition is: Long-term conditions cannot currently be cured but can be managed.
  15. Multimorbidity: Many people live with several long-term conditions at once.
  16. Self-management and supported self-care: People with long-term conditions manage their own health almost all of the time.
  17. Disability and health: About a quarter of people in the UK report a disability.

School 9: Public Health and Epidemiology

18 lessons

Public Health and Epidemiology: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. What public health is: Public health is about the health of whole populations rather than individual patients.
  2. A short history of public health: Modern public health grew out of the overcrowded, disease-ridden cities of the industrial revolution.
  3. The three domains of public health: Public health work is often divided into three domains: health protection, health improvement and improving health services.
  4. Levels of prevention: Prevention can act before disease starts, catch it early, or reduce its harm once it has developed.
  5. Health promotion and the Ottawa Charter: Health promotion enables people to increase control over their health and its determinants.
  6. The prevention paradox: Geoffrey Rose observed that most cases of disease come from the many people at moderate risk, not the few at high risk.
  7. Incidence and prevalence: Incidence and prevalence are the two basic measures of how common a disease is.
  8. Rates and standardisation: Comparing death or disease rates between places is misleading if their populations differ in age.
  9. Relative risk and odds ratios: Epidemiologists measure how strongly an exposure is linked with a disease by comparing groups.
  10. Epidemiological study designs: Different questions need different study designs.
  11. Bias, confounding and chance: Before deciding that an association is real, epidemiologists must rule out bias, confounding and chance.
  12. Causation and the Bradford Hill viewpoints: An association between an exposure and a disease does not prove that one causes the other.
  13. Outbreak investigation: An outbreak is more cases of a disease than expected in a particular place and time.
  14. Health surveillance: Public health surveillance is the continuous collection, analysis and use of health data to guide action.
  15. The global burden of disease: Measuring the burden of disease shows which conditions cause the most death and disability worldwide.
  16. Global health goals and organisations: Global health is shaped by international goals and by organisations that fund, coordinate and deliver health programmes.
  17. Climate change and health: The WHO describes climate change as the biggest health threat facing humanity.
  18. Pandemic preparedness: Pandemics are infrequent but cause enormous harm to health, society and the economy.

School 10: Health and Social Care Systems

18 lessons

Health and Social Care Systems: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The founding of the NHS: The National Health Service was launched on 5 July 1948 to provide health care for everyone, free at the point of use.
  2. How the NHS in England is organised: The NHS in England is a complex system of national bodies, local planning organisations and providers.
  3. Health care across the four UK nations: Health is a devolved responsibility, so the NHS in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland has developed differently.
  4. Primary care: Primary care is the first point of contact with health services for most people.
  5. Secondary and tertiary care: Secondary care is specialist care usually provided in hospitals after referral, and tertiary care is highly specialised care.
  6. Funding health care: Health care can be paid for through taxation, social insurance, private insurance or direct payments.
  7. Social care: Social care supports people who need help with daily living because of age, illness or disability.
  8. Integrated care: Integrated care joins up health and social care services around the needs of people rather than organisations.
  9. Urgent and emergency care: Urgent and emergency care covers services for people who need help quickly, from NHS 111 to ambulances and emergency departments.
  10. Choosing the right NHS service: Using the most appropriate service helps people get the right care quickly and eases pressure on the NHS.
  11. Mental health services: Mental health services range from self-help and talking therapies to crisis teams and inpatient care.
  12. Quality and safety in health care: High-quality health care is safe, effective and centred on patients.
  13. Regulation of health and social care: Regulators protect the public by setting standards for services and professionals and acting when they are not met.
  14. Consent and mental capacity: People have the right to decide what happens to their bodies.
  15. Confidentiality and health data: People share personal information with health and care services on the understanding that it will be kept confidential.
  16. Ethical principles in health care: Health and care professionals regularly face ethical choices.
  17. NICE and priority setting: No health system can afford every treatment, so choices must be made.
  18. Comparing health systems: Countries organise and fund health care in different ways, with different results.

School 11: Health Across the Life Course

13 lessons

Health Across the Life Course: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Preconception and pregnancy: The health of parents before and during pregnancy affects the health of the baby for life.
  2. Birth and the newborn: Birth and the first days of life are a critical time for the health of mother and baby.
  3. Infant health and development: The first year of life brings rapid growth and development.
  4. Child health and the Healthy Child Programme: The Healthy Child Programme is England's universal public health programme for children and young people from pregnancy to 19.
  5. Adolescent health: Adolescence brings rapid physical, emotional and social change and is when many health behaviours and mental health problems begin.
  6. Sexual and reproductive health: Sexual and reproductive health covers contraception, sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy choices and healthy relationships.
  7. Young adults and health: Young adulthood, roughly 18 to 25, involves major transitions in education, work and relationships that shape lifelong health.
  8. Adult health checks: Health checks and screening programmes aim to find risks and diseases early in adults.
  9. Menopause: Menopause is a natural stage of life when periods stop, with effects that can be short- and long-term.
  10. Healthy ageing: People are living longer, but not always in good health.
  11. Frailty and falls: Frailty and falls are major causes of loss of independence in older people.
  12. Carers and caring: Millions of people provide unpaid care to family members or friends.
  13. End of life care: Good end of life care helps people live as well as possible until they die and to die with dignity.
