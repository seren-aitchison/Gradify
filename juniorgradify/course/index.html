Primary: a free course on JuniorGradify

JuniorGradify's free Primary course runs from Number and Place Value to Geography: 167 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for children, 5 to 12.

For children learning with a grown-up. JuniorGradify Primary is for children aged 5 to 11, working alongside a parent, carer or teacher. It follows the national curriculum in England but is not a school, doesn't replace your child's teacher and isn't official SATs material. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been checked by a qualified primary school teacher, so they may contain mistakes: check anything important with your child's school. No personal data is collected: the only thing kept is progress, saved on this device.

Course overview

Schools and lessons

School 1: Number and Place Value

12 lessons

Number and Place Value: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Counting objects to 20: Counting tells us how many things there are.
  2. Counting forwards and backwards: Counting forwards makes numbers bigger and counting backwards makes them smaller.
  3. Reading and writing numbers to 100: Numbers can be written in digits, like 47, or in words, like forty-seven.
  4. Odd and even numbers: Every whole number is either odd or even.
  5. Tens and ones: The value of a digit depends on where it is in a number.
  6. Hundreds, tens and ones: Three-digit numbers have a hundreds digit as well as tens and ones.
  7. Thousands and beyond: The place value pattern keeps going: ten hundreds make a thousand, ten thousands make ten thousand, and so on up to millions.
  8. Comparing and ordering numbers: To compare numbers we decide which is bigger or smaller, and write it with the signs <, > or =.
  9. Rounding numbers: Rounding gives a simpler number that is close to the real one, such as saying "about 50" instead of 47.
  10. Counting in steps: Counting in steps means adding the same amount each time, such as counting in twos, fives or tens.
  11. Negative numbers: Negative numbers are numbers less than zero, such as −3.
  12. Roman numerals: The ancient Romans wrote numbers with letters: I, V, X, L, C, D and M. We still see Roman numerals on clocks, in film credits and in the names of kings and queens.

School 2: The Four Operations and Times Tables

12 lessons

The Four Operations and Times Tables: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Number bonds: Number bonds are pairs of numbers that add up to a total, such as 7 and 3 making 10.
  2. Adding in your head: Many additions are quicker in your head than on paper.
  3. Subtracting in your head: Subtraction can mean taking away or finding the difference between two numbers.
  4. Column addition: Column addition is the written method for adding bigger numbers.
  5. Column subtraction: Column subtraction is the written method for taking away bigger numbers.
  6. Checking with the inverse: Addition and subtraction undo each other, and so do multiplication and division.
  7. What multiplication means: Multiplication is a quick way of adding equal groups.
  8. Times tables: Knowing the times tables by heart, up to 12 × 12, makes almost every other kind of maths easier.
  9. Factors and multiples: Multiples are the numbers in a times table; factors are the numbers that divide exactly into a number.
  10. What division means: Division means splitting into equal parts.
  11. Written multiplication: To multiply bigger numbers on paper we use short multiplication (by a one-digit number) and long multiplication (by a two-digit number).
  12. Short division and remainders: Short division is the written method for dividing a big number by a one-digit number, sometimes called the bus stop method.

School 3: Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

14 lessons

Fractions, Decimals and Percentages: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Halves and quarters: A fraction is an equal part of a whole.
  2. Unit and non-unit fractions: Every fraction has a numerator on top and a denominator underneath.
  3. Equivalent fractions: Equivalent fractions look different but are worth the same amount, like ½ and 2/4.
  4. Comparing fractions: To decide which of two fractions is bigger, it helps to give them the same denominator.
  5. Adding and subtracting fractions: Fractions can be added and subtracted when their denominators are the same.
  6. Fractions of amounts: Finding a fraction of an amount, such as ¾ of 20, is a skill used every day with money, time and measures.
  7. Mixed numbers and improper fractions: Some amounts are more than a whole, like two and a half pizzas.
  8. Multiplying and dividing fractions: In Years 5 and 6 children learn to multiply fractions by whole numbers, multiply two fractions together, and divide a fraction by a whole number.
  9. Tenths and hundredths: When one whole is split into ten equal parts, each part is a tenth; split into a hundred equal parts, each is a hundredth.
  10. Decimal place value: Decimal numbers use the same place value system as whole numbers, carrying on to the right of the decimal point into tenths, hundredths and thousandths.
  11. Rounding decimals: Decimals can be rounded to the nearest whole number or to one, two or more decimal places.
  12. Percentages: Per cent means "out of a hundred", so 30% means 30 out of every 100.
  13. Fractions, decimals and percentages together: Fractions, decimals and percentages are three ways of writing the same amount.
  14. Ratio and proportion: Ratio compares one part with another, such as 2 red beads for every 3 blue beads.

School 4: Measurement, Geometry and Statistics

18 lessons

Measurement, Geometry and Statistics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Length and height: Length tells us how long something is, and height how tall.
  2. Mass and capacity: Mass is how heavy something is; capacity is how much a container can hold.
  3. Money: In the UK we use pounds (£) and pence (p), with 100p in every pound.
  4. Telling the time: Clocks tell us the time in hours and minutes.
  5. Perimeter: The perimeter is the distance all the way round the edge of a shape.
  6. Area: Area is the amount of flat space a shape covers, measured in squares.
  7. Volume: Volume is the amount of space a solid object takes up.
  8. Converting units: Converting units means changing a measurement from one unit to another, such as metres to centimetres.
  9. 2-D shapes: 2-D shapes are flat shapes with length and width, like squares, triangles and circles.
  10. 3-D shapes: 3-D shapes are solid shapes with length, width and height, like cubes, cylinders and pyramids.
  11. Angles: An angle is an amount of turn, measured in degrees.
  12. Lines of symmetry: A shape has a line of symmetry if one half is the mirror image of the other.
  13. Position and direction: Position and direction describe where things are and how they move.
  14. Coordinates: Coordinates are pairs of numbers that give the exact position of a point on a grid.
  15. Tally charts and pictograms: Tally charts and pictograms are simple ways to collect and show information.
  16. Bar charts and tables: Bar charts and tables organise information so it is easy to compare.
  17. Line graphs: A line graph shows how something changes over time, such as temperature through a day or a plant's height over weeks.
  18. The mean: The mean is a kind of average: it shares a total out equally.

School 5: Phonics and Spelling

12 lessons

Phonics and Spelling: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Letters and sounds: Phonics teaches the link between the sounds we hear in words and the letters we write.
  2. Blending to read: Blending means pushing sounds together to hear the word.
  3. Segmenting to spell: Segmenting is the opposite of blending: you break a spoken word into its sounds so that you can write each one.
  4. Digraphs and trigraphs: Some sounds are written with two letters (digraphs, like "sh") or three letters (trigraphs, like "igh").
  5. Tricky words: Some common words don't follow the usual phonics patterns, or use patterns children haven't learned yet.
  6. Alternative spellings of sounds: Many sounds in English can be spelled in more than one way, and some spellings make more than one sound.
  7. Adding suffixes: A suffix is a group of letters added to the end of a word to change its meaning or how it is used, like "-ing", "-ed", "-ness" and "-ful".
  8. Prefixes: A prefix is a group of letters added to the start of a word to change its meaning, like "un-" in "unhappy".
  9. Plurals: Plural means more than one.
  10. Homophones: Homophones are words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings, like "there", "their" and "they're".
  11. Silent letters: Some words contain letters that we write but don't say, like the k in "knee" and the b in "lamb".
  12. Spelling rules and exceptions: Spelling in English follows many patterns, but almost every rule has exceptions.

School 6: Reading and Comprehension

12 lessons

Reading and Comprehension: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Reading for pleasure: Reading for pleasure means choosing to read because you enjoy it.
  2. Reading fluently: Fluent readers read accurately, at a good speed and with expression, so they can think about what the words mean.
  3. Understanding new words: Good readers meet new words all the time.
  4. Retrieving information: Retrieval means finding information that is written in a text.
  5. Making inferences: Inference means working out something the writer hasn't said directly, using clues in the text.
  6. Predicting: Predicting means using what you have read so far to suggest what might happen next.
  7. Summarising: Summarising means giving the main ideas of a text in a few words, leaving out the details.
  8. The author's choice of language: Writers choose their words carefully to create pictures, feelings and effects in the reader's mind.
  9. Fiction and non-fiction: Fiction is made-up writing such as stories; non-fiction gives real information.
  10. Poetry: Poems use rhythm, rhyme, sound and imagery to create feelings and pictures in very few words.
  11. Reading a play: A play script is written to be performed.
  12. Answering comprehension questions: Comprehension questions test how well you understand a text.

School 7: Grammar, Punctuation and Writing

18 lessons

Grammar, Punctuation and Writing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Nouns and noun phrases: A noun is a word for a person, place, thing or idea.
  2. Verbs and tenses: Verbs are doing or being words, and their tense shows when something happens.
  3. Adjectives and adverbs: Adjectives describe nouns and adverbs describe verbs, adjectives or other adverbs.
  4. Pronouns: Pronouns take the place of nouns so we don't have to keep repeating them.
  5. Conjunctions: Conjunctions are joining words that link words, phrases and clauses.
  6. Prepositions: Prepositions show how one thing relates to another, often in place or time: under the table, before lunch.
  7. Clauses and sentences: A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense.
  8. Active and passive voice: In an active sentence the subject does the action; in a passive sentence the subject has the action done to it.
  9. Capital letters and full stops: Capital letters and full stops are the first punctuation children learn: a capital starts a sentence and a full stop ends it.
  10. Question marks and exclamation marks: Question marks end sentences that ask something; exclamation marks show strong feeling or surprise.
  11. Commas: Commas help readers by separating parts of a sentence.
  12. Apostrophes: Apostrophes have two jobs: showing where letters are missing in a contraction, and showing that something belongs to someone.
  13. Inverted commas: Inverted commas, also called speech marks, show the exact words someone says.
  14. Brackets, dashes, colons and semicolons: In upper Key Stage 2, children learn more advanced punctuation.
  15. Planning your writing: Good writing starts before you write a single sentence.
  16. Writing a story: A good story has interesting characters, a vivid setting and a plot with a problem that gets solved.
  17. Non-fiction writing: Non-fiction writing gives real information, instructions or opinions.
  18. Editing and improving: Good writers never stop at their first draft.

School 8: Plants, Animals and Habitats

16 lessons

Plants, Animals and Habitats: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Parts of a plant: Flowering plants are made of parts that each do an important job.
  2. What plants need to grow: Plants need the right conditions to grow well.
  3. How seeds and bulbs grow: Many plants grow from seeds and some grow from bulbs.
  4. Pollination and seed dispersal: Flowers make seeds through pollination, and plants spread their seeds in clever ways.
  5. Animal groups: Animals can be sorted into groups by their features and what they eat.
  6. The human body and senses: Our bodies have many parts, and our five senses help us find out about the world.
  7. Teeth and eating: Our teeth help us bite and chew food, and we need to look after them.
  8. Skeletons and muscles: Skeletons and muscles help bodies move, stay upright and stay protected.
  9. The digestive system: The digestive system breaks food down so the body can use it.
  10. The heart and circulation: The heart pumps blood around the body through blood vessels.
  11. Healthy living: Eating well, being active, sleeping enough and keeping clean help us stay healthy.
  12. Habitats: A habitat is the natural home of a living thing.
  13. Food chains: A food chain shows what eats what in a habitat.
  14. Classifying living things: Scientists classify living things into groups based on their features.
  15. Life cycles: All living things go through a life cycle of birth, growth, reproduction and death.
  16. Evolution and inheritance: Living things change over very long periods of time through evolution.

School 9: Materials, Forces and Magnets

12 lessons

Materials, Forces and Magnets: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Everyday materials: Everything around us is made from materials such as wood, plastic, metal and glass.
  2. Properties of materials: Materials have properties such as hardness, flexibility and transparency.
  3. Solids, liquids and gases: Materials can be solids, liquids or gases.
  4. The water cycle: The water cycle is the journey water takes between the land, the sea and the sky.
  5. Rocks and soils: Rocks are natural materials that make up the Earth's surface, and soils are made partly from rocks.
  6. Changes of materials: Materials can change in reversible and irreversible ways.
  7. Pushes and pulls: A force is a push or a pull.
  8. Friction: Friction is a force that acts when two surfaces rub together.
  9. Gravity: Gravity is the force that pulls things towards the Earth.
  10. Air and water resistance: Air resistance and water resistance are forces that slow down objects moving through air and water.
  11. Magnets: Magnets can attract or repel each other and attract some materials.
  12. Levers, pulleys and gears: Levers, pulleys and gears are simple machines that let a small force have a bigger effect.

School 10: Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space

13 lessons

Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Light and dark: We need light to see things, and dark is simply the absence of light.
  2. Shadows: Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light.
  3. How we see: We see things because light travels in straight lines into our eyes.
  4. How sounds are made: Sounds are made when things vibrate.
  5. Pitch and volume: Sounds can be high or low and loud or quiet.
  6. Electrical safety: Electricity is useful but can be very dangerous.
  7. Simple circuits: A circuit is a complete loop that lets electricity flow.
  8. Conductors and insulators: Some materials let electricity flow through them and others do not.
  9. Circuit symbols: Scientists use standard symbols to draw circuit diagrams.
  10. Seasons and weather: The weather changes from day to day and the seasons change through the year.
  11. Day and night: Day and night happen because the Earth spins.
  12. The Sun, Earth and Moon: The Earth orbits the Sun, and the Moon orbits the Earth.
  13. The solar system: The solar system is the Sun and everything that orbits it.

School 11: History

14 lessons

History: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Changes within living memory: Some changes have happened so recently that people alive today remember them.
  2. The Stone Age to the Iron Age: For thousands of years before the Romans, people in Britain lived in the Stone Age, the Bronze Age and the Iron Age.
  3. Roman Britain: The Romans ruled much of Britain for nearly 400 years.
  4. Anglo-Saxons and Scots: After the Romans left, Anglo-Saxons settled in England and Scots moved into what became Scotland.
  5. Vikings: From the late 700s, Vikings from Scandinavia raided and then settled in Britain.
  6. A local history study: History happened in your own area too.
  7. A theme beyond 1066: Some topics are best studied across many centuries.
  8. Ancient Egypt: Ancient Egypt was one of the earliest great civilisations, built along the River Nile.
  9. Ancient Greece: The ancient Greeks developed ideas that still shape our world, from democracy to the Olympic Games.
  10. The Maya: The Maya built a great civilisation in Central America, with cities, writing and astronomy.
  11. Events beyond living memory: Some important events happened long before anyone alive today was born.
  12. Significant people: Some people's actions changed the world.
  13. Sources and evidence: Historians use sources as evidence to find out about the past.
  14. Timelines and chronology: Chronology means putting events in order of time.

School 12: Geography

14 lessons

Geography: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The United Kingdom: The United Kingdom is made up of four countries.
  2. Continents and oceans: The world is made up of seven continents and five oceans.
  3. Countries and capitals: There are about 195 countries in the world, each with its own capital and government.
  4. Latitude, longitude and time zones: Lines of latitude and longitude help us find any place on Earth, and they explain why time is different around the world.
  5. Comparing places: Geographers compare places to understand how they are similar and different.
  6. Settlements: A settlement is a place where people live.
  7. Land use and trade: People use land in different ways and trade goods across the world.
  8. Rivers: Rivers carry water from the land to the sea and shape the landscape.
  9. Mountains: Mountains are high, steep landforms found on every continent.
  10. Volcanoes and earthquakes: Volcanoes and earthquakes happen where the Earth's plates meet.
  11. Climate zones and biomes: Different parts of the world have very different climates and living things.
  12. Maps and compasses: Maps help us find our way and understand places.
  13. Grid references: Grid references help us find exact places on a map.
  14. Fieldwork: Fieldwork means investigating geography outdoors.
