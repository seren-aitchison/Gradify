Art and Design practice questions with answers

36 questions from Gradify's free Art and Design course, with the answer and why behind each reveal. Answer before you open it: that's what makes it stick.

The Visual Language of Art and Design

  1. Which of these is best described as design rather than fine art? (Foundation recall)
    • A marble figure carved for a sculpture park
    • A typeface commissioned for a city's road signs
    • A video installation in a museum
    • An oil painting shown in a gallery
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A typeface commissioned for a city's road signs. A typeface answers a brief for users (drivers reading signs), which is the defining purpose of design; the others are made chiefly to be contemplated. Revise The Visual Language of Art and Design →

  2. Why is Duchamp's Fountain (1917) often used to discuss the definition of art? (Exam style)
    • It was presented as art although the artist did not make it by hand
    • It was rejected because of poor craftsmanship
    • It was the first sculpture made of porcelain
    • It was the first work sold at auction in New York
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It was presented as art although the artist did not make it by hand. The work's status rested on choice, signature and context, not handmade skill, which challenged skill-based and formalist definitions. Revise The Visual Language of Art and Design →

  3. A critic argues that a pile of bricks in a gallery is art only because the gallery shows it. Which theory is the critic using? (Stretch)
    • Expression theory
    • Formalism
    • The imitation (mimesis) theory
    • The institutional theory of art
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The institutional theory of art. The institutional theory (Dickie) defines art by its acceptance within the art world; formalism looks at visual form, mimesis at likeness, expression at feeling conveyed. Revise The Visual Language of Art and Design →

Drawing

  1. Which purpose of drawing does a storyboard for an animation mainly serve? (Foundation recall)
    • Communication and planning of a sequence
    • Expressive finished work
    • Observation from life
    • Recording texture by rubbing
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Communication and planning of a sequence. A storyboard plans and communicates the sequence and framing of shots to others. Revise Drawing →

  2. A student has only highly finished drawings copied from photographs. What would best improve evidence of the creative process? (Exam style)
    • Add a decorative border to each drawing
    • Add quick observational sketches, thumbnails and annotated studies showing decisions
    • Make the copies larger
    • Remove all drawings and use photographs only
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Add quick observational sketches, thumbnails and annotated studies showing decisions. Process is evidenced by exploratory drawing and annotation that show thinking and recording, not only finish. Revise Drawing →

  3. Which is the most accurate evaluation of Betty Edwards's approach? (Stretch)
    • Both her method and explanation are entirely unsupported
    • Drawing is innate, so the exercises cannot help
    • Her explanation is proven and her exercises are useless
    • Her perceptual exercises are useful, though her right-brain explanation is not supported by neuroscience
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Her perceptual exercises are useful, though her right-brain explanation is not supported by neuroscience. The exercises (edges, spaces, relationships) help many learners; the hemispheric explanation is an outdated simplification. Revise Drawing →

Colour Theory

  1. How many types of cone does the typical human retina contain? (Foundation recall)
    • Four
    • One
    • Three
    • Two
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Three. Trichromatic vision depends on short, medium and long wavelength cones. Revise Colour Theory →

  2. A painter's colours matched perfectly in the studio but look different under gallery spotlights. What explains this? (Exam style)
    • Colour constancy has stopped working
    • Metamerism: the pigments reflect differently under the new light
    • The canvas has absorbed the paint
    • The paint has dried a different colour overnight
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Metamerism: the pigments reflect differently under the new light. Colours that match under one illuminant can differ under another because their reflectance curves differ. Revise Colour Theory →

  3. What does the disagreement over "the dress" photograph (2015) best show? (Stretch)
    • Colour is a fixed property of fabric
    • Only colour-blind people saw it differently
    • Perceived colour depends on the brain's assumptions about lighting, not only on the wavelengths reaching the eye
    • Some viewers' screens were broken
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Perceived colour depends on the brain's assumptions about lighting, not only on the wavelengths reaching the eye. The ambiguous illumination led different visual systems to correct the same pixels differently. Revise Colour Theory →

Painting Media and Techniques

  1. What is the binder in watercolour? (Foundation recall)
    • Acrylic polymer
    • Egg yolk
    • Gum arabic
    • Linseed oil
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Gum arabic. Watercolour and gouache use gum arabic; oil uses linseed, tempera egg yolk, acrylic a polymer emulsion. Revise Painting Media and Techniques →

  2. A painter wants to blend skin tones slowly over several hours. Which medium suits best? (Exam style)
    • Acrylic
    • Egg tempera
    • Fresco
    • Oil paint
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Oil paint. Oil's slow curing allows extended blending; acrylic and tempera dry in minutes and fresco must be finished before the plaster sets. Revise Painting Media and Techniques →

  3. Why must oil painters work "fat over lean"? (Stretch)
    • Fat paint dries faster on top
    • It makes glazes opaque
    • Lean paint is brighter on the surface
    • Upper layers need more oil so they stay flexible and do not crack over faster-drying, less flexible layers beneath
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Upper layers need more oil so they stay flexible and do not crack over faster-drying, less flexible layers beneath. Lean lower layers dry and stiffen first; flexible upper layers prevent cracking as the layers move and cure. Revise Painting Media and Techniques →

Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D

  1. In which printmaking family does ink sit in grooves below the surface? (Foundation recall)
    • Intaglio
    • Planographic
    • Relief
    • Stencil
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Intaglio. Intaglio prints are made from ink held in incised or etched lines. Revise Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D →

  2. A collector is offered a signed "print" that is a photographic reproduction of a painting. How should it be described? (Exam style)
    • A monotype
    • A reproduction, not an original print
    • An artist's proof of a lithograph
    • An original intaglio print
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A reproduction, not an original print. An original print is made from a matrix created by the artist; a signature does not make a reproduction original. Revise Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D →

  3. Why might different impressions of a Rembrandt etching look significantly different? (Stretch)
    • All impressions are identical by definition
    • Prints fade into different images
    • Rembrandt painted each impression by hand
    • They may come from different states of the plate, or differ in inking and wiping
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They may come from different states of the plate, or differ in inking and wiping. States record changes to the plate; inking and wiping also vary between impressions. Revise Printmaking, Sculpture and 3D →

Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art

  1. What does the Latin phrase camera obscura mean? (Foundation recall)
    • Dark room
    • Light writing
    • Painted window
    • Small lens
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Dark room. Camera means room and obscura means dark, describing the darkened space where the image forms. Revise Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art →

  2. Which early process produced a single unique image with no negative? (Exam style)
    • The calotype
    • The daguerreotype
    • The Kodak roll film
    • Wet collodion on glass
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The daguerreotype. A daguerreotype was formed directly on a silvered plate, so it could not be printed again from a negative. Revise Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art →

  3. A student claims Vermeer certainly traced his paintings with a camera obscura. What is the most accurate response? (Stretch)
    • It is a debated theory, supported by some visual clues but without direct proof
    • It is a proven fact recorded in Vermeer's own letters
    • It is impossible because the camera obscura was invented after his death
    • It is irrelevant, since optical aids cannot help painting
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It is a debated theory, supported by some visual clues but without direct proof. The camera obscura existed in Vermeer's time and some features suggest optical study, but no documents prove he used one, so the claim is contested. Revise Photography, Moving Image and Digital Art →

Graphic and Communication Design

  1. What is the counter of a letter? (Foundation recall)
    • The enclosed or partly enclosed space inside it
    • The height of the capital letters
    • The line on which the letters rest
    • The small stroke at the end of a stem
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The enclosed or partly enclosed space inside it. Counters are the spaces within letters such as o, e and a, and they strongly affect legibility. Revise Graphic and Communication Design →

  2. A designer wants to reduce the awkward gap between the letters T and o in a headline. Which adjustment should they use? (Exam style)
    • Kerning
    • Leading
    • Point size
    • Tracking
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Kerning. Kerning changes the space between one particular pair of letters without affecting the rest of the word. Revise Graphic and Communication Design →

  3. Two typefaces are both set at 11 point, but one looks much larger on the page. What is the most likely reason? (Stretch)
    • It has a larger x-height
    • It has been kerned more tightly
    • It has more leading
    • It uses heavier serifs
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It has a larger x-height. Point size measures the type body, so a typeface with a taller x-height appears bigger at the same size. Revise Graphic and Communication Design →

Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance

  1. Which pigment gave prehistoric painters their reds and yellows? (Foundation recall)
    • Lead white
    • Ochre
    • Prussian blue
    • Ultramarine
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ochre. Ochre is an iron-rich earth that was widely available and used from the earliest times. Revise Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance →

  2. Why do many researchers think deep cave paintings had a ritual purpose? (Exam style)
    • They are in dark, hard-to-reach places rather than in living areas
    • They contain written explanations beside them
    • They were only discovered in the twentieth century
    • They were signed and dated by known artists
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They are in dark, hard-to-reach places rather than in living areas. Their location deep inside caves suggests special use rather than everyday decoration. Revise Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance →

  3. A student writes that cave art was definitely made to bring luck in hunting. What is the best response? (Stretch)
    • It is correct, because shamanism was invented much later
    • It is impossible, because prehistoric people did not hunt
    • It is one theory among several, since no written evidence explains the images
    • It is proven, because every cave shows hunters killing animals
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It is one theory among several, since no written evidence explains the images. Hunting magic is a possible explanation, but many images show no hunting, and other theories are equally plausible. Revise Art History: The Ancient World to the Renaissance →

Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism

  1. Which painter of The Calling of Saint Matthew is famous for tenebrism? (Foundation recall)
    • Caravaggio
    • Raphael
    • Vermeer
    • Watteau
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Caravaggio. Caravaggio's dramatic spotlighting against deep shadow became highly influential across Europe. Revise Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism →

  2. How does Bernini's David differ from Michelangelo's? (Exam style)
    • It is calm and still, waiting before the fight
    • It is much larger and carved from bronze
    • It shows David after the battle, holding the head
    • It shows the moment of action, twisting to throw the stone
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It shows the moment of action, twisting to throw the stone. Bernini chose peak movement, a typical Baroque choice that involves the viewer in the action. Revise Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism →

  3. Why did the Catholic Church favour dramatic, emotional images in the seventeenth century? (Stretch)
    • Because emotional art was cheaper to produce
    • Because Protestant patrons demanded calmer art
    • To discourage people from visiting churches
    • To strengthen faith and respond to Protestant criticism
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    To strengthen faith and respond to Protestant criticism. The Counter-Reformation used persuasive images to move believers and reaffirm Catholic teaching. Revise Art History: The Baroque to Mid-Century Modernism →

Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing

  1. Which material is typical of Minimalist art? (Foundation recall)
    • Carved marble figures of the human body
    • Gold leaf on carved wooden panels
    • Industrial materials such as steel and brick
    • Thick, expressive oil paint applied by hand
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Industrial materials such as steel and brick. Minimalists used industrial materials and fabrication. Revise Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing →

  2. Why did Michael Fried call Minimalism "theatrical"? (Exam style)
    • It always told dramatic stories about heroes
    • It depended on the viewer's presence and experience in space and time
    • It used painted scenery borrowed from theatres
    • It was performed on a stage by trained actors in costume
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It depended on the viewer's presence and experience in space and time. Fried thought art should be complete in itself, not reliant on a viewer's encounter. Revise Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing →

  3. What is the strongest defence of Equivalent VIII as art? (Stretch)
    • It makes viewers reconsider what sculpture can be and how objects occupy space
    • It represents a famous historical building
    • It was made by hand by the artist using rare materials
    • It was very expensive, so it must be valuable
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It makes viewers reconsider what sculpture can be and how objects occupy space. Its value lies in the questions it raises about sculpture and space. Revise Contemporary Art and Ways of Seeing →

The Psychology of Creativity

  1. According to the standard definition, which idea is creative? (Foundation recall)
    • A random design that nobody can use
    • An exact copy of a successful product
    • An unusual design that also solves the user's problem
    • Any idea that took a long time
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An unusual design that also solves the user's problem. Creativity needs originality and effectiveness together; random or copied ideas lack one of them. Revise The Psychology of Creativity →

  2. A Year 10 student invents a folding mechanism, unaware that an identical one was patented in 1950. How would Boden describe it? (Exam style)
    • Big-C creativity
    • H-creative but not P-creative
    • Neither P-creative nor H-creative
    • P-creative but not H-creative
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    P-creative but not H-creative. It is new to the student (P-creative) but not new in history (not H-creative). Revise The Psychology of Creativity →

  3. A critic argues a wildly unusual but unusable chair is "the most creative chair ever made". Which reply is strongest? (Stretch)
    • Only professional designers can make creative chairs
    • Originality alone is not enough; a creative design must also be effective for its purpose
    • Unusual objects are never creative because they break the rules
    • Usability is irrelevant to any judgement about creativity
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Originality alone is not enough; a creative design must also be effective for its purpose. Under the standard definition, novelty without effectiveness is not creativity, though as an art object its purpose might differ. Revise The Psychology of Creativity →

Art in Society and Professional Practice

  1. When did Tate Modern open? (Foundation recall)
    • 1753
    • 1824
    • 1897
    • 2000
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    2000. Tate Modern opened in a former power station in 2000. Revise Art in Society and Professional Practice →

  2. What is an artist-run space? (Exam style)
    • A national museum run directly by the government for the public
    • A shop that sells art materials to students
    • A venue organised by artists to show work, often outside the commercial market
    • An auction house that sells historic paintings to collectors
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A venue organised by artists to show work, often outside the commercial market. Artist-run spaces support experimental and emerging work. Revise Art in Society and Professional Practice →

  3. Which is the strongest argument for keeping national museums free? (Stretch)
    • Charging would make museums illegal under UK law
    • Free entry guarantees that every visitor is from a deprived area
    • Free entry widens access to shared cultural heritage, especially for those on low incomes
    • Free museums never need any other funding
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Free entry widens access to shared cultural heritage, especially for those on low incomes. Removing charges lowers a key barrier to participation. Revise Art in Society and Professional Practice →
