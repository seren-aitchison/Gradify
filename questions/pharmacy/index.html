Pharmacy practice questions

57 questions from Gradify's free Pharmacy course, with the answer and why behind each reveal. Answer before you open it: that's what makes it stick.

Foundations of Pharmacy Practice

  1. Which best describes medicines optimisation? (Foundation recall)
    • Making medicines cheaper
    • Manufacturing medicines
    • Person-centred safe and effective use of medicines
    • Reducing the number of pharmacies
    • Selling more medicines
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Person-centred safe and effective use of medicines. Medicines optimisation focuses on safe, effective use that gets the best outcomes for the person, not on cost or volume. Revise Foundations of Pharmacy Practice →

  2. What did the GPhC's 2021 education standards change for new pharmacists? (Exam style)
    • They must work in hospitals
    • They no longer dispense
    • They no longer need a degree
    • They register as independent prescribers
    • They regulate themselves
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They register as independent prescribers. The standards prepare pharmacists to prescribe from registration. Revise Foundations of Pharmacy Practice →

  3. A 15-year-old asks for emergency contraception. What applies? (Stretch)
    • Assess Gillick competence and follow the Fraser guidelines and safeguarding duties
    • Contact the young person's school for permission
    • Refer to the GP, as pharmacies can't supply under-16s
    • Refuse supply until a parent or guardian attends
    • Supply straight away, as no questions are allowed
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Assess Gillick competence and follow the Fraser guidelines and safeguarding duties. Under-16s can consent if competent; safeguarding must also be considered. Revise Foundations of Pharmacy Practice →

Pharmacology: How Drugs Act

  1. What does pharmacokinetics describe? (Foundation recall)
    • How drugs are licensed
    • How drugs are manufactured
    • How drugs are priced
    • What a drug does to the body
    • What the body does to a drug
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    What the body does to a drug. Pharmacokinetics covers absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion. Revise Pharmacology: How Drugs Act →

  2. Why are non-selective β-blockers avoided in asthma? (Exam style)
    • Blocking β2 receptors can cause bronchospasm
    • They block histamine release
    • They cause asthma to develop
    • They raise blood glucose sharply
    • They stimulate airway mucus glands
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Blocking β2 receptors can cause bronchospasm. β2 receptors mediate bronchodilation, so blocking them can trigger bronchospasm. Revise Pharmacology: How Drugs Act →

  3. Why does prednisolone take hours to reduce inflammation? (Stretch)
    • It acts on nuclear receptors to change gene transcription
    • It blocks ion channels slowly
    • It must first be converted to insulin
    • It needs to reach the bone marrow first
    • It only works after it's excreted
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It acts on nuclear receptors to change gene transcription. Nuclear receptor effects depend on new gene transcription and protein synthesis. Revise Pharmacology: How Drugs Act →

Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics

  1. Which process removes a drug from the body unchanged? (Foundation recall)
    • Absorption
    • Dissolution
    • Distribution
    • Excretion
    • Ionisation
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Excretion. Excretion (mainly renal or biliary) removes drug from the body; metabolism changes it chemically. Revise Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics →

  2. Why might two people on the same dose have different drug levels? (Exam style)
    • Because doses are always rounded
    • Because the tablets differ in colour
    • Differences in kidney function, age or interactions
    • Levels never differ between people
    • Only because of different diets
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Differences in kidney function, age or interactions. Organ function, age, genetics and interactions all change pharmacokinetics. Revise Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics →

  3. A slower-dissolving formulation has the same AUC but a lower Cmax. What changed? (Stretch)
    • The drug's clearance
    • The drug's half-life
    • The extent of absorption
    • The rate, not the extent, of absorption
    • The volume of distribution
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The rate, not the extent, of absorption. Same AUC means the same extent; a lower, later peak reflects slower absorption. Revise Pharmacokinetics and Biopharmaceutics →

Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry

  1. Which functional group is most likely to form an ionic bond with an aspartate residue in a receptor? (Foundation recall)
    • A chlorine substituent
    • A methyl group
    • A protonated amine
    • An aromatic ring
    • An ether linkage
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A protonated amine. A positively charged amine pairs with the negatively charged carboxylate of aspartate. Revise Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry →

  2. Why does adding a chlorine to an aromatic ring often prolong a drug's action? (Exam style)
    • It always increases receptor efficacy
    • It can block metabolism at that position
    • It makes the drug fully water soluble
    • It prevents the drug crossing membranes
    • It turns the drug into a prodrug
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It can block metabolism at that position. Halogens resist oxidation, so they can block a site of metabolism. Revise Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry →

  3. A drug contains an ester and a phenol. Which storage risks should you expect? (Stretch)
    • Evaporation and sublimation
    • Freezing and crystal growth only
    • Hydrolysis and oxidation
    • No risk, as both groups are stable
    • Polymerisation and racemisation only
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Hydrolysis and oxidation. Esters hydrolyse in moisture and phenols oxidise in air and light. Revise Medicinal and Pharmaceutical Chemistry →

Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms

  1. Which branch of pharmaceutics studies how dosage forms affect absorption? (Foundation recall)
    • Biopharmaceutics
    • Pharmacoeconomics
    • Pharmacognosy
    • Pharmacovigilance
    • Pharmacy law
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Biopharmaceutics. Biopharmaceutics links the dosage form to the rate and extent of absorption. Revise Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms →

  2. Which is an aim of dosage form design? (Exam style)
    • Avoiding any use of excipients
    • Ensuring every product is an injection
    • Keeping the drug stable until it is used
    • Making doses impossible to measure
    • Making the drug as bitter as possible
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Keeping the drug stable until it is used. Stability is a key aim alongside dose accuracy and acceptability. Revise Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms →

  3. Why may a modified-release and an immediate-release tablet of the same drug not be swapped dose for dose? (Stretch)
    • Immediate-release tablets are never absorbed
    • Modified-release tablets contain no drug
    • Their release rates and peak levels differ
    • They always contain different drugs
    • They are only different colours
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Their release rates and peak levels differ. Release profile changes peak concentration and duration of action. Revise Pharmaceutics: Formulation and Dosage Forms →

Pharmaceutical Calculations

  1. How many micrograms are in 2.5 mg? (Foundation recall)
    • 0.0025 micrograms
    • 2,500 micrograms
    • 25 micrograms
    • 25,000 micrograms
    • 250 micrograms
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    2,500 micrograms. 1 mg is 1,000 micrograms, so 2.5 mg is 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 micrograms. Revise Pharmaceutical Calculations →

  2. Which way of writing a dose is safest? (Exam style)
    • .5 mg
    • 0.5 mg
    • 0.50 mg
    • 1/2 mg
    • 500 mcg
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    0.5 mg. A leading zero prevents ".5" being read as 5; trailing zeros and abbreviations such as mcg cause misreading. Revise Pharmaceutical Calculations →

  3. How many mEq of calcium are in 2 mmol of calcium ions? (Stretch)
    • 0.5 mEq
    • 1 mEq
    • 2 mEq
    • 4 mEq
    • 8 mEq
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    4 mEq. Calcium is divalent: mEq = mmol × 2 = 4 mEq. Revise Pharmaceutical Calculations →

Cardiovascular Therapeutics

  1. What confirms a diagnosis of hypertension after a raised clinic reading? (Foundation recall)
    • A blood test
    • A second clinic reading the same day
    • A urine dipstick
    • Ambulatory or home blood pressure monitoring
    • An ECG alone
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ambulatory or home blood pressure monitoring. NICE NG136 recommends ABPM or HBPM to confirm. Revise Cardiovascular Therapeutics →

  2. What is the NICE clinic blood pressure target for an 84-year-old? (Exam style)
    • Below 120/80 mmHg
    • Below 130/80 mmHg
    • Below 140/90 mmHg
    • Below 150/90 mmHg
    • Below 160/100 mmHg
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Below 150/90 mmHg. NICE sets a less strict target at 80 and over, adjusted for frailty. Revise Cardiovascular Therapeutics →

  3. Someone on amlodipine develops ankle swelling. A diuretic is suggested. What's best? (Stretch)
    • Add a potassium supplement
    • Add a second CCB
    • Add the diuretic straight away
    • Double the amlodipine
    • Review the amlodipine first
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Review the amlodipine first. CCB oedema doesn't respond well to diuretics; adding one risks a prescribing cascade. Revise Cardiovascular Therapeutics →

Respiratory Therapeutics

  1. Which device is most suitable for a 3-year-old with asthma? (Foundation recall)
    • Breath-actuated inhaler without spacer
    • Dry powder inhaler
    • Metered-dose inhaler with spacer and face mask
    • Nebuliser at every dose
    • Soft mist inhaler without spacer
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Metered-dose inhaler with spacer and face mask. Young children can't coordinate or inhale forcefully enough for other devices. Revise Respiratory Therapeutics →

  2. What is first-line treatment for newly diagnosed asthma in a 30-year-old under NG245? (Exam style)
    • A long-acting antimuscarinic alone
    • As-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol
    • Montelukast alone
    • Oral prednisolone daily
    • Salbutamol alone as needed
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    As-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol. NG245 recommends AIR therapy with ICS/formoterol for people aged 12 and over. Revise Respiratory Therapeutics →

  3. Before stepping up asthma treatment, what should be checked first? (Stretch)
    • Adherence and inhaler technique
    • Bone density
    • Liver function tests
    • The person's blood group
    • Their cholesterol level
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Adherence and inhaler technique. Poor technique and adherence are common reasons for apparent treatment failure. Revise Respiratory Therapeutics →

Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics

  1. Why is insulin essential in type 1 diabetes? (Foundation recall)
    • Insulin cures the condition
    • It only lowers blood pressure
    • It prevents infection
    • It replaces dietary sugar
    • The pancreas makes little or no insulin
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The pancreas makes little or no insulin. Autoimmune destruction of beta cells removes insulin production. Revise Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics →

  2. Someone with type 1 diabetes has gastroenteritis and isn't eating. What should they do with their basal insulin? (Exam style)
    • Continue it and check glucose and ketones more often
    • Halve every dose for a week
    • Stop it until they eat again
    • Switch to tablets
    • Take it only if glucose is above 20
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Continue it and check glucose and ketones more often. Stopping basal insulin risks diabetic ketoacidosis. Revise Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics →

  3. What HbA1c target suits someone on a sulfonylurea? (Stretch)
    • 30 mmol/mol
    • 42 mmol/mol
    • 53 mmol/mol
    • 75 mmol/mol
    • 90 mmol/mol
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    53 mmol/mol. NICE suggests 53 when drugs can cause hypoglycaemia. Revise Endocrine and Diabetes Therapeutics →

Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics

  1. How soon do antidepressants usually start to work? (Foundation recall)
    • After 6 months
    • Immediately on the first dose
    • Only after a year
    • Within 2 to 4 weeks
    • Within a few hours
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Within 2 to 4 weeks. Benefit builds over weeks, so early review supports adherence. Revise Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics →

  2. A 22-year-old starts sertraline. When should they be reviewed? (Exam style)
    • After 1 year
    • After 3 months
    • Never, if no problems
    • Only if they ask
    • Within about 1 week
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Within about 1 week. Young people are reviewed early because suicide risk can rise at the start. Revise Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics →

  3. How should an SSRI usually be stopped after long-term use? (Stretch)
    • Gradually tapered over weeks or longer
    • Halved once and stopped
    • Replaced with St John's wort
    • Stopped abruptly
    • Swapped for alcohol
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Gradually tapered over weeks or longer. NICE recommends gradual tapering to reduce discontinuation symptoms. Revise Central Nervous System and Mental Health Therapeutics →

Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship

  1. Which organism is Gram-positive? (Foundation recall)
    • Escherichia coli
    • Haemophilus influenzae
    • Klebsiella pneumoniae
    • Pseudomonas aeruginosa
    • Staphylococcus aureus
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Staphylococcus aureus. Staphylococci have a thick peptidoglycan wall. Revise Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship →

  2. Why take a sample before starting antibiotics? (Exam style)
    • Because samples are always required by law
    • It doesn't matter when
    • So culture results can guide treatment
    • To increase the antibiotic's effect
    • To reduce the dose needed
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    So culture results can guide treatment. Antibiotics can stop organisms growing in the sample. Revise Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship →

  3. Which question is most useful when someone reports a penicillin allergy? (Stretch)
    • Do they like the taste?
    • Have they had a flu jab?
    • How much did it cost?
    • What exactly happened and how severe was it?
    • Which pharmacy did they use?
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    What exactly happened and how severe was it?. The reaction's nature and severity decide whether it's a true allergy. Revise Infection and Antimicrobial Stewardship →

Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics

  1. How long is an initial PPI course for uninvestigated dyspepsia? (Foundation recall)
    • 1 day
    • 2 years
    • 4 weeks
    • 6 months
    • Lifelong
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    4 weeks. NICE suggests a full-dose PPI for 4 weeks, then review. Revise Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics →

  2. Which feature in a person with indigestion needs urgent referral? (Exam style)
    • Burping
    • Difficulty swallowing
    • Occasional heartburn
    • Relief with antacids
    • Symptoms after a large meal
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Difficulty swallowing. Dysphagia is an alarm feature for oesophageal cancer. Revise Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics →

  3. Which combination most increases the risk of a bleeding ulcer? (Stretch)
    • Amoxicillin with a PPI
    • An NSAID with an SSRI and a corticosteroid
    • Lactulose with senna
    • Loperamide with fluids
    • Paracetamol with an alginate
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An NSAID with an SSRI and a corticosteroid. Each adds to gastrointestinal bleeding risk. Revise Gastrointestinal, Renal and Hepatic Therapeutics →

Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions

  1. Which COX isoform protects the stomach lining? (Foundation recall)
    • COX-1
    • COX-2
    • COX-3
    • Lipoxygenase
    • Phospholipase
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    COX-1. COX-1 produces protective prostaglandins in the stomach. Revise Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions →

  2. Which person is most at risk of NSAID-induced bleeding? (Exam style)
    • A 25-year-old with a sprain
    • A 30-year-old with toothache
    • A 75-year-old taking an SSRI and warfarin
    • A runner with knee pain
    • A teenager with period pain
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A 75-year-old taking an SSRI and warfarin. Age, anticoagulants and SSRIs all increase bleeding risk. Revise Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions →

  3. Which NSAID is associated with the highest cardiovascular risk? (Stretch)
    • Diclofenac
    • Low-dose ibuprofen
    • Naproxen
    • Topical diclofenac in small amounts
    • Topical ibuprofen
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Diclofenac. Diclofenac has cardiovascular risk similar to COX-2 inhibitors. Revise Musculoskeletal, Skin, Eye and Inflammatory Conditions →

Oncology, Haematology and Immunology

  1. What type of anaemia is typical of iron deficiency? (Foundation recall)
    • Aplastic
    • Haemolytic only
    • Macrocytic
    • Microcytic
    • Sickle cell
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Microcytic. Iron deficiency produces small red cells. Revise Oncology, Haematology and Immunology →

  2. Which medicine can cause B12 deficiency with long-term use? (Exam style)
    • Amoxicillin
    • Emollients
    • Metformin
    • Paracetamol
    • Salbutamol
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Metformin. Metformin reduces B12 absorption over time. Revise Oncology, Haematology and Immunology →

  3. Why does iron deficiency in a 65-year-old man need investigation? (Stretch)
    • Haemoglobin falls normally with age in men
    • Iron deficiency in older men is almost always dietary
    • It may come from gastrointestinal blood loss, including cancer
    • It shows he needs more red meat in his diet
    • Only women need iron deficiency investigated
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It may come from gastrointestinal blood loss, including cancer. Unexplained iron deficiency can signal bowel cancer. Revise Oncology, Haematology and Immunology →

Special Populations

  1. What is the UK reference for medicines in children? (Foundation recall)
    • BNF for Children
    • The Drug Tariff
    • The Green Book
    • The MEP only
    • The Orange Guide
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    BNF for Children. The BNFC covers paediatric doses and licensing. Revise Special Populations →

  2. Why are oral syringes supplied with children's liquids? (Exam style)
    • Because spoons are illegal
    • To increase the dose
    • To make the medicine taste better
    • To measure small doses accurately
    • To store the medicine
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    To measure small doses accurately. Household spoons vary in volume and cause errors. Revise Special Populations →

  3. Why are tenfold errors more likely in neonatal care? (Stretch)
    • Calculations aren't used
    • Neonates don't need weight-based doses
    • Neonates need large doses
    • Standard concentrations are banned
    • Very small doses and volumes make decimal errors easy
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Very small doses and volumes make decimal errors easy. Small volumes and decimals make errors common, so double checks are used. Revise Special Populations →

Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance

  1. Which body licenses medicines in the UK? (Foundation recall)
    • NICE
    • The GPhC
    • The Home Office
    • The MHRA
    • The PSNI
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The MHRA. The MHRA grants marketing authorisations and regulates medicines' safety. Revise Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance →

  2. Which legislation sets out the schedules of controlled drugs for healthcare use? (Exam style)
    • Human Medicines Regulations 2012
    • Medicines Act 1968
    • Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001
    • Pharmacy Order 2010
    • Veterinary Medicines Regulations 2013
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. The 2001 Regulations, made under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, set the five schedules. Revise Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance →

  3. A patient asks for an emergency supply of their morphine modified-release tablets. What applies? (Stretch)
    • Supply 30 days
    • Supply 5 days
    • Supply 72 hours' worth
    • Supply if the GP agrees by phone
    • The law doesn't allow it: morphine is Schedule 2
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The law doesn't allow it: morphine is Schedule 2. Schedule 1 to 3 CDs can't be supplied in an emergency at a patient's request, except phenobarbital for epilepsy. Revise Pharmacy Law, Ethics and Governance →

Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation

  1. Which question belongs to the clinical rather than the legal check? (Foundation recall)
    • Is the dose right for this person's kidneys?
    • Is the form the correct colour?
    • Is the patient's address present?
    • Is the prescriber's signature present?
    • Is the prescription in date?
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Is the dose right for this person's kidneys?. Renal dosing is part of the clinical check; the others are legal requirements. Revise Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation →

  2. A repeat prescription arrives for lithium; the last level was 9 months ago. What's the best action? (Exam style)
    • Ask the patient to stop it
    • Contact the prescriber about overdue monitoring
    • Halve the dose to be safe
    • Refuse to supply ever again
    • Supply and say nothing
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Contact the prescriber about overdue monitoring. Lithium needs regular monitoring; overdue tests should be raised with the prescriber. Revise Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation →

  3. A medicine on the GP list isn't in the person's own supply. What should you do? (Stretch)
    • Ask the family to buy it
    • Double the remaining doses
    • Find out whether it was stopped, and why, before continuing it
    • Ignore it as an error
    • Restart it straight away
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Find out whether it was stopped, and why, before continuing it. It may have been stopped deliberately; confirm with the person and the GP. Revise Clinical Pharmacy and Medicines Optimisation →

    • Ten years' experience
