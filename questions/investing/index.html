Investing practice questions

Money Foundations

  1. Which statement best describes investing? (Foundation recall)
    • Buying assets whose value can rise or fall, in the hope of higher long-run returns
    • Keeping cash at home for emergencies
    • Lending money to a friend at no interest
    • Putting money in an account that guarantees your capital back
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Buying assets whose value can rise or fall, in the hope of higher long-run returns. Investing means accepting that value can fall; a guarantee of capital is a feature of saving, not investing. Revise Money Foundations →

  2. Priya needs £5,000 for university fees in 12 months. Which is most suitable? (Exam style)
    • A fund of global shares, because shares grow fastest
    • A savings account with easy access or a fixed term ending before the fees are due
    • A single company's shares recommended online
    • Leaving it invested in property
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A savings account with easy access or a fixed term ending before the fees are due. Twelve months is too short to absorb a market fall, so money needed soon belongs in savings. Revise Money Foundations →

  3. Which is the strongest reason a 25-year-old might invest part of their pension savings in shares rather than cash? (Stretch)
    • A long horizon allows time to recover from falls, and cash has historically lost ground to inflation over decades
    • Cash savings are not protected in the UK
    • Shares never lose value over ten years
    • Shares pay a guaranteed dividend
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A long horizon allows time to recover from falls, and cash has historically lost ground to inflation over decades. Long horizons let investors ride out volatility in exchange for higher expected real returns; shares can still fall over ten years and dividends are never guaranteed. Revise Money Foundations →

How Financial Markets Work

  1. Which is a core function of the financial system? (Foundation recall)
    • Channelling savings to borrowers who can invest them productively
    • Collecting income tax
    • Running state schools
    • Setting the price of every good in shops
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Channelling savings to borrowers who can invest them productively. Moving money from savers to productive borrowers is central; tax and pricing goods are not financial system functions. Revise How Financial Markets Work →

  2. A company issues new bonds to fund a wind farm and a pension fund buys them. What has the financial system done? (Exam style)
    • Created money from nothing
    • Guaranteed the pension fund's return
    • Linked savers' money with a productive investment, with the wind farm's income paying the bond interest
    • Transferred risk to the government
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Linked savers' money with a productive investment, with the wind farm's income paying the bond interest. The pension fund's savers lend to the company; their return depends on the project's cash flows. Revise How Financial Markets Work →

  3. Which statement best evaluates the role of finance in the 2007–09 crisis? (Stretch)
    • Finance had no effect on the real economy
    • Only shareholders of banks were affected
    • The crisis proved financial systems are unnecessary
    • When the system failed, lending to households and firms fell sharply and the real economy suffered, showing both its value and its risks
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    When the system failed, lending to households and firms fell sharply and the real economy suffered, showing both its value and its risks. The crisis showed how dependent the economy is on functioning credit and how failures spread beyond finance. Revise How Financial Markets Work →

Asset Classes

  1. Which is a cash-type instrument issued by the UK government? (Foundation recall)
    • A 30-year conventional gilt
    • A FTSE 100 share
    • A REIT
    • A Treasury bill
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A Treasury bill. Treasury bills are short-term government debt; long gilts are bonds. Revise Asset Classes →

  2. Why might a long-term investor still hold some cash? (Exam style)
    • Because cash has the highest long-run return
    • Because cash is immune to inflation
    • Because cash is required in every ISA
    • For liquidity, stability in falls and the ability to rebalance or meet needs without selling
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    For liquidity, stability in falls and the ability to rebalance or meet needs without selling. Cash's value lies in liquidity and stability, not long-run growth. Revise Asset Classes →

  3. Which is the best evaluation of a 30-year-old holding 40% of their pension in cash for safety? (Stretch)
    • It is ideal, because cash never loses value
    • It is too risky
    • It makes no difference over 35 years
    • It reduces short-term falls but is likely to cost substantial long-run real growth, given the near-zero historical real return on cash
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It reduces short-term falls but is likely to cost substantial long-run real growth, given the near-zero historical real return on cash. The safety of cash in nominal terms comes at the cost of growth over long horizons. Revise Asset Classes →

Risk and Return

  1. A fund rises from £100 to £108 and pays £2 of income in a year. What is its holding period return? (Foundation recall)
    • 10%
    • 2%
    • 6%
    • 8%
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    10%. (8 + 2) ÷ 100 = 10%; leaving out income would give 8%. Revise Risk and Return →

  2. An advert says a fund "returned 80% over ten years". What is the fairest way to compare it with a savings rate? (Exam style)
    • Annualise it: 1.80.1 − 1 is about 6% a year
    • Compare 80% directly with the savings rate
    • Divide by ten: 8% a year
    • Ignore it, because all adverts are false
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Annualise it: 1.80.1 − 1 is about 6% a year. Annualising gives about 6.1% a year, the comparable figure; simple division overstates it. Revise Risk and Return →

  3. Two funds report five-year returns of 40% and 45%. Which further information matters most before concluding the second is better? (Stretch)
    • The fund managers' ages
    • The number of shares each holds
    • Whether both include income and are net of charges, and how volatile each was
    • Which fund has the longer name
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Whether both include income and are net of charges, and how volatile each was. Like-for-like comparison needs the same basis (total return, net of fees) and context on risk. Revise Risk and Return →

Diversification and Portfolio Theory

  1. What determines how much diversification reduces risk? (Foundation recall)
    • How closely the holdings' returns move together (their correlation)
    • The number of letters in each fund's name
    • The size of each company's head office
    • Whether shares pay dividends
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    How closely the holdings' returns move together (their correlation). Lower correlation means more offsetting and more risk reduction. Revise Diversification and Portfolio Theory →

  2. Two assets each have 20% volatility. If they are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), what is the volatility of a 50/50 portfolio? (Exam style)
    • 0%
    • 10%
    • 20%: with perfect correlation there is no diversification benefit
    • 28%
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    20%: with perfect correlation there is no diversification benefit. With ρ = 1 the portfolio volatility equals the weighted average, so no risk is removed. Revise Diversification and Portfolio Theory →

  3. In a severe global crash most of an investor's holdings fall together. Which is the best evaluation? (Stretch)
    • Diversification guarantees protection in crashes
    • Diversification has failed and should be abandoned
    • Diversification still helped against specific risk, but rising correlations in crises limit its protection against market-wide falls
    • The investor must not have owned any shares
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Diversification still helped against specific risk, but rising correlations in crises limit its protection against market-wide falls. Diversification is not insurance against systematic falls; bonds and cash may help more in such periods. Revise Diversification and Portfolio Theory →

Valuation

  1. Which statement best describes intrinsic value? (Foundation recall)
    • An estimate of worth based on the cash the business should generate
    • The average share price over the past twelve months
    • The nominal value printed on the share certificate
    • The price at which the share last traded on the exchange
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An estimate of worth based on the cash the business should generate. Intrinsic value is an opinion built from forecasts of future cash flows, not an observed price. Revise Valuation →

  2. An analyst values a company by comparing its price-to-earnings ratio with five similar listed firms. Which approach is this? (Exam style)
    • Asset-based valuation using break-up values
    • Intrinsic valuation using discounted cash flows
    • Relative valuation
    • Technical analysis using past price charts
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Relative valuation. Comparing multiples with peers is relative valuation; it tells you whether a share is cheap compared with others, not in absolute terms. Revise Valuation →

  3. Which investor most needs a wide margin of safety? (Stretch)
    • One buying a short-dated gilt to hold until it matures
    • One valuing a cash deposit with a fixed and known interest rate
    • One valuing a utility with regulated and very stable cash flows
    • One valuing a young loss-making firm with uncertain future revenues
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    One valuing a young loss-making firm with uncertain future revenues. The less predictable the cash flows, the larger the possible forecasting error and so the bigger the cushion needed. Revise Valuation →

Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts

  1. Which user is most interested in a company's ability to repay a loan? (Foundation recall)
    • A competitor's employees
    • A customer buying a single product
    • A lender
    • A local resident
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A lender. Lenders focus on liquidity, cash flow, gearing and interest cover. Revise Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts →

  2. Profit rose from £400,000 to £460,000. What is the percentage increase? (Exam style)
    • 13%
    • 15%
    • 6%
    • 60%
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    15%. Percentage change = change ÷ original: (460,000 − 400,000) ÷ 400,000 = 15%. Revise Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts →

  3. A company's gross margin is 25%. An analyst calls this poor. What is missing from the analysis? (Stretch)
    • Comparison with the company's previous years and with the industry
    • Nothing, because 25% is always a poor margin
    • The company's registered office address
    • The name of the company's auditor
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Comparison with the company's previous years and with the industry. Whether 25% is good depends on industry norms and the trend; supermarkets have low margins, software firms high ones. Revise Interpreting Financial Statements and Group Accounts →

Funds, Index Investing and Costs

  1. What does an investor in a fund own? (Foundation recall)
    • A fixed sum that the fund promises to return
    • A loan to the fund manager repaid with interest
    • A proportional share of the fund's whole portfolio
    • Direct legal title to each share the fund holds
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A proportional share of the fund's whole portfolio. Units or shares in a fund represent a proportion of all its holdings, so value rises and falls with the portfolio. Revise Funds, Index Investing and Costs →

  2. Why can a fund offer diversification to someone with only £100 a month? (Exam style)
    • Funds are allowed to borrow to buy extra shares for small savers
    • Pooled money buys many holdings at shared cost
    • Regulators guarantee the value of small monthly contributions
    • Small investors receive only the safest shares in the portfolio
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Pooled money buys many holdings at shared cost. Pooling spreads costs and lets each pound buy a slice of every holding. Revise Funds, Index Investing and Costs →

  3. A fund manager goes out of business. What happens to the fund's investments? (Stretch)
    • They are automatically converted into cash and paid to the government
    • They are handed to the FCA, which keeps them for five years
    • They are seized by the manager's creditors and sold at once
    • They stay with the depositary and are not used to pay the manager's debts
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They stay with the depositary and are not used to pay the manager's debts. Ring-fenced custody by an independent depositary protects fund assets if the manager fails. Revise Funds, Index Investing and Costs →

Behavioural Finance

  1. Which statement describes the rational investor of traditional finance? (Foundation recall)
    • Avoids all risk by keeping savings only in cash
    • Relies on gut feeling and the views of friends when investing
    • Trades often to take advantage of every price movement
    • Uses all relevant information and chooses the highest expected utility
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Uses all relevant information and chooses the highest expected utility. The rational investor weighs all information, updates correctly and maximises expected utility. Revise Behavioural Finance →

  2. Which is an example of an emotional bias rather than a cognitive bias? (Exam style)
    • Estimating a value by starting from a recent price
    • Misjudging probability from a small sample of data
    • Overweighting information that is easy to recall
    • Selling in a panic because a loss feels unbearable
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Selling in a panic because a loss feels unbearable. Emotional biases arise from feelings such as fear and regret; the others are information-processing errors. Revise Behavioural Finance →

  3. Why does it matter that investor biases are systematic rather than random? (Stretch)
    • Random errors are more expensive for investors than systematic ones
    • Random errors only affect professional fund managers
    • Systematic errors always cancel out across many investors
    • Systematic errors can be anticipated, and designed against, by investors and regulators
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Systematic errors can be anticipated, and designed against, by investors and regulators. Predictable errors can be anticipated, so they can be guarded against with rules and design. Revise Behavioural Finance →

UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax

  1. Which tax normally applies to dividends received in a general investment account? (Foundation recall)
    • Capital Gains Tax
    • Income tax
    • Inheritance Tax
    • Stamp duty
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Income tax. Dividends are income, taxed at dividend rates after the £500 allowance. Revise UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax →

  2. What is the main tax difference between an ISA and a pension? (Exam style)
    • An ISA gives no relief on payments in; a pension does, but most withdrawals are taxed
    • An ISA is for cash only; a pension is for shares only
    • An ISA taxes all withdrawals; a pension never taxes anything at all
    • There is no difference; both are taxed exactly like a GIA
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An ISA gives no relief on payments in; a pension does, but most withdrawals are taxed. ISAs are funded from taxed income with tax-free withdrawals; pensions get relief going in and are mostly taxed coming out. Revise UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax →

  3. An investor has £40,000 in a GIA producing interest, dividends and gains well above her allowances, and has not used this year's ISA allowance. What is the most sensible step? (Stretch)
    • Consider moving investments into an ISA over time to shelter future returns
    • Ignore tax, because it never affects long-term returns
    • Move everything into cash, because cash is never taxed in the UK
    • Stop investing, because tax makes investing pointless
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Consider moving investments into an ISA over time to shelter future returns. Sheltering returns in an ISA removes future income tax and CGT on them. Revise UK Wrappers, Pensions and Tax →

Regulation, Protection and Ethics

  1. What is a conflict of interest for an adviser? (Foundation recall)
    • Being tempted to recommend products that pay the adviser more
    • Charging a fee that is clearly disclosed
    • Disagreeing with a client about football
    • Working for two clients in different towns
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Being tempted to recommend products that pay the adviser more. Commission can bias recommendations away from what suits the client. Revise Regulation, Protection and Ethics →

  2. Why are long timescales a reason to regulate pensions? (Exam style)
    • Long timescales make pensions risk-free
    • Pension savers check their pots every day
    • Pensions are always paid out within a year of starting to save
    • Problems may only appear decades later, when it is too late to fix them
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Problems may only appear decades later, when it is too late to fix them. Consumers cannot learn from experience when outcomes arrive so late. Revise Regulation, Protection and Ethics →

  3. Which statement best describes the limits of regulation? (Stretch)
    • It controls how firms behave but cannot stop investments falling in value
    • It guarantees that all regulated investments will grow
    • It means advisers can never make mistakes
    • It removes all costs from financial products
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It controls how firms behave but cannot stop investments falling in value. Market risk remains even in well-regulated markets. Revise Regulation, Protection and Ethics →

Building Your Plan

  1. Which should usually come first in a financial plan? (Foundation recall)
    • A budget showing income and spending
    • Buying individual shares
    • Choosing a retirement annuity
    • Investing in property abroad
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A budget showing income and spending. A budget is the foundation of every other decision. Revise Building Your Plan →

  2. Why should expensive credit card debt usually be paid off before investing? (Exam style)
    • Credit card debt always disappears after a year
    • Investing is illegal while you have debt
    • It improves your pension tax relief
    • Its interest rate is usually higher than likely investment returns
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Its interest rate is usually higher than likely investment returns. Clearing 24% debt is like a guaranteed 24% return. Revise Building Your Plan →

  3. Who most needs protection insurance early in their plan? (Stretch)
    • A retired person with a full pension
    • A student with no dependants or debts
    • Someone with children who depend on their income
    • Someone with no income to protect
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Someone with children who depend on their income. Protection matters most where others rely on your earnings. Revise Building Your Plan →
