Number and Place Value

  1. Mia counts some shells: "one, two, three, four, five, six." How many shells are there? (Foundation recall)
    • 1
    • 5
    • 6
    • 7
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    6. The last number Mia says is six, so there are six shells. The first number, one, only tells you where she started. Revise Number and Place Value →

  2. Leo counts 9 cars, then counts again starting with a different car. What should he find? (Exam style)
    • 9 cars again
    • A different number
    • One fewer car
    • One more car
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    9 cars again. The total doesn't change when you start in a different place, so Leo should count 9 again. If he gets a different number, he has made a mistake somewhere. Revise Number and Place Value →

  3. Sam counts 10 pencils but touches one pencil twice. What answer will he probably get? (Stretch)
    • 10
    • 11
    • 20
    • 9
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    11. Touching one pencil twice adds an extra number, so Sam says eleven even though there are only ten pencils. Revise Number and Place Value →

The Four Operations and Times Tables

  1. Which pair is a number bond to 10? (Foundation recall)
    • 6 and 4
    • 6 and 5
    • 7 and 4
    • 8 and 3
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    6 and 4. 6 + 4 = 10. The other pairs make 11, so they are bonds to 11 instead. Revise The Four Operations and Times Tables →

  2. What must you add to 13 to make 20? (Exam style)
    • 17
    • 3
    • 33
    • 7
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    7. 3 + 7 = 10, so 13 + 7 = 20. You use the bond to 10 and keep the extra ten. Revise The Four Operations and Times Tables →

  3. Which fact is wrong, and so NOT in the same family as 30 + 70 = 100? (Stretch)
    • 100 − 30 = 30
    • 100 − 30 = 70
    • 100 − 70 = 30
    • 70 + 30 = 100
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    100 − 30 = 30. The family uses 30, 70 and 100. 100 − 30 is 70, not 30, so that fact is false. Revise The Four Operations and Times Tables →

Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

  1. A sandwich is cut into 4 equal pieces. What is each piece called? (Foundation recall)
    • A half
    • A quarter
    • A third
    • A whole
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A quarter. Four equal parts are quarters. Two equal parts would be halves. Revise Fractions, Decimals and Percentages →

  2. What is half of 16? (Exam style)
    • 32
    • 4
    • 6
    • 8
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    8. Share 16 into 2 equal groups and each has 8. 4 would be a quarter of 16, and 32 is double. Revise Fractions, Decimals and Percentages →

  3. A ribbon is cut into two pieces, one 10 cm long and one 6 cm long. Is each piece a half? (Stretch)
    • No, because a half must be 5 cm
    • No, because the pieces are not equal
    • Yes, because there are two pieces
    • Yes, because they add up to 16 cm
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    No, because the pieces are not equal. Halves must be equal. Each half of the 16 cm ribbon would be 8 cm, so these pieces are not halves. Revise Fractions, Decimals and Percentages →

Measurement, Geometry and Statistics

  1. Which unit is best for measuring the length of a football pitch? (Foundation recall)
    • Centimetres
    • Grams
    • Metres
    • Millimetres
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Metres. A football pitch is about a hundred metres long, so metres are sensible. Grams measure mass, not length. Revise Measurement, Geometry and Statistics →

  2. A crayon starts at 0 and ends at 7 cm and 4 small marks. How long is it? (Exam style)
    • 4 cm 7 mm
    • 7 cm 4 mm
    • 7 cm 40 mm
    • 74 cm
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    7 cm 4 mm. Each small mark is a millimetre, so the crayon is 7 cm and 4 mm long, which is 74 mm. Revise Measurement, Geometry and Statistics →

  3. Which length is longest? (Stretch)
    • 1 m 10 cm
    • 1,050 mm
    • 1.2 m
    • 115 cm
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    1.2 m. Change them all to centimetres: 120 cm, 115 cm, 105 cm and 110 cm. 1.2 m is longest. Revise Measurement, Geometry and Statistics →

Phonics and Spelling

  1. How many phonemes are in the word "dog"? (Foundation recall)
    • 1
    • 2
    • 3
    • 4
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    3. "Dog" has three sounds: /d/ /o/ /g/. Each sound is one phoneme. Revise Phonics and Spelling →

  2. Which is a grapheme? (Exam style)
    • The letters "sh" in "ship"
    • The picture of a ship
    • The sound you hear at the start of "ship"
    • The whole word "ship"
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The letters "sh" in "ship". A grapheme is the written letter or letters for one sound. "sh" is two letters writing one sound. Revise Phonics and Spelling →

  3. Why does English need graphemes with more than one letter? (Stretch)
    • It has more letters than sounds
    • It has more sounds than letters
    • Long words need more letters
    • Some letters are silent
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It has more sounds than letters. English has about 44 sounds but only 26 letters, so some sounds are written with two or three letters, like "sh" and "igh". Revise Phonics and Spelling →

Reading and Comprehension

  1. Where would you usually find a book's blurb? (Foundation recall)
    • In the index
    • On the back cover
    • On the first page of chapter 1
    • On the spine
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    On the back cover. The blurb is on the back cover and tells you what the book is about, to help you decide whether to read it. Revise Reading and Comprehension →

  2. In the five finger test, you find 2 words you don't know on a page. What does that suggest? (Exam style)
    • The book is about right for you
    • The book is far too hard
    • The book is too easy to read
    • You should stop reading
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The book is about right for you. Two or three unknown words means the book is "just right": challenging enough to learn from but not too hard. Revise Reading and Comprehension →

  3. Why might reading lots of stories help with science and history too? (Stretch)
    • It makes your handwriting neater
    • It means you don't need to read textbooks
    • Stories always contain science facts
    • You meet more words and ideas you can use in other subjects
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    You meet more words and ideas you can use in other subjects. Wide reading builds vocabulary and background knowledge, which help you understand what you read in every subject. Revise Reading and Comprehension →

Grammar, Punctuation and Writing

  1. Which word is a noun in "The cat slept on the warm mat"? (Foundation recall)
    • mat
    • on
    • slept
    • warm
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    mat. "Mat" names a thing. "Slept" is a verb, "warm" an adjective and "on" a preposition. Revise Grammar, Punctuation and Writing →

  2. Which is a proper noun? (Exam style)
    • country
    • mountain
    • river
    • Scotland
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Scotland. Scotland names a particular place, so it is a proper noun with a capital letter. The others are common nouns. Revise Grammar, Punctuation and Writing →

  3. Which sentence uses capital letters for proper nouns correctly? (Stretch)
    • On friday, leo Visited cardiff.
    • On friday, Leo visited cardiff.
    • On Friday, leo visited Cardiff.
    • On Friday, Leo visited Cardiff.
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    On Friday, Leo visited Cardiff.. Days of the week, names and places are proper nouns, so Friday, Leo and Cardiff all need capitals. Revise Grammar, Punctuation and Writing →

Plants, Animals and Habitats

  1. Which part of a plant takes in water from the soil? (Foundation recall)
    • The flowers
    • The fruit
    • The petals
    • The roots
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The roots. Roots grow into the soil and take in water and nutrients for the plant. Revise Plants, Animals and Habitats →

  2. What is the main job of a plant's leaves? (Exam style)
    • Attracting birds to nest
    • Holding the plant in the soil
    • Making food using sunlight
    • Making seeds for new plants
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Making food using sunlight. Leaves catch sunlight and use it, with air and water, to make food for the plant. Revise Plants, Animals and Habitats →

  3. Celery left in coloured water turns colour at the top. What does this show? (Stretch)
    • Leaves make water from sunlight
    • Plants drink through their leaves
    • Roots turn water into food
    • The stem carries water upwards
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The stem carries water upwards. The colour travels with the water, showing the stem carries water up through the plant. Revise Plants, Animals and Habitats →

Materials, Forces and Magnets

  1. Which of these is a material? (Foundation recall)
    • Spoon
    • Table
    • Window
    • Wood
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Wood. Wood is what things are made from, while a table, spoon and window are objects. Revise Materials, Forces and Magnets →

  2. Which material would be best for a pair of wellies? (Exam style)
    • Cotton wool
    • Glass
    • Paper
    • Rubber
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Rubber. Rubber is waterproof and bendy, so it keeps feet dry and can be worn comfortably. Revise Materials, Forces and Magnets →

  3. Why would a chocolate teapot be a bad idea? (Stretch)
    • Chocolate is too heavy to lift
    • Chocolate is transparent
    • Hot tea would melt it
    • Teapots must be magnetic
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Hot tea would melt it. A teapot must hold hot liquid without changing shape, and chocolate melts when heated. Revise Materials, Forces and Magnets →

Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space

  1. Which of these is a light source? (Foundation recall)
    • A candle flame
    • A mirror
    • A shiny coin
    • The Moon
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A candle flame. A candle flame gives out its own light, while the others only reflect light. Revise Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space →

  2. Why do cyclists wear reflective strips at night? (Exam style)
    • They give out their own light
    • They keep the cyclist warm
    • They make the bike go faster
    • They reflect car headlights so drivers see them
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They reflect car headlights so drivers see them. Reflective strips bounce light from headlights back towards drivers, making cyclists easier to spot. Revise Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space →

  3. Why is it unsafe to look at the Sun through binoculars? (Stretch)
    • They block all the light
    • They focus strong sunlight into your eyes
    • They make the Sun look smaller
    • They turn the light into heat outside
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They focus strong sunlight into your eyes. Binoculars gather and focus even more sunlight into the eye, which can cause permanent damage. Revise Light, Sound, Electricity, Earth and Space →

History

  1. Which is a change within living memory? (Foundation recall)
    • Smartphones replacing house telephones
    • The building of Stonehenge
    • The pyramids being built
    • The Romans arriving in Britain
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Smartphones replacing house telephones. People alive today remember using telephones before smartphones. Revise History →

  2. What is the best question to ask a grandparent about the past? (Exam style)
    • What did the Romans eat?
    • What games did you play when you were my age?
    • What is your favourite colour today?
    • What will happen in the future?
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    What games did you play when you were my age?. Questions about their own childhood use their memories. Revise History →

  3. Why are photographs useful for learning about living memory? (Stretch)
    • They always show everything that happened
    • They are drawings made by children today
    • They show what people, places and objects looked like at the time
    • They tell us exactly what people were thinking
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They show what people, places and objects looked like at the time. Photos are evidence of how people, places and things looked in the recent past. Revise History →

Geography

  1. What is the capital of Scotland? (Foundation recall)
    • Belfast
    • Cardiff
    • Edinburgh
    • Glasgow
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Edinburgh. Edinburgh is the capital, although Glasgow is Scotland's largest city. Revise Geography →

  2. Which sea lies between Great Britain and Ireland? (Exam style)
    • The English Channel
    • The Irish Sea
    • The Mediterranean Sea
    • The North Sea
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The Irish Sea. The Irish Sea separates the two islands to the west of Great Britain. Revise Geography →

  3. Which statement about Great Britain is correct? (Stretch)
    • It includes the whole island of Ireland
    • It is a city in England
    • It is another name for Northern Ireland only
    • It is the island containing England, Scotland and Wales
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It is the island containing England, Scotland and Wales. The UK includes Northern Ireland, which is not on the island of Great Britain. Revise Geography →
