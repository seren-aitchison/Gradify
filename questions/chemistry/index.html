Foundations of Chemistry

  1. Which branch of chemistry mainly studies carbon compounds? (Foundation recall)
    • Analytical chemistry
    • Inorganic chemistry
    • Organic chemistry
    • Physical chemistry
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Organic chemistry. Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds, including most molecules in living things and medicines. Revise Foundations of Chemistry →

  2. A chemist measures how quickly a medicine breaks down at different temperatures. Which branch is this mostly? (Exam style)
    • Biochemistry (drug metabolism)
    • Inorganic chemistry (salts)
    • Organic chemistry (synthesis)
    • Physical chemistry (kinetics)
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Physical chemistry (kinetics). Rates of reaction and their temperature dependence belong to physical chemistry. Revise Foundations of Chemistry →

  3. Which statement links all three levels of description correctly? (Stretch)
    • Limewater turns cloudy (macroscopic) because Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions form insoluble calcium carbonate (particulate), Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (symbolic)
    • Limewater turns cloudy (macroscopic) because the carbon dioxide gas condenses into tiny droplets (particulate), CO₂(g) → CO₂(l) (symbolic)
    • Limewater turns cloudy (particulate) because CO₂ molecules dissolve in the water (macroscopic), shown by the formula CaCO₃ alone (symbolic)
    • Limewater turns cloudy (symbolic) because calcium carbonate is a white solid (macroscopic), and Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O shows the ions (particulate)
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Limewater turns cloudy (macroscopic) because Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻ ions form insoluble calcium carbonate (particulate), Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (symbolic). Only this answer connects what is observed to the particles and to the equation. Revise Foundations of Chemistry →

Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry

  1. What did the large-angle deflection of a few alpha particles show? (Foundation recall)
    • Atoms are solid spheres
    • Electrons are in fixed orbits
    • Neutrons exist
    • Positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Only a small, dense, positive centre could turn back a fast alpha particle; most of the atom must be empty. Revise Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry →

  2. Why do heavy stable nuclei such as lead-208 have more neutrons (126) than protons (82)? (Exam style)
    • Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrical repulsion
    • Neutrons are needed to attract electrons
    • Neutrons repel protons
    • Protons are heavier than neutrons
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Extra neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electrical repulsion. As nuclei get bigger, proton–proton repulsion grows; additional neutrons provide binding without charge. Revise Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry →

  3. If Thomson's plum pudding model had been correct, what would Geiger and Marsden have seen? (Stretch)
    • Almost all alpha particles passing through with only small deflections
    • Many particles bouncing straight back
    • No particles passing through
    • Particles absorbed by electrons
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Almost all alpha particles passing through with only small deflections. Spread-out positive charge produces only weak forces, so no large deflections; the backscattering was the surprise. Revise Atomic Structure and Quantum Chemistry →

Periodic Table and Periodicity

  1. Who published a periodic table with gaps for undiscovered elements in 1869? (Foundation recall)
    • Dmitri Mendeleev
    • Henry Moseley
    • Johann Döbereiner
    • John Newlands
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Dmitri Mendeleev. Mendeleev's gaps let him predict new elements. Revise Periodic Table and Periodicity →

  2. Why did Mendeleev place tellurium before iodine despite its greater mass? (Exam style)
    • He made an error in the masses
    • Iodine is a metal
    • Tellurium was discovered later
    • Their properties matched the groups better in that order
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Their properties matched the groups better in that order. Grouping by properties was more important than strict mass order. Revise Periodic Table and Periodicity →

  3. Why did the discovery of germanium support Mendeleev's table? (Stretch)
    • It disproved all his predictions
    • It had no properties in common with silicon
    • It was already known in 1869
    • Its properties closely matched his predictions for eka-silicon
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Its properties closely matched his predictions for eka-silicon. Successful predictions are strong evidence for a theory. Revise Periodic Table and Periodicity →

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

  1. Which type of bond forms between a metal and a non-metal? (Foundation recall)
    • Covalent bonding
    • Hydrogen bonding
    • Ionic bonding
    • Metallic bonding
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ionic bonding. Electrons transfer from the metal to the non-metal, forming ions. Revise Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure →

  2. Which molecule breaks the octet rule? (Exam style)
    • Ammonia, NH₃
    • Boron trifluoride, BF₃
    • Methane, CH₄
    • Water, H₂O
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Boron trifluoride, BF₃. Boron in BF₃ has only six outer electrons, an exception to the rule. Revise Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure →

  3. Why does making bonds release energy? (Stretch)
    • Atoms lose mass when bonding
    • Attraction between charges lowers the potential energy of the system
    • Bonds store extra energy when they are formed
    • Electrons gain kinetic energy
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Attraction between charges lowers the potential energy of the system. Moving into an attractive arrangement lowers energy, which is released. Revise Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure →

States of Matter and Physical Chemistry

  1. What is the SI unit of pressure? (Foundation recall)
    • The atmosphere
    • The bar
    • The millimetre of mercury
    • The pascal
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The pascal. One pascal is one newton per square metre of area. Revise States of Matter and Physical Chemistry →

  2. Why are gases easily compressed? (Exam style)
    • Their particles are far apart with much empty space
    • Their particles are much larger than in liquids
    • Their particles are tightly packed together
    • Their particles cannot move at all
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Their particles are far apart with much empty space. Large gaps between particles let the volume shrink. Revise States of Matter and Physical Chemistry →

  3. Why must temperature be in kelvin for gas calculations? (Stretch)
    • Celsius cannot measure gases
    • Gas laws are proportional relationships starting from absolute zero
    • Kelvin is the only unit for liquids
    • Kelvin values are always smaller than Celsius
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Gas laws are proportional relationships starting from absolute zero. Proportionality only works on an absolute scale without negatives. Revise States of Matter and Physical Chemistry →

Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics

  1. Which type of system exchanges energy but not matter? (Foundation recall)
    • A closed system
    • A random system
    • An isolated system
    • An open system
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A closed system. Closed systems allow heat transfer but keep matter inside. Revise Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics →

  2. Where does the energy released by a burning fuel go? (Exam style)
    • Back into the fuel molecules
    • Into creating new atoms
    • Into the surroundings as heat and light
    • It is destroyed during burning
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Into the surroundings as heat and light. Energy is conserved and transferred to the surroundings. Revise Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics →

  3. How can energy changes in a reaction be detected? (Stretch)
    • By counting atoms
    • By measuring colour only
    • By measuring temperature changes in the surroundings
    • By weighing the products
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    By measuring temperature changes in the surroundings. Heat transferred changes the surroundings' temperature. Revise Chemical Energetics and Thermodynamics →

Chemical Kinetics

  1. Which method suits a reaction that produces a gas? (Foundation recall)
    • Collecting the gas in a gas syringe
    • Measuring the pH once
    • Timing a colour change only
    • Weighing the reactants before starting
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Collecting the gas in a gas syringe. Gas volume collected over time shows the rate. Revise Chemical Kinetics →

  2. Why does the rate of most reactions decrease over time? (Exam style)
    • Catalysts are consumed
    • Products speed up the reaction
    • Reactants are used up, so collisions become less frequent
    • Temperature always rises
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Reactants are used up, so collisions become less frequent. Lower concentration means fewer collisions per second. Revise Chemical Kinetics →

  3. Why is a colorimeter useful for measuring rate? (Stretch)
    • It measures concentration changes continuously without disturbing the reaction
    • It measures the gas produced
    • It speeds up the reaction
    • It weighs the products
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It measures concentration changes continuously without disturbing the reaction. Absorbance tracks coloured species as they change. Revise Chemical Kinetics →

Chemical Equilibrium

  1. Which colour is anhydrous copper(II) sulfate? (Foundation recall)
    • Black
    • Blue
    • Green
    • White
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    White. Removing water of crystallisation leaves a white powder. Revise Chemical Equilibrium →

  2. If a forward reaction has ΔH = −50 kJ mol⁻¹, what is ΔH for the reverse? (Exam style)
    • +50 kJ mol⁻¹
    • −100 kJ mol⁻¹
    • −50 kJ mol⁻¹
    • 0 kJ mol⁻¹
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    +50 kJ mol⁻¹. Reversing a reaction reverses the sign of its enthalpy change. Revise Chemical Equilibrium →

  3. Why don't reversible reactions go to completion in a closed system? (Stretch)
    • Energy runs out
    • Products keep reacting to re-form reactants
    • Reactants are destroyed
    • The reaction always stops halfway
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Products keep reacting to re-form reactants. Forward and reverse reactions both continue. Revise Chemical Equilibrium →

Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry

  1. What forms when an acid reacts with a metal? (Foundation recall)
    • A salt and carbon dioxide
    • A salt and hydrogen
    • A salt and water only
    • Only water
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A salt and hydrogen. Reactive metals displace hydrogen from acids to form a salt. Revise Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry →

  2. Which substance is a base but not an alkali? (Exam style)
    • Aqueous ammonia
    • Copper(II) oxide
    • Potassium hydroxide
    • Sodium hydroxide
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Copper(II) oxide. Copper(II) oxide neutralises acids but does not dissolve in water. Revise Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry →

  3. Why does limewater turn cloudy with the gas from acid and carbonate? (Stretch)
    • Carbon dioxide forms insoluble calcium carbonate with limewater
    • Hydrogen reacts with limewater
    • Oxygen precipitates calcium
    • Water vapour clouds the limewater
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Carbon dioxide forms insoluble calcium carbonate with limewater. CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ gives a white precipitate of CaCO₃. Revise Acids, Bases and Aqueous Chemistry →

Redox and Electrochemistry

  1. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, which species is oxidised? (Foundation recall)
    • Copper metal
    • Copper(II) ions
    • Zinc
    • Zinc ions
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Zinc. Zinc loses two electrons to become Zn²⁺, so zinc is oxidised. Revise Redox and Electrochemistry →

  2. Why is sodium burning in chlorine a redox reaction? (Exam style)
    • Chlorine loses electrons to sodium
    • No electrons are transferred
    • Oxygen is added to sodium
    • Sodium loses electrons and chlorine gains them
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Sodium loses electrons and chlorine gains them. Electron transfer defines redox even without oxygen. Revise Redox and Electrochemistry →

  3. Why is the electron-transfer definition preferred? (Stretch)
    • It covers redox reactions that involve no oxygen
    • It ignores electrons
    • It is the oldest definition
    • It only applies to combustion reactions
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It covers redox reactions that involve no oxygen. Many redox reactions involve electrons but not oxygen. Revise Redox and Electrochemistry →

Inorganic Chemistry

  1. Which is an area of inorganic chemistry? (Foundation recall)
    • Chemistry of alkanes only
    • Coordination chemistry of transition metals
    • Plant photosynthesis biology
    • Polymer biochemistry of DNA
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Coordination chemistry of transition metals. Coordination chemistry studies metal complexes. Revise Inorganic Chemistry →

  2. Why is inorganic chemistry important in biology? (Exam style)
    • Living things contain no metals in their cells
    • Metal ions are always toxic to every organism
    • Metal ions are essential in proteins such as haemoglobin
    • Only carbon compounds matter in living biology
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Metal ions are essential in proteins such as haemoglobin. Iron, zinc and copper act in enzymes and carriers. Revise Inorganic Chemistry →

  3. Why does iron appear in many areas of inorganic chemistry? (Stretch)
    • It is a noble gas
    • It is the only metal in the periodic table
    • It never forms compounds
    • Its variable oxidation states and metallic properties suit many roles
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Its variable oxidation states and metallic properties suit many roles. Iron's chemistry spans alloys, catalysis, biology and corrosion. Revise Inorganic Chemistry →

Organic Chemistry Foundations

  1. Which of these is an organic compound? (Foundation recall)
    • Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃
    • Ethanol, C₂H₅OH
    • Sodium chloride, NaCl
    • Water, H₂O
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ethanol, C₂H₅OH. Ethanol is built on a chain of carbon atoms bonded to hydrogen. Revise Organic Chemistry Foundations →

  2. Why was Wöhler's urea synthesis important? (Exam style)
    • It proved that urea is a metal compound
    • It showed organic compounds can be made without living things
    • It showed that carbon cannot form chains
    • It was the first synthesis of water
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It showed organic compounds can be made without living things. Making urea from an inorganic salt challenged the idea of a vital force. Revise Organic Chemistry Foundations →

  3. Why is CO₂ usually treated as inorganic although it contains carbon? (Stretch)
    • It contains no carbon atoms
    • It is a metal oxide with ionic bonding
    • It is a simple compound with no C–H bonds and behaves like inorganic oxides
    • It is only found in living things
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It is a simple compound with no C–H bonds and behaves like inorganic oxides. Convention groups simple carbon oxides and carbonates with inorganic chemistry. Revise Organic Chemistry Foundations →

Organic Reaction Mechanisms

  1. What does a fishhook arrow show? (Foundation recall)
    • The direction of heat flow
    • The movement of a single electron
    • The movement of an electron pair
    • The transfer of a proton only
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The movement of a single electron. Half-headed arrows are used in radical mechanisms. Revise Organic Reaction Mechanisms →

  2. Why must an arrow never start at a positive charge? (Exam style)
    • A positive charge has no electrons to donate
    • Arrows only start at nuclei
    • Positive charges always carry lone pairs
    • Positive charges move in the opposite direction
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A positive charge has no electrons to donate. Arrows track electrons, so they must begin where electrons are. Revise Organic Reaction Mechanisms →

  3. In hydroxide attacking chloromethane, why are two arrows needed? (Stretch)
    • A single arrow can never show a reaction
    • Hydroxide needs two arrows to lose its charge
    • One arrow is needed for each hydrogen
    • One forms the C–O bond and one breaks the C–Cl bond so carbon keeps four bonds
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    One forms the C–O bond and one breaks the C–Cl bond so carbon keeps four bonds. Without the second arrow carbon would have five bonds. Revise Organic Reaction Mechanisms →

Functional Group Chemistry

  1. Which alcohol is tertiary? (Foundation recall)
    • 2-methylpropan-2-ol
    • Butan-2-ol
    • Ethanol
    • Propan-1-ol
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    2-methylpropan-2-ol. The carbinol carbon carries three alkyl groups. Revise Functional Group Chemistry →

  2. Why do alcohols boil higher than alkanes of similar mass? (Exam style)
    • Alcohols are ionic compounds
    • Alcohols contain more electrons than alkanes
    • Alkanes form hydrogen bonds but alcohols do not
    • Their –OH groups form hydrogen bonds between molecules
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Their –OH groups form hydrogen bonds between molecules. Hydrogen bonds are stronger than London forces alone. Revise Functional Group Chemistry →

  3. Why is methanol poisonous although ethanol is less so? (Stretch)
    • Ethanol is converted into methanol in the body
    • Methanol cannot be metabolised by the body
    • Methanol is a strong base that burns tissue
    • The body oxidises methanol to toxic methanal and methanoic acid
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The body oxidises methanol to toxic methanal and methanoic acid. Toxic metabolites damage the optic nerve and other tissues. Revise Functional Group Chemistry →

Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy

  1. What is the matrix in an analysis? (Foundation recall)
    • Everything in the sample except the analyte
    • The final reported result
    • The instrument used for measurement
    • The pure analyte standard
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Everything in the sample except the analyte. Matrix components can interfere with measurement. Revise Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy →

  2. Why is representative sampling so important? (Exam style)
    • An unrepresentative sample gives misleading results however precise the instrument
    • Modern instruments correct for all sampling errors
    • Only a single small sample is ever needed for analysis
    • Sampling has no effect at all on final results
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An unrepresentative sample gives misleading results however precise the instrument. The sample must reflect the composition of the whole. Revise Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy →

  3. Why must results be reported with uncertainty? (Stretch)
    • It hides errors in the method
    • Results without units are clearer
    • Uncertainty makes results look more precise
    • Users need to know how reliable the value is when comparing with limits
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Users need to know how reliable the value is when comparing with limits. A value near a legal limit needs its uncertainty to be judged. Revise Analytical Chemistry and Spectroscopy →

Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science

  1. Which symmetry element rules out a permanent dipole moment? (Foundation recall)
    • A single mirror plane
    • A twofold rotation axis alone
    • An inversion centre
    • No symmetry at all
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    An inversion centre. Opposite equivalent atoms cancel any dipole. Revise Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science →

  2. Why are measured bond lengths averages? (Exam style)
    • All bonds in molecules have exactly the same length
    • Bonds change length only when they are broken
    • Instruments cannot measure bond lengths at all
    • Molecules constantly vibrate about their equilibrium positions
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Molecules constantly vibrate about their equilibrium positions. Vibrational motion blurs the exact positions. Revise Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science →

  3. Why is symmetry useful in spectroscopy? (Stretch)
    • It changes the overall mass of the molecule
    • It determines which vibrations and transitions are allowed
    • It has no effect at all on observed spectra
    • It removes all vibrations from the molecule
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It determines which vibrations and transitions are allowed. Selection rules follow from symmetry, so it decides which bands appear. Revise Physical Chemistry and Molecular Science →

Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry

  1. Which class of material is hard, brittle and heat resistant? (Foundation recall)
    • Ceramics, with ionic or covalent networks
    • Liquids, with no fixed shape
    • Metals, with delocalised electrons
    • Polymers, with long flexible chains
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ceramics, with ionic or covalent networks. Strong directional bonds resist deformation and heat. Revise Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry →

  2. Why are aircraft parts often made from composites? (Exam style)
    • They are always cheaper than metals
    • They combine high strength with low weight
    • They conduct electricity better than copper
    • They melt at very low temperatures
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They combine high strength with low weight. Combining materials unites useful properties. Revise Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry →

  3. Why does grain size affect a metal's strength? (Stretch)
    • Grain boundaries block dislocation movement, so finer grains are stronger
    • Grain size has no effect on any property
    • Grains only affect the colour of the metal
    • Larger grains always make metals much stronger
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Grain boundaries block dislocation movement, so finer grains are stronger. More boundaries hinder slip between planes. Revise Materials, Environmental and Industrial Chemistry →

    • A safety data sheet from the supplier
