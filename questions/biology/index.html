Foundations of Biology

  1. Which statement is part of cell theory? (Foundation recall)
    • All cells contain a nucleus
    • Cells can arise from non-living matter today
    • Cells only arise from existing cells
    • Only animals are made of cells
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Cells only arise from existing cells. Cell theory holds that all organisms are made of cells and that new cells come only from the division of existing cells; prokaryotes have no nucleus. Revise Foundations of Biology →

  2. A biologist studies how the population of a bird species changes over 30 years as its habitat warms. Which level of biology is this mainly? (Exam style)
    • Anatomy
    • Cell biology
    • Molecular biology
    • Population ecology and evolution
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Population ecology and evolution. Changes in the size and inherited traits of a population over generations are studied in ecology and evolutionary biology. Revise Foundations of Biology →

  3. Which example best shows how biology's levels connect? (Stretch)
    • A bird migrates south in winter
    • A cell divides by mitosis
    • A gene mutation changes an enzyme, altering a cell's metabolism and the organism's survival, and the allele's frequency changes over generations
    • A leaf photosynthesises in sunlight
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A gene mutation changes an enzyme, altering a cell's metabolism and the organism's survival, and the allele's frequency changes over generations. Only the first traces one cause from the molecular level through cells and organisms to a change in a population. Revise Foundations of Biology →

Cell Biology

  1. Who stated that all cells arise from pre-existing cells? (Foundation recall)
    • Charles Darwin
    • Gregor Mendel
    • Robert Hooke
    • Rudolf Virchow
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Rudolf Virchow. Virchow popularised "omnis cellula e cellula" in 1855, building on Remak's work; Hooke named cells, Mendel studied inheritance and Darwin evolution. Revise Cell Biology →

  2. A student says a light microscope at ×2000 should show ribosomes clearly. Why is this wrong? (Exam style)
    • Magnification above ×1000 is impossible
    • Ribosomes are about 25 nm across, far below the light microscope's resolution of about 0.2 µm
    • Ribosomes are only found in bacteria
    • Ribosomes are transparent to light
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Ribosomes are about 25 nm across, far below the light microscope's resolution of about 0.2 µm. Resolution, not magnification, decides what can be seen; ribosomes need an electron microscope. Revise Cell Biology →

  3. Which observation would most directly challenge the third principle of cell theory? (Stretch)
    • A cell with no nucleus
    • A new cell forming from non-living chemicals in a sealed sterile flask
    • A very large single-celled organism
    • Cells dividing faster in warm conditions
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A new cell forming from non-living chemicals in a sealed sterile flask. Only the formation of a cell without a parent cell contradicts "all cells arise from pre-existing cells"; the others are consistent with cell theory. Revise Cell Biology →

Biochemistry and Biomolecules

  1. Which element can form four covalent bonds and so build the skeletons of biological molecules? (Foundation recall)
    • Carbon
    • Chlorine
    • Helium
    • Sodium
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Carbon. Carbon's four bonds allow chains, rings and branched structures. Revise Biochemistry and Biomolecules →

  2. What happens in a hydrolysis reaction? (Exam style)
    • A bond is broken by adding a water molecule
    • A molecule is heated until it melts
    • An electron is removed from an atom
    • Two molecules join and release water
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A bond is broken by adding a water molecule. Hydrolysis uses water to split bonds, often catalysed by enzymes. Revise Biochemistry and Biomolecules →

  3. Why are weak interactions such as hydrogen bonds important in large biological molecules? (Stretch)
    • Many of them together stabilise shape while still allowing changes needed for function
    • They are stronger than covalent bonds
    • They hold atoms together within each monomer
    • They prevent any change in molecular shape
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Many of them together stabilise shape while still allowing changes needed for function. Collectively stable but individually breakable bonds allow both stability and flexibility. Revise Biochemistry and Biomolecules →

Genetics and Molecular Biology

  1. What does the central dogma describe? (Foundation recall)
    • The division of cells by mitosis
    • The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein
    • The movement of proteins into the nucleus
    • The structure of the cell membrane
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein. DNA is transcribed into RNA, which is translated into protein. Revise Genetics and Molecular Biology →

  2. Which experiment showed that DNA is the genetic material? (Exam style)
    • Avery, MacLeod and McCarty's transformation experiments
    • Darwin's observations of finches in the Galápagos
    • Mendel's crosses of tall and short pea plants
    • The sequencing work of the Human Genome Project
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Avery, MacLeod and McCarty's transformation experiments. They showed DNA could transform bacteria, carrying genetic information. Revise Genetics and Molecular Biology →

  3. Why can two people with the same gene variant show different characteristics? (Stretch)
    • DNA changes daily
    • Environment also affects how genes are expressed
    • Genes never affect characteristics
    • Proteins are not made from genes
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Environment also affects how genes are expressed. Many traits depend on gene-environment interaction. Revise Genetics and Molecular Biology →

Cell Division and Development

  1. Which phase includes mitosis and cytokinesis? (Foundation recall)
    • G₁ phase
    • G₂ phase
    • M phase
    • S phase
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    M phase. M phase is when the nucleus and cytoplasm divide. Revise Cell Division and Development →

  2. What happens to DNA content during S phase? (Exam style)
    • It doubles from 2C to 4C
    • It halves from 2C to 1C
    • It is destroyed and rebuilt
    • It stays exactly the same
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It doubles from 2C to 4C. DNA replication produces two sister chromatids per chromosome. Revise Cell Division and Development →

  3. Why is interphase described as an active phase rather than a resting phase? (Stretch)
    • Cells divide repeatedly during it
    • Cells do nothing during interphase
    • Cells grow, make proteins and replicate DNA during it
    • DNA is destroyed during interphase
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Cells grow, make proteins and replicate DNA during it. Interphase involves growth and preparation for division. Revise Cell Division and Development →

Evolution and Natural Selection

  1. When was On the Origin of Species published? (Foundation recall)
    • 1758
    • 1859
    • 1900
    • 1953
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    1859. Darwin published his book in 1859, a year after the joint presentation with Wallace. Revise Evolution and Natural Selection →

  2. Why is Lamarck's mechanism of evolution not supported? (Exam style)
    • Characteristics acquired during life are not generally inherited
    • Fossils show no extinct species
    • Natural selection has never been observed
    • Species never change over time
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Characteristics acquired during life are not generally inherited. Changes from use or disuse are not passed on through DNA. Revise Evolution and Natural Selection →

  3. What did Darwin's theory lack that the modern synthesis supplied? (Stretch)
    • A mechanism of inheritance from genetics
    • Any evidence from fossils
    • The concept of competition
    • The idea of variation
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    A mechanism of inheritance from genetics. Mendelian genetics explained how variation is inherited. Revise Evolution and Natural Selection →

Human Anatomy and Physiology

  1. Which tissue type covers body surfaces and lines cavities? (Foundation recall)
    • Connective tissue
    • Epithelial tissue
    • Muscle tissue
    • Nervous tissue
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Epithelial tissue. Epithelium forms coverings and linings throughout the body. Revise Human Anatomy and Physiology →

  2. Which tissue type includes bone, blood and adipose tissue? (Exam style)
    • Connective tissue
    • Epithelial tissue
    • Nervous tissue
    • Smooth muscle tissue
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Connective tissue. Connective tissues support, bind and transport, and include these forms. Revise Human Anatomy and Physiology →

  3. Why does exercise show that organ systems are interdependent? (Stretch)
    • Exercise affects only the skeleton
    • Muscles, heart, lungs and nerves must all respond together
    • Only the muscles change during exercise
    • Organ systems never interact
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Muscles, heart, lungs and nerves must all respond together. Delivering oxygen to working muscle needs several systems at once. Revise Human Anatomy and Physiology →

Plant Biology

  1. Which plastid stores starch in plant cells? (Foundation recall)
    • Amyloplast
    • Chromoplast
    • Mitochondrion
    • Ribosome
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Amyloplast. Amyloplasts are starch-storing plastids found in roots and tubers. Revise Plant Biology →

  2. Why does a plant wilt when it lacks water? (Exam style)
    • Its cell walls dissolve completely
    • Its cells lose turgor pressure and become flaccid
    • Its chloroplasts stop working
    • Its roots grow too quickly
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Its cells lose turgor pressure and become flaccid. Without water, vacuoles shrink and cells stop pressing on walls. Revise Plant Biology →

  3. What is the symplast pathway? (Stretch)
    • Movement inside xylem vessels only
    • Movement through cell walls only
    • Movement through cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata
    • Movement through the air spaces of leaves
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Movement through cytoplasm connected by plasmodesmata. The symplast links the cytoplasm of many cells into one pathway. Revise Plant Biology →

Microbiology

  1. Which group of microbes is non-cellular? (Foundation recall)
    • Bacteria
    • Fungi
    • Protists
    • Viruses
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Viruses. Viruses are particles that replicate only inside host cells. Revise Microbiology →

  2. What did Koch's postulates establish? (Exam style)
    • Criteria for proving a microbe causes a specific disease
    • That all microbes found in sick people are harmful
    • That bacteria arise spontaneously
    • That viruses can always be grown on nutrient agar
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Criteria for proving a microbe causes a specific disease. Koch linked specific microbes to specific diseases. Revise Microbiology →

  3. Why do Koch's postulates not apply to all pathogens? (Stretch)
    • All pathogens can be grown easily
    • Koch's postulates were disproved entirely
    • Postulates only apply to viruses
    • Some pathogens cannot be grown in pure culture
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Some pathogens cannot be grown in pure culture. Unculturable microbes and carriers break the rules. Revise Microbiology →

Immunology

  1. Where are all immune cells originally made? (Foundation recall)
    • In the bone marrow
    • In the lymph nodes
    • In the spleen tissue
    • In the thyroid gland
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    In the bone marrow. Haematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow give rise to immune cells. Revise Immunology →

  2. Why do lymph nodes swell during infection? (Exam style)
    • Lymphocytes in them multiply to fight it
    • They fill with digested food
    • They make extra red blood cells
    • They stop draining lymph fluid
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Lymphocytes in them multiply to fight it. Swelling reflects an active immune response in the node. Revise Immunology →

  3. What happens when immune tolerance fails? (Stretch)
    • Allergies always disappear
    • Pathogens are cleared faster
    • The immune system attacks the body's own cells
    • The thymus grows larger
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The immune system attacks the body's own cells. Loss of tolerance causes autoimmune disease. Revise Immunology →

Ecology and Environmental Biology

  1. Which is a biotic factor? (Foundation recall)
    • Annual rainfall
    • Light intensity
    • Predation by foxes
    • Soil pH level
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Predation by foxes. Predation involves living organisms, so it is a biotic factor. Revise Ecology and Environmental Biology →

  2. What does an ecosystem include that a community does not? (Exam style)
    • Only one species
    • Only plants
    • Only predators
    • The non-living physical environment
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    The non-living physical environment. Ecosystems combine living and non-living parts. Revise Ecology and Environmental Biology →

  3. Why are long-term ecological studies valuable? (Stretch)
    • They are always cheaper than short studies
    • They need no data collection
    • They only study one organism
    • They reveal slow changes that short studies miss
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    They reveal slow changes that short studies miss. Long records show trends such as climate effects. Revise Ecology and Environmental Biology →

Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering

  1. Which is an example of modern biotechnology? (Foundation recall)
    • Breeding cattle for more milk
    • Brewing beer with wild yeast
    • Making insulin in engineered bacteria
    • Making yoghurt from milk
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Making insulin in engineered bacteria. Inserting a human gene into bacteria is genetic engineering. Revise Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering →

  2. Which area of biotechnology includes GM crops? (Exam style)
    • Blue biotechnology
    • Green biotechnology
    • Red biotechnology
    • White biotechnology
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Green biotechnology. Green biotechnology covers agriculture and plant breeding. Revise Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering →

  3. Why was the Cohen and Boyer experiment a turning point? (Stretch)
    • It discovered yeast fermentation
    • It produced the first vaccine
    • It proved DNA is a double helix
    • It showed genes could be transferred and expressed in bacteria
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    It showed genes could be transferred and expressed in bacteria. Recombinant DNA opened the way to genetic engineering. Revise Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering →

Bioinformatics and Computational Biology

  1. Which fields does bioinformatics combine? (Foundation recall)
    • Biology, computer science and statistics
    • Chemistry, art and music
    • History, law and geography
    • Physics, sport and drama
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Biology, computer science and statistics. Bioinformatics is an interdisciplinary field. Revise Bioinformatics and Computational Biology →

  2. Why is computing essential for genomics? (Exam style)
    • Biologists cannot read
    • Computers sequence DNA without samples
    • Datasets are far too large to analyse by hand
    • Genomes are very short
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Datasets are far too large to analyse by hand. Billions of bases need automated analysis. Revise Bioinformatics and Computational Biology →

  3. Why do bioinformaticians need biological knowledge? (Stretch)
    • Because computers cannot run code
    • Because data are always correct
    • To ask meaningful questions and interpret results
    • To avoid using statistics
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    To ask meaningful questions and interpret results. Analysis is only useful when interpreted biologically. Revise Bioinformatics and Computational Biology →

Experimental Biology and Research Methods

  1. In the seedling experiment, what is the dependent variable? (Foundation recall)
    • Amount of light provided
    • Fertiliser concentration
    • Seedling height
    • Tray position in the room
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Seedling height. Height is what is measured in response to the treatment. Revise Experimental Biology and Research Methods →

  2. Why are seedlings randomly assigned to trays? (Exam style)
    • To increase the number of variables
    • To make the experiment finish faster
    • To prevent hidden differences biasing one treatment
    • To remove the need for controls
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    To prevent hidden differences biasing one treatment. Randomisation spreads unknown factors evenly between groups. Revise Experimental Biology and Research Methods →

  3. Why can't statistics rescue a poorly designed experiment? (Stretch)
    • Bias and confounding built into the data cannot be removed later
    • Poor designs have no data
    • Statistics can always fix any kind of data
    • Statistics only work on large samples
    Reveal the answer and mark-scheme reasoning

    Bias and confounding built into the data cannot be removed later. Design flaws distort the data themselves. Revise Experimental Biology and Research Methods →
