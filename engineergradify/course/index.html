Engineering: a free course on EngineerGradify

EngineerGradify's free Engineering course runs from Engineering Mathematics to Projects and Professional Practice: 191 lessons in 12 schools, each with notes, flashcards and check questions at three levels (Foundation, Application, Evaluation). It's written for engineering students.

Education, not engineering advice or design approval. EngineerGradify teaches engineering for study. The lessons were drafted with AI assistance and haven't yet been reviewed by a chartered engineer, so they may contain errors; check important points against your course materials, textbooks and the relevant standards. Worked examples use simplified, illustrative numbers: never use them to size, certify or build anything real. Real designs must follow current codes and standards and be checked by a competent, appropriately qualified engineer, and work must follow health and safety law and the HSE's guidance. This course is not an accredited degree and does not lead to professional registration.

Schools and lessons

School 1: Engineering Mathematics

19 lessons

Engineering Mathematics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. SI units and dimensional analysis: Every engineering number carries a unit, and every valid equation balances its dimensions.
  2. Uncertainty, significant figures and estimation: No measurement is exact, and an engineering result is only useful with an honest statement of how uncertain it is.
  3. Engineering models and assumptions: Engineers predict how things will behave by building models: simplified representations that can be calculated.
  4. Functions and graphs in engineering: Engineers describe how one quantity depends on another with functions and read behaviour off graphs.
  5. Exponentials and logarithms in engineering: Whenever a rate of change is proportional to the amount present, the result is exponential growth or decay.
  6. Trigonometry and sinusoids: Trigonometry resolves forces into components and describes everything that oscillates: alternating current, vibration and rotating machinery.
  7. Complex numbers and phasors: Complex numbers make AC circuits, vibrations and control systems solvable with algebra instead of trigonometry.
  8. Vectors and their products: Forces, velocities, moments and fields all have direction as well as size.
  9. Matrices and simultaneous equations: Engineering analysis often produces many linear equations at once: forces at the joints of a truss, currents in a circuit, displacements in a finite element model.
  10. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors: An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches, and its eigenvalue is the stretch factor.
  11. Differentiation and rates of change: The derivative measures how fast one quantity changes with another.
  12. Partial differentiation and error propagation: Most engineering quantities depend on several variables.
  13. Integration in engineering: Integration adds up a quantity that varies continuously: work from a varying force, displacement from velocity, charge from current, and the centroids and second moments that describe a cross-section.
  14. Taylor series and linearisation: Near any point, a smooth function looks like a polynomial.
  15. First-order differential equations: A first-order differential equation relates a quantity to its own rate of change.
  16. Second-order differential equations: Systems with two energy stores, such as a mass and a spring or an inductor and a capacitor, obey second-order differential equations.
  17. Numerical methods: Most real engineering equations have no neat closed-form answer, so they are solved numerically.
  18. Engineering statistics: Materials, measurements and manufactured parts all vary.
  19. Probability and reliability: Reliability engineering asks how likely a component or system is to keep working.

School 2: Statics

16 lessons

Statics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Forces as vectors: A force has size and direction, so it is a vector.
  2. Moments and couples: A moment is the turning effect of a force about a point or axis.
  3. Equivalent force systems: Any system of forces and couples on a rigid body can be replaced by a simpler equivalent system.
  4. Free-body diagrams: A free-body diagram isolates a body and shows every external force and moment acting on it.
  5. Equilibrium of a particle: A particle is in equilibrium when the resultant force on it is zero.
  6. Equilibrium of a rigid body in two dimensions: A rigid body is in equilibrium when both the resultant force and the resultant moment are zero.
  7. Equilibrium in three dimensions: In three dimensions a rigid body has six equilibrium equations: three for forces and three for moments.
  8. Statical determinacy and stability: A structure is statically determinate if equilibrium alone can find all its reactions and internal forces.
  9. Plane trusses: the method of joints: A truss is a framework of straight members joined at their ends.
  10. Plane trusses: the method of sections: The method of sections finds the forces in particular truss members directly, by cutting the truss and applying equilibrium to one part.
  11. Frames and machines: Frames and machines are structures with at least one multi-force member, so their members carry bending as well as axial force.
  12. Distributed loads: Many loads are spread along a beam rather than acting at a point: self-weight, floor loads, snow, water pressure.
  13. Centroids and centres of gravity: The centre of gravity is where a body's weight acts; the centroid is the geometric centre of a shape.
  14. Second moment of area: The second moment of area measures how a cross-section's area is distributed about an axis, and so how well it resists bending.
  15. Dry friction: Dry friction resists sliding between surfaces in contact.
  16. Shear force and bending moment diagrams: Shear force and bending moment diagrams show how internal forces vary along a beam, and so where it is most highly stressed.

School 3: Dynamics

18 lessons

Dynamics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Rectilinear motion: Rectilinear motion is motion along a straight line.
  2. Curvilinear motion: rectangular components: When a particle moves along a curved path, its position, velocity and acceleration are vectors.
  3. Normal and tangential components: Normal and tangential components describe motion along a curve relative to the path itself.
  4. Projectile motion: A projectile moves under gravity alone after launch, giving constant downward acceleration and constant horizontal velocity.
  5. Relative motion: Motion depends on the frame of reference.
  6. Newton's second law for particles: Newton's second law links force and acceleration and is the basis of kinetics.
  7. Work and energy: The work–energy principle relates the work done by forces to the change in kinetic energy, avoiding the need to find accelerations.
  8. Power and efficiency: Power is the rate of doing work, and efficiency compares useful output with input.
  9. Impulse and momentum: Impulse and momentum methods relate forces acting over time to changes in velocity.
  10. Impacts: An impact is a short, violent collision between two bodies.
  11. Rotation about a fixed axis: Many machine parts rotate about a fixed axis.
  12. General plane motion and the instantaneous centre: General plane motion combines translation and rotation, as in rolling wheels and connecting rods.
  13. Mass moment of inertia: The mass moment of inertia measures a body's resistance to angular acceleration about an axis.
  14. Kinetics of rigid bodies in plane motion: Rigid bodies in plane motion obey Newton's second law for translation and a moment equation for rotation.
  15. Energy methods for rigid bodies: The work–energy principle extends to rigid bodies by including rotational kinetic energy.
  16. Free undamped vibration: A mass on a spring vibrates at its natural frequency when disturbed.
  17. Damped vibration: Real vibrations die away because of damping.
  18. Forced vibration and resonance: When a periodic force drives a system, it vibrates at the driving frequency, and the response can be very large near the natural frequency.

School 4: Mechanics of Materials

15 lessons

Mechanics of Materials: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Normal stress and strain: Stress is internal force per unit area, and strain is deformation per unit length.
  2. Shear stress and strain: Shear stress acts parallel to a surface.
  3. Hooke's law and elastic constants: In the elastic range, stress is proportional to strain.
  4. Axially loaded members: Bars, rods and columns under axial load stretch or shorten.
  5. Thermal stress: Temperature changes make materials expand or contract.
  6. Torsion of circular shafts: Shafts transmit power by twisting.
  7. Bending stress in beams: Bending creates tension on one side of a beam and compression on the other.
  8. Shear stress in beams: Beams carry shear force as well as bending moment.
  9. Beam deflection: Beams must be stiff enough not to deflect too much in service.
  10. Combined loading: Many members carry more than one type of load at once.
  11. Stress transformation and Mohr's circle: Stresses at a point depend on the orientation of the plane considered.
  12. Failure theories: Failure theories predict when a material will yield or fracture under combined stresses.
  13. Buckling of columns: Slender columns can fail by buckling sideways at stresses well below their crushing strength.
  14. Thin-walled pressure vessels: Pressure vessels such as tanks, pipes and boilers hold fluids under pressure.
  15. Strain energy: When a material deforms elastically, it stores energy.

School 5: Materials Science

17 lessons

Materials Science: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Atomic bonding in materials: The way atoms bond largely determines a material's properties.
  2. Crystal structures: Most metals and many ceramics are crystalline, with atoms in regular repeating patterns.
  3. Defects and dislocations: Real crystals contain defects that control their strength and ductility.
  4. Phase diagrams: Phase diagrams map which phases exist in an alloy at each temperature and composition.
  5. The iron–carbon system: Steel is the most important engineering alloy, and its properties depend on the iron–carbon phase diagram.
  6. The tensile test: The tensile test is the most widely used mechanical test of materials.
  7. Hardness and impact testing: Hardness and impact tests are quick, practical measures of a material's resistance to indentation and to sudden loading.
  8. Fracture and fracture toughness: Cracks concentrate stress, and fracture mechanics predicts when they will grow.
  9. Fatigue: Most mechanical failures in service are caused by fatigue: cracks that grow under repeated loading at stresses below yield.
  10. Creep: At high temperatures, materials deform slowly under constant load.
  11. Corrosion and degradation: Corrosion and degradation cost economies trillions each year.
  12. Metals and alloys: Metals are the backbone of engineering because they combine strength, toughness and formability.
  13. Polymers: Polymers are long-chain molecules with low density and versatile properties.
  14. Ceramics and glasses: Ceramics and glasses are hard, heat-resistant and stiff, but brittle.
  15. Composites: Composites combine two or more materials to achieve properties neither has alone.
  16. Heat treatment of steels: Heat treatment transforms the properties of steel without changing its composition.
  17. Materials selection and Ashby charts: Choosing the right material means matching properties to the demands of a design.

School 6: Thermodynamics

16 lessons

Thermodynamics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Systems, properties and states: Thermodynamics begins by drawing a boundary around the thing being studied.
  2. Energy, work and heat: Energy crosses a system boundary in only two ways: as work or as heat.
  3. Properties of pure substances and steam tables: Water is the working fluid of most power stations, and it changes phase.
  4. Ideal gases: Air and combustion gases behave very nearly as ideal gases at the pressures used in engines.
  5. The first law for closed systems: Energy is conserved.
  6. The first law for steady-flow systems: Turbines, pumps and boilers run with fluid flowing steadily through them.
  7. Nozzles, turbines, compressors and heat exchangers: The steady-flow energy equation simplifies neatly for each common device.
  8. The second law and heat engines: The first law says energy is conserved; the second says which way processes go and limits how much heat can become work.
  9. The Carnot cycle: The Carnot cycle sets the maximum efficiency any heat engine can reach between two temperatures.
  10. Entropy: Entropy turns the second law into numbers.
  11. Isentropic efficiency: Real turbines and compressors fall short of the ideal reversible process.
  12. The Rankine cycle: Most of the world's electricity from coal, nuclear, biomass and concentrated solar plants comes from the Rankine steam cycle.
  13. Gas power cycles: Otto, Diesel and Brayton: Petrol engines, diesel engines and gas turbines are modelled with air-standard cycles.
  14. Refrigeration and heat pumps: Refrigerators and heat pumps use work to move heat from cold to hot.
  15. Heat transfer by conduction: Thermodynamics says how much heat flows; heat transfer says how fast.
  16. Convection and radiation: Heat also leaves surfaces by convection to fluids and by radiation.

School 7: Fluid Mechanics

14 lessons

Fluid Mechanics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Fluid properties: A fluid deforms continuously under shear.
  2. Pressure and manometry: Pressure in a fluid at rest increases with depth.
  3. Hydrostatic forces on surfaces: Dams, tanks and gates must resist the force of the fluid they hold.
  4. Buoyancy and stability: Ships, buoys and submarines rely on buoyancy and must remain stable.
  5. Conservation of mass and continuity: Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a flow.
  6. The Bernoulli equation: The Bernoulli equation relates pressure, velocity and height along a streamline.
  7. The momentum equation and jet forces: Changing the momentum of a flowing fluid requires a force.
  8. Flow measurement: Engineers need to measure flow in pipes, channels and air streams.
  9. Viscous flow and the Reynolds number: Flow can be smooth and layered or chaotic and mixing.
  10. Pipe flow and friction losses: Friction in pipes causes a loss of head that pumps must overcome.
  11. Minor losses and pipe systems: Bends, valves, entrances and exits add further losses to a pipe system.
  12. Pumps and system curves: Pumps supply the energy to move fluid through systems.
  13. Boundary layers, drag and lift: Bodies moving through fluids experience drag and, if shaped suitably, lift.
  14. Dimensional analysis and similitude: Dimensional analysis reduces the number of variables in a problem and allows small-scale models to predict full-scale behaviour.

School 8: Electrical Circuits and Electronics

18 lessons

Electrical Circuits and Electronics: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Charge, current, voltage and power: Every circuit calculation rests on four quantities: charge, current, voltage and power.
  2. Ohm's law and resistor networks: Ohm's law links voltage, current and resistance, and lets complicated networks of resistors be reduced to a single equivalent.
  3. Kirchhoff's laws: Kirchhoff's two laws express conservation of charge and of energy in a circuit and are the basis of all circuit analysis.
  4. Nodal and mesh analysis: Nodal and mesh analysis turn Kirchhoff's laws into a systematic method that always gives the right number of equations.
  5. Thévenin and Norton equivalents: Any linear two-terminal network, however complex, behaves like a single source with a single resistance.
  6. Maximum power transfer: A source delivers the most power to a load when the load is matched to the source's internal resistance.
  7. Capacitors and inductors: Capacitors store energy in an electric field and inductors store it in a magnetic field.
  8. RC and RL transients: When a circuit containing a capacitor or inductor is switched, voltages and currents change exponentially before settling.
  9. AC fundamentals: Mains electricity and most signals are alternating.
  10. Impedance and AC circuit analysis: In AC circuits, resistors, inductors and capacitors are represented by complex impedances, so the DC methods of circuit analysis carry over.
  11. AC power and power factor: In AC circuits with reactance, not all the current does useful work.
  12. Three-phase systems: Electricity is generated, transmitted and used in large amounts as three-phase AC.
  13. Diodes and rectifiers: A diode lets current flow easily in one direction only.
  14. Transistors as switches and amplifiers: Transistors are the building blocks of modern electronics, used as switches in digital and power circuits and as amplifiers in analogue circuits.
  15. Operational amplifiers: The operational amplifier is a versatile integrated circuit used for amplification, filtering, signal conditioning and comparison.
  16. Digital logic: Digital systems represent information with two states and process it with logic gates.
  17. Transformers: Transformers change AC voltages efficiently and make long-distance electricity transmission possible.
  18. Electric motors and generators: Electric machines convert between electrical and mechanical energy and account for a large share of electricity use.

School 9: Control Systems and Signals

14 lessons

Control Systems and Signals: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. Introduction to control systems: A control system makes a physical process behave as we want, despite disturbances.
  2. Laplace transforms: The Laplace transform turns linear differential equations into algebraic equations, which makes analysing dynamic systems much easier.
  3. Transfer functions and block diagrams: A transfer function describes how a linear system turns an input into an output in the Laplace domain.
  4. Modelling physical systems: To design a controller we first need a mathematical model of the system.
  5. First-order system response: Many systems, from heaters to motors, respond like first-order systems.
  6. Second-order system response: Second-order systems, such as suspensions and positioning systems, can oscillate.
  7. Steady-state error: Steady-state error is the difference between the desired and actual output after transients die away.
  8. Stability and the Routh–Hurwitz criterion: A control system must be stable before anything else.
  9. Feedback and PID control: The PID controller is the most widely used controller in industry.
  10. Root locus: The root locus shows how the closed-loop poles move as a gain changes, giving a visual guide to stability and response.
  11. Frequency response and Bode plots: Frequency response describes how a system responds to sinusoidal inputs of different frequencies.
  12. Sensors and actuators: Sensors measure the variables a control system needs, and actuators apply its decisions to the physical world.
  13. Digital control and sampling: Most modern controllers run on microprocessors that sample signals at discrete times.
  14. Signals and the Fourier series: Any periodic signal can be built from sinusoids.

School 10: Design and Manufacturing

15 lessons

Design and Manufacturing: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The engineering design process: Engineering design turns a need into a product or system that can be made and used safely.
  2. Requirements and specifications: A good specification defines what a design must achieve without dictating how.
  3. Concept generation and selection: Good designs usually come from exploring many concepts before choosing one.
  4. Engineering drawing and CAD: Engineering drawings and CAD models communicate exactly what is to be made.
  5. Tolerances and fits: No part can be made to an exact size, so engineers specify how much variation is acceptable.
  6. Design for manufacture and assembly: The way a part is designed largely determines how easily and cheaply it can be made and assembled.
  7. Factors of safety and design codes: Engineers cannot know loads and strengths exactly, so they design with margins.
  8. Bolted and welded joints: Most machines and structures are held together by bolts or welds.
  9. Shafts and bearings: Shafts transmit power by rotating, and bearings support them.
  10. Gears and power transmission: Gears, belts and chains transmit power between shafts while changing speed and torque.
  11. Casting and forming: Many metal parts are shaped by casting molten metal or by deforming solid metal.
  12. Machining processes: Machining removes material to produce accurate shapes and fine finishes.
  13. Additive manufacturing: Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer from digital models.
  14. Quality, metrology and inspection: Quality means meeting requirements consistently.
  15. Lean manufacturing and production systems: Lean manufacturing aims to deliver value to customers with minimum waste.

School 11: Engineering in Society

15 lessons

Engineering in Society: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The statement of ethical principles: The Engineering Council and the Royal Academy of Engineering publish a joint Statement of Ethical Principles that guides all UK engineers.
  2. Professional responsibility and whistleblowing: Engineers have duties to their employers, clients, profession and the public, and these can conflict.
  3. Learning from engineering failures: Many advances in engineering have come from studying failures.
  4. Codes, standards and regulation: Engineers work within a framework of laws, regulations, codes of practice and standards.
  5. Health and safety law and the HSE: The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 is the foundation of workplace safety law in Great Britain.
  6. Risk assessment: Risk assessment is the legal and practical basis for controlling workplace hazards.
  7. Hazard analysis: FMEA and HAZOP: Systematic hazard analysis finds problems before they cause harm.
  8. Human factors and safety culture: Most accidents involve people as well as hardware.
  9. Sustainable development and engineering: Sustainable development asks engineers to meet today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
  10. Life-cycle assessment: Life-cycle assessment measures the environmental impacts of a product or service from raw materials to disposal.
  11. Energy systems and net zero: The UK has a legal target of net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050.
  12. The circular economy and materials efficiency: A circular economy keeps products and materials in use for as long as possible instead of making, using and throwing away.
  13. Embodied carbon in infrastructure: Building and maintaining infrastructure releases large amounts of carbon before any user benefits.
  14. Engineering for global development: Billions of people still lack safe water, sanitation, energy and transport.
  15. Diversity and inclusion in engineering: Engineering serves everyone, so it benefits from people with different backgrounds and from designs that work for all users.

School 12: Projects and Professional Practice

14 lessons

Projects and Professional Practice: key terms, sample flashcards and a quick quiz

  1. The project lifecycle and planning: Engineering work is usually delivered as projects with a defined goal, budget and timescale.
  2. Scheduling: Gantt charts and the critical path: A schedule shows when each activity happens and which activities control the finish date.
  3. Engineering economics: Engineers must show that projects are worth their cost.
  4. Risk management in projects: Every project faces uncertain events that could harm or help its objectives.
  5. Teamwork and roles: Engineering is almost always a team activity.
  6. Technical report writing: Technical reports communicate engineering work so that others can understand, check and act on it.
  7. Presentations and technical communication: Engineers must explain their work to clients, colleagues and the public.
  8. Searching literature and standards: Engineers need reliable information from journals, books, standards and data.
  9. Laboratory practice and experimental design: Laboratory work tests ideas and generates data that others rely on.
  10. Professional registration: EngTech, IEng and CEng: Professional registration shows that an engineer has met recognised standards of competence and commitment.
  11. The UK-SPEC competences: UK-SPEC describes the competence and commitment expected of registered engineers and technicians.
  12. Continuing professional development: Engineering knowledge changes quickly, so registered engineers must keep learning throughout their careers.
  13. Intellectual property and contracts: Engineers create valuable ideas and work under legal agreements.
  14. The individual project: Most engineering degrees end with an individual project in which students investigate a problem independently.
